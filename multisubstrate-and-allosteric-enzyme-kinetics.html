1. When one substrate is not enough

Imagine an enzyme transferring a phosphate from ATP to glucose. Glucose alone cannot provide the phosphate; ATP alone cannot receive it. A productive encounter must involve two chemically different reactants. The familiar Michaelis–Menten question, how rapidly does an enzyme work at a given substrate concentration, becomes a two-dimensional problem. How does the enzyme arrange the reactants, and how does the concentration of each determine the measured rate?

The preceding chapters introduced active sites, the steady-state enzyme–substrate complex and the effects of inhibitors on different enzyme forms. We now extend that logic to another genuine substrate. A second substrate differs from an inhibitor because it participates in the chemical transformation and is consumed or converted during productive turnover. Nevertheless, its binding can change the population of enzyme states available to the first substrate. The rate measured at a particular first-substrate concentration depends on the concentration of the second as well.

This chapter has two distinct themes. Multisubstrate kinetics describes the order of chemical participants in a catalytic cycle. Cooperativity and allostery describe communication between binding sites or conformational states. A two-substrate enzyme need not show cooperative binding, and a cooperative protein need not use two different substrates. A single catalytic centre can perform a two-substrate reaction; an oligomer can respond cooperatively to several molecules of the same substrate. Keeping these questions separate is essential for interpreting examination graphs.

The biological relevance is immediate. Transferases transfer chemical groups between donor and acceptor molecules, oxidoreductases exchange electrons with diffusible cosubstrates, and ligases use energetic reactants to join molecules. Cells must adjust these reactions as nutrient supply, ATP and regulatory metabolites change. We shall first trace the individual catalytic intermediates, derive their measurable rate laws, and then examine how conformational transitions change the response to ligands.

2. Substrates, products and enzyme intermediates

Write a generic reversible reaction as A + B ⇌ P + Q, where A and B are substrates and P and Q are products. E denotes the free enzyme, EA the enzyme containing A and EAB the enzyme containing both substrates. In the Cleland nomenclature, Bi Bi describes two substrates and two products, Bi Uni two substrates and one product, and Uni Bi one substrate and two products. These names count chemical participants, not active sites or polypeptide chains. The stoichiometric equation does not tell us which molecule binds first.

Distinguish a diffusible cosubstrate from a cofactor retained throughout many turnovers. In many dehydrogenase reactions NAD⁺ is converted to NADH, released, and replaced by a fresh NAD⁺ molecule in a later catalytic cycle. It therefore behaves as a substrate. Tightly bound FAD in certain flavoproteins may change oxidation state without dissociating each cycle. Whether a chemical compound is a cosubstrate or a prosthetic group depends on the particular enzyme. E′ will indicate a modified form of E, not a new enzyme molecule.

During initial-rate measurements we assume products have not accumulated sufficiently to affect the forward velocity, substrates are not appreciably depleted, and the concentrations of enzyme intermediates are approximately at steady state. Free substrate concentration can often be approximated by added concentration when substrate is in great excess over enzyme. In other situations this approximation needs care. For example, Mg²⁺ forms complexes with ATP, so total ATP need not equal the concentration of the catalytically relevant species. A two-substrate rate law is meaningful only when the concentrations refer to the appropriate chemical forms.

For a Bi Bi reaction, the first product and second product are ultimately formed in equal amounts per complete turnover. They need not be released simultaneously from an individual enzyme molecule. A rapid transient immediately after mixing may reflect the accumulation of an intermediate, while steady-state rates report the repeated complete cycle. An initial-rate experiment is normally designed to measure the latter before depletion or appreciable reverse reaction.

3. Sequential catalysis: bringing both substrates together

In a sequential mechanism all substrates bind before the first product is released. For a Bi Bi reaction, the productive pathway includes a ternary substrate complex EAB. This arrangement suits a reaction in which chemical transfer requires donor and acceptor to be positioned together. Their bonds may be strained, reactive groups oriented and charges stabilised by the active site. After chemistry, the products leave and the enzyme is regenerated.

In an ordered sequential mechanism the productive binding order is obligatory. If A binds first, EA accepts B; free E does not enter a productive cycle by binding B first. The structural explanation may be that A induces the formation of a B-binding pocket, or that it positions a catalytic group required for productive B binding. Do not equate obligatory order with an absolute inability of B to contact free E: an unproductive binding mode can exist. Product release may also follow an obligatory order.

A classical example is lactate dehydrogenase, commonly represented by an ordered scheme for pyruvate reduction. NADH binds before pyruvate, the ternary complex allows hydride transfer, lactate leaves and NAD⁺ dissociates. In the reverse reaction the route is traversed in the reverse order. The example explains why an organic coenzyme can be a kinetic substrate. It does not imply that every dehydrogenase must bind its nicotinamide coenzyme first.

In a random sequential mechanism both EA and EB can lead to EAB. Either A or B can enter first. Random does not mean equal affinities, equal binding rates or equal flux through both paths. When A is abundant and B scarce, the EA route may dominate; reversing their concentrations can favour the EB route. Creatine kinase is commonly taught as an example of random sequential transfer between phosphocreatine and ADP. Both binding partners must still be present in the productive ternary complex before transfer.

An important experimental limitation follows immediately. The overall stoichiometric equation is identical for ordered and random mechanisms, and a structure containing both substrates is compatible with either. To identify the order, we need evidence about whether each binary complex is productive, not just proof that both ligands can eventually occupy the enzyme. In an examination, if a question supplies only intersecting reciprocal lines, it usually supports the sequential class but does not establish which of these two variants is operating.

4. Ping-pong catalysis: the enzyme carries a chemical group

A different possibility is to transfer a group from the first substrate to the enzyme before the second substrate arrives. In this double-displacement or ping-pong mechanism, E binds A, releases P and becomes E′. E′ then binds B, releases Q and becomes E again. The defining observation is that one product has left before the second substrate enters the productive cycle. There is no obligatory ternary complex containing both original substrates in the simple ping-pong scheme.

The enzyme is not consumed in the reaction. It acts as a temporary chemical carrier. The first half-reaction leaves a chemical change in E′, such as a modified cofactor or a transferred group. The second half-reaction reverses that change. Both half-reactions are necessary for sustained turnover even when each can proceed independently for a limited time.

Aspartate aminotransferase provides a biologically meaningful example. It catalyses aspartate + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ oxaloacetate + glutamate. During the first half-reaction, pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) accepts the amino group of aspartate and becomes pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP), allowing oxaloacetate to leave. During the second, α-ketoglutarate accepts the amino group from PMP, glutamate leaves, and PLP is regenerated. Following the nitrogen atom explains how two substrates participate without sharing an obligatory ternary complex. PLP is enzyme-associated rather than a net reactant.

If the first substrate is added to the enzyme in the absence of the second, a limited amount of first product may form as the enzyme accumulates in its modified state. Continued production cannot occur indefinitely without regeneration of E. If the modified enzyme can then react with B in the absence of A, that is chemically informative evidence for two separated half-reactions. An isotope-exchange experiment between A and P in the absence of B may also support such a route if the first half-reaction is reversible and the exchange is thermodynamically possible.

A temporary covalent bond directly to protein is not required in every ping-pong mechanism. In the aminotransferase example the relevant change is in the enzyme-associated cofactor. Conversely, observing a covalent intermediate somewhere in a catalytic cycle does not establish that the first product has been released before the next substrate binds. The distinction is the productive order of events.

5. How to design a discriminating initial-rate experiment

A single plot of rate against A at one fixed concentration of B generally cannot distinguish the two simple mechanisms. Both can yield rectangular hyperbolas at fixed B. To obtain the missing information, measure a matrix of initial velocities: choose several fixed B concentrations and at each measure a range of A concentrations. Substrate levels should span the informative region below and above the characteristic kinetic constants, where practical. Keep active enzyme concentration, temperature, pH and ionic environment constant.

At a very low B, the enzyme may bind abundant A but cannot complete many cycles because the B-dependent step is slow. Raising A alone cannot recover the rate measured when both substrates are abundant. Every fixed-B curve thus has its own apparent maximum. It equals the true limiting rate only when B is sufficiently saturating. A single fitted maximum is not automatically an intrinsic enzyme constant; the other assay conditions define its meaning.

To analyse the matrix, one can fit candidate rate equations to the untransformed initial-rate observations. Double-reciprocal plots are useful for displaying ideal mechanistic differences and remain important in NET problems, but reciprocation exaggerates uncertainty in small velocities. A small absolute error at a low rate creates a relatively large error in its reciprocal. The apparent visual precision of a straight line should not be confused with precision in its underlying parameters. Real experiments require replicate measurements, appropriate fitting and attention to uncertainty.

If an assay measures NADH disappearance by optical absorbance, verify that the signal reports the intended reaction. In a coupled assay the auxiliary reaction must not be rate limiting. If high substrate causes inhibition, if product appears too rapidly, or if the enzyme changes its conformation slowly during the measured interval, the simple noncooperative steady-state models may fail. A visibly curved reciprocal plot should not be forced into a straight line solely to classify it as sequential or ping-pong.

The most informative habit is to predict how the fitted apparent parameters ought to respond to the second substrate. We can derive those predictions using equations in which the concentration-dependent terms have clear physical meaning.

6. The two-substrate rate equations

A standard sequential Bi Bi initial-rate equation can be written in terms of free substrate concentrations A and B. V is the maximum rate at joint saturation, KA and KB are characteristic kinetic constants with concentration units, and C is a positive constant with concentration-squared units. For a standard ordered Bi Bi mechanism C = KiA KB, with KiA the first substrate's intercept dissociation constant in that model. Kinetic constants are not automatically microscopic dissociation constants in more general mechanisms.

A particularly transparent derivation comes from rapid-equilibrium random binding with independent affinities. Assume E, EA, EB and EAB rapidly equilibrate and that chemistry from EAB is rate limiting with rate constant k. Mass action gives [EA] = [E]A/KA, [EB] = [E]B/KB and [EAB] = [E]AB/(KAKB). Since the enzyme concentration Et is the sum of these four populations, Et = [E](1 + A/KA + B/KB + AB/(KAKB)). Dividing [EAB] by Et gives the fraction of enzyme in the productive state. Multiply by kEt to obtain the rate. Multiplying numerator and denominator by KAKB yields the standard sequential expression with C = KAKB and V = kEt. In this special case the result equals the product of two independent fractional saturations times V. Interacting sites or ordered steady-state binding need not have that factorisation.

The standard ping-pong equation has the same form except that C is absent. A simple waiting-time model illustrates why. Suppose a cycle contains two serial substrate-dependent waits, 1/(kA A) and 1/(kB B), and a total substrate-independent mean time t0. Mean times add because the enzyme completes one half-reaction and then the other. Mean turnover rate is the reciprocal of their sum. With V = Et/t0, KA = 1/(kA t0) and KB = 1/(kB t0), multiplying by AB gives the ping-pong equation. This is an illustrative irreversible waiting-time model rather than the full microscopic derivation for a reversible enzyme.

Both expressions have units of rate: numerator is rate times concentration squared and every denominator term is concentration squared. At a fixed positive B, increasing A without limit gives VB/(KB+B). Taking B to infinity as well gives V. Taking B to infinity first recovers VA/(KA+A), the familiar one-substrate hyperbola. If either substrate is absent, sustained forward turnover stops. For the ping-pong formula at A = B = 0, the physical velocity is zero even though substitution produces an indeterminate 0/0.

At very small A and B, the constant term of the sequential denominator may dominate, making v approximately VAB/C. If A and B are increased together from very small equal values, the leading rate dependence is quadratic in concentration. For simple ping-pong kinetics the denominator is then dominated by the linear concentration terms, making the leading dependence linear when both increase together. This limiting comparison helps detect a missing term in a proposed equation. It is not a recommended sole experimental test because rates near zero can be difficult to measure accurately.

Sequential: v = VAB/(C + KBA + KAB + AB)

Ping-pong: v = VAB/(KBA + KAB + AB)

7. Why reciprocal lines intersect or remain parallel

Divide the sequential expression by VAB, and group all terms containing reciprocal A. For fixed B the result is a straight line: 1/v = [(KA + C/B)/V](1/A) + (1 + KB/B)/V. Both the slope and vertical intercept depend on B. As B is lowered, the C/B term increases the slope and the KB/B term raises the intercept. For two different B values, subtract their line equations. Their difference is proportional to (C/A + KB)(1/B1 − 1/B2), so the common extrapolated intersection has horizontal coordinate −KB/C. With C = KiA KB this is −1/KiA. The negative value is an extrapolated reciprocal coordinate, not a physical negative concentration. Its vertical position can be above, on or below the horizontal axis depending on the parameters.

The ping-pong equation gives 1/v = (KA/V)(1/A) + (1 + KB/B)/V. Now B changes the intercept but not the slope. The ideal family of lines is parallel. The contrast has a mathematical cause: the sequential reciprocal equation contains a mixed term C/(VAB), and the simple ping-pong equation does not. One can make the same comparison by exchanging the experimental roles of A and B. The algebra need not reflect a chemically symmetrical binding order.

A parallel family should not automatically be called uncompetitive inhibition. In a single-substrate inhibitor experiment, an uncompetitive inhibitor can decrease apparent Km and Vmax proportionally. Here B is another genuine substrate; decreasing its concentration reduces the available throughput of the catalytic cycle. Similar algebra can arise from different molecular situations. Identify what was varied before interpreting a graph.

Finally, a sequential mechanism can appear nearly parallel if the B values are all so high that C/B is negligible compared with KA. The apparent constancy of slope may arise from an inadequate concentration range. A family of truly intersecting lines is consistent with standard sequential mechanisms, but cannot distinguish ordered from random addition. In practical data analysis compare both rate equations on the original velocity matrix and report the uncertainty in the inferred parameters.

Sequential slope = (KA + C/B)/V; ping-pong slope = KA/V

8. Apparent kinetic constants and worked calculations

At fixed B, the sequential denominator can be rearranged to A(KB+B) + C + KAB, where KAB in this expression means KA multiplied by B, not a new constant. Dividing numerator and denominator by (KB+B) puts the expression into the familiar form v = Vapp A/(KA,app+A). The same manipulation applies to the ping-pong equation by setting C to zero. Both mechanisms have the same form of apparent maximum, VB/(KB+B). Their apparent Michaelis constants differ because the sequential mechanism retains the constant C.

Take V = 120 μmol min⁻¹, KA = 2 mM, KB = 3 mM and C = 12 mM². At B = 3 mM the sequential Vapp is 120 × 3/6 = 60 μmol min⁻¹ and KA,app is (12+2×3)/6 = 3 mM. At A = 3 mM the rate is half the apparent maximum, or 30 μmol min⁻¹. Direct substitution gives 1080/(12+9+6+9) = 30. The reciprocal slope is 3/60 = 0.05 mM min μmol⁻¹.

For ping-pong kinetics with the same V, KA and KB, at B = 3 mM the apparent maximum is again 60 but KA,app is 2×3/6 = 1 mM. At A = 3 mM, v = 60×3/(1+3) = 45 μmol min⁻¹. Its reciprocal slope is 1/60 = 0.0167 mM min μmol⁻¹. The different velocities arise from the chosen constants; one cannot conclude that ping-pong enzymes are always faster than sequential enzymes.

Repeat the comparison at B = 6 mM. Sequential Vapp = 80 and KA,app = (12+12)/9 = 2.667 mM, giving slope 0.0333. Ping-pong Vapp = 80 and KA,app = 12/9 = 1.333 mM, giving slope 0.0167 again. The two sequential slopes differ; the two ping-pong slopes agree. This ratio method is often the fastest way to analyse a Part C question containing apparent Km and Vmax rather than a graph.

For an independent rapid-equilibrium random sequential mechanism C = KAKB, so KA,app simplifies to KA regardless of B. The apparent maximum still changes, so the reciprocal slope still changes. The observation that Km appears unchanged is therefore not enough to classify the mechanism. Always compare both parameters and the predictions of their joint dependence on B.

9. What additional experiments reveal binding order?

Cleland's systematic kinetic framework in the 1960s extended the earlier single-substrate rate approach to multi-reactant mechanisms. It allowed scientists to express hypothetical routes through enzyme intermediates as experimentally testable predictions. The historical lesson is not that a particular plot is infallible, but that a mechanism must account for more than one observation.

For example, direct binding of B to free enzyme in the absence of A can test whether EB is possible. Detecting a binary EB complex is compatible with random binding but does not prove that the complex is catalytically productive. Failing to detect EB may be consistent with an ordered route, but weak affinity or limited assay sensitivity could also account for the result. An inhibitor or nonreactive substrate analogue may help identify enzyme states that bind a ligand, but its specificity and binding behaviour must be established.

Product inhibition offers additional information because a product binds to states accessible during the reverse reaction. When a product competes with a substrate, this may suggest that they bind mutually exclusive forms, but interpretation requires knowing the full kinetic context and concentrations of other participants. A single inhibition pattern does not uniquely establish binding order. Combining product-inhibition observations with initial-rate data and evidence for productive binary complexes is stronger.

Chemical evidence is particularly helpful for ping-pong enzymes. If one half-reaction generates a modified form that can be detected, and that form reacts with the second substrate to regenerate the original enzyme, the proposed relay receives direct support. Isotope exchange may establish reversible chemical steps even when sustained net turnover cannot occur. Structural observations can identify bound ligands and potentially important catalytic residues, but a static picture cannot by itself give the chronological order of every binding and release event.

Thus there is a hierarchy of inference. Initial-rate patterns constrain mechanism classes; inhibition and binding experiments distinguish accessible enzyme forms; chemical evidence establishes what happens to atoms or cofactors. No single method establishes every microscopic detail. This is exactly the sort of evidence-based distinction that Part C problems are designed to test.

10. From catalytic routes to cooperative responses

The simple rate laws so far assume that the enzyme can be described by a noncooperative catalytic scheme. Many biological proteins, however, have interacting sites or conformations. Occupancy at one site changes the free energy of binding or reaction at another. The measured substrate response may then depart dramatically from a Michaelis–Menten hyperbola.

Recall haemoglobin and myoglobin from the previous protein chapter. Myoglobin's one oxygen-binding site gives a hyperbolic equilibrium saturation curve; haemoglobin's interacting sites produce a sigmoidal curve. Haemoglobin is not an oxygen-catalysing enzyme, so its curve is a binding curve rather than an initial-rate curve. It is nevertheless a useful demonstration of positive cooperativity. Regulatory enzymes can show a similar steep response when substrate binding changes the probability of functionally different conformations.

Positive cooperativity means that occupancy of one site favours subsequent occupancy relative to an appropriate noninteracting reference. For equivalent independent sites, binding to one does not change the intrinsic affinity of the remaining sites. A cooperative protein may shift toward a higher-affinity conformation as ligand is added, causing the remaining sites to bind more favourably. Negative cooperativity makes subsequent occupancy less favourable and can broaden the overall binding transition. In both cases the interaction concerns the relationship among binding events, not merely whether the graph rises.

Allostery refers to functional communication between different sites or states of a protein. Often a regulatory metabolite binds away from the catalytic site, but substrate binding itself can affect other substrate sites. Homotropic interaction means the same ligand species influences its own binding or activity at other sites; heterotropic interaction involves a chemically different ligand. Either type may be activating or inhibitory. A heterotropic regulator need not produce a sigmoidal curve; positive homotropic cooperativity does not require an additional regulator.

A hyperbolic response increases from one-third to two-thirds as substrate increases from one-half of the half-response concentration to twice it. A positively cooperative response can change much more sharply over the same interval, giving a cell a concentration-sensitive regulatory mechanism. At very high substrate both curves can approach the same plateau. Cooperativity therefore need not mean greater maximum activity, and an allosteric effect need not mean a change in substrate affinity alone.

11. The Hill equation: measuring steepness rather than counting sites

In 1910 A. V. Hill proposed a mathematical description of the cooperative oxygen-binding response of haemoglobin. For a single binding site at equilibrium, mass action gives Y = S/(Kd+S), where Y is fractional occupancy and Kd is the dissociation constant. Rearrange to obtain Y/(1−Y) = S/Kd. An idealised all-or-none association E + nS ⇌ ESₙ would instead give odds proportional to S raised to the nth power. Real oligomers often populate partially occupied states, so the all-or-none model is not literally universal. The Hill equation replaces the exponent with an empirical response coefficient nH.

The half-response parameter S0.5 has concentration units. At S = S0.5, Y is exactly one-half for every positive nH. When analysing initial rates, define Y = v/V and call the half-response concentration K0.5 if desired. This is not automatically the mechanistic Michaelis constant and is not necessarily a microscopic binding dissociation constant. A binding assay and an activity assay measure different quantities even if their fitted equations have the same shape.

Divide Y by (1−Y) and take logarithms. The slope of an ideal Hill plot equals nH: log[Y/(1−Y)] = nH log(S/S0.5). Both logarithm arguments are dimensionless; the same logarithm base must be used throughout. At half response the vertical coordinate is zero. Actual Hill plots may be curved over their full concentration range. A local Hill coefficient can be defined as the derivative of log odds with respect to log concentration at half saturation; an empirical fitted value over a wider range need not equal that local slope.

Take S0.5 = 4 mM and nH = 2. At S = 2 mM the fractional rate is 4/(16+4) = 0.2. At S = 8 mM it is 64/(16+64) = 0.8. With V = 100 μmol min⁻¹ the velocities are 20 and 80. The comparable hyperbolic enzyme gives 33.3 and 66.7. Both have the same midpoint and limiting rate, but their sensitivities differ. This is the meaning of the Hill exponent.

The odds form gives a useful calculation shortcut. At Y = 0.2, the odds are 1/4. Doubling substrate when nH = 2 multiplies odds by four, giving odds one and Y = 0.5. Doubling again gives odds four and Y = 0.8. If instead experimental fractions are 0.2 at 1 mM and 0.8 at 4 mM, the odds increase sixteenfold while concentration increases fourfold; the secant Hill slope is log(16)/log(4) = 2. An ideal Hill fit would place the midpoint at 2 mM. Two data points alone do not prove a global Hill model.

Y = SnH/(S0.5nH + SnH)

log[Y/(1−Y)] = nH log(S/S0.5)

12. The limitations of interpreting Hill coefficients

For ordinary equilibrium binding to equivalent sites, a Hill slope greater than one supports positive cooperativity, a value near one is consistent with independent binding, and a value less than one can indicate negative cooperativity. However, a mixture of independent sites with different affinities also broadens a binding curve. High-affinity sites fill at low ligand concentrations and lower-affinity sites at higher concentrations. A fitted value below one is not enough to distinguish heterogeneous independent binding from genuine negative coupling.

For equilibrium binding to an oligomer with N ligand sites, the Hill slope cannot exceed N under the usual binding assumptions. It commonly falls below N because partially liganded states occupy an appreciable part of the response range. A tetramer may have four physical ligand sites yet give nH = 2.6. There cannot be 2.6 physical sites, and rounding to three does not determine stoichiometry. The all-or-none idealisation approaches the maximum possible equilibrium steepness; ordinary sequential filling spreads out the transition. At very low and very high ligand levels, local Hill slopes commonly approach one in standard models because individual binding steps dominate the limiting behaviour.

For enzyme rate measurements the Hill coefficient is instead an empirical kinetic response parameter. A sigmoid can occur in a monomer with one substrate-binding site if catalytic turnover and slow conformational transitions are coupled. Mammalian glucokinase is a familiar example of monomeric kinetic cooperativity. At high glucose concentrations the enzyme can bind substrate again before relaxing to a less active state, favouring rapid repeated turnover. Equilibrium site count and kinetic response steepness must therefore not be conflated. The equilibrium binding upper bound cannot be blindly applied to non-equilibrium catalytic rate data.

It is also possible for a regulatory ligand to alter activity without detectable homotropic cooperativity. An allosteric inhibitor may reduce the plateau or shift the midpoint without changing the Hill coefficient. Conversely, an enzyme may display cooperative substrate response without any distinct regulator. The Hill equation is a useful description of a curve, not a complete explanation of the protein's mechanism. To address mechanism we must consider conformational models.

13. The concerted MWC model: a ligand selects a conformation

Monod, Wyman and Changeux proposed their concerted model in 1965 to account for cooperative regulatory proteins. The classical model assumes a symmetric oligomer that interconverts between two pre-existing conformations, T (tense) and R (relaxed). All subunits in a given oligomer share the same conformational state. A mixed occupancy is permitted, for example one ligand bound to an R dimer, but a mixed T/R conformation within one oligomer is excluded by strict concerted symmetry.

In the usual positively cooperative example, R has a higher affinity for the ligand than T. Without ligand, T may dominate although some R already exists. Adding ligand stabilises R preferentially, shifting the population toward the high-affinity state. It is not necessary for every binding site to be occupied before the conformational transition occurs. The model describes conformational selection: the ligand changes the relative population of pre-existing states.

Within each conformation the classical sites are equivalent and independent. How, then, does cooperativity arise? The first binding event changes the relative probability that the entire oligomer is in R rather than T. The second ligand is now more likely to encounter an oligomer already in its high-affinity state. The coupling is mediated by a shared conformational equilibrium, even though intrinsic site affinities within one fixed conformation remain unchanged.

Define L0 = [T0]/[R0], the unliganded population ratio. KR and KT are single-site dissociation constants in the R and T states respectively; when R binds more strongly, KR is smaller than KT and c = KR/KT is less than one. For N equivalent sites the weights of R and T relative to unliganded R are WR = (1+S/KR) raised to N and WT = L0(1+S/KT) raised to N. Each binomial includes all possible occupancy states: for a dimer the factor 1+2x+x² counts the unliganded state, two singly liganded possibilities and the doubly liganded state. This is just independent binding within a fixed conformation.

The R fraction is WR/(WR+WT), whereas the total fractional site occupancy is the population-weighted average of S/(KR+S) and S/(KT+S). These are different observables. R can exist without ligand and T can bind ligand. If the conformations also differ in catalytic activity, the rate depends on both occupancy and catalytic constants; the R fraction cannot automatically be equated with v/V.

Take N = 2, L0 = 9, KR = 1 mM and KT = 10 mM. Without ligand the R fraction is 0.10. At S = 5 mM, WR = 36 and WT = 9×1.5² = 20.25, giving R fraction 36/56.25 = 0.64. Overall site occupancy is 0.64×5/6 + 0.36×5/15 = 0.6533. Both observables increase, but they are not equal. A heterotropic activator preferring R can reduce the effective T/R ratio, and an inhibitor preferring T can increase it. The resulting activity changes connect ligand binding to the population of enzyme conformations.

R fraction = (1+S/KR)N/[(1+S/KR)N+L0(1+S/KT)N]

14. The KNF model: local induced changes

In 1966, Koshland, Némethy and Filmer developed an alternative model based on sequential conformational changes. Ligand binding can induce a local change in the subunit to which it binds. Neighbouring subunits may initially retain their previous conformations; the changed interface modifies their binding propensities. Further binding can produce further conformational changes. Mixed conformational states are therefore permitted in the oligomer.

Imagine a dimer in which both unoccupied subunits have conformation A. Binding to one converts that subunit to B. If the resulting B–A interface makes it easier for the neighbouring subunit to bind and convert, the interaction is positively cooperative. If the interface makes the neighbouring conversion less favourable, the interaction is negatively cooperative. The KNF framework naturally accommodates either sign of cooperativity. The basic symmetric two-state MWC model for one ligand with different affinities in T and R instead produces positive homotropic cooperativity. This statement concerns the classical idealisations, not every extension of either model.

Could a sigmoid alone identify one model? No. Both may generate sigmoidal binding curves. Observing a stable, functionally relevant oligomer containing a clearly T-like and R-like subunit at the same time challenges strict concerted symmetry and is compatible with sequential change. On the other hand, an all-R final structure does not prove MWC, because a sequential mechanism may ultimately convert all subunits. A population of conformations before ligand addition supports conformational selection; subsequent local ligand-induced adjustment can also occur in real proteins.

The distinction between the two classical models remains important for examinations because their assumptions are clear and testable. Real proteins need not follow either ideal perfectly. Their structural ensembles and interfaces may exhibit both selection and induced adjustment. Avoid equating sequential conformational change with sequential multisubstrate kinetics: the former concerns communication among subunits, the latter concerns when substrates bind and products leave. A single enzyme could exhibit both, neither, or one without the other.

15. Allosteric effectors: K-type and V-type responses

Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) in Escherichia coli catalyses carbamoyl phosphate plus aspartate in pyrimidine biosynthesis. Its catalytic and regulatory subunits form a communicating assembly. Aspartate can exhibit cooperative kinetics, CTP acts as an allosteric inhibitor, and ATP can act as an activator. This provides a natural bridge from conformational models to metabolic regulation. The pathway logic is developed in the next chapter; here we focus on the kinetic evidence.

In an ATCase activity experiment, carbamoyl phosphate must be controlled while aspartate is varied. If both concentrations change, altered aspartate response may be due to the ordinary two-substrate kinetic dependence rather than a regulatory interaction. This links the two halves of the chapter. A convincing allosteric interpretation requires knowledge of the chemical participants and control of their concentrations.

A K-type effect primarily changes the substrate concentration required for half-maximal activity, without substantially changing the limiting rate. A V-type effect primarily changes the limiting rate, without substantially changing the half-response concentration when each curve is normalised to its own maximum. Some effectors change both and may alter the Hill coefficient. These categories are descriptions of observed behaviour, not proof of particular binding mechanisms. An allosteric inhibitor need not correspond to a pure noncompetitive inhibitor in the Michaelis–Menten sense.

Suppose V = 100 units, S0.5 = 5 mM and nH = 2. An activator lowers S0.5 to 2.5 mM without changing V or nH. At S = 5 mM the control rate is 50, while activated rate = 100×25/(6.25+25) = 80. Both rates approach 100 at very high substrate. This illustrates K-type activation. If instead a regulator decreases V to 60 with S0.5 unchanged, the rate at 5 mM becomes 30 and the plateau becomes 60. This is an ideal V-type inhibitory effect. Normalising both curves to their own maxima would hide that second effect if the absolute rates were not also reported.

Phosphofructokinase-1 provides a useful example of a molecule with two roles. ATP is a catalytic substrate for phosphate transfer to fructose-6-phosphate, yet at high concentration it can also exert inhibitory regulatory effects in appropriate enzyme forms and conditions. Increasing ATP may initially support catalysis and later reduce activity relative to its optimum. Free Mg²⁺ and other regulators influence the observed response. One chemical species can act at a catalytic site and at a regulatory site; the two binding contexts must be distinguished.

Allostery describes functional coupling. Feedback inhibition describes a pathway architecture in which a downstream product reduces an earlier reaction's activity. Allostery is one way feedback can be implemented, but not every allosteric effector is a feedback signal. A metabolite can also act as a feedforward signal or report a change in cellular energy status. The next node connects those categories to phosphorylation, proteolytic activation and other means of regulation.

16. Putting the evidence together

Suppose an unknown enzyme requires A and B, exhibits a sigmoid when A is varied at high B, and responds to an additional metabolite. First establish whether A and B are chemically consumed and what the overall stoichiometry is. A two-substrate matrix tests the ordinary rate dependence, but if the curves are not hyperbolic the simple equations may be inadequate. Do not force allosteric kinetic data into straight reciprocal lines.

Next compare equilibrium ligand binding with catalytic activity. If equilibrium binding of A is sigmoid, cooperative occupancy is supported under the appropriate model assumptions. If only initial-rate curves are sigmoid, a kinetic conformational mechanism is also possible. An equilibrium measurement reports how molecules distribute among states without sustained catalytic flux. A rate assay reports the speed of chemical turnover, which can depend on the history of those states. Different response shapes do not necessarily contradict one another.

For an additional metabolite, determine whether the plateau, half-response concentration, or steepness changes. If the regulator is one of the substrates, control its chemical form and test whether the ordinary catalytic concentration dependence explains the observation. Evidence for another binding site, a regulator-sensitive conformational transition or a selective change upon mutation can support an allosteric interpretation. Mutation may also destabilise the oligomer, so an altered Hill coefficient should not be assigned to a particular interface without checking assembly and stability.

Finally distinguish the strongest claim justified by each observation. Intersecting reciprocal lines support a standard sequential rate law; they do not prove ordered addition. Parallel lines support a simple ping-pong law over the measured range; they do not identify the chemical carrier. A Hill coefficient greater than one reports a steep response, not an exact site count. Mixed stable conformations challenge strict MWC symmetry but are not unique proof of KNF. A single experiment constrains hypotheses rather than proving every detail.

This completes a causal progression from binding to chemistry to protein communication: the enzyme's structure determines accessible molecular states; their populations and transitions determine velocities; cellular ligands change those states and thereby alter metabolic flux. In the next chapter we shall examine how these kinetic principles support the regulation of entire biochemical pathways.

17. Part B — conceptual practice

These are original NET/SET-style questions. Try to explain the reason before revealing the answer.

1. A Bi Bi mechanism releases P before B binds. Which class fits?

A. Ordered sequential; B. Random sequential; C. Ping-pong; D. Single-substrate

Answer and explanation

C. E′ can carry a chemical change between two half-reactions. Both sequential mechanisms require both substrates before first product release.

2. Ordered versus random sequential differs primarily in what?

A. Productive binding order; B. Subunit count; C. Number of products; D. Presence of EAB

Answer and explanation

A. Both may have the same stoichiometry and EAB. Random allows either productive route.

3. Parallel reciprocal lines at fixed different B concentrations support what?

A. Random binding proof; B. Ping-pong; C. B is uncompetitive inhibitor; D. No binding of A

Answer and explanation

B. The ideal ping-pong slope does not depend on B. The other interpretations do not follow.

4. Which quantity has concentration units?

A. Hill slope; B. Fractional occupancy; C. S0.5; D. Binding odds

Answer and explanation

C. The other quantities are dimensionless. S0.5 is not automatically microscopic Kd.

5. Which state is excluded by strict classical MWC?

A. Unliganded R; B. Partly occupied T; C. Mixed T/R subunits; D. Fully liganded R

Answer and explanation

C. The concerted model constrains conformation, not occupancy.

6. A chemically distinct metabolite increases substrate response. What is its role?

A. Negative homotropic; B. Positive heterotropic; C. Obligatory substrate; D. Irreversible inhibitor

Answer and explanation

B. A different ligand increases activity; neither consumption nor irreversible modification follows.

7. A tetramer's equilibrium Hill coefficient is 2.6. What follows?

A. Exactly 2.6 sites; B. Exactly three sites; C. Positive cooperativity supported; D. Independent binding

Answer and explanation

C. Hill slope measures steepness. The physical site count requires separate stoichiometric data.

8. A one-site monomer exhibits sigmoidal rates. What is possible?

A. Kinetic cooperativity; B. Necessarily four sites; C. Strict concerted oligomer mechanism; D. No catalysis

Answer and explanation

A. Slow conformational transitions may create kinetic cooperativity without multiple equilibrium sites.

9. What does PMP do in aminotransferase?

A. Carries amino group temporarily; B. Is consumed each turnover; C. Is the amino donor substrate; D. Forces EAB

Answer and explanation

A. PMP transfers the amino group to the acceptor and regenerates PLP.

10. An effector decreases limiting velocity but leaves half-response unchanged. Classify it.

A. K-type only; B. V-type; C. Necessarily competitive; D. Necessarily covalent

Answer and explanation

B. The plateau changes. A unique inhibition mechanism cannot be inferred.

18. Part C — quantitative and analytical practice

The following original data and reasoning problems require mechanistic interpretation, calculations and careful attention to assumptions.

1. Sequential velocity

V=90 units, KA=2 mM, KB=4 mM, C=8 mM². Calculate v at A=2, B=4 mM.

Answer and explanation

Numerator 720, denominator 8+8+8+8=32. v=22.5 units. Since C=KAKB, independent fractional saturations each equal 1/2 and yield V/4.

2. Ping-pong comparison

With the preceding parameters set C=0. Find v.

Answer and explanation

Denominator 24, so v=30 units. This is a consequence of the chosen model and constants, not a universal speed advantage.

3. Apparent constants

V=100, KA=6 mM, KB=2 mM, ping-pong. Compute apparent parameters at B=2 and 6 mM.

Answer and explanation

At B=2, Vapp=50, Kapp=3. At B=6, Vapp=75, Kapp=4.5. Slopes both 0.06 mM per rate unit.

4. Identify pattern

Two B levels give Vapp 20 and 50, Kapp 4 and 5 mM. Is simple ping-pong compatible?

Answer and explanation

Slopes 0.2 and 0.1 differ. Simple ping-pong predicts identical slopes; sequential is compatible but not uniquely proven.

5. Intersection

Sequential C=18 mM², KB=3 mM. Find common reciprocal-A coordinate.

Answer and explanation

−KB/C = −1/6 mM⁻¹. It is a mathematical extrapolation.

6. Hill rate

nH=3, S0.5=2 mM. Calculate Y at 1 and 4 mM.

Answer and explanation

Ratios (S/S0.5)³ are 1/8 and 8. Y=1/9 and 8/9 respectively.

7. Hill slope

Y changes from 0.1 at 1 mM to 0.9 at 9 mM. Find secant slope.

Answer and explanation

Odds change by factor 81, concentrations by 9. log(81)/log(9)=2. This is not an exact site count.

8. K-type effect

S0.5 changes from 4 to 8 mM, V=120, nH=2. Compare rates at S=4 mM.

Answer and explanation

Control 60. Regulated 120×16/(64+16)=24. Same plateau, right shift: K-type inhibition.

9. MWC population

Dimer L0=4, KR=1 mM, KT=5 mM, S=5 mM. Find R fraction.

Answer and explanation

WR=36, WT=16, R fraction=36/52=0.6923. Without ligand it is 1/5=0.2.

10. MWC occupancy

Use the previous example to calculate site occupancy.

Answer and explanation

Weighted Y=(36/52)(5/6)+(16/52)(1/2)=38/52=0.7308. Not identical to R fraction.

11. Assertion–reason

A: Intersecting lines cannot distinguish ordered from random. R: Both can contain EAB and have B-dependent slopes.

Answer and explanation

Both true; R explains A. Extra binding/order evidence is required.

12. Assertion–reason

A: A tetramer with nH=2.2 has exactly two ligand sites. R: Hill slope counts sites.

Answer and explanation

Both false. Partial occupancy broadens the curve and the fitted slope need not be an integer.

13. Model discrimination

A stable partially occupied dimer has one T-like and one R-like subunit. What does this support?

Answer and explanation

Mixed conformations conflict with strict classical MWC and are compatible with KNF. They do not uniquely prove every KNF assumption.

14. Confounding substrate

ATP shifts the response of an enzyme that uses ATP and X. Is allostery established?

Answer and explanation

No. ATP concentration alone alters two-substrate kinetics. Control free catalytic ATP/Mg²⁺ and investigate extra binding or regulatory-site effects.

15. Negative cooperativity

A binding curve gives nH=0.65. Name two explanations and a discriminating approach.

Answer and explanation

Negative coupling or independent sites with heterogeneous affinities. Analyse a homogeneous oligomer, measure site-specific affinities/stoichiometry and compare explicit binding models.

19. Consolidation and references

Begin revision by drawing the productive routes. Sequential: all substrates bind before any product leaves. Ping-pong: first product release precedes second substrate binding, and E′ is regenerated to E. Derive both reciprocal expressions and explain the mixed C/(VAB) term. Calculate apparent parameters at two fixed B levels and compare their slopes. Check the limit B → infinity to recover the ordinary Michaelis–Menten form.

Next sketch two substrate curves with equal half-response and plateau, one hyperbolic and one sigmoid. Convert fractional response into odds and derive the Hill plot. Distinguish the equilibrium binding Hill coefficient from a kinetic response coefficient. Explain why neither is a literal count of sites. Draw the concerted two-state model and a sequential mixed-conformation intermediate. Predict how a ligand preferring R or T would alter the population, and distinguish K-type from V-type effects.
