1. The physiological problem: producing a rhythm without a command for every beat

The cardiovascular system needs more than a set of chambers and valves. Pressure must be generated repeatedly, and the order of contraction must be coordinated with the direction in which blood can travel. In a mammal, the atria normally activate before the ventricles. The ventricles then produce a large rise in pressure, eject blood and relax sufficiently to accept another volume. If all these muscle cells were activated independently at unrelated times, the mechanical work of one region could oppose the work of another. The biological challenge is therefore to establish a rhythm, distribute it through an organised tissue and convert excitation into mechanical tension.

A muscle cell is excitable when an appropriate stimulus can produce a rapid change in its membrane voltage. Excitability alone does not guarantee rhythmic activity. A typical ventricular cardiomyocyte remains at a relatively stable negative membrane potential until a neighbouring cell depolarises it. An autorhythmic cell, in contrast, develops repetitive electrical activity without receiving a separate initiating impulse for each cycle. A group of such cells can form a pacemaker: a source that sets the timing of a larger excitable system. In the adult mammalian heart the principal physiological pacemaker is the sinoatrial node, situated near the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium.

Automaticity, rhythmicity, excitability, conductivity and contractility describe different functions. Automaticity is spontaneous impulse generation. Rhythmicity concerns the temporal pattern of recurring events. Excitability describes the ability to respond to electrical stimulation; conductivity describes transmission of excitation through tissue. Contractility concerns force generation under specified loading conditions. A preparation might show a regular electrical rhythm but almost no contraction because calcium delivery to the myofilaments has failed. Another preparation might contract in response to every externally applied stimulus while showing no spontaneous electrical activity. These two cases illustrate why an electrical measurement cannot automatically substitute for a mechanical one.

The nervous system adjusts cardiovascular performance according to the needs of the animal. This adjustment is not equivalent to supplying an initiating nerve impulse for every mammalian heartbeat. A viable isolated mammalian heart can continue beating when it receives appropriate perfusion, nutrients and oxygen. The experiment establishes that external neural connections are not essential for ordinary impulse generation. It does not establish that cardiac rhythm remains unchanged when those connections are removed, or that an isolated heart can continue beating indefinitely without metabolic support.

2. Myogenic and neurogenic origins of the heartbeat

A heartbeat is termed myogenic when its initiating rhythm arises within specialised muscle cells. It is termed neurogenic when the primary rhythmic drive is supplied by neurons. These terms describe the origin of excitation rather than whether the resulting contraction depends on calcium, whether the animal possesses a closed circulation, or whether nervous regulation is present. Vertebrate hearts are familiar examples of myogenic organs. Several invertebrate groups also possess myogenic hearts. Adult decapod crustaceans, including familiar crab and lobster models, and the horseshoe crab provide classic examples of neurogenic cardiac rhythm.

The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster provides a useful contrasting invertebrate example. Its tubular heart contains intrinsic myogenic pacing activity that can respond to neurotransmitters and hormones, as demonstrated in physiological studies of the fly's cardiac pacemaker. This comparison avoids assuming that arthropod ancestry determines a single origin of heartbeat: a fly and a decapod crustacean can use different primary oscillators even though both possess open circulatory systems. The relevant evidence is discussed in Modulation of the cardiac pacemaker of Drosophila: cellular mechanisms.

A decapod cardiac ganglion is a compact rhythmic neuronal network located in association with the heart. In the extensively studied nine-neuron organisation, four small neurons participate in rhythmic drive and five larger motor neurons deliver excitation to the cardiac muscle. Their organised bursts are translated into contractions through neuromuscular transmission. The ganglion can generate rhythmic output in isolation under suitable conditions. Feedback from the contracting tissue and modulatory signals influence its behaviour in the intact animal. It would be misleading to claim that such a heart depends on a fresh command from the brain for each beat: its local neuronal network is itself a rhythm generator.

An experimental comparison requires care. Imagine disconnecting the extrinsic nerve supply in a mammal and in a crab while leaving the cardiac ganglion intact in the crab. Continued beating in both preparations does not distinguish the two origins, because neither manipulation necessarily removes the actual oscillator. To examine the crab mechanism one must record from or selectively interfere with the local ganglion and establish whether its rhythmic bursts precede muscle excitation. A mammalian experiment can instead record spontaneous potentials in isolated nodal tissue or cells and show that those electrical events persist without neural inputs. Appropriate oxygenation, temperature and preparation viability are necessary controls in either comparison.

The broad classifications should not be converted into a rigid taxonomic rule. The mode of pacing can differ among species and developmental stages. In addition, the term myogenic autoregulation, used later in vascular physiology, describes a local smooth-muscle response to stretch and is not the same concept as the myogenic origin of heartbeat. The comparison matters because similar mechanical tasks can be solved by different biological oscillators. In both cases a rhythm must be transmitted to contractile tissue, and the rhythm can be modified by feedback without being created anew by each feedback signal.

3. From rhythm location to experimental proof

A tissue's ability to respond to stimulation must be separated from its ability to initiate a pattern. A ventricular strip may contract reliably when an electrode delivers an impulse but remain electrically silent between stimuli. An isolated nodal preparation may generate a sequence of action potentials without imposed pacing. Comparing preparations under identical temperature and perfusion conditions therefore addresses two questions: whether the cellular machinery for excitation is present and whether the preparation contains an oscillator capable of driving itself.

The historical frog-heart ligature experiments, commonly associated with Stannius, illustrate how regional anatomy and rhythm generation are linked. The amphibian heart includes a sinus venosus upstream of the atrial chambers. Interruption of communication between the sinus region and more downstream tissue can separate their rhythms. The sinus region may continue to beat while downstream chambers initially cease contracting or subsequently display a slower rhythm. The central inference is that normally dominant excitation originates upstream and that subsidiary cardiac regions can possess latent rhythmicity. It is essential to interpret the demonstration as a regional interruption experiment rather than the discovery of a mammalian sinoatrial node in exactly the same anatomical form.

A ligature can damage muscle, compress vessels or alter mechanical loading as well as interrupt conduction. Observing a downstream pause is therefore not a complete cellular explanation. Modern electrical recordings can discriminate a failure of stimulus generation from a failure of stimulus transmission. Simultaneous electrodes positioned on either side of a conduction boundary reveal whether the upstream waveform persists while downstream activation is missing. Applying a controlled stimulus below the boundary tests whether the downstream tissue is still excitable. If it produces an action potential, the absence of ordinary activation is not simply due to tissue death.

Consider three preparations in a conceptual teaching experiment. In preparation A, upstream electrical rhythm continues while downstream voltage remains stable until directly stimulated. The downstream response demonstrates residual excitability. In B, the upstream rhythm slows and downstream rhythm slows proportionally with preserved delay. In C, both upstream and downstream electrical rhythms remain regular, but downstream force falls. These outcomes distinguish propagation, automaticity and excitation–contraction coupling as first-order explanatory categories. Further interventions are needed to establish the exact affected channel or protein in each preparation.

A perfused isolated heart permits the investigator to maintain coronary delivery without ordinary connections to the body. In the classic retrograde perfusion arrangement associated with Langendorff, perfusate is supplied through the aorta so that the closed aortic valve directs flow into the coronary arteries. Appropriate perfusion keeps myocardium viable, but this does not reproduce all mechanical circumstances of the normal working heart. Electrical activity recorded in such an organ can establish intrinsic rhythm and permit controlled ionic or autonomic interventions. Measurements of pressure and force require an explicit description of preparation and load; an unloaded preparation cannot be treated as an ordinary ejecting heart.

At the level of a single cell, isolation removes the normal electrical load imposed by neighbouring cells. An isolated nodal cell may show automaticity, yet its precise rate can differ from the rate of the intact nodal network. Within the organ, heterogeneous cells interact through electrical coupling. The rate of the tissue is an emergent outcome of those interactions. Thus an isolated cell establishes that the molecular machinery of spontaneous depolarisation can reside in a cardiomyocyte, whereas the intact tissue preparation establishes that such activity can organise a propagated cardiac rhythm.

4. How the conduction system became an anatomical and functional concept

Early investigators faced a difficulty that is easy to overlook now that the names of specialised tissues are familiar. A heart may continue contracting after removal from the body, but the observation does not identify where the impulse arises or how it crosses from one chamber to another. The ordinary myocardium appears as an interconnected muscular wall. If it were electrically continuous across all interfaces, it would be difficult to explain why an atrial contraction usually precedes a ventricular contraction in a reproducible sequence. Resolving the problem required anatomical evidence of specialised pathways and physiological evidence that those pathways have particular conduction properties.

The work of Walter Gaskell on cardiac conduction and block helped establish that the sequence of cardiac activity could depend on the properties of muscle tissue at chamber junctions. The logic was to disturb the communication between regions and observe whether timing changed in a predictable manner. A continuing upstream contraction accompanied by missing downstream contractions indicated that a downstream region could fail to receive its usual excitation without cessation of upstream rhythmic activity. This was a powerful distinction because it moved discussion away from a single undifferentiated muscular rhythm towards a regional organisation of excitation.

Purkinje identified distinctive myocardial cells in the nineteenth century. The bundle described by Wilhelm His Jr. provided a candidate route between the atrial and ventricular musculature. In 1906 Sunao Tawara published his anatomical analysis connecting the atrioventricular nodal region, the bundle and its distal ramifications. His observations provided a structural framework in which the previously described specialised cells formed parts of a connected conducting system rather than isolated histological curiosities. Histology alone could not measure conduction velocity, but a continuous arrangement of specialised tissue provided a testable substrate for electrophysiological experiments.

Arthur Keith and Martin Flack published their description of the sinoatrial node in 1907. They recognised distinctive tissue at the junction of the systemic venous inflow and atrial myocardium, including similarities to the recently described atrioventricular node. A regional origin of the normal heartbeat had already been suggested by physiological experiments. The identification of a corresponding anatomical region brought structure and function together. Subsequent studies comparing local activation with the normal sequence strengthened the association between the node and initiation of rhythm. The historical progression is reviewed in the account of the discovery of the sinus node published in Heart in 2007.

In interpreting these discoveries, a student should distinguish the evidence available at different levels. A histological section shows a structure in a particular position and reveals its microscopic appearance. A regional lesion or interruption tests the necessity of a route for ordinary activation but may affect multiple local structures. An intracellular recording shows the timing and electrical phenotype of a cell. Selective recording and perturbation of channels can identify the processes underlying that phenotype. These methods answer different questions; none by itself establishes every level of the mechanism.

A modern anatomical and electrophysiological interpretation would predict the following patterns. An impulse normally arises in the sinus nodal region, appears in atrial tissue, traverses the AV junction and then appears rapidly at spatially distributed sites within the ventricles. If only one component of the downstream conducting axis is interrupted, other regions may still be excited through alternative routes within working myocardium, albeit with altered timing. This is why the location of an interruption can affect the synchrony of a contraction rather than simply switching the whole heart off.

It is equally important that specialised conducting tissue is not a miniature nervous system. Its location, cellular continuity and ion-channel properties are adapted to propagating excitation within cardiac muscle. Neural terminals can regulate the properties of those cells, but the impulse spread through a Purkinje fibre is a cardiac action potential. The historical discovery of a specialised network therefore complements, rather than contradicts, the myogenic explanation of rhythm.

5. Cardiomyocytes and electrical continuity across cell boundaries

The working myocardium is constructed from striated cardiac muscle cells. Their sarcomeres contain ordered thick and thin filaments, and their mitochondria support sustained oxidative metabolism. Adult mammalian ventricular cells typically have transverse tubules that bring the surface membrane into close proximity with calcium stores in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This arrangement helps translate a voltage change at the membrane into calcium delivery throughout a cell. Nodal cells differ in their organisation and electrical properties because their principal function is pacing or conduction rather than generating the major pumping force.

The ends and branches of cardiomyocytes meet at intercalated discs. Several specialised junctional structures occupy these interfaces. Adherens junctions transmit contractile forces between neighbouring cells through connections involving the actin cytoskeleton. Desmosomes contribute mechanical cohesion by linking intermediate filament systems. Gap junction channels provide pathways through which ions and small molecules can pass between neighbouring cytoplasms. Six connexin subunits form a connexon in one membrane; docking with a compatible connexon in the neighbouring membrane establishes an intercellular channel. The two cells retain distinct membranes and identities.

Electrical coupling is useful because a depolarised region can supply current to an adjacent resting region. The neighbouring membrane charges until its voltage-sensitive channels produce a regenerative action potential of its own. The gap junction does not transport a complete action potential as a material object. It permits electrical current, while the voltage change is regenerated locally. This distinction explains why conduction can fail even when the initiating cell remains excitable: the current delivered to its neighbour may be insufficient to activate the neighbour's channels.

The myocardium behaves as a functional syncytium, a population of distinct electrically connected cells. It is not a single multinucleated skeletal-muscle-like fibre. Moreover, electrical continuity is organised rather than uniform. The fibrous tissue between the atria and ventricles normally constrains direct electrical spread, and the atrioventricular conducting axis supplies the physiological route between them. Gap-junction expression, cell size, tissue orientation and connective tissue also affect how rapidly excitation travels. An excitation wave may move more readily along the long axis of a well-coupled chain of myocytes than across it. This directional dependence is called anisotropy.

The molecular composition of junctions also varies across the conducting system. Connexin 43 is prominent in working ventricular myocardium, whereas connexin 40 is important in rapidly conducting regions and connexin 45 occurs in several cardiac tissues including nodal regions. These are useful associations rather than one-to-one rules: multiple connexins can coexist and tissue conduction reflects geometry and excitability as well as junctional composition. The review of intercalated-disc propagation explains the evidence for this distinction.

Mechanical and electrical junctional defects have different first-order effects. If coupling conductance is reduced, adjacent cells receive less depolarising current, so local propagation may slow or fail even when individual action-potential generation is preserved. If mechanical adhesion is weakened, electrical excitation may initially be transmitted yet the tissue may no longer tolerate repetitive force normally. Real tissue changes can affect both properties together, so an observation of impaired contraction alone is insufficient to specify which junctional component has changed.

6. Source–sink relationships and directional conduction

A depolarised cell acts as a source of current for neighbouring cells that have not yet been excited. The neighbouring cells constitute an electrical sink because their membrane capacitance must be charged and their background ionic currents must be overcome. A single source connected to a very large resting region may fail to bring that region to threshold. A sufficiently large coordinated source can activate a larger sink. This source–sink relationship helps explain why an isolated cardiomyocyte can be highly excitable while impulse initiation or propagation fails in a multicellular preparation.

Electrical coupling cannot be interpreted using the slogan that more coupling always increases the success of pacing. In a chain of identical working cells, stronger coupling commonly facilitates current delivery. In a small pacemaker region surrounded by a large resting myocardium, however, very strong coupling can impose an electrical load on the oscillator. The spatial organisation of nodal tissue helps balance protection of its spontaneous rhythm with the requirement to activate the atrial muscle. The optimum conditions for generating a rhythm need not coincide with the optimum conditions for fast conduction through working myocardium.

Imagine a cell whose voltage rises above that of its neighbour by 40 mV. If the effective junctional conductance is 5 nS, the instantaneous intercellular current estimated by the conductance relation is 200 pA. If the conductance falls to 1 nS with the same voltage difference, current falls to 40 pA. In a real pair of cells the voltage difference is itself affected by coupling, so the two calculated currents do not fully predict the neighbour's final voltage. The simplified calculation isolates the direct influence of conductance at a specified instantaneous voltage gradient.

A second limiting factor is the availability of regenerative inward current in the receiving cell. A resting ventricular cell may have many sodium channels available. If its diastolic voltage becomes less negative for a prolonged period, an increased fraction of channels may remain inactivated. The cell could lie closer to the nominal activation range while having less capacity for a regenerative sodium upstroke. The prediction that depolarising a cell always increases excitability therefore fails when sustained depolarisation impairs channel availability. Elevated extracellular potassium provides a physiological example of conditions that may shift diastolic voltage and alter excitation.

Conduction is a wave of local regeneration rather than a packet of charged ions travelling from the sinoatrial node to the ventricular apex. In a tissue strip, an electrode can stimulate one region, and a series of neighbouring electrodes can measure the arrival of activation. Plotting activation time against distance produces an approximately linear relation in a uniformly conducting segment. The slope is time per distance, the inverse of propagation velocity. A discontinuity in that relation could indicate a region of delayed propagation, a change in geometry or a new activation route. Structural knowledge is needed to decide among the explanations.

Conduction can be direction-dependent because a ventricular myocyte is elongated and is coupled to neighbours in a spatially organised manner. In an experimental tissue slab, equal distances travelled parallel and perpendicular to the dominant fibre orientation may therefore produce different activation times. The result is evidence of electrical anisotropy. It is not evidence that individual sodium channels have different intrinsic speeds when the electrode is rotated. The measured quantity belongs to a tissue and incorporates its geometry and cell connections.

7. Membrane voltage, ion gradients and excitability

Membrane potential is the voltage inside a cell relative to its exterior. A resting ventricular myocyte is typically near −85 to −90 mV under physiological conditions, whereas the voltage in a central sinoatrial nodal cell changes spontaneously between beats. The negative interior does not imply that the entire cytoplasm carries a large net negative charge. A small separation of charge at the membrane surfaces creates the voltage, while the bulk solutions are almost electrically neutral. Selective ion permeability and active maintenance of concentration gradients allow the voltage to change in a reproducible manner.

Potassium is more concentrated inside a typical cardiac cell; sodium and calcium are more concentrated outside it when free cytosolic calcium is considered. Each ion is influenced by both its concentration gradient and the electrical field. The Nernst potential is the membrane voltage at which these two influences balance for a particular ion. It does not state what the voltage of a real membrane must be when several ion species can cross simultaneously. At 37 °C, the approximate Nernst expression is Eion = (61.5 mV/z) log10([ion]out/[ion]in), with z the signed ionic valence. Concentrations must use the same units and ideal dilute-solution behaviour is assumed.

For potassium concentrations of 4 mmol/L outside and 140 mmol/L inside, log10(4/140) is approximately −1.544. Multiplying by 61.5 mV gives EK approximately −95.0 mV. For sodium concentrations of 140 and 12 mmol/L, the same calculation gives ENa approximately +65.6 mV. These are illustrative concentrations, not a measurement of a particular heart. A membrane at −85 mV lies far below ENa, favouring inward sodium current when sodium channels open. It lies slightly above EK, favouring outward potassium current through open potassium channels.

A useful simplified current relation is Iion = gion(Vm − Eion), where gion is conductance, Vm is membrane voltage and Eion is equilibrium potential. Outward conventional current is positive in this notation. With conductance measured in nanosiemens and voltage in millivolts, their product has units of picoamperes. If a sodium conductance of 10 nS opens at −80 mV with ENa = +60 mV, the calculated current is −1400 pA, indicating inward positive charge. The conductance is in fact variable because channels open, close and inactivate; this example describes an instant, not a full action potential.

Membrane capacitance describes how much separated charge must accumulate to change voltage. A useful balance is Cm dVm/dt = −ΣIion when applied and intercellular currents are absent and outward ionic current is defined as positive. Inward net current therefore drives depolarisation. During a plateau, large inward and outward currents may nearly cancel, so the voltage changes slowly despite substantial ion movement. During pacemaker depolarisation, even a small imbalance sustained over time can bring a cell to threshold.

The sodium–potassium ATPase maintains sodium and potassium gradients through repeated transport of three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in per ATP consumed. It is electrogenic but does not itself cause the rapid repolarisation of each ventricular action potential. Repolarisation occurs primarily through changes in channel conductance. Pump function remains essential over many cycles because gradual dissipation of the gradients compromises excitability, calcium handling and normal membrane voltage.

8. Potassium gradients, channel availability and electrical stability

Potassium provides a useful example of how a change in extracellular concentration influences electrical behaviour. At a fixed intracellular potassium concentration of 140 mmol/L, increasing extracellular potassium from 4 to 8 mmol/L changes the Nernst potential from approximately −95 mV to −76.4 mV at 37 °C. The difference is approximately 18.5 mV, which can also be calculated directly as 61.5 log10(2). This is a logarithmic concentration dependence rather than a doubling of membrane voltage. Actual resting voltage also depends on the permeability and conductance of other ions.

A more positive potassium equilibrium potential can reduce outward potassium driving force at a specified membrane voltage. It can also shift the diastolic membrane voltage to a less negative value in a cell with substantial potassium permeability. Initially a small shift might bring some membranes closer to their activation range. Sustained or more severe depolarisation, however, can reduce sodium-channel availability and thereby impair action-potential amplitude and conduction. Students should distinguish the immediate electrochemical effect of a changed equilibrium potential from the subsequent change in the distribution of channel states.

The converse experiment requires equal caution. Decreasing extracellular potassium makes the equilibrium potential more negative, but some potassium conductances themselves depend on extracellular potassium. Consequently repolarisation changes cannot be inferred from the Nernst potential alone. A conductance is not an immutable constant: voltage, ionic concentrations, phosphorylation and gating history can modify it. Quantitative exercises that hold conductance fixed are valid when they are explicitly defined as simplified models, not as complete predictions of a biological perturbation.

The terms activation and inactivation describe different transitions in channel behaviour. A sodium channel can activate in response to depolarisation and conduct for a short interval. It can then enter an inactivated state despite continued depolarisation. Returning the voltage to a sufficiently negative level promotes recovery. A conditioning pulse followed by a variable recovery interval and a test pulse permits researchers to quantify availability. If the test sodium current grows as the interval increases, the result suggests progressive recovery. Control of the conditioning voltage is important because availability depends on voltage as well as time.

During repetitive ventricular activation the state of one action potential can influence the next. At higher rates, there is less time between beats for channels to recover and for calcium stores to return to their preceding conditions. This rate dependence does not imply that all action potentials must change identically with frequency. Different currents have different kinetics. An examination question should specify what is held constant, which tissue is being considered and which current is altered. When those assumptions are missing, a qualitative prediction should be expressed as a tendency rather than an inevitable outcome.

Repolarisation reserve describes the capacity of multiple outward-current pathways to support timely recovery of membrane voltage. Reduction of one repolarising current may have a modest effect when another remains available, yet a much larger effect when multiple pathways are compromised. A simple comparison of isolated conductances cannot determine the precise duration of an action potential. The concept does explain why interacting perturbations may produce effects that exceed expectations based on either perturbation alone. It also clarifies why the cardiac plateau is a dynamic property of a network of ionic currents.

9. Electrical energy, current density and interpretation of voltage-clamp results

The Nernst equation can be understood from the electrochemical potential of an ion. At equilibrium the chemical contribution proportional to RT ln concentration and the electrical contribution proportional to zFV must sum to the same value on both sides of the membrane. Writing the inside-minus-outside voltage as Vm leads to zFVm = RT ln(Cout/Cin) at equilibrium for an ideal ion. Dividing by zF and converting the natural logarithm to a base-ten logarithm gives the practical form introduced earlier. The factor near 61.5 mV applies to a monovalent ion at body temperature; temperature and valence must be accounted for when the assumptions change.

A divalent ion provides an instructive extension. Suppose free calcium outside a cell is 1.8 mmol/L and free cytosolic calcium at rest is 0.1 micromol/L. Expressing both concentrations in micromol/L gives 1800 outside and 0.1 inside, a ratio of 18,000. The base-ten logarithm of this ratio is approximately 4.255. With z = +2 the temperature-dependent prefactor is approximately 30.75 mV, yielding an illustrative ECa of +131 mV. The large positive equilibrium potential helps explain why opening calcium channels at membrane voltages near zero still permits strong inward calcium movement.

This calculation uses free concentration, not the total amount of calcium associated with cytoplasmic proteins or the sarcoplasmic reticulum. It also assumes activities can be approximated by concentrations. If the local calcium concentration near a channel rises substantially during release, the local driving force differs from that calculated using resting bulk cytosolic calcium. The Nernst equation remains an electrochemical relationship, but interpreting the relevant concentration becomes a spatial problem. That distinction becomes particularly important when considering calcium signalling in narrow junctions.

Membrane current is frequently normalised to cell capacitance in electrophysiology. A larger cell may contain more channels simply because it has more membrane. Measuring its whole-cell current without accounting for size might suggest an increased channel density even when channels occur at an unchanged density. Capacitance is a practical estimate of electrically accessible membrane area, although differences in membrane architecture and access can complicate the estimate. Current density expressed in pA/pF allows comparisons between cells of different sizes under specified recording conditions.

For example, suppose ventricular cell A has a peak inward current of −1000 pA and capacitance of 100 pF, while cell B has −1500 pA and capacitance of 200 pF. The estimated peak current density is −10 pA/pF for A and −7.5 pA/pF for B. B has the larger total inward current, but A has the greater magnitude of current per unit capacitance. A question about channel expression per electrically accessible membrane area is therefore not answered simply by selecting the cell with the larger whole-cell current.

The ratio of current to capacitance also clarifies the scale of voltage changes. If a hypothetical net inward current of 5 pA acts on a membrane capacitance of 50 pF and all other current contributions have already been included, dVm/dt = 5/50 V/s = 0.1 V/s, or 100 mV/s. A small sustained imbalance can therefore produce a physiologically meaningful change in membrane potential. Actual currents vary as voltage changes, and the calculation cannot be extrapolated as a constant slope throughout an entire action potential unless that simplifying assumption is stated.

In voltage-clamp experiments the measured net current is not automatically the current of the channel of interest. To identify a channel, investigators compare conditions in which its contribution is expected to differ while other conditions are controlled. They may exploit voltage dependence, ion selectivity, characteristic kinetics or pharmacological sensitivity. A reversal potential determined from the zero crossing of an isolated current–voltage relation provides evidence about ionic selectivity. For a mixed ion channel, the zero crossing need not equal the Nernst potential of either ion alone. This is one reason the funny current cannot be classified simply by observing its direction at a single test voltage.

10. The specialised mammalian conduction system

The sinoatrial node initiates the normal rhythm. Excitation leaves its pacemaker region and spreads through the atrial myocardium, allowing atrial activation before ventricular activation. Preferential atrial routes help organise spread, but descriptions of completely insulated internodal cables should not be inferred from a simple textbook drawing. The atrioventricular node is located in the lower interatrial septal region near the atrioventricular junction. Propagation through this region is relatively slow, allowing time for atrial activation and associated filling before ventricular systole.

The impulse then passes through the atrioventricular bundle, also called the bundle of His, into right and left bundle branches and their Purkinje network. These specialised cells distribute excitation to the ventricular myocardium rapidly. Much ventricular activation proceeds from inner myocardial regions towards outer regions in an organised spatial sequence, rather than simply travelling down one external line from the atrium. Purkinje fibres have fast-response electrical properties and are electrically coupled to working ventricular tissue. They are modified cardiomyocytes, not axons of motor neurons.

Slow atrioventricular conduction reflects several interacting properties: small cells, tissue geometry, cell coupling and an action-potential upstroke that relies heavily on calcium current in nodal regions. The delay is not a pause imposed by an external neural controller. Rapid conduction in the His–Purkinje system reflects a different combination of membrane excitability and structural properties. The entire atrioventricular interval is not attributable to the AV node alone, since atrial and His–Purkinje conduction also take time.

Suppose two sites are separated by 12 mm along a known pathway and activation arrives at the second site 40 ms after the first. The average apparent conduction velocity is 0.012 m divided by 0.040 s, or 0.30 m/s. This calculation assumes that the measured distance approximates the actual propagation route. When the route is tortuous, direction changes or activation enters from more than one source, dividing straight-line distance by delay may not represent local tissue velocity. A conduction experiment is stronger when several recording sites and known stimulus timing establish a consistent activation sequence.

The SA node normally dominates because its intrinsic timing is faster than that of subsidiary pacemaker regions. When slower automatic cells are repeatedly activated by a faster source they cannot ordinarily complete their own spontaneous interval before the next conducted impulse. Overdrive suppression can additionally reduce their spontaneous firing. If the dominant impulse ceases, a subsidiary pacemaker may eventually initiate an escape rhythm. If an atrial impulse fails to reach the ventricles, atrial and ventricular rhythms may become independent. The ability of subsidiary tissue to generate impulses does not mean every working cardiomyocyte is an effective normal pacemaker.

11. Activation time maps, regional delays and the pacemaker hierarchy

The electrical sequence in a large heart is often described by the names of the structures through which an impulse passes, but the functional issue is timing. A chamber can develop coordinated tension only when a sufficient fraction of its muscle becomes active within an appropriate interval. If ventricular excitation spreads slowly through ordinary muscle instead of being distributed through specialised fast pathways, different regions will enter the tension-producing phase at different times. This can reduce the effectiveness of contraction even when each isolated muscle cell can still generate force.

To investigate the timing of excitation, researchers can use electrodes at several positions and mark a reproducible feature of each local signal as the activation time. In intracellular records this may be the steep upstroke or a defined voltage crossing. In extracellular tissue recordings the criterion differs because the signal represents nearby current sources rather than the intracellular voltage itself. Comparisons are most useful when the same activation criterion and timing reference are used at all sites. An activation map then describes the spatial progression of an impulse by connecting positions activated at similar times.

Consider an illustrative sequence with the sinoatrial activation assigned time zero. An atrial recording becomes active at 35 ms, a recording near the entry to the AV nodal region at 50 ms, one near its exit at 130 ms, a distal His–Purkinje site at 150 ms and a ventricular recording at 165 ms. The difference across the measured nodal region is 80 ms, while the interval between the initial sinoatrial signal and the ventricular signal is 165 ms. The longer interval contains several regions and must not be assigned entirely to the AV node. These values are hypothetical and are used to practise calculating delays, not to establish clinical normal limits.

Suppose a second measurement retains the first two atrial times but shifts the nodal exit to 170 ms, distal activation to 190 ms and ventricular activation to 205 ms. The extra delay has appeared within the region bracketed by the two nodal recordings, while the later segment delays remain unchanged. This pattern points towards a regional alteration of atrioventricular conduction rather than a general reduction in velocity throughout the heart. If all activation times after the initial stimulus were shifted by the same amount, a change in reference timing or the earliest propagation interval would instead merit consideration.

Fast conduction and intrinsic pacemaker rate are independent properties. Purkinje tissue is specialised to distribute impulses rapidly through the ventricles yet normally has a slower spontaneous rate than the sinoatrial node. The AV nodal region conducts slowly but may also serve as a subsidiary rhythm source. The central SA node can initiate a rhythm without producing the most rapid upstroke or the fastest propagation in the organ. Questions that rank tissues by conduction velocity and those that rank them by automatic firing rate are therefore asking for different physiological orders.

A simple model of competing oscillators shows why a faster pacemaker can suppress a slower one. If tissue A fires spontaneously every 800 ms and tissue B would fire every 1200 ms in isolation, activation from A can repeatedly reset B before B reaches its own threshold, assuming conduction is reliable. When the faster drive stops, B may eventually initiate an escape beat. The first escape interval need not equal B's steady intrinsic period, because prior repetitive activation may modify ionic conditions. An escape rhythm is evidence of a surviving secondary source, not necessarily evidence that the original pacemaker has recovered.

At very rapid atrial activation, the AV node may fail to transmit every impulse because recovery of excitability is incomplete. This property can limit transmission of excessive atrial rates, although the resulting ventricular sequence depends on the detailed pattern of nodal recovery. In a simple example, transmission of alternate impulses gives a ventricular rate half the atrial rate. If all impulses are transmitted with a prolonged but fixed delay, the steady frequencies remain equal. This distinction between delay and failure of transmission is essential for the later interpretation of electrical recordings.

12. Slow-response action potentials and coupled pacemaker mechanisms

In a central sinoatrial nodal cell the most negative voltage after repolarisation is called the maximum diastolic potential, not a stable resting potential. During phase 4 the membrane slowly depolarises towards threshold. The phase 0 upstroke depends mainly on L-type calcium current, while phase 3 repolarisation depends on outward potassium current and declining calcium current. The familiar fast-response phases 1 and 2 are not separately prominent in this central nodal pattern. Peripheral nodal cells are heterogeneous and may also express functionally significant sodium current.

Hyperpolarisation-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channels carry the funny current, If. They are unusual in opening in response to sufficiently negative voltage. Their pores conduct both sodium and potassium. At physiological diastolic voltages the net current is generally inward because the combined reversal potential is more positive than the membrane potential. It is inaccurate to call If a pure sodium current or to infer that activation by hyperpolarisation must cause further hyperpolarisation. Channel gating and the direction of ionic current are different properties.

Following repolarisation, activation of If, a decline in outward potassium current and other inward currents create the net depolarising balance. T-type calcium channels can contribute during late diastolic depolarisation in relevant nodal cells; L-type calcium channels become important as voltage approaches the threshold region and generate much of the nodal upstroke. Calcium channels subsequently inactivate, potassium conductance increases and the membrane repolarises. Repolarisation restores the conditions for the next spontaneous cycle.

Intracellular calcium cycling can also participate in pacing. During diastole, local calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum may stimulate forward sodium–calcium exchange. The exchanger typically imports three sodium ions while exporting one calcium ion, moving one net positive charge inward per transport cycle. The resulting inward current can accelerate late diastolic depolarisation. The interaction of membrane channels and intracellular calcium cycling is often described as a coupled-clock mechanism. The relative importance of its components varies with species, cell type and physiological conditions. A useful examination answer should neither ignore If nor claim that it is the sole molecular origin of every cardiac rhythm.

A simple timing exercise separates the influences on spontaneous rate. If maximum diastolic potential is −60 mV and threshold is −40 mV, the membrane must depolarise by 20 mV. At a hypothetical average slope of 40 mV/s this takes 0.50 s. If the remaining upstroke and repolarisation occupy another 0.30 s, the cycle duration is 0.80 s and the calculated rate is 60/0.80 = 75 beats/min. A slope of 50 mV/s decreases the diastolic interval to 0.40 s and, with other timing unchanged, increases rate to about 85.7 beats/min. Actual phase 4 is nonlinear and its threshold and maximum diastolic potential may also change. Thus the calculation is a causal teaching model, not a fitted prediction of intact nodal behaviour.

13. The discovery and experimental identification of the funny current

Spontaneous diastolic depolarisation initially posed an experimental problem. If the membrane has returned to its most negative voltage at the end of a beat, what makes the next depolarisation begin? A decline in outward current could allow a background inward current to become dominant. Alternatively a new inward conductance could activate during the diastolic interval. Both mechanisms can produce a similar gradual voltage rise in a current-clamp recording, so observing a phase 4 slope alone cannot distinguish them.

In their 1979 Nature report, Brown, DiFrancesco and Noble described an additional current that becomes active over the pacemaker voltage range and discussed its relevance to adrenergic acceleration of the heart. This work helped establish the significance of a hyperpolarisation-activated inward component in cardiac rhythm generation (1979 report). Later molecular studies linked the current to HCN channels. The historical importance of the result was that a voltage range normally associated with recovery from excitation could itself activate a conductance contributing to the next depolarisation.

A conceptual voltage-clamp experiment can illustrate the distinguishing evidence. The investigator holds a viable nodal cell at a selected voltage and then steps to a series of more negative voltages. If a conductance develops slowly during the negative step rather than appearing only during a depolarising step, that time dependence suggests activation by hyperpolarisation. The investigator can return the membrane to several test voltages and examine the direction and amplitude of the remaining current before the channels fully deactivate. This approach separates activation behaviour from the electrical driving force through open channels.

Suppose a simplified mixed sodium–potassium conductance has an effective reversal potential of −25 mV. At −65 mV its driving force is −40 mV, giving inward conventional current if its conductance is positive. At −25 mV the net current through that conductance is zero even if the channels are open. At −10 mV the driving force is +15 mV, so the direction is reversed. These illustrative numbers are chosen for reasoning rather than reporting a measured universal HCN reversal potential. The voltage required to activate channels and the voltage at which their net current reverses are different experimental quantities.

A useful further test examines how a cyclic nucleotide changes activation. In a hypothetical experiment an activation fraction of 0.20 is measured at a selected diastolic voltage in control solution and 0.50 at the same voltage after increasing intracellular cAMP. If the maximum conductance and ionic driving force remain fixed, more available channels increase current magnitude at that voltage. A shift in voltage dependence can therefore accelerate the depolarising part of a pacemaker cycle without requiring a change in the concentrations of sodium or potassium. The relationship of cAMP binding to HCN gating is supported by subsequent physiological and molecular work reviewed by DiFrancesco.

No single experiment should be interpreted as showing that If is necessary and sufficient for every spontaneous heartbeat. A partial intervention may leave residual HCN activity, and spontaneous depolarisation also involves calcium cycling, potassium-current decay and other conductances. A strong experimental design examines whether a perturbation changes the predicted current and whether changes in rhythm follow. If the rhythm persists at a slower rate after a substantial reduction of If, the finding supports a contribution to normal timing rather than an exclusive role as the sole oscillator.

This distinction is especially important in comparing species and cardiac regions. Some preparations express different proportions of HCN channel isoforms or differ in the role of intracellular calcium cycling. The coupling between membrane voltage and local calcium release means that an intervention affecting one part of the system may indirectly modify another. The most useful model for ordinary examination reasoning is a set of interacting currents and calcium-dependent processes, with If as a major identifiable component, not a single current drawing a predetermined straight line to threshold.

14. Fast-response action potentials in working myocardium

A ventricular action potential begins when depolarising current brings a resting cell into the activation range of voltage-gated sodium channels. During phase 0 rapid sodium entry drives a steep increase in voltage. This sodium conductance activates and inactivates quickly. The rate of the upstroke is important for conduction because a rapid increase in voltage helps an excited cell supply depolarising current to surrounding tissue. The ventricular action potential is therefore different from the central SA nodal upstroke, which relies mainly on calcium current.

Phase 1 is the early partial repolarisation observed in many ventricular cells. It follows sodium current inactivation and contributions from transient outward potassium current. Phase 2 is the plateau, during which inward L-type calcium current and outward potassium currents approximately balance over a substantial interval. The plateau is not a phase during which no ions move. Nor is it simply a period in which all potassium channels are closed. The magnitude and shape of the plateau vary among ventricular regions and species, and some cells have a more triangular profile.

Phase 3 is the principal repolarising phase. Calcium current diminishes through inactivation, while repolarising potassium currents become increasingly effective. Delayed rectifier potassium currents, including rapidly and slowly activating components, contribute to this process. The inward rectifier potassium current helps stabilise negative voltage and contributes during terminal repolarisation. Phase 4 denotes the relatively stable diastolic voltage of an ordinary working ventricular cell. Inward rectification means a channel has a voltage-dependent asymmetric current–voltage relation; it does not mean the channel can only move potassium into the cell.

A decrease in fast sodium channel availability reduces the maximum upstroke rate and can slow propagation without directly abolishing sarcomere function. A reduction in L-type calcium current may shorten the plateau and diminish the trigger for calcium release, although compensating currents and changes in calcium loading affect the final outcome. A decrease in repolarising potassium current tends to prolong repolarisation if the other currents are unchanged. These predictions are useful as controlled perturbations, but an intact cell contains interacting voltage-sensitive currents, so one cannot predict every secondary effect by considering an isolated channel.

A central nodal cell and a ventricular cell may both depolarise, but the waveform alone must be interpreted with time scale, voltage scale and recording context. A trace with slow diastolic depolarisation, a calcium-dependent upstroke and no obvious plateau suggests a nodal phenotype. A trace with stable negative diastolic voltage, a steep sodium-dependent upstroke and a prominent plateau suggests a working ventricular phenotype. Purkinje cells illustrate why automaticity and fast-response excitation need not be mutually exclusive: specialised conducting cells can exhibit latent automaticity while retaining a fast upstroke.

15. Quantifying action-potential shape and distinguishing waveform from recovery

Action-potential diagrams are useful for identifying phases, but investigators also require reproducible measures of waveform shape. A ventricular action potential can be described by its initial voltage, peak voltage, amplitude, maximum upstroke slope and duration to a specified degree of repolarisation. Each measure describes a different part of the event. A change in one measure does not guarantee a proportional change in the others. For example, the upstroke may remain almost unchanged while the plateau becomes shorter because a calcium or potassium current changes later in the cycle.

Action-potential duration at ninety percent repolarisation is commonly denoted APD90. To calculate it from a trace, first determine the action-potential amplitude relative to the chosen resting baseline. If the resting voltage is −85 mV and the peak is +25 mV, the amplitude is 110 mV. Ninety percent repolarisation corresponds to a fall of 99 mV from the peak, reaching −74 mV. If the reference point for activation is at 10 ms and the falling trace crosses −74 mV at 260 ms, APD90 is 250 ms. The value is a duration, not the absolute time coordinate of the final voltage crossing.

The same trace could have an APD50 of a different value because half repolarisation corresponds to a voltage of −30 mV. Differences between early and late repolarisation measures can be informative when the shape of the plateau or late recovery changes. When comparing experiments, the definition of the starting point and repolarisation percentage must be consistent. A duration determined by crossing a fixed voltage may be inappropriate if the baseline or peak has changed, because the same voltage then represents a different fraction of repolarisation.

APD90 does not equal the effective refractory period by definition. A ventricular cell's ability to produce a propagated response depends on recovery of channel availability and the surrounding tissue's excitability. Under some conditions, substantial repolarisation may occur before enough sodium channels have recovered for effective propagation. A paired-stimulus experiment tests this functional property directly. The two measures can correlate strongly without being interchangeable, and their difference can help reveal altered recovery kinetics.

Atrial and ventricular myocardium also differ in typical action-potential morphology. In many mammalian atrial cells, repolarisation is more rapid and the plateau less prominent than in ventricular cells. Purkinje fibres may show prolonged fast-response action potentials and latent diastolic depolarisation. Species differences are pronounced; for example, common small rodent ventricular action potentials are generally shorter and less plateau-like than human ventricular action potentials. Thus a diagram used to explain a human-type ventricular waveform should not be applied as an invariant trace for every laboratory animal.

The broad phases can be related to net current without requiring exact magnitudes of individual channel conductances. A steep upward trace implies a large net inward current relative to capacitance. An approximately horizontal trace implies that net membrane current is small, not that each ionic current is small. A downward trace implies net outward current. This reasoning follows directly from the capacitive current balance, making it possible to infer the sign of net current from the direction of a voltage trace while recognising that the identities of individual currents require additional evidence.

An examination exercise may show two action potentials at matched stimulation frequency. If trace B has the same initial and peak voltage as A but reaches ninety percent repolarisation later, B has a longer APD90. Possible explanations include greater sustained inward current or reduced repolarising current; the trace alone cannot identify which change occurred. If an additional voltage-clamp measurement shows a selective decrease in an outward potassium component, the inference becomes more specific. Combining measurements at different levels allows a student to move from describing a waveform to testing a mechanistic hypothesis.

16. Refractoriness and why sustained tetanus is unsuitable for the heart

Following an action potential, a cardiac cell cannot immediately generate another normally propagated response. During the absolute refractory period, a second stimulus cannot elicit another action potential in the usual sense. The effective refractory period is defined operationally by the inability to generate a propagated response; local electrical changes may be possible before propagation is restored. During the relative refractory period some channels have recovered, and a sufficiently strong stimulus may produce a response with altered amplitude or propagation speed. Exact boundaries depend on the cell, stimulus and measurement criterion.

Fast sodium channels recover from inactivation primarily after the membrane returns towards negative potentials. During the prolonged ventricular plateau, the membrane remains depolarised and many sodium channels are unavailable. Consequently the refractory period overlaps much of the mechanical contraction. The action potential and calcium transient are much longer than a typical skeletal muscle action potential. Skeletal muscle can receive repetitive action potentials while tension is still developing and can show temporal summation and fused tetanus. The normal working heart is protected from this form of sustained contraction by the prolonged refractory interval.

Relaxation is indispensable for ventricular filling. A sustained contraction would not function as an effective repetitive pump even if the muscle developed considerable force. This explains why a prolonged action potential can be physiologically advantageous despite limiting maximum firing frequency. It does not mean that a heart is immune to rapid or disorganised electrical activity. Premature impulses, abnormal automaticity and re-entry can disturb coordinated activation without producing the same normal fused tetanus characteristic of stimulated skeletal muscle.

A useful illustrative paired-pulse experiment applies a conditioning stimulus at time zero and tests responses at increasing delays. At 100 ms no second response is observed, at 220 ms a small local response fails to propagate, and at 280 ms a stronger-than-usual stimulus produces a propagated response. The observations suggest continuing refractoriness at 100 ms, a difference between local excitability and tissue propagation at 220 ms, and partial recovery by 280 ms. They do not establish exact absolute or effective refractory endpoints without testing more intervals and stimulus strengths. A response is meaningful only when its recording site and propagation criterion are stated.

17. Abnormal activation as a test of normal electrophysiological principles

A normal rhythm requires an initiating source, sufficient conduction, appropriate recovery and an organised termination of each excitation wave. Disturbance of any one of these processes can create an abnormal activation pattern. A subsidiary region may initiate an impulse at an unusual time, a pathway may transmit only some incoming impulses, or a wave may travel around a circuit and re-excite a region that has already recovered. These are distinct mechanisms even though each can alter the observed intervals between contractions.

Enhanced automaticity describes an increase in spontaneous impulse generation by a region with pacing capability or an abnormal acquisition of such activity by tissue that normally remains silent. Triggered activity differs because it depends on a preceding action potential. A depolarising disturbance during repolarisation is called an early afterdepolarisation; one occurring after repolarisation is a delayed afterdepolarisation. Whether either disturbance produces a propagated action potential depends on its amplitude, timing and the electrical load of neighbouring tissue. Naming a depolarisation is not the same as establishing a sustained rhythm disturbance.

A delayed afterdepolarisation can illustrate the interaction between calcium handling and membrane voltage. If excess intracellular calcium is released spontaneously from the SR, forward sodium–calcium exchange may generate an inward current as calcium is extruded. This current can depolarise the membrane after the ordinary action potential has ended. If depolarisation reaches a sufficient level in tissue with available regenerative channels, it can initiate an additional excitation. The mechanism connects a disturbance of intracellular calcium distribution to an electrical event rather than treating electrical rhythm and contraction as independent systems.

Early afterdepolarisations are associated with disturbances during the repolarising part of the action potential and may occur when repolarisation is abnormally prolonged. A prolonged plateau can allow some inward-current pathways to reactivate or remain effective under appropriate conditions. The exact contributing currents vary. Reducing repolarisation reserve can favour such behaviour, but a longer action potential alone does not establish that an early afterdepolarisation will occur. The timing relative to ordinary repolarisation is the essential distinction in elementary physiological reasoning.

Re-entry does not require every cell in a pathway to have spontaneous automaticity. It depends instead on the propagation of excitation into tissue that has recovered by the time the wave returns. Consider two branches around a region of nonconducting tissue. An early impulse may fail to enter one branch because that branch remains refractory while entering another branch that has recovered. If propagation along the available route takes enough time, the initially blocked region may have recovered when approached from its distal side. Excitation can then return through that region and potentially repeat the circuit.

This reasoning requires at least three distinctions. A permanent bidirectional block prevents movement through the affected segment in either direction. A temporary unidirectional block can allow a returning wave to use a route that was unavailable initially. Slow conduction gives previously excited tissue more time to recover but can also cause propagation failure if source current is inadequate. Consequently, changing one parameter does not always increase the probability of re-entry monotonically. The simple wavelength model is most useful when conduction velocity and effective refractoriness can be treated as approximate local averages.

When electrical recordings reveal an unexpected ventricular impulse, the mechanism cannot be assigned solely from the fact that it arrived early. An ectopic automatic source, triggered activity or a returning wave may each produce premature activation. Multi-site recordings help determine where the wave originates and how it spreads. Manipulations of pacing interval and calcium handling may help discriminate mechanisms. The physiological goal here is to understand how abnormalities expose the importance of normal automaticity, coupling and refractoriness; detailed clinical classification is beyond this chapter's scope.

18. Excitation–contraction coupling and the calcium transient

Cardiac contraction requires an increase in free calcium concentration near the myofilaments. Electrical activation reaches the sarcolemma and, in ventricular cells with a well-developed transverse-tubule system, penetrates deep into the cell through invaginations of that membrane. L-type calcium channels open with depolarisation and admit a local calcium signal. Nearby ryanodine receptors of the RyR2 type in the sarcoplasmic reticulum respond to this signal by releasing additional calcium from the intracellular store. This process is calcium-induced calcium release, commonly abbreviated CICR.

The narrow junction between a transverse tubule and a terminal region of the sarcoplasmic reticulum is a dyadic signalling space. The local calcium concentration near an open L-type channel may be much higher than the average cytosolic concentration. Local release events therefore depend on spatial organisation, not simply on a uniform whole-cell rise. The summation of many local release events produces the cell-wide calcium transient. The transient is not identical to the action potential: it has its own timing and amplitude, depending on trigger current, store content, release probability, buffering and removal.

Free calcium binds to troponin C in the thin filament regulatory complex. Changes in the troponin complex shift tropomyosin away from inhibitory positions on actin, allowing more productive interaction between actin and myosin. Myosin uses ATP in repeated cross-bridge cycles that generate force and, if the load permits, shortening. Electrical excitation is upstream of this process but does not itself supply the mechanical energy. ATP is also needed for calcium reuptake and maintenance of ionic gradients, making repeated contractions dependent on metabolic support.

In mammalian skeletal muscle, voltage-dependent conformational coupling between the L-type channel voltage sensor and the principal skeletal ryanodine receptor provides a major route of sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium release. In ventricular cardiac muscle, entry of extracellular calcium through L-type channels is a crucial trigger for RyR2 release. Consequently reducing extracellular calcium can strongly affect cardiac force even while membrane excitation remains detectable. The extent of dependence varies with preparation and species; the comparison is a distinction of coupling mechanisms rather than a claim that extracellular calcium never matters in skeletal muscle physiology.

A two-measurement experiment illustrates the causal sequence. Record action potentials and force from a paced ventricular preparation while progressively inhibiting L-type calcium entry. If sodium-dependent excitation is still detected but force falls, a defect between electrical excitation and mechanical activation is implicated. Measuring the calcium transient provides a further test. If the transient decreases with force, impaired calcium delivery is plausible. If the transient stays comparable but force falls, altered myofilament responsiveness or cross-bridge function deserves attention. Simultaneous measures constrain explanations more effectively than force alone.

19. Calcium sparks: evidence for spatially organised release

A central question in excitation–contraction coupling is how a relatively small calcium influx can trigger a much larger intracellular release without causing all calcium stores to empty unpredictably. A model in which the entire sarcoplasmic reticulum responds simultaneously to a uniform increase in cytosolic calcium has difficulty explaining the graded and stable response of the heart. Spatial separation of release sites provides a way to obtain strong local amplification while retaining control over the number of regions recruited during a beat.

In 1993 Cheng, Lederer and Cannell reported local transient increases in intracellular calcium in rat cardiac cells using a calcium-sensitive fluorescent indicator and laser-scanning confocal microscopy. These local signals were termed calcium sparks (1993 report). The observation provided direct evidence that calcium release is organised into spatially restricted events. The experiment was significant because a whole-cell measurement of average calcium concentration could reveal only the sum of such events; it could not establish that the release occurs at discrete locations.

The fluorescence approach has an important interpretive limitation. An indicator reports changes in its optical properties as calcium binds, and the measured signal depends on dye distribution, optical resolution and calibration. A spark is therefore an observed local calcium-dependent signal, interpreted together with physiological evidence about the release channels and stores. An optical signal does not constitute a direct count of individual calcium ions or prove that every participating RyR2 channel opened simultaneously. The strength of the interpretation comes from the consistency of spatial observations and independent channel manipulations.

Cannell, Cheng and Lederer subsequently investigated the relation between triggered local calcium events and the calcium current across the surface membrane. Their work supported a model in which local L-type calcium-channel activity can initiate a spark and recruitment of many such events generates a graded whole-cell response (1995 report). This resolves an apparent contradiction: the release process at an individual site can be strongly regenerative while the total response is modulated by the number and timing of recruited sites. Local regulation reduces the need for a single global threshold that activates the entire store at once.

Consider a schematic cell with 100 potentially recruitable release regions. If a particular depolarisation recruits 30 regions and each contributes one arbitrary release unit, the total in this simplified model is 30 units. A stronger recruitment condition activating 60 regions gives 60 units without requiring twice the release from each individual region. Real release amplitudes vary, neighbouring regions interact, and store content affects the probability and extent of release. The calculation nevertheless illustrates how local all-or-none-like events can generate a graded aggregate signal.

Another useful quantity is an operational excitation–contraction coupling gain, the amount of calcium released from the SR relative to the amount entering through the surface membrane during a defined interval. In an illustrative experiment, if trigger entry is 8 arbitrary calcium units and triggered SR release is 40 units, the ratio is 5. In a second condition, entry is 12 and release is 48, giving gain 4. The second condition releases more total calcium but has a smaller release-to-entry ratio. These values distinguish absolute calcium delivery from amplification and should not be confused with a universal fixed gain.

A student should also recognise that the gain depends on how entry and release are measured. Peak current multiplied by an arbitrary duration is not necessarily equivalent to total calcium entry, because current changes during the event. Integrating calcium current provides charge; converting charge to the amount of calcium requires accounting for the divalent charge and the relevant current component. Estimates of SR release require additional information about cytosolic buffering and removal. At the examination level the key is to state the assumptions of an integrated flux ratio rather than to attempt a specialist reconstruction of all intracellular concentrations.

The local-control concept provides a causal explanation for the importance of the transverse tubules and dyadic architecture. Disorganisation of the spatial relation between trigger channels and release sites could make calcium delivery less synchronous even if the cell retains a substantial total calcium-channel current. A reduced mechanical response could therefore arise from impaired coupling efficiency rather than loss of all electrical excitability. This joins the structural discussion of cardiomyocytes to experimental physiology and the later analysis of mechanical cardiac performance.

20. Relaxation, calcium removal and beat-to-beat balance

For a cardiomyocyte to relax, free cytosolic calcium must decrease. The sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase SERCA2a transports calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum using ATP. Sodium–calcium exchange in the surface membrane normally contributes to net calcium extrusion. A smaller component is handled by the plasma membrane calcium ATPase and other cellular pathways. Mitochondria contribute to calcium signalling and energetics but are not normally the principal rapid route for clearing the global ventricular calcium transient.

The difference between recycling and extrusion is important. Calcium that SERCA returns to the sarcoplasmic reticulum remains within the cell and can be released again on a subsequent beat. Calcium removed by forward sodium–calcium exchange leaves the cytosol for the extracellular space. At steady state, net entry of calcium across the cell membrane over a complete cycle is balanced by net extrusion. Intracellular release and reuptake do not add or subtract calcium from the whole-cell inventory. Failure to distinguish these two balances leads to incorrect predictions about calcium accumulation.

For an illustrative integrated flux example, let 12 arbitrary calcium units enter across the membrane during a beat while 48 units are released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. If 48 units are returned to the store and 12 are extruded, both store content and total cellular calcium return to their initial values. If SERCA initially returns only 38 units and extrusion remains 12, the store loses 10 units while 10 remain elsewhere in the cell at the end of the interval. In later beats, the altered free calcium and store content will change release and extrusion. The initial arithmetic cannot be extrapolated indefinitely as if the system were not regulated.

Phospholamban modulates SERCA activity in cardiac muscle. In its inhibitory state it reduces the apparent calcium affinity of the pump; phosphorylation can relieve inhibition, accelerating calcium sequestration and supporting relaxation. This can also increase store loading and influence the force generated on subsequent beats. The ability to relax more rapidly is called positive lusitropy. It is distinct from positive inotropy, an increase in contractile state, although the same signalling pathway can influence both.

Sodium–calcium exchange relies on the sodium gradient and membrane voltage. Its direction is not permanently fixed: the electrochemical conditions can favour reverse exchange during parts of a cycle or under altered ionic conditions. In the ordinary beat-to-beat balance it is an important route of calcium extrusion. Inhibiting the sodium–potassium pump can increase intracellular sodium and thereby alter calcium extrusion, but it also changes electrical and metabolic conditions; the resulting cellular response cannot be reduced to one transporter acting in isolation.

21. Calcium buffering and the distinction between calcium signal and force

Calcium concentration is not the same as total calcium content. Much intracellular calcium is bound to proteins or sequestered in intracellular stores. The free cytosolic fraction changes rapidly during a contraction and supplies a signal to the contractile apparatus. A calcium buffer can bind additional calcium when free concentration rises and release it as free concentration falls. Consequently an identical calcium influx need not generate an identical free-calcium transient in cells with different buffering properties.

The sarcoplasmic reticulum contains proteins that bind calcium and permit substantial storage without an equivalently large free calcium concentration inside the store. Calsequestrin is an important example. The biological advantage is that a large releasable pool can be maintained while the concentration of free ions remains constrained. A change in store content or buffering can alter release on subsequent beats even if the surface membrane action potential has not changed detectably. Measuring only the action potential would miss this form of regulation.

At the myofilaments, the fraction of calcium-sensitive regulatory sites occupied increases as free calcium rises. A schematic saturating relation can be written F/Fmax = C^n/(K^n + C^n), where C is free calcium concentration, K is the concentration giving half of the maximal model force, and n is an apparent cooperativity coefficient. The ratio F/Fmax is dimensionless. This Hill-type expression is useful to describe a steady-state relationship at specified conditions, but the parameters are not universal constants and the relation does not reproduce the entire time-dependent twitch.

For example, using an illustrative K of 0.5 micromol/L and n = 2, a calcium concentration of 0.5 micromol/L gives F/Fmax = 0.5. At C = 1.0 micromol/L the numerator is 1.0 in squared concentration units and the denominator is 1.25 in the same units, yielding 0.8. Doubling calcium in this example therefore does not double force. At concentrations much higher than K, the response approaches saturation. The calculation shows why the relationship between a change in a calcium signal and the resulting mechanical response need not be linear.

Now suppose two preparations have the same maximal force but different values of K. A smaller K indicates that half-maximal activation occurs at a smaller calcium concentration. At a given submaximal calcium level the more calcium-sensitive preparation can produce more force. An unchanged calcium transient with an increased force could therefore reflect altered myofilament sensitivity. This inference is strongest when muscle length, temperature, loading and indicator calibration are held constant. A change in measured shortening under different load cannot automatically be assigned to a change in calcium sensitivity.

The mechanical twitch is also affected by the rates at which cross-bridges form and detach. Force can outlast the peak of the calcium transient because calcium binding, thin filament regulation and cross-bridge cycling have finite kinetics. The falling phase of a fluorescence trace and the falling phase of a force trace therefore need not coincide exactly. In a controlled experimental series, accelerating calcium uptake may shorten the calcium transient, but the extent to which mechanical relaxation accelerates also depends on downstream kinetic processes.

22. Calcium balance, ATP use and changes with repeated stimulation

The cardiac cell must restore two kinds of state after a beat: the electrical state required for another action potential and the calcium distribution required for another contraction. Restoring membrane voltage does not by itself restore sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium content. Similarly, returning calcium into the store does not by itself restore sodium-channel availability. These processes occur in parallel and depend on distinct proteins. The physiological interval between beats must accommodate both electrical recovery and sufficient mechanical relaxation.

ATP is required for active ion transport and cross-bridge cycling. SERCA transfers calcium against its electrochemical gradient using ATP, while the sodium–potassium ATPase helps maintain the sodium gradient that drives forward calcium exchange. Although the exchanger does not directly hydrolyse ATP during each transport cycle, its ability to remove calcium depends on gradients maintained partly through ATP-dependent processes. It is therefore a secondary active transporter in its ordinary calcium-extrusion mode. This distinction connects cardiac physiology with the membrane-transport principles studied in cellular organisation.

The actomyosin cycle also requires ATP for detachment of myosin from actin and for energising subsequent cross-bridge movements. If ATP supply is severely impaired, relaxation may become abnormal through both ion-handling and cross-bridge mechanisms. An observation that force persists after stimulation does not therefore prove that the action potential has remained prolonged. Measuring electrical activity, calcium and mechanical behaviour is particularly important under metabolic stress because several mechanistic levels can be affected at once.

Repeated stimulation provides a natural way to alter calcium cycling. A shorter interval between stimuli gives less time for removal of cytosolic calcium and for recovery of channels and release sites. At the same time, more calcium entry events occur per unit time. In many healthy mammalian ventricular preparations an increase in stimulation rate can increase the force generated per beat over a range of frequencies, a phenomenon often called a positive force–frequency relation. Its magnitude and even direction depend on species, temperature, preparation conditions and calcium-handling capacity.

The force–frequency relation must be distinguished from the Frank–Starling mechanism, which concerns the effect of initial muscle length or filling on force and pump performance. A change in stimulation frequency at controlled muscle length can modify calcium loading without a change in initial length. A change in length at constant frequency can modify mechanical performance through length-dependent activation. The two mechanisms may coexist in a beating organ; controlled experiments distinguish them by holding one variable fixed while changing the other.

Suppose a paced cell initially receives ten units of calcium per beat at one beat per second. The input over one minute is 600 units. If frequency increases to two beats per second and entry per beat stays at ten units, input per minute rises to 1200 units. At a new steady state the total calcium extrusion per minute must match the new net entry. It does not follow that the cytosolic concentration doubles or that force doubles; changes in store loading, release, buffering and extrusion can establish a new balance. The arithmetic gives a constraint on flux, not a full prediction of concentration.

A time-dependent relaxation measure can also be useful in experiments. If a calcium transient decays approximately exponentially over a selected interval, C(t) − Cbaseline = A exp(−t/τ), where τ is a time constant in the same units as t and A is the initial excess concentration. After one time constant, the excess has fallen to approximately 36.8 percent of its starting value. A decrease in τ therefore indicates faster decay within the assumptions of the model. In a real cell, multiple removal processes and calcium buffers can make the decay depart from a single exponential.

For an illustrative comparison, a preparation with τ = 100 ms retains approximately 37 percent of excess calcium after 100 ms. A second preparation with τ = 200 ms retains exp(−0.5), approximately 61 percent, after the same interval. If the next impulse arrives early, the second preparation may start from a more elevated calcium level. The calculation does not predict exact residual force because cross-bridge kinetics and initial transient amplitude may differ. It illustrates why a physiological mechanism accelerating relaxation can be especially important when heart rate increases.

23. Autonomic regulation without loss of myogenic identity

Cardiac sympathetic nerves release noradrenaline, and circulating adrenaline also influences the heart. Beta1 adrenergic receptors couple predominantly to Gs, stimulating adenylyl cyclase and increasing cyclic AMP. In pacemaker cells, cAMP can bind directly to HCN channels and shift their activation towards less negative voltages, increasing availability in the diastolic range. Protein kinase A dependent processes also modify calcium channels and calcium handling. Together these effects ordinarily accelerate pacemaker depolarisation and increase the rate of spontaneous impulse generation.

Parasympathetic vagal activity releases acetylcholine, acting through M2 muscarinic receptors. Gi-associated signalling reduces adenylyl cyclase activity and cAMP. G protein beta-gamma subunits can also activate acetylcholine-sensitive potassium channels, promoting outward potassium current at relevant voltages. A more negative diastolic potential and reduced inward pacemaker drive can increase the time required to reach threshold. The result is typically a slower sinoatrial rhythm. Parasympathetic effects on AV nodal conduction can also be substantial.

Chronotropy describes effects on rate, dromotropy on conduction, inotropy on contractile state and lusitropy on relaxation. Sympathetic stimulation usually increases each of these in the mammalian heart under appropriate conditions. Parasympathetic stimulation prominently slows SA rate and AV conduction; direct effects on ventricular force are generally less prominent than effects on nodal and atrial tissue. These words identify outcomes, not a separate set of nerves for each process.

Removing autonomic input leaves a viable myogenic pacemaker capable of generating impulses. It may beat faster than a resting intact heart when tonic vagal restraint is removed. Conversely, a preparation lacking sympathetic stimulation can still increase its rate under certain intrinsic or pharmacological conditions. To distinguish direct effects on rhythm from direct effects on contractile state, researchers can maintain a fixed stimulation rate and compare force before and after a receptor agonist. If force changes despite the same activation frequency, a mechanism beyond simple chronotropy must be considered. Later chapters will extend this cellular explanation to feedback control of arterial pressure and integrated cardiovascular responses.

24. Autonomic signalling and separation of rate from force

A sympathetic signal reaches a beta1 receptor in a membrane whose downstream proteins are not identical in every cardiac cell. In nodal tissue, an increase in cAMP can alter HCN gating and interact with calcium-clock mechanisms, reducing the time between spontaneous impulses. In ventricular cells, phosphorylation of proteins controlling calcium entry, storage and myofilament regulation can increase the strength of the twitch and accelerate relaxation. The same extracellular messenger therefore produces different combinations of physiological effects depending on the cellular expression of effector proteins.

Direct binding of cAMP to HCN channels must be distinguished from phosphorylation by protein kinase A. Both processes can be consequences of beta-adrenergic stimulation, but they are not the same molecular event. In the former, a cyclic nucleotide binds to an intracellular regulatory region of the channel and changes gating behaviour. In the latter, the kinase catalyses transfer of phosphate from ATP to suitable protein substrates. A question that asks specifically how cAMP changes HCN activation should not automatically be answered with phosphorylation of every HCN channel as the necessary intermediate.

Parasympathetic regulation has an equally important distinction. Reduced cAMP can diminish the inward depolarising drive associated with pacemaker channels, while acetylcholine-sensitive potassium conductance can increase outward current. These actions may coexist. An intervention that blocks the potassium conductance need not remove all muscarinic slowing, because cAMP-dependent mechanisms may remain. Conversely, suppressing only the cAMP pathway may not abolish direct G protein activation of the potassium conductance. This logic illustrates how parallel signalling pathways can generate a robust physiological response.

Consider an illustrative perfused-heart experiment at a controlled baseline temperature. In condition A the spontaneous heart rate is 70 beats/min and developed force is 1.0 relative unit. After adrenergic stimulation the rate increases to 90 beats/min and force to 1.4 units. These observations alone cannot determine how much of the force change reflects rate-dependent calcium cycling and how much is independent of rate. In condition B, the preparation is electrically paced at the same rate before and after stimulation; an increase in force at matched rate supports an additional direct inotropic effect. The experiment still requires equivalent mechanical loading.

A related experiment evaluates atrioventricular transmission while pacing the atrium at a fixed rate. If autonomic stimulation changes the delay between atrial and ventricular activation without changing the imposed atrial period, it demonstrates that conduction can be modulated independently of spontaneous rate. If some paced atrial impulses fail to propagate under the new condition, the ventricular frequency may become different from the pacing frequency. Observations should therefore report both local activation intervals and the fraction of impulses transmitted.

The heart's autonomic control is not an on/off switch. Normal resting rate reflects both intrinsic pacemaker activity and ongoing neural modulation. The magnitude of each influence differs among species, developmental stages and behavioural states. Changes in temperature or circulating hormones can alter intrinsic properties at the same time that neural input changes. An isolated preparation establishes which effects can arise without ordinary central connections, whereas intact-animal measurements reveal the integrated outcome of those effects. Detailed baroreceptor and cardiovascular reflex circuits belong to the later neural regulation chapter.

25. Comparing tissue types and predicting the sites of intervention

A student who has learned a single textbook action potential may be tempted to assign it to every cardiac cell. The mammalian heart instead contains a range of cellular phenotypes. Ordinary atrial and ventricular myocytes principally generate force and propagate fast-response action potentials. Central SA nodal cells principally generate rhythm through slow-response action potentials. AV nodal cells conduct slowly and can provide subsidiary pacing. His–Purkinje tissue combines rapid conduction with potential subsidiary automaticity. These distinctions are functional generalisations, not rigid boundaries between every cell in the organ.

A useful prediction is obtained by comparing a selective reduction of fast sodium current with a reduction of L-type calcium current. The former directly reduces the regenerative inward current supporting rapid ventricular upstroke. The latter directly affects the principal upstroke in central nodal cells and the calcium trigger for working-myocyte contraction. Both interventions may alter the intact organ's rhythm and mechanical output, but the earliest measurable changes should occur in different physiological variables. In a mixed preparation, identifying the cell type and recording voltage plus contraction helps locate the principal effect.

A third intervention reduces gap-junctional conductance without directly altering voltage-gated sodium channels. An isolated ventricular cell might retain a relatively normal upstroke, whereas conduction across a tissue strip becomes slower or discontinuous. In comparison, a direct sodium-channel perturbation can be detected even in isolated cells. This experiment shows why both single-cell and tissue-level measurements are valuable. An observation at only one level cannot establish whether the primary defect lies in the membrane machinery of a cell or in the connections between cells.

A fourth intervention changes sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium uptake. If electrical pacing is maintained, rate need not change immediately in a ventricular tissue preparation. Cytosolic calcium clearance may become slower, while the store's ability to refill may be reduced. On later beats calcium release and force can change in ways that depend on the balance of entry and extrusion. The direct effect on relaxation and the secondary effect on subsequent systolic contraction must be distinguished in time.

The possibility of identifying a primary effect does not mean all interventions are perfectly selective. An inhibitor may affect multiple proteins, and a genetic alteration may permit long-term compensatory changes. A useful experimental control measures the proposed target function independently. For example, testing sodium current under controlled voltage conditions provides more direct evidence for a sodium-channel effect than observing slowed heartbeat alone. Restoration of a normal phenotype after reversal of an intervention strengthens the causal interpretation but does not prove that every downstream variable has returned to normal.

In an integrated question, the most informative starting point is the earliest variable that has changed. If the first change is a slower spontaneous depolarisation, investigate pacing. If the first change is reduced upstroke velocity in working myocardium, investigate regenerative excitation. If isolated-cell action potentials are normal but activation times diverge across tissue, investigate coupling and geometry. If calcium delivery changes despite preserved electrical activation, investigate excitation–contraction coupling. If voltage and calcium appear preserved while force changes, investigate myofilaments and loading. This approach converts a collection of named structures into a logical diagnostic framework for physiological experiments.

26. Experimental reasoning: distinguish rhythm, propagation and force

Electrophysiological experiments become more informative when the measured variable is explicitly identified. A current-clamp recording measures voltage as the cell responds to its ionic currents and applied stimulation. In voltage clamp, feedback circuitry holds membrane voltage near a chosen value and measures the current required to do so. Voltage steps to a series of potentials can reveal channel activation ranges and current direction, particularly when combined with ionic substitutions or sufficiently selective blockers. A recorded whole-cell current is the sum of components unless the experiment separates them.

To test whether an inward current is activated by hyperpolarisation, compare currents at progressively more negative test voltages after an appropriate holding condition. A slowly developing inward component that is reduced by an HCN-targeting intervention supports the identification of If. A rigorous interpretation also considers driving force, specificity of the intervention and other voltage-dependent conductances. The fact that a current appears at a negative voltage is not alone sufficient to identify its molecular carrier. Changing cAMP and examining the voltage dependence of activation tests a different feature of HCN regulation.

A paired-site activation experiment can distinguish a change in pacemaker rate from a conduction defect. Record successive activation times in an upstream atrial region and a downstream ventricular region. If both intervals lengthen equally while the atrial-to-ventricular delay remains unchanged, altered rhythm generation is a plausible first interpretation. If the atrial interval remains fixed but the ventricular activation becomes progressively delayed or some atrial events fail to produce ventricular events, impaired propagation is suggested. A single recording from the ventricle cannot reliably distinguish these possibilities.

An optical calcium indicator reports changes in calcium-dependent fluorescence. Its signal depends on indicator properties, concentration, binding kinetics and calibration. It is not automatically a quantitative absolute calcium measurement. Motion during contraction can introduce artefacts; recording voltage, fluorescence and force together helps determine their temporal relationship. If voltage precedes the calcium signal and calcium precedes force, the order supports excitation–contraction coupling. Selective perturbation of the putative intermediate is required to establish more than temporal association.

A conceptual re-entry model introduces one important exception to orderly activation. For an excitation wave to recur around a pathway, tissue ahead of the wave must have recovered excitability by the time it returns. Unidirectional block, sufficiently slow conduction and an available route can support re-entry. In an idealised homogeneous pathway the excitation wavelength may be approximated as conduction velocity multiplied by effective refractory period. A velocity of 0.40 m/s and refractory period of 0.25 s yield 0.10 m, or 10 cm. A 15 cm loop can in principle accommodate this wavelength, but geometry, source–sink relationships and actual recovery patterns determine whether re-entry occurs. This is a reasoning model, not a clinical diagnosis.

These experiments establish the conceptual bridge to electrocardiography. A surface recording measures voltage differences generated by coordinated electrical activity in tissue, rather than a direct record of the membrane potential of a single cell. A student who can distinguish local voltage, propagation, calcium delivery and force is prepared to interpret surface electrical events without confusing them with pressure changes or valve movements.

27. Reading a complete hypothetical experiment: from observations to a defensible explanation

Suppose a laboratory compares three cardiac tissue preparations under the same controlled temperature and perfusion. All preparations are stimulated at a fixed interval of 800 ms to remove differences in spontaneous firing rate from the first stage of analysis. The control preparation has an activation delay of 30 ms between two electrodes, a normalised calcium transient amplitude of 1.00 and a developed force of 1.00. Preparation X has an activation delay of 60 ms, calcium amplitude of 0.98 and force of 0.95. Preparation Y has a delay of 31 ms, calcium amplitude of 0.55 and force of 0.45. These numbers are deliberately hypothetical and do not reproduce a published dataset.

The first task is to decide which observation provides the strongest evidence about the primary difference between X and control. The marked increase in activation delay suggests altered propagation. The calcium amplitude and force remain relatively close to control at the recorded site. Possible explanations include reduced coupling, reduced sodium-channel availability or changed propagation geometry. It would be premature to select a particular connexin mutation from the delay alone. A recording of isolated-cell action-potential upstroke and a measure of intercellular conductance could separate some of these hypotheses.

If the two electrodes are 12 mm apart along the same known route in all preparations, the control apparent conduction velocity is 0.012/0.030 = 0.40 m/s. In X the velocity is 0.012/0.060 = 0.20 m/s. The conduction speed is halved despite the fact that the delay doubles. In Y the speed is approximately 0.387 m/s, close to control. The calculation quantifies a difference already suggested by the qualitative observations and reinforces the distinction between propagation speed and force generation.

Preparation Y instead exhibits a large reduction in calcium amplitude accompanied by a reduction in developed force. The propagation delay changes little. An alteration of calcium entry, calcium release, calcium buffering or store loading is therefore a stronger initial hypothesis than a major failure of conduction. The simultaneous reduction of calcium signal and force is consistent with impaired calcium delivery. It does not demonstrate that a particular channel is responsible. Voltage-clamp measurement of L-type current and a suitable estimate of releasable store content could provide additional evidence.

A fourth preparation Z might have a delay of 30 ms, calcium amplitude of 1.02 and force of 0.60. The calcium signal is approximately preserved, yet force falls substantially. If the calcium measurement is valid and loading conditions remain equivalent, the results shift attention to the relationship between calcium and myofilament force. Changes in calcium sensitivity, cross-bridge cycling or the mechanical integrity of tissue could account for the observations. Even here, a local fluorescence signal may not reveal all spatial irregularities in calcium release, so the inference should remain conditional on measurement quality.

The next stage removes fixed pacing and measures spontaneous rhythm only in preparations containing an appropriate pacemaker region. If the spontaneous interval changes while the conduction delay at matched paced rate remains unchanged, a rhythm-generation effect is supported. If spontaneous intervals are preserved yet ventricular responses become intermittent, propagation or recovery becomes a more plausible explanation. A working ventricular strip lacking normal pacemaker tissue is not an appropriate model in which to infer loss of sinoatrial automaticity merely from its failure to beat spontaneously.

An appropriate experiment therefore requires a defined preparation, matched conditions, an intervention linked to a testable hypothesis, and more than one measured variable whenever different mechanisms can produce the same outcome. A conclusion should be proportionate to the evidence. It is defensible to say that the delay in X supports impaired propagation. It is not defensible to say that X necessarily has a defect in a particular connexin without a corresponding measurement. This distinction between a supported physiological category and an unverified molecular identity is central to analytical CSIR-NET questions.

The full sequence now joins structure, voltage, timing, calcium delivery and force. The next chapter will examine how distributed electrical events generate voltage differences that can be measured outside the heart. An electrical surface signal can reveal important features of cardiac activation, but it cannot directly measure intracellular calcium, myofilament sensitivity or the mechanical work of the pump. Understanding these limits provides a sound foundation for interpreting electrocardiography without confusing its signals with the underlying mechanical cycle.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All arthropods have neurogenic hearts
Pacing origin is species-specific; adult decapod and horseshoe crab examples do not establish a universal arthropod rule. Why the trap occurs: Taxonomic categories are easy to memorise as universal rules. Exam reasoning: Compare two named arthropods and identify the actual cell type initiating each rhythm.
Myogenic means unregulated by nerves
The oscillator arises in muscle, but autonomic nerves modify its timing. Why the trap occurs: Students confuse independence of initiation with absence of regulation. Exam reasoning: Ask whether the nerve is necessary to initiate each beat, or instead changes an existing rhythm.
Intercalated discs fuse cells
Cells retain separate membranes; gap junctions establish electrical coupling. Why the trap occurs: The term syncytium can suggest cytoplasmic fusion. Exam reasoning: Distinguish anatomical continuity from current flow through paired connexons.
Purkinje fibres are nerves
They are specialised cardiac muscle cells. Why the trap occurs: A conducting fibre sounds like an axon. Exam reasoning: Identify the specialised tissue's muscle origin and fast-response cardiac action potential.
If is a sodium-only current
HCN channels conduct sodium and potassium, with net inward current at relevant diastolic voltages. Why the trap occurs: Its inward direction is often memorised as sodium entry alone. Exam reasoning: Use mixed permeability and the reversal potential to predict net current.
The ventricular plateau is electrically inactive
Inward and outward currents can nearly balance even while ions continue crossing. Why the trap occurs: A flat voltage trace is mistaken for zero ion flux. Exam reasoning: Apply the net-current balance: inward and outward contributions may cancel.
SERCA removes calcium from the entire cell
SERCA recycles calcium into the SR; membrane extrusion changes whole-cell calcium. Why the trap occurs: Removing calcium from cytosol sounds identical to removing calcium from the cell. Exam reasoning: Draw the sarcolemma and SR as separate boundaries and track the direction of each transporter.
A slow ventricular rate proves a slow SA node
Failure of conduction can produce a slower ventricular rate despite a normal atrial rate. Why the trap occurs: A downstream beat count is mistaken for direct sinus rate. Exam reasoning: Compare atrial and ventricular intervals and the fraction of impulses conducted.
A ligature experiment identifies the exact ion channel
It interrupts a region and can separate rhythms; voltage recordings and selective molecular perturbations are needed to identify channels. Why the trap occurs: Students treat a regional interruption as a molecular perturbation. Exam reasoning: Use a controlled voltage-clamp or channel-specific test to identify a molecular current.
Greater coupling always improves pacemaking
Coupling facilitates transmission but an excessive surrounding electrical sink can load a small oscillator. Why the trap occurs: Electrical coupling is often treated as uniformly beneficial. Exam reasoning: Distinguish transmission to a neighbour from electrical loading of a small pacemaker source.
Depolarising the resting membrane always increases excitability
Sustained depolarisation can inactivate sodium channels and reduce the regenerative response. Why the trap occurs: Threshold proximity is confused with sodium-channel availability. Exam reasoning: Predict both the voltage shift and the inactivation state before deciding whether conduction improves.
A calcium fluorescence signal measures force directly
Fluorescence tracks indicator-dependent calcium changes, while force depends on myofilaments and loading. Why the trap occurs: Calcium and tension transients rise in approximately the same sequence. Exam reasoning: Check what the indicator measures, hold load constant and test myofilament sensitivity separately.
A longer action potential guarantees stronger contraction
Calcium entry, release, store loading and sensitivity determine force; the voltage duration alone is insufficient. Why the trap occurs: The plateau supports calcium entry, encouraging a linear force prediction. Exam reasoning: Compare trigger influx, SR content, buffering and force–calcium sensitivity instead of duration alone.
A blocker proves a single molecular target
Off-target effects and compensation may occur; independent target measurement and suitable controls strengthen causal inference. Why the trap occurs: Selective interventions are idealised in many textbook questions. Exam reasoning: Distinguish a stated perfectly selective model from an actual experiment needing specificity controls.
Every β1 effect requires direct phosphorylation of HCN
cAMP directly regulates HCN gating, while kinase-dependent effects act on other proteins as well. Why the trap occurs: cAMP and PKA are often memorised as one obligatory pathway. Exam reasoning: Separate direct cyclic-nucleotide binding to HCN from phosphorylation of other targets.

Original examination practice

These questions are original practice items, not past examination questions. Questions 1–12 use short-response format; subsequent items use multiple-choice or assertion–reason format. Attempt each question before revealing its explanation.

1. Part B

Which characteristic specifically describes spontaneous impulse generation?

Answer and reasoning

Automaticity. Conductivity measures propagation; contractility concerns force; excitability describes responsiveness.

2. Part B

What is the immediate principal depolarising current during phase 0 in a central SA nodal cell?

Answer and reasoning

L-type calcium current. Fast sodium current is prominent in working ventricular myocardium, not the central nodal upstroke.

3. Part B

What is the principal role of gap junctions in intercalated discs?

Answer and reasoning

Electrical coupling through ion movement. Desmosomes and adherens junctions primarily serve mechanical functions.

4. Part B

What activates the HCN channel?

Answer and reasoning

Hyperpolarisation, modulated by cAMP. HCN conductance carries mixed sodium and potassium current; activation voltage does not dictate current direction.

5. Part B

Which transporter replenishes SR calcium?

Answer and reasoning

SERCA2a. Forward NCX extrudes calcium rather than recycling it into the SR.

6. Part B

Which protein directly binds calcium in thin filament regulation?

Answer and reasoning

Troponin C. Tropomyosin shifts its position downstream of changes in troponin.

7. Part C

Two nodal cells have identical threshold and maximum diastolic potential, but A has a steeper phase 4 slope. Other phase durations are equal. Which fires faster?

Answer and reasoning

Cell A. The voltage excursion is identical, so the greater average slope shortens the time to threshold.

8. Part C

A preparation retains a rapid sodium-dependent action potential after L-type calcium-current reduction, but its calcium transient and force decline. What is the most direct explanation?

Answer and reasoning

Reduced trigger for RyR2-mediated release. Preserved excitation argues against complete loss of excitability; decreased calcium delivery explains reduced force.

9. Part C

Atrial activation recurs every 800 ms. Only alternate atrial activations produce ventricular activation. What are the two rates?

Answer and reasoning

75 atrial and 37.5 ventricular beats per minute. The atrial period is 0.8 s, ventricular period 1.6 s; rate equals 60 divided by period in seconds.

A 20 mm pathway has a 50 ms propagation delay. Compute average velocity.

Answer and reasoning

0.40 m/s. Convert to 0.020 m and 0.050 s, then divide. Assumes measured distance represents the pathway.

Electrical pacing and calcium transient amplitude remain constant while tension decreases. Which site merits investigation?

Answer and reasoning

Myofilament calcium responsiveness or cross-bridge function. A force defect with comparable measured calcium and excitation shifts attention downstream, while calibration and loading still require controls.

An idealised pathway has conduction velocity 0.5 m/s and effective refractory period 200 ms. Compute wavelength.

Answer and reasoning

0.10 m. Multiply 0.5 by 0.200 s. A pathway longer than this may support re-entry under other necessary conditions; length alone cannot prove it.

Which of the following is an electrical rather than a mechanical property?

C. Conduction velocity quantifies the spread of activation. A and B describe mechanical properties, while D is a pump-volume measure.

The physiological bridge through the fibrous atrioventricular junction is which structure?

B. The bundle of His conducts excitation across the insulating junction. The other structures have venous or mechanical roles.

A. Rapid voltage-gated sodium current drives the upstroke. Potassium efflux supports repolarisation, SERCA recycles calcium, and HCN channels contribute to spontaneous diastolic depolarisation.

Answer and reasoning

A. The plateau reflects a net balance of currents, including inward calcium and outward potassium. The other descriptions ignore that dynamic balance.

Which statement about refractory periods is correct?

B. Effective refractoriness is defined operationally by the lack of a propagated response. Channel states vary; a normal second response is not guaranteed.

What is the main function of RyR2 during cardiac activation?

B. RyR2 is an SR calcium-release channel. The other alternatives confuse it with transporters or contractile processes.

At 37 °C, free external calcium is 1 mmol/L and free internal calcium is 0.1 micromol/L. Calculate approximate ECa.

A. +123 mV; B. −123 mV; C. +246 mV; D. +30.75 mV

Answer and reasoning

A. The ratio is 1000/0.1 = 10,000; log10 is 4. ECa = 30.75 × 4 = +123 mV, using calcium valence +2.

43. Part C

A cell with capacitance 80 pF has an inward net current of 8 pA. Estimate dVm/dt at that instant.

A. −100 mV/s; B. +100 mV/s; C. +10 mV/s; D. +640 mV/s

Answer and reasoning

B. With outward-positive convention the current is −8 pA. dVm/dt = −I/C = 0.1 V/s = +100 mV/s.

44. Part C

The control resting and peak voltages are −80 and +20 mV. Which voltage represents 90% repolarisation?

A. −90 mV; B. −70 mV; C. −20 mV; D. +10 mV

Answer and reasoning

B. Amplitude is 100 mV. Ninety percent repolarisation is a 90 mV fall from +20 mV, giving −70 mV.

45. Part C

An atrial impulse reaches the entry of a region at 45 ms and its exit at 115 ms. After an intervention the times are 45 and 155 ms. Distal propagation after exit is unchanged. What is the added delay?

A. 40 ms within the bracketed region; B. 70 ms within distal myocardium; C. 110 ms before entry; D. No delay

Answer and reasoning

A. The regional interval changes from 70 to 110 ms, adding 40 ms. The unchanged distal interval does not account for the additional delay.

46. Part C

Trigger calcium entry is 5 units and SR release is 35 units in A; entry is 10 and release is 50 in B. Which has higher operational release-to-entry gain?

A. A; B. B; C. Both equal; D. Cannot compute the defined ratio

Answer and reasoning

A. A has 35/5 = 7; B has 50/10 = 5. B releases more calcium in absolute terms but has lower gain.

47. Part C

In an ideal exponential calcium decay, a cell with τ = 50 ms is compared with one with τ = 100 ms. What fraction of initial excess remains at 100 ms in each?

A. 13.5% and 36.8%; B. 36.8% and 13.5%; C. 50% and 100%; D. Both 0%

Answer and reasoning

A. exp(−100/50) = exp(−2) ≈ 0.135; exp(−100/100) = exp(−1) ≈ 0.368. The smaller time constant clears the model excess faster.

48. Part C

A mixed cation current has reversal potential −30 mV. At −60 mV its conductance is 2 nS. At the same voltage conductance increases to 4 nS. What changes?

A. Inward current magnitude doubles; B. Reversal potential necessarily doubles; C. Current becomes outward; D. Current disappears

Answer and reasoning

A. At fixed driving force −30 mV, current changes from −60 to −120 pA. Conductance change alone does not reverse current or change the specified reversal potential.

49. Part C

A paced ventricular strip shows reduced force while the calcium signal and activation delay are unchanged. Which follow-up best tests a myofilament mechanism?

A. Force versus controlled calcium at matched muscle length; B. Count red blood cells; C. Measure only SA spontaneous rate; D. Measure body temperature without controlling it

Answer and reasoning

A. A controlled force–calcium relationship tests sensitivity downstream of calcium delivery. The other alternatives do not directly test the proposed mechanism.

50. Part C

An impulse traverses a 12 cm loop with speed 0.3 m/s. ERP is 300 ms. Which statement is correct in the simplified model?

A. Travel time is 400 ms, longer than ERP; re-entry is possible but not guaranteed; B. Travel time is 40 ms; C. Wavelength is 90 cm; D. Re-entry is certain

Answer and reasoning

A. 0.12/0.3 = 0.4 s; wavelength 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09 m. Geometry, block and propagation still govern whether re-entry occurs.

51. Part C

A depolarising event appears after full repolarisation and is reduced by suppressing spontaneous SR calcium release. Which mechanism is consistent?

A. Delayed afterdepolarisation involving inward NCX; B. Ordinary phase 0 caused by nerve impulse necessarily; C. Reduced calcium storage proves absence of membrane current; D. Early afterdepolarisation by definition

Answer and reasoning

A. A post-repolarisation event linked to spontaneous calcium release is consistent with a delayed afterdepolarisation and inward exchanger current. The timing distinguishes it from an early event.

52. SET

Assertion: Normal mammalian heart rhythm is myogenic. Reason: Autonomic nerves can alter pacemaker frequency.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason does not explain assertion; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning

B. Both statements are true. Myogenic identity refers to the origin of rhythm in cardiac muscle; autonomic modulation alone does not establish its origin.

53. SET

Which sequence places the events in their normal causal order?

A. Troponin binding → SA excitation → Ca²⁺ entry; B. SA excitation → ventricular activation → Ca²⁺ release → tension; C. Tension → RyR2 opening → conduction; D. SR uptake → SA excitation is the sole cause

Answer and reasoning

B. Normal excitation spreads to working myocardium, initiates calcium entry and release, and activates contraction. The other sequences reverse causal events.

54. SET

Which statements are correct? I. Gap junctions enable electrical coupling. II. SERCA returns calcium to SR. III. The ventricular plateau requires zero ionic current through every channel.

A. I only; B. II only; C. I and II only; D. All three

Answer and reasoning

C. I and II are correct. III confuses near balance of total current with absence of individual inward and outward currents.

55. SET

Assertion: Prolonged ventricular refractoriness limits physiological fused tetanus. Reason: The refractory interval overlaps much of contraction.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Both false

Answer and reasoning

A. A new normally propagated response cannot occur through much of the existing twitch, limiting repeated electrical stimulation during sustained contraction.

Revision and mastery

Mechanistic summary

Start with the biological distinction between myogenic and neurogenic pacing. In mammalian hearts the SA node sets the ordinary rhythm; atrial excitation reaches the slowly conducting AV node, crosses the fibrous junction through the His bundle, and spreads rapidly through bundle branches and Purkinje fibres. Electrical coupling joins individual muscle cells into a functional syncytium. This organisation enables a timely sequence of mechanical activation.

Central nodal cells show phases 4, 0 and 3: spontaneous diastolic depolarisation, mainly Ca²⁺-dependent upstroke and potassium-dependent repolarisation. Working ventricular cells show fast Na⁺ upstroke, early repolarisation, a calcium-supported plateau, potassium-dependent recovery and relatively stable diastolic voltage. The long refractory interval prevents normal fused tetanus. The calcium trigger from L-type channels recruits RyR2 release; calcium binds troponin C and enables cross-bridge cycling. SERCA replenishes the SR, and NCX contributes to whole-cell extrusion. Autonomic pathways adjust rate, conduction, contractile state and relaxation.

For a trace, first identify the recorded variable, axes, preparation, activation reference and manipulated condition. Compare nodal with working myocardial phenotypes before naming currents.
