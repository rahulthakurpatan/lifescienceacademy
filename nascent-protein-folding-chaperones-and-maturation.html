1. From a translation product to a working protein

A ribosome makes a covalent chain of amino acids. It does not make an active site in the same manner that it makes a peptide bond. The active site of an enzyme may contain residues contributed by several distant regions of the sequence, while a receptor may have a binding surface that exists only after two domains approach one another. Thus, determining the order of amino acids solves one problem of protein production, whereas positioning those amino acids in space solves a second problem. A cell must achieve both results, often thousands of times in parallel, without allowing the newly made chains to interfere with one another.

Consider a newly translated enzyme whose catalytic residues occur at positions 25, 89 and 160. During synthesis of the first 100 residues, the first two residues can acquire local structural relationships but the third residue does not yet exist. Consequently, even a perfectly accurate partial sequence cannot generate the entire catalytic arrangement. After the missing region becomes available, interactions between the regions can bring the residues together. The acquisition of activity depends on three-dimensional organization, not merely on the number of residues synthesized. The same distinction holds for structural proteins: a chain may contain every residue needed for a filament yet fail to make the appropriate interface with its neighboring subunits.

The word nascent refers to a protein chain that is being produced or has very recently been produced. Strictly, a ribosome-bound nascent chain is attached through its carboxyl end to a tRNA, until the ester linkage is hydrolyzed at termination. The amino-terminal part is older than the carboxyl-terminal part because peptide synthesis proceeds from the amino terminus toward the carboxyl terminus. This temporal asymmetry matters. A cell does not normally receive all the residues of a long polypeptide simultaneously, as one might imagine when looking at a static drawing of a protein. The amino-terminal region has opportunities to interact with its surroundings while later sequence regions are still inaccessible.

Folding is the acquisition of conformational organization by a polypeptide chain. Maturation is a broader term describing the acquisition of a state appropriate for biological function. Folding can be one aspect of maturation, but other aspects may include covalent modification, removal of a segment, association with a cofactor, association with additional subunits or movement to a suitable compartment. A particular protein may need only a subset of these events. A small cytosolic enzyme might become active directly after folding, whereas a secreted protein may require oxidatively formed disulfides and cleavage of a precursor. Neither model applies to every protein.

A mature protein is not necessarily rigid. Molecular movements are indispensable to many functions, including ligand binding, catalysis and allosteric regulation. A functional protein can occupy an ensemble of closely related conformations rather than a unique motionless geometry. Moreover, intrinsically disordered regions can serve as interaction platforms or regulatory elements without forming an independently stable compact structure. Therefore, it would be misleading to define successful maturation as the conversion of every flexible chain into a compact globule. The appropriate endpoint is a state or set of states compatible with the protein's biological role and environment.

When a folding pathway fails, the products may be soluble inactive molecules, abnormal oligomers or insoluble aggregates. These are distinguishable possibilities. A soluble product may have the wrong geometry despite remaining dispersed in water. A large aggregate may contain chains that are only partly folded, and an assembly of multiple chains can also be completely normal if it has the correct interfaces and function. Students should avoid treating solubility, folding, assembly and activity as interchangeable observations. Each can be investigated with different experimental measurements.

This separation is central to the interpretation of a mutation. A mutant enzyme with low activity might be synthesized slowly, might fold inefficiently, might fail to bind its cofactor, might be degraded rapidly, or might form a stable native conformation with a damaged catalytic center. Identifying which mechanism is responsible requires evidence beyond the activity value itself. Measuring RNA, newly synthesized protein, total protein, soluble protein and catalytic activity provides distinct information about successive stages of production. A cell can therefore display normal RNA abundance and normal translation while losing most of the functional protein output.

2. What the amino acid sequence contributes to folding

A sequence provides a chemical pattern. Side chains can be nonpolar, polar, positively charged or negatively charged under a specified set of conditions. Some carry groups capable of covalent disulfide formation, some can coordinate metal ions and some impose strong conformational preferences. The backbone supplies repeated amide and carbonyl groups capable of forming hydrogen bonds. As the chain changes its geometry, these chemically different groups approach or move away from one another. Favorable arrangements depend on interactions among residues as well as interactions between the chain and water, ions, ligands or a membrane.

For many soluble globular proteins, exposing extensive nonpolar surface to water is unfavorable relative to forming a compact structure in which much of that surface is buried. When hydrophobic regions associate within a chain, they may help create a core around which other residues can pack. Burial is not simply a universal attraction between any two hydrophobic residues. An inappropriate contact can obstruct the correct arrangement of the backbone or bury a polar group without satisfying its hydrogen-bonding requirements. Folding therefore requires compatible patterns of interaction rather than the indiscriminate collapse of every nonpolar segment.

The chemical environment changes which arrangements are favored. A stretch of nonpolar residues destined for the lipid bilayer can be favorable at a membrane-facing surface. The same stretch, exposed in aqueous cytosol for too long, may associate abnormally with other hydrophobic chains. A protein destined for an oxidizing environment may acquire a disulfide linkage that is rarely stable in a reducing compartment. Consequently, knowing the sequence is not equivalent to knowing all the boundary conditions of a folding experiment. The relevant compartment, partners and solution conditions are part of a scientifically meaningful description.

A domain is a structural region that can often fold or function with a degree of independence. Two domains joined by a flexible linker may acquire substantial structure at different times during translation. However, domains are not guaranteed to be independently stable. Some require contacts with neighboring regions or an oligomeric partner. The term domain describes a useful organizational unit, not an absolute rule that each block of sequence can be removed and remain functional. A fragment that adopts secondary structure need not have acquired the complete tertiary organization of a domain.

The native structure is generally favored under the conditions in which a protein normally operates, but thermodynamic stability and folding speed address different properties. A structure may be highly favorable yet difficult to reach. For example, a chain may first form an arrangement containing numerous stabilizing contacts that are incompatible with the final arrangement. Breaking those contacts before establishing correct ones creates a kinetic barrier. Chaperones can help the chain avoid or escape such obstacles without specifying a new amino acid sequence. This distinction explains why a protein can contain all the structural information in its sequence and still depend strongly on a cellular folding system.

One should also distinguish folding assistance from repair of translation errors. The ribosome and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are responsible for reading and supplying amino acids with high fidelity. A chaperone generally cannot recognize that a particular codon should have specified another residue and then replace the incorrect amino acid in the peptide backbone. Instead, its binding behavior responds to properties of the protein it encounters, such as a hydrophobic segment that remains exposed. A folding defect can reveal an amino acid substitution indirectly, but the chaperone is not another genetic decoding apparatus.

3. How the classic refolding experiment changed the problem

A crucial historical question was whether a cell had to impose a three-dimensional structure on every protein or whether the covalent sequence itself was sufficient to guide the process under suitable conditions. Studies of bovine pancreatic ribonuclease A by Christian Anfinsen and colleagues supplied an experimentally tractable answer. This enzyme has eight cysteine residues engaged in four intramolecular disulfides in its native state. Its ability to hydrolyze RNA offers a functional assay that is independent of merely detecting protein mass.

Two perturbations can be separated in the experiment. A denaturant such as urea disrupts the noncovalent interactions that maintain native structure; a reducing agent breaks disulfide linkages by converting paired cysteines into thiols. Neither treatment, in the intended experimental conditions, is designed to hydrolyze the backbone peptide bonds. Together they produce a much less ordered chain with chemically reduced cysteine groups and very low catalytic activity. Because the sequence survives the treatment, recovery of activity after restoring appropriate conditions can test whether the sequence retains the necessary conformational information.

If denaturation and reduction are reversed under suitable conditions, ribonuclease can regain activity. This result would be difficult to explain by a model in which a separate, permanent structural template is essential for every individual molecule. The system recovers the functional arrangement without the original native structure acting as a physical mold. The reasoning is stronger when recovery is assessed by enzyme activity rather than appearance alone: a compact but incorrectly organized structure might not catalyze RNA cleavage.

The order of restoring the conditions matters. If the cysteines are allowed to oxidize while the chain remains denatured, many nonnative linkages can form. Removal of denaturant then leaves a population whose covalent constraints interfere with the proper arrangement. Allowing a limited amount of thiol-disulfide exchange gives incorrect pairings an opportunity to rearrange and enables recovery toward the native arrangement. This illustrates the difference between simply having four disulfides and having the correct four disulfides.

The numerical reasoning highlights the selectivity of the process. If eight labeled cysteines are paired into four intramolecular disulfides without restrictions, one cysteine can choose any of seven partners. Of the six remaining cysteines, a selected one can choose among five partners. The next choice has three possibilities, and the last pair has one. Therefore the number of distinct complete pairings is 7 × 5 × 3 × 1 = 105. The procedure counts each pairing once because a particular remaining cysteine is chosen at every step; it does not count different orders of forming the same bonds as different final pairings.

A naive model in which all complete pairings have equal probability would assign the native pattern a probability of one in 105, or about 0.95 percent. This is not a measured activity value and must not be presented as one. Different pairings have different probabilities and stabilities. The native pattern can be favored because the spatial organization of the sequence brings appropriate groups together, while exchange permits unfavorable linkages to be replaced. The calculation is useful precisely because it demonstrates why the number of disulfide bonds by itself is insufficient to identify a folded protein.

The result does not establish that every polypeptide refolds rapidly after arbitrary denaturation. Denaturing conditions can promote irreversible aggregation, chemical modification or loss of an essential partner. Some proteins require an appropriate membrane, cofactor or assembly context. A sequence that is well suited to folding as it emerges from a ribosome may not find the same path efficiently when all its segments are released simultaneously from a denatured preparation. The classic experiment establishes an important principle without requiring that the behavior of a relatively small soluble enzyme be universal.

A useful experimental control would be native enzyme exposed to the final assay conditions without prior denaturation. It provides the activity expected from undamaged material. Denatured and reduced enzyme assayed before restoration of folding conditions serves as a contrasting control. An additional preparation in which incorrect disulfides are trapped tests whether the ability to rearrange those bonds affects the outcome. Equal quantities of enzyme and matching assay conditions are needed to distinguish genuine activity recovery from differences in concentration, pH or residual denaturant. For historical context, the 1972 Nobel lecture and the accompanying research literature describe the central role of this work; the suggested controls here also provide a general teaching framework for interpreting such experiments.

4. Nascent chain emergence and the time dimension

The catalytic center of the large ribosomal subunit lies within the ribosome, and the new chain passes from that center through an exit tunnel before reaching the external medium. A stretch near the carboxyl end of a ribosome-attached chain remains within or near the tunnel, whereas the amino-terminal region can be accessible outside. In many ribosomes, the tunnel accommodates a chain segment of a few tens of residues in an extended conformation; the exact number depends on the chain's conformation and the geometry used to define its boundaries. For examination reasoning it is more useful to understand the restricted availability of the chain than to assume a single fixed residue count.

The exit tunnel is not a featureless pipe. Its dimensions constrain the conformations that can form and can allow certain local structures, including short helical segments, in suitable regions. A large independently folded globular domain generally cannot form inside the narrow portion. As additional residues emerge, their ability to contact one another changes. This makes ribosome-associated folding a temporally ordered process rather than an instantaneous event triggered only by the final peptide bond.

Suppose a protein contains two domains. The first occupies residues 1–120 and the second residues 140–280, separated by a short linker. When the ribosome has synthesized residue 180, a substantial part of the first domain and its linker can be outside the exit tunnel. The first domain may therefore fold while the second is incomplete. If the two domains must interlock extensively, however, the first region may remain partly structured until the second becomes available. The outcome depends on the sequence, relative stability of the regions and the local molecular environment.

An important consequence is that folding of a protein synthesized sequentially can differ from folding of the full chain after experimental denaturation. During synthesis, a domain may bury a hydrophobic region before a later, potentially competing segment appears. In the denatured full-length chain, both regions can interact from the outset and may establish an incorrect contact. Thus the route taken to a final state can affect the yield, even when the final sequence is identical. Cotranslational folding is not evidence against sequence-encoded structure; it shows that accessibility of the sequence changes during the journey.

The ribosome can also influence the behavior of nearby chains through surface interactions and steric restriction. Chaperones associated with the ribosome are positioned to capture exposed segments soon after they emerge. For soluble cytosolic proteins these interactions can keep potentially aggregation-prone regions available for later productive folding. For a membrane or secretory protein, the emergence of a targeting signal can instead initiate a pathway that places the chain in a different environment. Detailed recognition and translocation are considered in the subsequent targeting chapters.

The speed of elongation sets the time available for structural events between the emergence of successive regions. In a simplified picture, if the average translation speed is 10 residues per second and an additional 60 residues must be made before another domain becomes accessible, the earlier domain has about six seconds before that event. Changing the speed to 20 residues per second decreases that interval to three seconds. This arithmetic describes the time between emergence events under constant speed; it does not determine the probability of correct folding without further information about the kinetics.

Translation speed is influenced by multiple factors, including availability of charged tRNAs, codon context and interactions of the nascent peptide with the ribosome. Some synonymous sequence changes can therefore alter folding efficiency without changing the encoded amino acid sequence. However, not every rare codon is a programmed folding pause, and not every synonymous change affects folding. A changed nucleotide sequence may also alter mRNA stability, secondary structure or initiation. An experiment that aims to attribute a change specifically to elongation should control for these possibilities rather than infer the mechanism from codon usage alone.

A distinguishable category is post-translational folding: substantial conformational organization occurs after release of the completed chain. For some proteins this is the major route, whereas others begin folding during synthesis and finish after termination. Release provides the carboxyl-terminal region with full accessibility and removes the tether to tRNA. At that point, previously incomplete domains can form, contacts between domains can be established and chaperonin-assisted folding can occur for appropriate substrates. The two terms describe timing relative to translation, not mutually exclusive types of protein.

The cell consequently manages a continuously changing set of protein conformations. The number of immature chains depends on translation rate and the time taken to mature them. If synthesis accelerates without a matching increase in the ability to process those chains, transient nonnative states can accumulate. This creates a link between the regulation of translation studied in the previous chapters and the availability of folding machinery studied here.

5. Why cellular folding requires protection

A purified protein at low concentration is surrounded primarily by solvent and a few selected reagents. The cytoplasm contains many proteins, nucleic acids, ribosomes, metabolites and other macromolecules. Their presence affects diffusion, excluded volume and the opportunities for association. Crowding can sometimes favor compact conformations, but it can also promote association between proteins. It is not correct to describe crowding as uniformly harmful or beneficial. Its consequences depend on the relative sizes, interactions and concentrations of the species involved.

A newly made chain commonly exposes segments that will later be buried in a folded core or in the interface of an assembled complex. While exposed, such segments can bind to inappropriate regions of the same chain or to another chain. Intermolecular associations are especially consequential because they can create progressively larger complexes in which several chains are mutually trapped. A molecular chaperone can temporarily bind an exposed surface, preventing it from making an inappropriate contact while allowing subsequent opportunities to acquire the correct structure.

Many chaperones recognize the nonnative state rather than the identity of one particular protein. This is possible because many unrelated proteins expose similar kinds of interaction surfaces when incompletely folded. A chaperone that recognizes a short accessible hydrophobic segment can interact with many substrates without requiring a different chaperone gene for every sequence. Its specificity is not unlimited: accessible segments differ in affinity, and cochaperones, localization and the stage of synthesis determine which substrates are encountered.

This explains why a chaperone need not be part of the mature structure of a client protein. It can bind for a period, release the protein, and then act on a different chain. The mature client generally retains its own structural information. Chaperones themselves are proteins that must acquire appropriate conformations, and some depend on other chaperones during their biogenesis. The process is a network of molecular assistance rather than a linear chain ending in a single universal folding enzyme.

Aggregation prevention and acceleration of productive folding are not identical effects. A binding protein that temporarily sequesters a vulnerable region can improve the final yield by reducing aggregation even when the intrinsic rate of folding of a freely available monomer does not increase. Other systems can actively alter the conformation of kinetically trapped substrates or repeatedly provide opportunities for folding. In either case, the final activity recovered can increase, but the underlying cause is different. An experiment that records only the final amount of activity cannot always discriminate between these mechanisms.

6. The first line of protection near a bacterial ribosome

A useful starting point for understanding chaperone organization is the bacterium Escherichia coli. Unlike a eukaryotic cell, it does not have an endoplasmic reticulum, but it still has to handle newly emerging proteins in a crowded aqueous cytoplasm. One important bacterial folding factor, trigger factor, associates with the large ribosomal subunit near the exit tunnel. This placement means that it can encounter a chain before a freely diffusing chaperone necessarily encounters it. It is neither a ribosomal RNA component nor a translation elongation factor, even though its position places it close to the machinery of translation.

Trigger factor makes transient contacts with emerging chains and provides a protected region in which certain segments can avoid inappropriate intermolecular interactions. A binding surface need not recognize a single strict amino acid motif to perform this role. Broad recognition allows the same molecule to interact with chemically diverse newly translated proteins. The nature and duration of contact depend on the available sequence and the chain's structural state. A folded region can become less attractive as a substrate when it buries surfaces that would otherwise interact with the factor.

Trigger factor contains a peptidyl-prolyl isomerase domain, but its contribution to nascent-chain protection should not be reduced to that catalytic activity alone. A molecule can provide substantial chaperone assistance by interacting with a substrate even if a particular catalytic activity is dispensable for that protective effect. This is a useful general example of a protein having multiple mechanistically distinguishable activities. Mutating the site that catalyzes isomerization need not give the same phenotype as deleting the entire factor or disrupting its association with the ribosome.

Unlike the Hsp70 cycle discussed below, trigger factor does not require ATP hydrolysis to perform its characteristic chaperone role. Association and dissociation follow the reversible molecular interactions of factor, ribosome and substrate. An ATP-independent system can nevertheless improve folding yield because simply avoiding an inappropriate association changes the probability of reaching a productive state. Energy consumption is not the definition of a chaperone. The defining functional concept is assistance with the acquisition or maintenance of an appropriate protein conformation or assembly state.

Trigger factor and the bacterial Hsp70 system can have overlapping responsibilities. If one system is absent, another may take on a greater fraction of the vulnerable nascent chains. A moderate defect in one mutant therefore does not prove that the missing factor has no function. Testing combinations of defects, or changing the conditions under which a protein must fold, may reveal dependence that is masked in an otherwise well-equipped cell. The same principle is familiar from genetic redundancy: an observable phenotype depends on what compensatory functions remain available.

Eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes are also associated with factors that influence nascent-chain handling, including Hsp70-related machinery. The nascent polypeptide-associated complex, abbreviated NAC, interacts with ribosomes and has roles in early protein biogenesis and targeting decisions. It should not be described as the eukaryotic version of bacterial trigger factor in the sense of being the same protein under a different name. The general biological problem is conserved, while the individual components and their organization may differ. This distinction becomes important in comparative questions that ask about functions rather than exact identities.

7. Hsp70: the molecular logic of reversible substrate binding

The Hsp70 family illustrates how nucleotide binding and hydrolysis can control an interaction between a chaperone and a nonnative protein. Members of this family occur in different organisms and compartments. In E. coli the major bacterial example is DnaK. Eukaryotic cytosolic Hsp70s perform related functions, and BiP is an Hsp70 family member in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum. Mitochondria contain their own Hsp70-related machinery. A family name describes conserved molecular principles, not a single protein with identical localization in every cell.

An Hsp70 molecule has a nucleotide-binding domain and a substrate-binding domain. The first domain interacts with ATP or ADP and participates in hydrolysis. The second contains a region that can accommodate a short peptide segment from a substrate. A movable structural element associated with the substrate-binding region helps control access and retention. The two domains communicate: changing the bound nucleotide changes the conformational behavior of the substrate-binding region. Such coupling between distant sites is an instance of allostery.

The substrate commonly presents an accessible peptide segment enriched in hydrophobic residues. In a properly folded soluble protein, many such segments are inaccessible because they form part of the internal core. A newly emerging or misfolded chain presents more of these potential binding sites. However, exposed hydrophobicity is not a perfect definition of a damaged protein. Some functional membrane proteins possess hydrophobic segments as part of their normal destination signals or transmembrane regions. Chaperone recognition must be understood in the context of substrate location, binding partners and timing.

When Hsp70 carries ATP, its substrate-binding region generally has rapid exchange behavior: peptide segments can associate and dissociate relatively quickly. The ATP-bound state is often described as open and relatively low affinity. Rapid association is helpful because the protein can sample available segments. Rapid dissociation would be a disadvantage if the aim were to retain a particularly vulnerable sequence for longer. Conversion to the ADP-bound state solves this second requirement.

Hydrolysis of ATP converts the nucleotide-bound chaperone toward a state with slower substrate dissociation and more stable peptide binding. Describing this state as closed and high affinity is a useful simplified model. Retention can protect the captured segment against inappropriate contacts while other parts of the substrate remain free to move. ATP hydrolysis does not make a new peptide bond in the client, and the nucleotide is not incorporated into its structure. The role of the chemical reaction is to bias the behavior of the chaperone's conformational cycle.

Hsp70 is not normally studied in isolation when explaining its cellular cycle. J-domain proteins, classically represented by the bacterial Hsp40 DnaJ, stimulate ATP hydrolysis by Hsp70. Many J proteins also help recognize or deliver substrates. Substrate binding and J-domain stimulation are coordinated so that a relatively transient encounter can be converted into a more persistent interaction where it is useful. The role of Hsp40 is therefore different from that of the ribosome, which synthesizes the chain, and different from that of Hsp70, which is the nucleotide-controlled peptide-binding factor.

If Hsp70 remained in the ADP-bound state indefinitely, substrate retention would create a different problem. A captured segment might need to become buried in the client's own hydrophobic core before the final conformation can be achieved. In that case the chaperone must release it. Nucleotide exchange factors promote dissociation of ADP from the nucleotide-binding domain; in a cell with sufficient ATP, ATP can then bind and restore the more rapidly exchanging state. In the standard bacterial DnaK system the exchange factor is GrpE. Eukaryotic Hsp70s use several different families of exchange factors, so GrpE should not be assigned indiscriminately to every compartment.

Following release, the substrate has an opportunity to establish intramolecular contacts or associate with the correct partner. A client that has reached a suitable conformation may no longer expose the binding segment and can leave the cycle. A client that remains nonnative may be captured again. Multiple rounds are therefore possible. There is no fixed requirement that each substrate consume exactly one ATP before becoming active. The number of productive and unproductive cycles depends on the client's behavior, the available cochaperones and environmental conditions.

The sequence of nucleotide-dependent events also provides a framework for predicting mutant phenotypes. A DnaK variant that retains ATP binding and substrate recognition but hydrolyzes ATP very slowly is expected to have difficulty stabilizing a captured segment in the normal long-lived state. This can increase the probability of aggregation for susceptible clients. By contrast, a variant that hydrolyzes ATP normally but fails to release ADP can retain substrate excessively. The latter mutant may produce a pool of soluble chaperone-bound clients without a corresponding increase in active protein. Both defects can reduce productive folding, although the underlying nucleotide state is opposite.

A J-protein defect may affect selected substrates more strongly than others because J proteins often contribute to substrate recruitment and localization. If the only measured phenotype is a reduction in total cellular activity, one cannot conclude that ATP hydrolysis has failed globally. Measuring the ATPase behavior of isolated Hsp70 in the presence and absence of its J partner, together with substrate binding and release, could distinguish these explanations. Similarly, a defect in a nucleotide exchange factor can often be reasoned about as a problem of reopening the binding cycle rather than a loss of initial recognition.

The importance of the cycle lies in its ability to couple timing to molecular interactions. A purely reversible peptide-binding factor can protect a client, but an ATP-driven cycle can regulate the residence time through stages whose rates are influenced by other proteins and the nucleotide environment. The system operates away from equilibrium when cellular metabolism continually supplies ATP. This does not mean that ATP hydrolysis directly determines the final geometry of the client. It enables a regulated sequence of capture, retention and release, through which the client can sample productive folding routes.

One can make a simple energetic distinction without introducing unnecessary biophysical mathematics. Suppose an isolated Hsp70 system performs 240 ATP hydrolysis events while 60 newly synthesized client molecules acquire measurable activity. The ratio is four ATP hydrolysis events per active molecule recovered. This is a system-level ratio for the observed interval, not a claim that each active molecule experienced exactly four cycles. Some cycles might have been spent on clients that never became active, and some clients might have folded after only one interaction. Interpreting ATP consumption requires attention to what is counted in the numerator and denominator.

When ATP becomes scarce, several processes change at once. Translation, transport, chaperone cycling and other ATP-dependent functions can all be affected. In an intact cell, ATP depletion followed by loss of protein activity cannot on its own establish a specific Hsp70 defect. A reconstituted system containing a defined client, Hsp70, its cochaperones and controlled nucleotides can isolate the dependence more directly. Even then, the experiment should distinguish the effect of ATP on the chaperone from any direct requirement of the client enzyme for ATP during its activity assay.

8. Chaperonins: folding with spatial separation

Hsp70 protects accessible segments of individual substrates. Some folding intermediates need a different kind of assistance: a larger fraction of the chain benefits from being physically separated from other chains while conformational changes occur. Chaperonins are large oligomeric chaperones that provide a central chamber. In bacteria the best studied system is GroEL together with GroES. Its organization makes clear how a protein complex can change the local environment of a client rather than merely shielding one short segment.

GroEL consists of two stacked rings, each built from seven subunits. The two rings create chambers at opposite ends of the complex. Within each subunit, regions involved in nucleotide binding, substrate binding and conformational transmission cooperate. The exposed surfaces near the opening of an available ring can bind nonnative protein. The substrate is not normally a permanent structural component of GroEL. Its association represents one stage in a cycle that subsequently allows release.

GroES is a separate oligomeric cochaperonin that forms a lid over a GroEL ring. In the well-established simplified cycle, nonnative substrate binds an accessible GroEL ring. Binding of ATP and GroES drives major conformational changes that reduce the affinity of the original exposed substrate-binding surfaces and enlarge the enclosed chamber. The substrate is released from the binding sites into the chamber. Its opportunity to fold is now changed because the chamber physically separates the client from other substrate molecules in the surrounding solution.

The enclosed environment is compatible with many soluble proteins, but the protective effect does not arise because GroES supplies a mold whose shape is complementary to every client. Many structurally unrelated proteins can be assisted. Within the chamber, the substrate still has to form contacts dictated by its sequence. The capacity of a chamber is finite, and not every cellular protein can be completely enclosed. Some substrates interact with GroEL in ways that do not fit the simplest full-encapsulation model. For introductory and NET reasoning, the capped folding chamber is an important standard model, not a universal description of every interaction.

ATP hydrolysis changes the nucleotide state of the capped ring and contributes to timing the cycle. ATP binding at the opposite ring can trigger release of the GroES lid, nucleotide and substrate from the first ring. The detailed coordination between the two rings can depend on experimental conditions; an exam diagram often represents them alternating between substrate-processing stages. One should identify the ring that is enclosed with the client as the cis ring and the opposite ring as trans relative to that client. These labels are relational, not permanently attached to one anatomical half of the complex.

After lid opening, a substrate that has folded can leave the chaperonin and proceed toward its biological role. One that remains nonnative may interact again with GroEL or another chaperone. Encapsulation therefore gives the substrate a folding opportunity rather than guaranteeing that a native structure emerges in one attempt. The distinction matters when interpreting experiments in which several ATP cycles are required for activity recovery. Repeated binding and release need not indicate that the protein is defective; it may reflect the kinetics of the normal maturation route.

GroEL and GroES illustrate a second use of ATP in chaperone function. Nucleotide-dependent structural changes organize the availability, closure and reopening of the chamber. The free energy supplied by the nucleotide cycle enables directional progression of the chaperone machinery through these stages. It does not substitute for the energetic interactions stabilizing the finished client. A newly folded client does not contain a new covalent bond merely because it passed through the chaperonin chamber.

Consider three hypothetical mutations. One affects the initial substrate-binding surface, one prevents effective GroES binding and one prevents the chamber from reopening. The first reduces recruitment of nonnative client. The second can permit recruitment without the normal enclosed environment. The third might permit client encapsulation but interfere with substrate release and reuse of the machinery. All three can lower the amount of free active protein, but their effects on the quantities of bound and encapsulated substrate should differ. Mapping an observed phenotype onto a step requires choosing measurements capable of distinguishing those intermediate states.

A useful biological comparison is mitochondrial Hsp60 with its cochaperonin Hsp10. These proteins are related to the bacterial group I chaperonin system and assist folding in the mitochondrial matrix. This compartment is especially relevant because many proteins arrive through membrane translocation in conformations not yet ready for biological activity. A protein can cross the membrane successfully yet fail to acquire the active conformation after import. Correct localization is therefore not sufficient proof of successful maturation.

A particularly informative genetic investigation reported in 1989 showed that mitochondrial Hsp60 was essential for proper assembly of proteins imported into yeast mitochondria. Subsequent work demonstrated the connection between complex formation with Hsp60, ATP hydrolysis and folding of imported substrates. These observations shifted attention from the question of whether proteins entered the organelle to the distinct question of what happened after entry. A mitochondrial fraction containing an imported protein is not evidence that the protein has become an active component of a respiratory or metabolic complex. The experiment needs an independent functional or assembly readout.

Chloroplasts contain related chaperonin machinery and a historically significant example is assistance in the biogenesis of the large subunit of Rubisco. Rubisco catalyzes carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, but synthesis of one of its polypeptides does not automatically create an active multisubunit enzyme. Folding of the large subunit and association with other components are distinct stages. The example connects protein biogenesis to photosynthesis without requiring that a learner memorize every plant-specific assembly factor at this stage.

Eukaryotic cytosolic CCT, also called TRiC, represents a group II chaperonin. Like group I systems it forms a double-ring complex, but the subunits of each ring are not simply seven copies of the bacterial GroEL protein. In the standard eukaryotic CCT complex each ring contains eight related but distinct subunits. The chamber can close through structural elements of the chaperonin itself rather than through a separately binding GroES lid. It is therefore incorrect to assign GroES as the universal cochaperonin for all chaperonins. Important cytosolic substrates include actin and tubulin, linking the success of folding to the integrity of cytoskeletal systems.

A comparison of Hsp70 and chaperonins should focus on how they assist rather than on which family is more important. Hsp70 is a nucleotide-regulated peptide-binding machine that can act early on emerging segments. GroEL/GroES and CCT/TRiC are larger systems that provide a chamber for appropriate clients. They can work in sequence for some substrates, but not every protein passes through both. A protein may fold independently, receive assistance from one system or require several systems with distinct functions. Cellular dependence is selective, not a compulsory identical itinerary for every chain.

9. Chaperones under stress and across the protein life cycle

The names Hsp70 and Hsp60 reflect historical associations with the heat shock response and approximate molecular masses, not a restriction to function at high temperatures. Many chaperones are present during normal growth because new protein synthesis is a continual source of vulnerable chains. Additional chaperone production or redistribution can become important when environmental conditions destabilize previously folded proteins. Consequently, the same basic molecular functions can assist newly made proteins and proteins that have become partly unfolded after maturation.

In an acute heat stress, the concentration of accessible nonnative binding surfaces can rise rapidly. Existing chaperones become occupied by more substrates. The proportion of free chaperone available to newly synthesized chains may therefore fall even if the total chaperone amount has not changed. Increasing chaperone abundance or temporarily reducing the influx of newly synthesized proteins can ease this imbalance. This provides an intuitive connection between proteostasis and regulation of translation; the latter is already covered in the preceding node.

Small heat shock proteins are examples of ATP-independent chaperones that can capture aggregation-prone clients under stress. They often function as holdases rather than independently guaranteeing that a captured client acquires a native structure. A held substrate may subsequently require another chaperone system for productive refolding. Such division of labor shows why the absence of ATP use by one protein does not imply that the overall pathway is ATP independent. The stages must be distinguished when drawing conclusions from an energetic experiment.

Hsp90 provides another instructive example of specialization. Many of its cytosolic clients are signaling proteins, including certain kinases and steroid hormone receptors. These clients can require assistance at later stages of conformational maturation or functional maintenance. The family participates in nucleotide-regulated interactions with clients and cochaperones. It should not be treated as a substitute name for Hsp70 or as a universal folding chamber. Loss of Hsp90 function can impair the activity or stability of selected signaling proteins, potentially affecting many downstream cellular responses without necessarily blocking ribosomal peptide synthesis.

In bacteria and certain other organisms, disaggregase systems can help recover proteins from aggregates. A distinction between preventing aggregation and reversing an established aggregate is useful. Protecting a newly exposed segment requires preventing an unfavorable interaction before it is formed. Recovering a protein from a preexisting assembly requires overcoming contacts that already hold multiple chains together. The latter process may need a different combination of chaperones and energy-dependent actions. Not all aggregates are recoverable, and recovery need not be the preferred fate for every damaged molecule.

The evolutionary conservation of molecular chaperones reflects the generality of the problem of nonnative protein interactions. It does not establish that every species possesses the same collection of cochaperones or the same division of functions. Differences in temperature, compartment organization, metabolic resources and protein repertoires affect how cells organize this machinery. Examination comparisons are most reliable when they identify a conserved molecular principle first and then distinguish the organism-specific examples.

The next aspect of maturation introduces a different kind of molecular change. Chaperones can assist a chain in rearranging noncovalent interactions; some clients also require covalent disulfide formation or catalyzed rearrangement of peptide-bond conformations. These reactions require attention to chemistry and compartment conditions, not just to the exposed surfaces recognized by chaperones.

10. Disulfide formation: how chemical maturation reinforces structure

Among the chemical reactions associated with folding, formation of a disulfide bond is especially instructive because the reaction can create a covalent connection between two distant parts of a protein. A cysteine side chain contains a thiol group, represented as R–SH. When two appropriately positioned thiols are oxidized, they can form a disulfide represented as R–S–S–R. In a simplified half reaction, two thiols yield one disulfide, two protons and two electrons. An electron acceptor is required for the overall oxidation; the reverse reduction requires reducing equivalents. The carbon–nitrogen peptide backbone is not broken during this change.

An intrachain disulfide connects two cysteines in the same polypeptide. It may stabilize a loop or link regions that are distant in primary sequence but close in the folded structure. An interchain disulfide connects cysteines belonging to distinct polypeptides. Both types can occur in the same mature protein. When interpreting a structural diagram, identify the chain boundaries before classifying a disulfide. It is not enough to observe that two cysteines are separated by a large number of residues; if they belong to one backbone, the bond is intrachain.

Disulfides can stabilize a native conformation in part by restricting the conformational freedom of the unfolded chain. If a linkage between two regions remains intact, the unfolded state cannot sample all the arrangements that an unlinked chain could sample. This restriction can favor the folded state relative to the unfolded state under particular conditions. But stability depends on whether the linkage is geometrically compatible with the native structure. An inappropriate disulfide may instead trap a nonnative conformation. The chemical strength of a linkage does not establish the biological usefulness of its position.

Disulfide formation can occur as a chain folds, and formation of some contacts can bring particular thiols into proximity. In other cases an initial disulfide constrains subsequent structural changes. Therefore, there is no general rule that a protein must finish folding before any disulfide forms, or that all disulfides must form before any folding occurs. The chemical and conformational events can influence one another. The Anfinsen experiment demonstrates this coupling: the native arrangement of cysteines is recovered preferentially when the chain has an opportunity to adopt appropriate contacts and when nonnative bonds can rearrange.

The compartment in which a protein resides strongly affects the stability and rate of formation of its disulfides. In many eukaryotic cells the cytosol is maintained in a relatively reducing state. Cellular redox systems help sustain thiols in their reduced forms, protecting many cytosolic enzymes that require free cysteine groups. The endoplasmic reticulum lumen, in contrast, supports oxidative folding of many secreted and membrane proteins. Specific oxidoreductases and other redox partners help transfer electrons during disulfide formation. It is this chemically organized environment, rather than the mere presence of a membrane, that makes oxidative folding efficient for suitable substrates.

The reducing cytosol and oxidizing ER distinction is a useful examination generalization, but it must not become an absolute prohibition. Some cytosolic proteins can contain redox-responsive or otherwise stabilized disulfides, and stress can change cellular redox conditions. In addition, the ER must maintain a balance: indiscriminate oxidation can make it difficult to correct inappropriate linkages. Efficient oxidative folding involves both the ability to form bonds and the ability to reduce or rearrange bonds that are incorrectly positioned.

In Gram-negative bacteria the periplasm provides another important example of a compartment that supports disulfide formation. The Dsb system includes proteins that promote oxidation and others that assist rearrangement of inappropriate linkages. A bacterial secretory protein with disulfides may therefore fold successfully after export even though the bacterial cytoplasm is normally reducing. Eukaryotic ER proteins and bacterial periplasmic proteins solve related chemical problems through compartment-specific machinery. The comparison should not be confused with an assertion that bacteria have an ER.

A mutant replacing a required cysteine with serine illustrates how the chemical identity of a residue affects maturation. Both amino acids have side chains containing a heteroatom, but serine has a hydroxyl group rather than a thiol and does not participate in the same ordinary cysteine disulfide chemistry. If the missing bond stabilizes a secreted protein, the mutant may become less stable, fail quality control or be secreted inefficiently. It does not follow that every cysteine-to-serine substitution causes misfolding: some cysteines are not paired, some are involved in catalysis or metal binding, and others may be dispensable under the tested conditions.

An additional difficulty is that a mutation removing one cysteine from a native disulfide can leave its original partner unpaired. The remaining thiol might form a nonnative bond with another cysteine within the same molecule or with a different molecule. Therefore, the effect need not be limited to loss of stabilization from one missing bond. The mutation can alter the network of alternative interactions available during maturation. An experiment that detects new covalent dimers may reveal this indirect consequence, especially if the dimers disappear when reduced.

Disulfide chemistry also explains a standard use of electrophoresis. In denaturing sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, many noncovalent interactions are disrupted. If reducing reagent is absent, disulfide-linked chains can remain covalently connected. Addition of reducing reagent can separate those chains by cleaving disulfides. A band of apparent mass 60 kDa under nonreducing conditions that becomes two bands of approximately 40 and 20 kDa under reducing conditions is consistent with a disulfide-linked heterodimer. This is an interpretation under stated assumptions, not proof of the native three-dimensional structure. Other covalent connections, anomalous migration and incomplete reduction should be considered if observations are inconsistent.

An intramolecular disulfide does not join two independent peptide backbones. Accordingly, reducing an isolated monomer containing only intrachain disulfides need not create several smaller chain bands on a denaturing gel. Its migration may still change because intrachain constraints affect how it behaves in the experimental system. Distinguishing covalent chain connectivity from compactness is essential when interpreting gel data.

11. Protein disulfide isomerase and the correction of nonnative linkages

Protein disulfide isomerase, often abbreviated PDI, is a major folding catalyst in the ER lumen. Its role becomes clear when a protein has several cysteine residues that can form multiple possible combinations. Forming a disulfide at the first available contact is not necessarily productive. A cell needs reactions that allow exchange between thiols and existing disulfides so that the network of bonds can be corrected as the chain reorganizes.

PDI family proteins contain catalytic domains with cysteine-containing active-site motifs. The catalytic cysteines participate in reversible formation and reduction of disulfides. In a simplified oxidation mechanism, an oxidized active site carries a disulfide between its own cysteines. A substrate thiol attacks that disulfide, forming a temporary mixed disulfide between the catalyst and substrate. A second substrate thiol can resolve the intermediate to produce a new substrate disulfide and a reduced catalyst. The catalyst is regenerated through interaction with other redox partners in the ER.

This mechanism illustrates why enzyme and substrate can be covalently connected during catalysis without forming a permanent part of one another's mature structures. A transient mixed disulfide is an intermediate. Release of the substrate is necessary for the catalyst to act again. If a mutation prevents resolution of the mixed disulfide, the amount of catalyst-associated substrate can increase even while the rate of production of mature client falls. In enzyme kinetic reasoning, accumulation of an intermediate often indicates slowed conversion to a later state, not increased completion of the reaction.

The reduced form of PDI can also initiate exchange with an existing substrate disulfide. Attack by a catalytic thiol creates an intermediate that permits the original pairing to be broken. The subsequent reaction can yield an alternative substrate pairing or a more reduced substrate that has another opportunity to fold. The precise route depends on the redox state of the catalyst, the cysteines available in the substrate and the surrounding environment. It is useful to think of PDI as a mediator of reversible exchange rather than a machine that simply adds one fixed bond at a genetically predetermined position.

The cell maintains the activity of oxidative folding machinery through redox partners. Ero1 is one important example of an oxidizing system that can help restore oxidized PDI. Other pathways also contribute to ER redox control. The detailed organization of these pathways varies with organism and cell type, so an introductory diagram should not present one redox partner as the exclusive possible source of oxidative capacity. A balanced redox environment must support the ongoing production of disulfides while retaining enough capacity for correcting incorrect ones.

Some PDI family members also bind nonnative proteins and have chaperone properties in addition to catalyzing thiol-disulfide reactions. A mutant of the catalytic cysteines can therefore retain some substrate-binding activity while losing much of its exchange capacity. Such a distinction is valuable for designing an experiment. If adding a PDI variant improves the solubility of a substrate but fails to restore its native disulfides or activity, the data support a separation between binding assistance and catalysis. If adding the wild-type protein improves both outcomes, the catalytic cycle is likely to contribute to the additional improvement.

A useful controlled refolding experiment can use a purified disulfide-rich client with scrambled linkages. One sample receives active PDI under suitable redox conditions, another receives an inactive enzyme variant and a third receives no added catalyst. The initial amount of substrate, temperature, buffer and duration are matched. At several times, investigators measure native activity and the state of the disulfides using appropriate chemical or analytical techniques. Faster recovery in the active-PDI sample would support its catalytic role, particularly if the inactive variant retains binding but does not accelerate correction. Merely detecting more oxidized cysteines would not establish that the correct pairings have been achieved.

This example also demonstrates the difference between a folding catalyst and an enzyme that modifies the final protein as a regulatory switch. PDI-mediated disulfide formation changes a covalent connection that can be essential to maturation. A phosphorylation event on an already active signaling protein may instead switch activity without requiring broad refolding. Some modifications contribute to both processes, which is why the categories overlap. The following node on post-translational modification will discuss the general chemistry and regulation of such changes; here the emphasis is on obtaining an appropriate conformational state.

12. Peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerization as a folding bottleneck

A polypeptide's backbone is not equally flexible at every bond. The peptide bond has partial double-bond character, which restricts rotation around its carbon–nitrogen connection. Peptide bonds can be described as cis or trans according to the relative positions of the alpha-carbon groups on either side. The trans arrangement predominates for most peptide bonds because it generally reduces steric conflict. Bonds preceding proline can populate the cis configuration more often than many other peptide bonds.

Proline has a side chain connected back to the backbone nitrogen, giving its backbone geometry distinctive constraints. A particular folded protein may require a specific X–Pro peptide bond to be cis or trans, where X represents the residue preceding proline. If a denatured chain contains that bond in the incompatible configuration, much of the rest of the protein may be ready to fold while conversion of the bond remains slow. The problem is not the identity of the amino acid; the sequence is already correct. The obstacle is a conformational barrier associated with isomerization.

Peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerases accelerate interconversion of these configurations. Cyclophilins, FKBPs and parvulin family proteins are representative examples. These enzymes are different from peptidases that cleave peptide bonds. Isomerization changes the spatial arrangement around an existing bond without changing the order of residues or dividing the chain. A folding experiment can therefore show a substantial change in the time required for activity recovery without detecting a difference in polypeptide molecular mass.

Catalysis reduces the kinetic barrier between states and can accelerate approach to equilibrium. A catalyst does not, by itself, change the equilibrium free-energy difference between the states of a substrate in a closed system. If a protein's native conformation favors one configuration, accelerating the approach to that conformation can improve the rate of recovery of function. In an intact cell, where aggregation, degradation and continual synthesis create competing pathways, a faster folding step can additionally increase the fraction of chains that survive to maturity. That increase is not evidence that the isomerase changed the isolated substrate's thermodynamic equilibrium.

Consider a client that can refold rapidly when its critical proline bond is already in the appropriate configuration and slowly when the bond is not. Immediately after dilution from denaturant, a mixed population can display a rapid phase of activity recovery followed by a slower phase. Adding a relevant isomerase can speed the slow phase. A mutation replacing the important proline may remove the original bottleneck, but it can also disrupt the native structure itself. It is not safe to predict that replacing every proline will improve folding. The structural contribution of the residue must be distinguished from the kinetics of isomerization.

Unlike the Hsp70 nucleotide cycle, simple catalysis of peptide-bond isomerization does not require stoichiometric ATP hydrolysis for each cis–trans conversion. This is another example of folding assistance that is not defined by nucleotide consumption. If ATP is required in a larger reconstituted system containing both an isomerase and an ATP-dependent chaperone, the two contributions should be examined separately.

13. Quality-controlled folding in the endoplasmic reticulum

A secretory cell faces a particularly demanding form of protein maturation. It can synthesize large quantities of proteins that must leave the cytoplasm and acquire structures compatible with extracellular function. The ER lumen is an early environment for many of these proteins. In addition to supporting disulfide formation, it contains molecular chaperones and enzymes that help coordinate folding with other processing events. This organization makes the ER more than a passageway through which already completed proteins move.

BiP, an ER-localized Hsp70 family protein, can bind exposed segments of newly translocated or incompletely folded clients. Its ATP-dependent cycle follows the broad Hsp70 principle established earlier, although its cochaperones and environment are specialized for the ER. Incomplete assembly can leave surfaces available for BiP binding even when individual domains have acquired substantial structure. Therefore, persistent association of a protein with BiP can be consistent with an assembly problem as well as a folding problem.

Many ER clients receive an N-linked oligosaccharide during synthesis or early processing. A subset of the resulting glycan structures acts as a recognition signal for the lectin chaperones calnexin and calreticulin. Calnexin is membrane associated, whereas calreticulin is a soluble luminal protein. Their carbohydrate recognition complements the peptide recognition of chaperones such as BiP. This distinction explains how a covalent modification can become part of a quality-control system rather than merely a decoration added to a protein.

In the standard cycle, glucose residues are trimmed from a newly transferred oligosaccharide. A monoglucosylated glycan permits interaction with calnexin or calreticulin. Associated folding factors, including the oxidoreductase ERp57 in relevant cases, can assist conformational maturation. Removal of the remaining glucose releases the client from the lectin-binding state. If the protein is still nonnative, the enzyme UGGT can recognize features of its nonnative conformation and restore a glucose residue to an appropriate glycan. This allows renewed entry into the lectin cycle.

The system connects a covalent recognition mark to an assessment of conformational state. Glycan trimming is not itself a guarantee that the protein has folded. Similarly, reglucosylation does not repair the backbone sequence. It supplies another opportunity for folding assistance by making a client eligible for lectin binding again. Proteins that lack a suitable N-linked glycan cannot use this particular cycle in the same way, but they may receive assistance from other ER machinery. The presence of glycosylation-independent pathways is important when comparing different substrates.

A defect in glucosidase activity may prevent appropriate progression into or out of a lectin-binding state, depending on which reaction is disrupted. A defect in UGGT may reduce the ability of certain nonnative glycoproteins to reenter the cycle after glucose removal. Neither prediction implies that all ER proteins will cease folding. The effect depends on the client's reliance on the lectin pathway and on compensatory assistance. For a useful exam experiment, compare a glycosylated client with a nonglycosylated client while checking that their synthesis and arrival in the ER are not independently impaired.

The ER also coordinates maturation with export. A protein can remain in the compartment because it carries a retention signal, because it binds to resident quality-control machinery or because it has not achieved a state compatible with further transport. Localization alone therefore cannot identify the underlying cause. A mutant protein accumulating in the ER may be incorrectly folded despite possessing an intact targeting signal. Another mutant may fold well but lack a recognition feature required for export. Distinguishing these possibilities calls for structural, functional and localization evidence.

When the influx of vulnerable proteins exceeds processing capacity, unfolded or misfolded proteins can accumulate in the ER. Cells can respond through the unfolded protein response, adjusting synthesis, folding capacity and disposal of persistent failures. In mammalian cells, one aspect of the response can reduce general translational initiation through phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha, while other response branches increase the expression of folding and quality-control machinery. These consequences connect the chapter to regulation of translation and cell signaling, without requiring a detailed treatment of every stress sensor.

The response is not simply a mechanism for turning all protein synthesis off. Secretory cells still need to make proteins, and prolonged stress responses can have complex consequences. Reducing influx temporarily can help restore the relationship between production and maturation, while increasing folding capacity can support recovery. A student should recognize the causal problem: the relevant load is the concentration and lifetime of vulnerable clients, not merely the absolute number of ribosomes or the presence of one misfolded molecule.

Persistent failures can be directed toward degradation. ER-associated degradation provides a route for disposing of certain nonnative ER proteins through recognition, movement toward cytosolic degradation machinery and proteasomal processing. Other disposal routes may also be important for selected substrates or large aggregates. The detailed ubiquitin–proteasome machinery belongs to the later degradation chapter. Here its relevance is that failure to mature can change the lifetime of the newly synthesized protein and, therefore, the amount available for biological function.

These principles prepare the way for a different form of chemical maturation in which a protein loses part of its original amino acid chain. Unlike a disulfide exchange or a proline isomerization, proteolytic cleavage changes backbone connectivity, creating a new amino or carboxyl terminus and sometimes separate mature chains. The next section will examine why cells use such an irreversible transformation to make functional proteins.

14. Proteolytic maturation: a controlled change in the primary structure

Many proteins are initially synthesized as precursors that differ from the form used in their final biological role. A precursor can contain an amino-terminal signal sequence, a propeptide that controls activation, an internal connecting segment or a combination of these features. The cell can remove selected segments by hydrolysis of specific peptide bonds. This is proteolytic processing. In contrast to the conformational changes discussed previously, it changes the covalent continuity of the original polypeptide. Reversing the cleavage would ordinarily require formation of a new peptide bond, rather than simply reversing a change in conformation.

Why would a cell deliberately synthesize a segment that it later removes? A transient segment may solve a problem that exists only during production. A signal sequence can help direct a newly synthesized protein to a compartment, even though the mature protein does not need that particular sequence. A connecting peptide can permit regions destined to become separate chains to be synthesized together, keeping them close enough to establish their correct structural relationships. An inhibitory segment can prevent activity while a protein moves through a location where its premature action would be harmful. In all these cases, the function of the removed segment is understood by considering the pathway, not just the final structure.

A signal peptide and a propeptide are therefore not equivalent concepts. The former contributes to localization; the latter can contribute to regulated activation, folding or other maturation steps. A protein may contain both. When a diagram shows an initial large precursor converted to two successively shorter products, one should ask what each cleavage accomplishes. The first might reflect arrival in a new compartment, while the second might convert the protein from an inactive precursor into its active form. The decrease in mass alone does not distinguish those possibilities.

Proteases catalyze cleavage by recognizing substrates and hydrolyzing selected peptide bonds. Some recognize amino acid patterns near the cleavage site, while the accessibility and conformation of the substrate also influence cleavage. The presence of a potential recognition sequence does not guarantee processing if that sequence is buried or inaccessible. Conversely, a mutation can alter processing by changing the shape of the protein without directly replacing a residue at the cleavage site. These distinctions are especially important when predicting the effect of changes in a protein sequence.

The timing of cleavage depends on the biological role of the precursor. A segment removed during passage through a membrane may be recognized by processing machinery on the appropriate side of that membrane. A proenzyme may instead be activated by a protease encountered after it reaches a secretory compartment or extracellular environment. Such order helps restrict activity to appropriate places and times. The sequence of events is not necessarily identical for all precursors: cleavage, folding and assembly may be interdependent and can occur at different points in different pathways.

Protein maturation by limited proteolysis must also be distinguished from protein degradation. In limited proteolysis, removal of a defined region or cleavage of a particular linkage generates a product that is useful in a biological pathway. In degradation, destruction of a protein or substantial conversion into peptides and amino acids terminates its previous function and contributes to cellular disposal or recycling. Both involve hydrolysis of peptide bonds, but the extent and biological outcomes differ. Cleavage of a precursor into an active enzyme should not be classified as wholesale destruction merely because a protease participates.

This relationship also explains how an apparently irreversible activation can be regulated. A protein may be maintained in an inactive form until a specific protease acts. Once the activating cleavage has occurred, the same precursor molecule generally cannot be switched back by a simple reversal of the reaction. Its activity may instead be regulated through inhibitors, environmental changes or degradation. Cells can thus use proteolysis when a strong directional transition is advantageous, whereas reversible covalent modifications can provide repeated switching of the same molecule.

15. Insulin as an example of maturation through folding and processing

Insulin provides a clear illustration of why the translation product and functional secreted product need not have the same chain organization. Human insulin is initially synthesized as preproinsulin, a single precursor containing an amino-terminal signal peptide followed by the regions that will become the B chain, connecting peptide and A chain. The signal sequence directs entry into the secretory pathway. Its removal yields proinsulin, which still contains the regions of the future A and B chains joined through the connecting segment.

Proinsulin must adopt an appropriate conformation in which three disulfides are established. Two will connect the eventual A and B chains, and one lies within the A chain. At the proinsulin stage, however, all three disulfides are technically intramolecular because the A and B regions remain parts of a single continuous backbone. After processing separates the A and B chains, two of these same linkages become interchain disulfides. This change in classification does not require the formation of new sulfur–sulfur bonds. It reflects the change in backbone connectivity produced by proteolysis.

The connecting region helps keep the A and B regions on the same molecule during oxidative folding. The probability of establishing suitable contacts is different when the two regions are physically tethered than when they must find each other by diffusion through solution. The precursor arrangement thus allows a mature structure with two separate chains to be produced initially from one translation event. This is more reliable than independently synthesizing two isolated chains and assuming that all will automatically encounter their correct partners.

In the usual pathway of pancreatic beta cells, proinsulin folds in the ER and proceeds through the secretory system. Subsequent processing in secretory granules involves cleavage at sites adjacent to the connecting peptide by prohormone convertases and trimming of remaining basic residues by carboxypeptidase E. The products include mature insulin and C-peptide. Human mature insulin contains 21 amino acids in the A chain and 30 in the B chain. Human C-peptide contains 31 residues. These numbers should not be used to infer that a single cleavage simply subtracts 31 residues from the precursor, since additional residues at the processing sites and the signal peptide are also removed during maturation.

The final insulin molecule is held together by its interchain disulfides despite having two distinct peptide backbones. A reducing treatment can separate the chains under conditions that disrupt noncovalent associations, whereas a protease directed at the connecting segment is needed to generate the normal processed product from proinsulin. Reduction is not equivalent to precursor cleavage. The former breaks sulfur–sulfur linkages; the latter breaks carbon–nitrogen peptide bonds. An exam question may present both treatments and ask which yields independent chains at a particular processing stage.

The processing pathway is informative for interpreting mutations. If the amino-terminal signal sequence is severely disrupted, proinsulin may fail to enter the ER efficiently, preventing normal access to the oxidative folding environment and processing machinery. If a required cysteine is replaced, the protein may enter the ER but fail to establish the native disulfide arrangement. If a convertase recognition site is altered while folding and transport remain normal, the precursor may accumulate at a later stage because cleavage is defective. The final result of all three perturbations can be a reduction in mature insulin, but they act at different points.

A time-resolved labeling experiment can distinguish these possibilities more effectively than an endpoint assay. Detecting the initial labeled precursor shows whether the sequence was translated. Monitoring its migration and susceptibility to relevant processing or reducing conditions can indicate conversion to later products. Localization and assessment of secretion supply additional evidence. If the normal intermediate appears but the mature product does not, a defect after formation of that intermediate becomes more plausible. If the initial precursor is already missing, an explanation restricted to the final cleavage reaction is insufficient.

Insulin production also illustrates the interaction between folding quality and cellular physiology. A beta cell that increases precursor synthesis must simultaneously handle the increased folding and processing load. Incorrect disulfides can interfere with productive maturation, and nonnative molecules can engage ER quality-control machinery. A failure of protein quality control can therefore affect hormone availability even when transcription of the hormone gene is unchanged. Detailed disease-specific mechanisms are not required to understand the fundamental causal link.

C-peptide provides an additional example of the difference between a processing product and an arbitrary degradation fragment. In the usual pathway, processing of one proinsulin molecule yields one insulin molecule and one C-peptide molecule. This is a stoichiometric relationship at the production step, not a claim that their circulating concentrations must be identical. Distribution and clearance can differ. If a hypothetical experiment completely converts 500 proinsulin molecules by the ordinary processing pathway, 500 insulin molecules and 500 C-peptide molecules are expected before subsequent loss or clearance. A lower measured C-peptide concentration at a later time would not by itself prove that the cleavage stoichiometry was different.

16. Zymogens and controlled activation

A zymogen is an inactive or much less active precursor of an enzyme that can be converted into an active form, commonly by limited proteolysis. Digestive proteases illustrate why this mechanism is biologically valuable. These enzymes can hydrolyze proteins, so producing them in a highly active state in the wrong cellular location can damage cellular constituents. Synthesis of a precursor and activation at the appropriate destination allow production and catalytic action to be separated.

Trypsinogen, for example, is secreted as a precursor. In the intestinal lumen, enteropeptidase initiates its activation by cleaving a specific amino-terminal activation peptide. The resulting trypsin can activate additional trypsinogen and other digestive zymogens. Thus, a small initial activation event can be amplified by subsequent cleavage reactions. The significance for protein maturation is the change from precursor to active catalytic conformation. The complete physiology of pancreatic secretion and digestion is treated in the animal physiology unit.

In some zymogens, a prosegment obstructs access to the catalytic site or stabilizes an inactive conformation. In others, cleavage creates a new terminus whose interactions help establish a catalytically competent arrangement. These are related but not identical structural explanations. A student should not assume that an activation peptide always acts as a physical plug. Removal of a short sequence can reorganize the conformation of a much larger protein, including regions far from the cleavage site.

Zymogen activation can also be distinguished from ordinary substrate turnover by the mature enzyme. When enteropeptidase acts on trypsinogen, the substrate of the activating reaction is itself an enzyme precursor. When active trypsin subsequently acts on a dietary protein, the substrate is not necessarily destined to become another enzyme. A proteolytic cascade occurs when one activated enzyme generates another active enzyme, creating successive steps of regulation or amplification. Not every protease reaction is a cascade and not every cleaved protein is a zymogen.

A cleavage-resistant zymogen can provide an instructive hypothetical mutation. Suppose it is synthesized, secreted and folded in the same manner as the wild type, but the relevant peptide bond cannot be efficiently cleaved by the physiological activating protease. The expected phenotype is retention of precursor with reduced production of active enzyme. A different mutant might be cleaved normally yet fail to attain an active conformation after cleavage. Testing the appearance of the shorter chain together with catalytic activity would separate these defects. Detecting a processed product is necessary evidence for cleavage but is not sufficient evidence for catalytic success.

Conversely, a mutation that makes an enzyme active without its normal activating cleavage could alter the spatial or temporal restriction of its action. Such a result would not imply that every zymogen can be activated by a simple deletion of its prosegment. A prosegment may itself participate in successful folding; removing it before synthesis is complete can have an outcome different from cleaving it after the precursor has formed. This is another consequence of the time dimension in protein maturation.

17. Assembly into multimeric protein complexes

A folded chain can be structurally complete as a single polypeptide but still need one or more partners to become functional. Quaternary organization describes the arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits in a protein complex. A homooligomer contains multiple copies of the same subunit, whereas a heterooligomer contains distinct subunit types. The correct stoichiometry and arrangement of subunits determine properties such as catalytic sites at interfaces, cooperative behavior and structural stability.

Association of subunits depends on their interaction surfaces. Noncovalent contacts at an interface can include hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions and van der Waals contacts. Some complexes additionally contain interchain disulfides or cofactors. A protein that is stable as an isolated monomer can fail to assemble because the geometry or chemistry of its interface is altered. A mutation far from the monomer's catalytic site can therefore impair the activity of a complex if the relevant catalytic arrangement depends on association.

Subunit folding and complex assembly may occur in different orders. Some proteins first acquire nearly complete monomeric structures and subsequently associate. Others form a stable structure only in the presence of a partner, so assembly and folding are coupled. Association can begin while one of the interacting partners is still attached to a ribosome, a situation called cotranslational assembly. Such timing can bring compatible regions together before competing interactions take place. However, it should not be assumed that every multisubunit protein follows a cotranslational route.

The stoichiometry of synthesis matters when subunits are produced independently. Imagine a complex containing two molecules of subunit A and one molecule of subunit B. If a cell has 150 assembly-competent A molecules and 100 B molecules, at most 75 complete complexes can form, provided that association is efficient and no other factor is limiting. Twenty-five B molecules would remain unused in this simplified calculation. Increasing B synthesis alone will not increase the theoretical maximum because A is limiting. Increasing the correctly folded supply of A, rather than its total synthesis regardless of folding, is the relevant way to overcome the limitation.

Unassembled subunits can expose regions that would be buried at native interfaces. Such surfaces may attract chaperones, and some unassembled subunits are unstable or subject to quality-control degradation. This is why overexpression of a single subunit does not always increase the amount of intact complex. It can instead increase the pool of unassembled protein and impose a folding burden. Cells often coordinate expression, assembly and disposal of surplus subunits to maintain useful protein stoichiometries.

Consider an enzyme with two identical subunits, each individually inactive because the active site is formed across their interface. A mutation that impairs dimerization may give a protein with essentially normal secondary structure but negligible catalytic activity. Circular dichroism, which reports broad conformational features, may suggest that substantial structure remains. Size-exclusion chromatography or a native separation may instead reveal a change from dimer to monomer. An activity assay then establishes the functional consequence. No single measurement is sufficient to establish all three properties.

Hemoglobin illustrates a different importance of assembly. Its adult major form contains two alpha and two beta subunits, each carrying a heme group. The interactions between subunits are essential to its characteristic cooperative oxygen binding. A protein preparation containing heme-bound globin chains is not necessarily equivalent to a properly assembled hemoglobin tetramer. Folding, cofactor incorporation and complex assembly represent distinct requirements, even though they can influence one another. The detailed oxygen-binding curve belongs to physiology and biomolecular function; here the example clarifies why correct assembly has consequences beyond structural appearance.

Chaperones may assist assembly by preserving exposed surfaces until the correct partner becomes available. They may also support conformational maturation of one or more of the subunits before association. The resulting mature complex need not retain those temporary helpers. Distinguishing a transient assembly factor from a permanent subunit is important when interpreting a copurification experiment: detection of a protein in a preparation can suggest interaction, but it does not automatically establish final stoichiometry. Changes over time and analysis of mature active complexes help distinguish these roles.

18. Quantitative reasoning about folding yield and competition

The rate of maturation matters because newly synthesized chains can face several competing outcomes. A useful simplified model considers an immature soluble state U that can either become a functional state F or enter a nonproductive state A. Suppose these conversions have first-order rate constants kf and ka respectively. The assumption of first-order loss to A is an effective description, appropriate only under conditions in which the competing factor can be treated as having constant availability or when a first-order nonproductive step is explicitly assumed. Actual intermolecular aggregation often depends on concentration in a more complicated way.

If U is initially present at concentration U0 and neither product returns to U, the rate of change of U is minus the sum of the two rate constants times its concentration. Thus U(t) = U0 exp[−(kf + ka)t]. The rate of formation of F is kf U(t). Integrating from zero to time t yields F(t) = U0 kf/(kf + ka) multiplied by [1 − exp(−(kf + ka)t)]. The long-time fraction of the original immature population reaching the functional state is kf/(kf + ka), under the assumptions of the model.

The derivation is useful because it separates two ways to improve functional yield. Increasing kf makes the productive route faster relative to the competing route. Decreasing ka makes loss less likely even if the productive rate constant remains unchanged. A chaperone that prevents aggregation can improve yield through the second mechanism. A catalyst that accelerates an otherwise slow folding step can improve yield through the first. Both may have the same endpoint effect, so the final activity alone does not identify the responsible microscopic change.

For example, suppose kf = 0.20 per minute and ka = 0.30 per minute. The productive fraction predicted by the model is 0.20/0.50 = 0.40, or 40 percent. If a chaperone lowers the competing rate to 0.05 per minute without changing kf, the yield increases to 0.20/0.25 = 80 percent. This doubling does not require the intrinsic productive folding step to become faster. Notice that the initial slope of F(t), for equal U0, is unchanged at kf U0. Later, however, the protected sample retains more immature molecules available to complete folding. Time-course measurements can therefore provide information that an endpoint lacks.

The half-time for disappearance of U in the first condition is ln(2)/0.50, approximately 1.39 minutes; in the protected condition it is ln(2)/0.25, approximately 2.77 minutes. The protected sample loses its immature population more slowly but eventually obtains a larger amount of active protein. This can appear paradoxical only if one equates disappearance of an intermediate with productive maturation. In this model disappearance occurs through both productive and nonproductive routes. A rapidly disappearing immature population is not necessarily a sign of an efficient folding system.

A more realistic representation of an initial bimolecular aggregation event considers two exposed chains associating. If aggregates arise through 2U → A with event rate k2 U², the loss of U due to that reaction is 2k2 U² because each event consumes two molecules. Here k2 has units of concentration inverse per time. The productive rate kf U has units of concentration per time, as does 2k2 U². Comparing these terms gives the instantaneous ratio of aggregation-associated chain loss to productive folding as 2k2 U/kf. Increasing U increases this ratio under the assumed model.

For instance, let kf be 0.10 per minute, and let k2 be 0.02 per micromolar per minute. At U = 1 micromolar, productive folding consumes U at 0.10 micromolar per minute, whereas the modeled aggregation reaction consumes U at 0.04 micromolar per minute. At U = 5 micromolar, these rates become 0.50 and 1.00 micromolar per minute respectively. The aggregation-associated loss becomes larger than the productive rate because of its stronger concentration dependence. This calculation is an illustration of competing reaction orders, not a claim that all aggregates form by a single irreversible dimerization step.

The model helps explain why dilution can sometimes improve recovery of an isolated denatured protein. Dilution decreases the frequency of encounters between vulnerable chains, whereas an intramolecular folding step can continue without requiring collision with another chain. Yet dilution is not universally advantageous. Assembly of an oligomer may itself require intermolecular encounters, and very low concentrations can slow productive association with necessary partners. The effect of concentration depends on which step limits the desired final product.

An experiment using these models should measure more than soluble protein. A substrate bound to a chaperone may remain in the soluble fraction while still awaiting release. A soluble misfolded intermediate may be counted as soluble protein without being active. Measuring time-dependent recovery of catalytic activity, soluble substrate abundance and aggregation-associated loss in parallel makes the kinetic interpretation more informative. The constants used in these examples are hypothetical educational values; real rates depend on the protein, assay and environmental conditions.

19. Experimental diagnosis of protein-biogenesis failures

A useful way to investigate the maturation pathway is to follow a defined cohort of newly synthesized molecules. In a pulse-chase experiment, newly made proteins are labeled during a short interval, called the pulse. Unlabeled precursor is subsequently supplied during the chase so that the initially labeled cohort can be followed over time. If a specific protein is isolated at successive times, investigators can determine whether it remains in precursor form, acquires a new molecular mass, associates with partners, is secreted or disappears. A pulse-chase does not by itself identify every chemical change: the associated analytical readout determines what can be inferred.

Suppose the labeled precursor appears in equal amounts at the start of a chase in wild-type and mutant cells. In wild-type cells the precursor decreases while a processed product increases; in mutant cells the precursor persists and the processed product is scarce. The starting observation argues against a major difference in production of the labeled precursor during the pulse, under the experimental assumptions. The subsequent difference points toward a processing or maturation defect, but localization and conformation should also be examined. A precursor that cannot reach the compartment containing its processing enzyme may give a pattern similar to that of a cleavage-site mutation.

In a second hypothetical experiment, the labeled protein disappears quickly in the mutant without a corresponding increase in mature product. This pattern could reflect degradation of a nonnative or unstable client. A comparison of degradation-sensitive and degradation-inhibited conditions may help test that explanation. However, disappearance from the sampled fraction could also reflect redistribution, secretion or loss of the detection epitope. Recovering material from additional fractions and using complementary detection methods can improve the inference. These alternatives matter particularly when an exam question gives only one measurement.

Chaperone binding can be investigated by immunoprecipitation of a chaperone and detection of associated labeled substrate. Transient association followed by release as activity rises supports a maturation intermediate. Persistent association with a mutant can indicate repeated recognition or failed release, but it does not uniquely distinguish those mechanisms. A nucleotide-cycle defect can trap a substrate even if the substrate sequence is normal. An intrinsically defective substrate can remain associated with a normal chaperone because it repeatedly exposes the recognition surface. Reciprocal experiments with normal and mutant chaperones and clients can help assign the source of the failure.

For a newly synthesized enzyme, specific activity is often more useful than total activity. If a preparation contains the same amount of enzyme protein but catalyzes only one quarter of the normal reaction rate under saturating substrate conditions, its apparent activity per unit enzyme is reduced. If the individual active molecules have unchanged catalytic properties, a plausible interpretation is that only one quarter of the protein is active. This inference assumes that activity scales linearly with active enzyme amount and that substrate is not limiting. A mutation affecting the catalytic rate of each molecule would violate the unchanged-catalysis assumption, so activity measurements should not automatically be interpreted as an exact folding fraction.

Several experimental readouts can be combined. A denaturing gel can assess chain size and covalent processing. Reducing versus nonreducing conditions can reveal certain disulfide connections. A native separation can distinguish some oligomeric states. An activity assay measures functional output. Fractionation or microscopy can assess localization. Protease sensitivity and spectroscopic measurements may reveal differences in structural accessibility or organization. Each method answers a narrower question, and none should be treated as proof of every aspect of maturation.

The strongest interpretation comes from comparing predictions across independent observations. If total protein is normal, the mature cleavage product is present, the protein is in the correct compartment, but native analysis reveals a monomer instead of an active dimer, an assembly defect is more strongly supported than a translation initiation defect. If activity is normal per assembled molecule but the number of assembled molecules is reduced, the problem concerns yield rather than necessarily the intrinsic catalytic mechanism. If total protein and synthesis are both reduced, folding defects may still exist, but the data do not isolate them.

20. From successful maturation to protein quality control

Protein biogenesis is not a one-way guarantee of success. A molecule can mature correctly and subsequently become damaged through oxidation, heat or other stresses. A newly made molecule can fail because of a sequence mutation, a missing cofactor, delayed processing, an absent partner or an unfavorable environment. Cells maintain a proteostasis network that coordinates production, folding, assembly, maintenance and removal of proteins. The distinction between newly made and previously mature clients is important because the appropriate intervention can differ.

A chaperone can make another folding attempt possible, whereas degradation removes a client that may be persistently nonnative or no longer needed. These outcomes are not mutually exclusive stages of the cellular system. A client can receive several opportunities for maturation and then enter a degradation pathway. Some nonnative proteins may instead be sequestered, especially when the processing load exceeds capacity. The choice depends on substrate properties, cellular condition and the available machinery. There is no universal timer that all misfolded chains obey before being destroyed.

A straightforward balance model can clarify the relationship between synthesis, maturation and degradation. Let S be the number of newly synthesized chains per unit time and p the fraction that becomes functional. If functional protein is subsequently lost with a first-order rate constant kd, the rate of change of functional protein number F is pS − kdF, assuming constant synthesis and maturation fraction. At steady state F = pS/kd. A reduction of p from 0.8 to 0.4 halves the predicted steady-state functional amount without changing S or kd. A doubling of kd would have the same effect if p and S remain fixed. The result demonstrates why steady-state abundance by itself cannot distinguish a maturation defect from increased loss.

If S equals 100 molecules per minute, p equals 0.8 and kd equals 0.02 per minute, the predicted steady-state number is 4,000 functional molecules. Reducing p to 0.4 predicts 2,000. The calculation assumes no other input, a single first-order loss process and a time-independent maturation fraction; a real cell may change translation rate, chaperone abundance or degradation in response to stress. The model is nonetheless a useful way to reason about the relationship between RNA expression, protein production and activity.

In recombinant protein production, the same conceptual distinction applies. Increasing expression of a target gene can increase total chain synthesis while decreasing the fraction that matures appropriately if cellular folding capacity is overloaded. A host that provides a suitable redox environment, folding machinery or assembly partners may yield more functional protein without producing more total target polypeptide. Comparing activity with total and soluble protein helps identify which stage is limiting. The purpose is not to memorize a universal production protocol, but to understand why a translation measurement and a functional yield measurement can disagree.

The chapter thus completes a transition from the mechanics of translation to the acquisition of protein function. A sequence determines chemical possibilities, while the cellular environment controls when those possibilities become accessible and how vulnerable intermediate states are handled. Chaperones and folding catalysts assist conformational maturation; disulfide formation and limited proteolysis change covalent structure; assembly brings chains together; quality control decides the fate of persistent failures. The next chapter will develop the broader range of covalent post-translational modifications, and subsequent chapters will explain how localization and regulated degradation complete the life cycle of proteins.

Question 1

Which property most directly distinguishes a nascent ribosome-bound chain from a completed released chain?

A. During elongation the growing chain is ester-linked to tRNA via its carboxyl end. mRNA provides a template, not a covalent anchor; the chain already has peptide bonds and may contain diverse residues.

D. The recovered activity after reversible denaturation supports sequence-dependent structure for this protein. It does not establish universal spontaneous folding, a role for disulfides in decoding, or universal ATP consumption.

Which region of a ribosome-bound polypeptide normally emerges first?

  2. Carboxyl terminus
  3. Amino terminus
  4. Central domain only

C. Translation extends the carboxyl end, so the amino end is made earlier and can emerge first. The C terminus stays near the peptidyl-transferase center while attached to tRNA.

C. Exposed peptide segments can serve as substrate-binding sites for Hsp70. Stop codons and phosphorylation are not universal recognition signals for this mechanism, and buried sites are not directly accessible.

  4. Loss of the initial oligosaccharide transfer reaction in all glycoproteins
Answer and explanation

A. UGGT restores a glucose residue to suitable nonnative clients. Its loss impairs renewed recognition rather than directly abolishing initial glycan transfer or enforcing permanent binding.

Question 37

A proinsulin mutant reaches secretory granules and has native disulfide pairing but accumulates uncleaved precursor. Which defect is most directly suggested?

  1. Impaired recognition or cleavage at maturation sites
  2. Failure of the precursor to reach the secretory pathway
  3. Complete arrest of precursor translation
  4. Failure to establish all native disulfides
Answer and explanation

A. The mutant reaches granules and has native disulfides, so the direct evidence points to late processing. The alternatives contradict the observations.

Question 38

A mutant enzyme has native-like circular dichroism, a normal denaturing mass, but migrates as a monomer rather than active dimer. Which defect is best supported?

  1. A defect primarily in peptide-bond synthesis
  2. A complete loss of secondary structure
  3. A cleavage defect established by a different denaturing mass
  4. Quaternary assembly defect
Answer and explanation

D. The native separation reveals a change in oligomeric state, while the mass and broad secondary structure are preserved. This supports an assembly problem, not a demonstrated loss of synthesis, secondary structure or altered cleavage mass.

Question 39

A pulse-chase shows equal labeled precursor at time zero. Wild type subsequently accumulates processed product, but mutant precursor disappears without product. What next test best discriminates degradation from secretion or redistribution?

  1. Assume that loss from the sampled fraction quantitatively measures degradation
  2. Measure only total cellular RNA after the chase
  3. Repeat only the time-zero measurement
  4. Track labeled material in additional fractions and compare degradation-sensitive conditions
Answer and explanation

D. Following label across fractions and perturbing removal tests both degradation and movement. The alternatives do not resolve why the labeled product disappeared.

Question 40

Functional protein F has steady-state value pS/kd. If S = 60 molecules/min, p = 0.5 and kd = 0.03 min⁻¹, what is F?

  1. 1800
  2. 1000
  3. 30
  4. 120
Answer and explanation

B. Input is 30 functional molecules/min and steady state is 30/0.03 = 1000 molecules. 30 is a flux, not a pool.

Assertion–reason

Question 41

Assertion: Hsp70 ATP hydrolysis can improve maturation without incorporating ATP into the client. Reason: nucleotide state controls substrate binding and release.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. ATP-dependent changes regulate the chaperone cycle and thus folding opportunities. The nucleotide is not a structural component of the client.

Question 42

Assertion: a reducing treatment can split mature insulin A and B chains under denaturing conditions. Reason: peptide bonds are routinely hydrolyzed by reducing agents.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

C. Reduction breaks interchain disulfides, not ordinary peptide backbone bonds. The assertion is true under the stated conditions, the reason false.

Question 43

Assertion: an enzyme can lose activity without a reduction in initial translation. Reason: proper folding and assembly may be required after synthesis.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. An unchanged supply of chains does not guarantee a correctly organized active protein; folding or assembly can independently limit activity.

Question 44

Assertion: GroES is required by every known chaperonin. Reason: eukaryotic CCT has intrinsic chamber-closing elements.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Group II CCT uses an intrinsic lid mechanism rather than the bacterial GroES cochaperonin. The reason is true and the universal assertion is false.

Question 45

Assertion: a PPIase can accelerate recovery of a native enzyme. Reason: it lowers the kinetic barrier for cis–trans interconversion at suitable peptide bonds.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. The catalyst can relieve a slow conformational bottleneck without changing the primary sequence or equilibrium of an isolated isomerization reaction.

Question 46

Assertion: an ER glycoprotein may interact repeatedly with calnexin. Reason: every client remains permanently monoglucosylated regardless of folding state.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

C. Trimming and possible UGGT-mediated reglucosylation enable repeated rounds. The glycan is not permanently in the recognition state.

Multiple statements

Question 47

Select the correct statements: I. Hsp70 can recognize exposed peptide segments. II. GroEL provides a chamber for appropriate clients. III. All newly made proteins must pass through both systems.

  1. I and III
  2. I and II only
  3. II and III
  4. All three
Answer and explanation

B. I and II describe standard mechanisms. III is false because dependence differs among substrates and organisms.

Question 48

Select the correct statements: I. Cytosolic disulfides are absolutely impossible. II. The ER supports oxidative folding. III. An incorrect disulfide can trap a nonnative state.

  1. I only
  2. All three
  3. II and III only
  4. I and II
Answer and explanation

C. The ER supports oxidative folding and erroneous pairings can impede maturation. Cytosolic disulfides are unusual under reducing conditions, not impossible.

Question 49

Select the correct statements: I. Zymogen activation often involves limited proteolysis. II. Signal cleavage and activation cleavage can solve different problems. III. Every processing event destroys the entire protein.

  1. I and II only
  2. III only
  3. All three
  4. II and III
Answer and explanation

A. Limited processing generates useful products and can regulate location or activity. III conflates processing with degradation.

Question 50

Select the correct statements: I. A soluble protein can be nonnative. II. A native oligomer is not necessarily an aggregate. III. The specific activity of a preparation can help estimate active fraction under stated assumptions.

  1. II only
  2. I, II and III
  3. I only
  4. I and III only
Answer and explanation

B. All three observations concern different properties and are compatible. Activity-based estimates require unchanged per-molecule activity and matched assay conditions.

Match 1 DnaJ, 2 GrpE, 3 GroES, 4 PDI to P nucleotide exchange, Q chamber lid, R stimulates Hsp70 hydrolysis, S disulfide exchange.

Match 1 pulse-chase, 2 reducing SDS-PAGE, 3 native size separation, 4 activity assay to P oligomeric state estimate, Q cohort fate, R functional output, S disulfide-sensitive chain connectivity.

Additional quantitative practice

Question 54

A translation rate is 15 residues/s. A domain needs 90 additional residues synthesized before a later interaction region emerges. Under constant speed, what is the interval?

  1. 15 s
  2. 6 s
  3. 1350 s
  4. 90 s
Answer and explanation

B. 90 residues divided by 15 residues/s equals 6 s. This only predicts the time interval, not folding probability.

Question 55

From 250 correctly folded proinsulin molecules, 80% are fully processed by the normal pathway. Ignoring clearance, how many insulin and C-peptide molecules result?

  1. 400 insulin and 200 C-peptide
  2. 200 each
  3. 200 insulin and no C-peptide
  4. 250 each
Answer and explanation

B. 250 × 0.8 = 200 processing events; each produces one insulin and one C-peptide. The other values violate the stated conversion fraction or stoichiometry.

Question 56

A reconstituted system hydrolyzes 450 ATP while 150 molecules become active. What is the measured system-level ATP-to-product ratio?

  1. 150 ATP/product
  2. 0.33 ATP/product
  3. 3 ATP/product
  4. 450 ATP/product
Answer and explanation

C. 450/150 = 3 ATP per active product on average for the whole system. It does not establish exactly three cycles for every client.

Question 57

In a hypothetical paired assay, sample A contains 40 μg enzyme and produces 80 units of activity; sample B contains 50 μg and produces 25 units. Relative apparent specific activity of B vs A?

  1. 50%
  2. 31.25%
  3. 62.5%
  4. 25%
Answer and explanation

D. A = 80/40 = 2 units/μg; B = 25/50 = 0.5 units/μg. The ratio is 0.25. Comparing raw activity alone gives 31.25%, which ignores enzyme mass.

Examination traps

Translation equals maturation

Correct synthesis provides the chain; conformational organization, processing and assembly may still be needed.

Sequence is irrelevant if chaperones act

Chaperones change access to folding pathways; the substrate's sequence still determines its interaction possibilities.

All folding starts at termination

N-terminal regions can fold before synthesis is complete; later interactions may require release.

Every chaperone consumes ATP

Trigger factor and small Hsps provide ATP-independent assistance.

Hsp70–ATP is the tightly retaining state

The usual ADP state releases peptide more slowly; ATP promotes exchange.

DnaJ and GrpE have the same role

DnaJ stimulates hydrolysis; GrpE promotes nucleotide exchange.

GroES is universal

Group II CCT has intrinsic closure elements; GroES is the canonical group I lid.

Disulfide count identifies native structure

Different complete pairings have the same count; geometry and connectivity matter.

Cytosol can never contain a disulfide

Ordinary cytosol is reducing, but redox-sensitive and other exceptions exist.

PDI cleaves peptide backbones

PDI catalyzes thiol–disulfide exchange; proteases cleave peptide bonds.

PPIase is a protease

The bond remains intact during cis–trans interconversion.

All ER folding requires a glycan

Lectin-dependent cycles require suitable glycans; BiP and other factors also assist clients.

Mature insulin is translated as two chains

One precursor produces both chains; processing changes backbone continuity.

Proteolytic processing always destroys function

Limited cleavage can create an active product, such as trypsin.

Soluble or localized means functional

Use independent activity and assembly measures; a soluble or imported chain may remain nonnative.

Revision and active recall

Think in terms of distinct stages: sequence → vulnerable intermediate → conformational organization → chemical processing and assembly → biological activity. The sequence is necessary, but accessibility, compartment, partners and quality control determine how efficiently functional protein is obtained.

What does a nascent-chain C terminus remain connected to?

Peptidyl-tRNA, until release.

Why does cotranslational folding differ from refolding a full chain?

Sequence regions appear in a particular time order.

Why are 105 pairings possible for eight cysteines?

7 × 5 × 3 × 1, counting distinct partner choices.

Explain the two major nucleotide states of Hsp70.

ATP supports rapid exchange; hydrolysis favors ADP-associated retention.

What do DnaJ and GrpE do?

Stimulate hydrolysis and promote exchange, respectively.

How do GroEL and CCT differ in closure?

GroES is a separate group I lid; CCT closes through intrinsic elements.

What distinguishes PDI from PPIase?

Thiol-disulfide exchange versus peptide-bond geometry isomerization.

What does UGGT accomplish?

Reglucosylates suitable nonnative glycoproteins for renewed lectin binding.

Why does cleavage change disulfide classification in proinsulin?

It creates separate backbones; two former intramolecular links become interchain.

How can assembly fail despite normal monomer folding?

Interface changes or missing partners prevent productive association.

Why can a holdase increase yield without changing kf?

It reduces competing loss.

What controls distinguish degradation from redistribution?

Track labeled material in multiple fractions and test loss mechanisms.

What does pS/kd represent?

Steady-state functional abundance under a constant-input first-order loss model.
