1. Why loss of cellular homeostasis can become lethal

A living cell is not simply a bag of molecules. Its interior has a chemical composition different from that of its environment, and it must spend energy to maintain that difference. Its plasma membrane separates two aqueous solutions; its mitochondria supply much of the ATP used in energy-dependent transport; its proteins and nucleic acids require an appropriate chemical environment. A cell can tolerate considerable disturbance as long as these systems can be restored. Necrosis becomes understandable when we ask what happens when the disturbance overwhelms the capacity for recovery.

Consider a piece of tissue supplied by a small blood vessel. If flow is briefly interrupted, cells may reduce their normal activity, consume stored fuel and experience a temporary fall in ATP. After blood flow returns, surviving cells may regain their normal ion concentrations and volume. If deprivation continues, however, mitochondria and membranes may be injured to such a degree that restoration of blood flow is no longer sufficient. The cells are not merely performing their normal functions more slowly: they have lost the ability to sustain the organized processes necessary for life.

Cell injury refers to disturbances of cellular structure or function caused by a harmful condition. Reversible injury means that removal of the harmful condition permits recovery. Irreversible injury means that a cell has passed a point beyond which it cannot regain sustained viability. Death is the irreversible loss of the living state. Necrosis describes a pattern of cell death commonly characterized by swelling, loss of membrane integrity and degradation of cellular components. In tissues it often leads to an inflammatory response. It is not simply another word for any injury, and it is not defined by swelling alone.

The older contrast between accidental necrosis and programmed apoptosis was valuable because it drew attention to two strikingly different patterns of dying cells. Modern experimental work has shown that the contrast is not absolute. Some cells die after catastrophic physical injury with no effective opportunity for regulatory intervention; others undergo regulated molecular processes that culminate in a necrotic appearance. Therefore, the appearance of a dying cell and the mechanism that produced that appearance are related but distinct kinds of information. The first is a morphological description; the second is a causal explanation.

This chapter develops that distinction from basic membrane transport, osmotic balance and mitochondrial energy production. By the end, a student should be able to trace the classical injury cascade, explain why injured cells swell, distinguish temporary stress from irreversible death, identify features of necrosis in a tissue or cell culture, calculate leakage-based cytotoxicity and interpret combinations of experimental markers. Prior knowledge of the membrane, mitochondria and lysosome is helpful: the essential features of each are introduced again where they are needed.

2. Different injuries converge on vulnerable cellular systems

An injury is not defined by a single chemical reaction. Different initiating events disturb different cellular components, but the consequences can converge. Hypoxia means an inadequate supply of oxygen to support the metabolic requirements of a tissue. Ischemia means inadequate blood flow; it commonly reduces oxygen and substrate delivery while also interfering with removal of metabolic products. Thus, ischemia can be more damaging than an equivalent isolated reduction in oxygen. Cells exposed to low oxygen may continue to receive glucose in an experimental medium, whereas cells deprived of blood flow can lose access to both oxygen and fresh fuel.

In an oxygen-dependent cell, the electron transport chain normally transfers electrons to oxygen. When oxygen becomes limiting, the chain cannot maintain its normal rate of electron transfer and proton pumping. ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation declines. How quickly the ATP pool falls depends on the remaining capacity for glycolysis, available substrates, and the rate of energy consumption. A mature mammalian red blood cell is an important exception to the usual mitochondrial story: it lacks mitochondria and normally produces ATP through glycolysis. It can nevertheless be damaged by osmotic stress, oxidants and membrane injury.

Toxic chemical injury may begin at a different location. A compound that inhibits mitochondrial energy production can mimic some consequences of oxygen deprivation even when oxygen is present. An oxidant can modify membrane lipids or proteins, creating a permeability defect before any large measured fall in ATP. A substance that directly disrupts a membrane can abolish the separation between cytosol and environment rapidly. These examples teach why a universal order of biochemical changes should not be imposed on every case of necrosis. ATP depletion is central in classical ischemic injury but need not be the first event in every necrotic death.

Physical injury supplies a third route. Severe mechanical distortion can tear membranes, excessive heat can denature proteins, and extreme osmotic stress can alter cell volume beyond its capacity for compensation. The biological question is not merely which insult occurred but which indispensable cellular function was lost. A sudden breach can bypass the gradual chain of metabolic events typical of hypoxia. A smaller lesion can sometimes be resealed using membrane repair processes, provided energy and intact cellular machinery remain available.

Dose and duration matter together. An insult that is harmless for a short interval can become lethal if prolonged. Different cell types experience different outcomes under apparently identical conditions because their metabolism, reserve fuel, antioxidant defenses, membrane transport and repair capacities differ. Even neighboring cells within ischemic tissue may receive unequal amounts of residual oxygen. For this reason, one often observes a mixture of surviving, reversibly injured, apoptotic and necrotic cells rather than a perfectly uniform population. An examination question that specifies a tissue or cell type may supply exactly the information needed to explain different outcomes.

3. ATP depletion links mitochondrial dysfunction to cell injury

ATP is the immediate energy currency used by many enzymes and membrane pumps. During oxidative phosphorylation, electron transport establishes an electrochemical gradient of protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane. ATP synthase couples proton movement down that gradient to the formation of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the respiratory chain. Removing it does not directly destroy every molecule of ATP, but it reduces the rate at which oxidative metabolism can replenish the ATP that is continually being consumed.

At first, cells can partially compensate by increasing glycolytic ATP production. Glycolysis converts glucose into pyruvate and provides a net gain of two ATP per glucose under the usual accounting for free glucose. Under conditions of limited oxidative metabolism, conversion of pyruvate into lactate regenerates NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue. This compensation is limited by fuel supply and the much lower ATP yield per glucose relative to complete oxidation. Rapidly consuming cells such as cardiac muscle may not be able to satisfy their energy demands through glycolysis alone.

Acidification often accompanies ischemic metabolic disturbance because ATP hydrolysis continues and proton removal is impaired. Lactate accumulation is a useful sign of altered metabolism, but describing lactate formation itself as the sole direct source of all excess protons is an oversimplification. A lower intracellular pH can change the charge and conformation of proteins, modify enzyme activity and contribute to chromatin clumping. Severe disturbance reduces the efficiency of the very reactions required to restore energy balance.

ATP decline affects many processes at once. Protein synthesis falls as translation and its associated processes require energy; ribosomes may detach from swollen rough endoplasmic reticulum. ATP-dependent ion pumps slow down. The cytoskeleton becomes less able to maintain its normal organization. Membrane repair and removal of damaged proteins are compromised. A cell that normally responds to a small perturbation by spending energy now faces a conflict: its maintenance needs increase precisely when its energy supply decreases.

A useful way to reason about ATP is to treat its amount as a pool rather than a fixed property. Over a short interval, change in ATP amount equals the amount synthesized minus the amount consumed. If synthesis falls below consumption, the pool declines; if production again exceeds use, it can recover. Suppose a teaching model begins with 100 arbitrary units of ATP. Synthesis is 8 units per minute and consumption is 14 units per minute. With these rates held fixed, the net loss is 6 units per minute and after five minutes the pool is 70 units. If synthesis subsequently returns to 18 units per minute and consumption stays 14, the pool initially recovers at 4 units per minute. These are not universal physiological rates, and real cells change consumption and production over time. The model simply shows why a rate imbalance can be reversible before structural failure.

A low ATP reading therefore does not prove that a cell is dead. A temporarily stressed cell can regain ATP, while a sample of many cells can show a low total ATP signal because it contains fewer cells or because metabolism per cell has declined. A recovery experiment and an independent indicator of membrane integrity are necessary to distinguish metabolic suppression from terminal injury.

4. Failure of ion gradients and the origin of swelling

The plasma membrane has low permeability to most hydrated ions, but it contains channels, transporters and pumps that determine the net movement of ions. The sodium–potassium ATPase is particularly important in animal cells. For each ATP hydrolyzed it typically transports three Na⁺ ions out of the cell and two K⁺ ions in. This operation opposes passive ion movements, maintains high extracellular Na⁺ and high intracellular K⁺, and makes a small direct electrogenic contribution to the membrane potential. The potential itself also depends strongly on selective membrane permeability, especially to K⁺ in many resting cells.

If ATP availability falls, pump activity may become insufficient to compensate for leak pathways. Na⁺ tends to enter down its electrochemical gradient through available pathways; K⁺ tends to leave when its electrochemical gradient favors efflux. The membrane potential commonly becomes less negative as the gradients and selective conductances change. Counterion movements and other transporters influence the net solute balance. The fact that the pump normally exchanges three ions for two is not, by itself, enough to calculate the volume change of an injured cell. One must know the movements of other osmotically active substances and the behavior of water.

Why does the cell tend to swell? A cell contains many large intracellular molecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, that cannot freely leave an intact membrane. Normally active transport and selective permeability help balance their osmotic effect. When ions accumulate in the cytosol, accompanied by counterions or other solute changes, the total concentration of effectively retained solutes can rise relative to the external solution. Water crosses the membrane, directly or through aquaporins, toward the side with lower water chemical potential. Water entry dilutes the intracellular solutes but increases volume. This is hydropic swelling; it may affect the cell as a whole and membrane-bound organelles such as endoplasmic reticulum.

For a simple quantitative model, suppose the membrane permits water movement but retains a fixed number of effective osmoles. At osmotic equilibrium the effective internal concentration approximately equals the effective external concentration if hydrostatic pressure effects can be neglected. If the number of intracellular osmoles is N and volume is V, then C = N/V. In two equilibrium states with N constant, C₁V₁ = C₂V₂, so V₂/V₁ = C₁/C₂. If a model cell initially equilibrated at 300 mOsm/L is placed in a 240 mOsm/L effectively hypotonic medium, its ideal final volume is 300/240 = 1.25 times its initial volume, an increase of 25%. This assumes no volume regulation, no solute leakage, no membrane rupture and negligible nonosmotic volume. In ischemic injury the number of intracellular osmoles need not remain constant, so this calculation is an illustration of osmotic reasoning rather than a universal ischemic swelling equation.

The same reasoning explains why preventing water entry could delay the visible swelling without necessarily rescuing the cell. The energy deficit, abnormal ion composition and mitochondrial injury could persist even if an osmotic intervention limited the change in volume. Conversely, a cell that swells in a reversible hypotonic environment is not necessarily undergoing necrosis. If returned to suitable conditions before irreversible damage, it may recover its original volume and normal function. Volume is an important observable, but viability is a separate question.

5. Calcium overload turns altered transport into structural damage

Calcium ions are useful signaling molecules precisely because the resting concentration of free Ca²⁺ in the cytosol is kept very low relative to extracellular fluid and certain intracellular stores. A transient increase can regulate contraction, secretion or enzyme activity. The cell then restores the low concentration through calcium pumps, exchangers, sequestration into organelles and binding proteins. Sustained calcium elevation is different from a normal brief signal: it can activate enzymes for longer than their physiological targets and compartments can tolerate.

When ATP-dependent calcium pumps slow, removal of Ca²⁺ from the cytosol becomes less effective. Damage to intracellular stores can release previously sequestered calcium. Plasma membrane injury or altered channel activity may allow additional entry from the extracellular environment. In some ischemic cells, accumulation of intracellular Na⁺ also impairs normal calcium extrusion through sodium–calcium exchange and can favor reverse exchange under appropriate electrochemical conditions. These effects are not identical in every cell type, but all illustrate how failure of one ion gradient can influence another.

Several families of enzymes translate calcium elevation into physical damage. Calcium-activated proteases such as calpains cleave susceptible proteins, including components of the cytoskeleton and membrane-associated structures. The weakened cortex supports the plasma membrane less effectively; abnormal protrusions and fragility can result. Calcium-dependent phospholipase activity can contribute to breakdown of membrane phospholipids, altering the integrity of the lipid bilayer. Activation of nucleases can contribute to DNA damage, while certain ATP-consuming processes can intensify the energy deficit.

The consequence is a feedback loop rather than a one-directional sequence. As membrane injury increases, ions enter through pathways that were previously restricted, and cytosolic calcium may increase further. As mitochondria accumulate excessive calcium, their ability to maintain ATP production may deteriorate. As ATP production deteriorates, calcium removal becomes even less effective. It is this reinforcement between disturbances that helps explain how a slowly developing injury can progress rapidly once cellular reserves are exhausted.

A normal calcium transient and a pathological calcium load can therefore involve the same ion but produce very different outcomes. A sustained rise in free cytosolic calcium must also be distinguished from total calcium content: most cellular calcium may be bound or sequestered, and a change in total amount does not necessarily predict the concentration at a particular enzyme. The distinction lies in magnitude, duration, location and the availability of homeostatic responses. A brief signal in a healthy cell can terminate when pumps restore resting calcium; in a severely injured cell those recovery mechanisms are compromised. In a hypothetical experiment, reducing calcium entry may delay damage initiated by metabolic inhibition, but it need not prevent death caused by complete mechanical disruption of the plasma membrane. A protective effect identifies a contribution in the tested condition; it does not establish a universal requirement for all necrosis.

6. Mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress and positive feedback

Mitochondria are not merely victims of injury. Their damage can amplify the original disturbance because they are central to ATP production and calcium handling. Reactive oxygen species, abbreviated ROS, are chemically reactive oxygen-derived molecules. Examples include superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and the hydroxyl radical. Hydrogen peroxide is not itself a free radical, although it can participate in reactions that generate radicals. Healthy cells form ROS during normal metabolism and control them with antioxidants and enzymes such as superoxide dismutases, catalase and glutathione-dependent systems.

Oxidative stress occurs when the generation of reactive species exceeds the capacity of cellular systems to control their damaging effects. Membrane lipids containing polyunsaturated fatty acids are susceptible to chain reactions of lipid peroxidation. Oxidation of a lipid can initiate further reactions in neighboring lipids, changing membrane properties and producing reactive breakdown products. Oxidation may also impair proteins involved in transport and metabolism and damage nucleic acids. The functional effect depends on which molecules are modified and whether damage is repaired.

A particularly important mitochondrial event in many models of necrotic injury is the mitochondrial permeability transition. Under conditions such as excessive matrix calcium and oxidative stress, the inner mitochondrial membrane can become abnormally permeable to small solutes through a high-conductance permeability transition. This differs from the normal selective channels and carriers that preserve the proton gradient. Sustained transition dissipates the mitochondrial membrane potential and compromises oxidative phosphorylation; solute and water movement can cause matrix swelling and secondary damage to the outer membrane.

The distinction between inner and outer membranes is crucial. The inner membrane supports the proton gradient required for ATP synthesis. The intrinsic apoptotic pathway commonly involves permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane and release of cytochrome c, which can promote caspase activation. These are different mechanistic descriptions, even though both events can occur in damaged mitochondria and may overlap in an injured cell. Observing a fall in mitochondrial membrane potential does not uniquely identify necrosis, and a damaged mitochondrial appearance does not establish that permeability transition was the initiating event.

Consider ischemia followed by reperfusion, the restoration of blood flow. Restored oxygen and substrate delivery are essential for saving cells that remain viable. However, metabolic intermediates, calcium imbalance and altered mitochondrial function established during ischemia may create a condition in which reoxygenation increases oxidative damage and favors sustained permeability transition. This explains the apparent paradox that some additional injury develops during recovery of blood flow. The biological implication is not that restoration of oxygen is intrinsically harmful, but that the outcome depends on the condition of the cellular machinery when oxygen returns.

A hypothetical experimental comparison clarifies the causal argument. Cells exposed to ischemia-like stress may show an initial fall in ATP, followed by calcium elevation, loss of mitochondrial potential and finally membrane leakage. A treatment that delays permeability transition could reduce leakage and preserve a population of cells. To infer a protective effect, investigators must compare matched stress and control groups, verify that the treatment itself does not interfere with the measurement, and examine long-term recovery. A lower signal at one time point could merely mean that death was delayed. A mitochondrial contribution is supported by such comparisons, not by the assumption that all cells must pass through exactly the same sequence.

Evidence that mitochondrial injury can be mechanistically distinct from apoptosis

In 2005, Baines and colleagues compared normal mice and cells with counterparts lacking the gene Ppif, which encodes the mitochondrial regulator cyclophilin D. Their question was whether the permeability transition was required for different forms of cell death. Cells lacking cyclophilin D were substantially less susceptible to calcium overload and oxidative stress, whereas death induced through certain Bcl-2-family-associated pathways remained possible. The comparison matters because the same intervention did not prevent every kind of death. This argues against the idea that mitochondrial permeability transition is an indispensable intermediate in all apoptotic and necrotic processes.

What does such a genetic experiment establish? A change in sensitivity after deleting a gene supports involvement of its product in the tested injury, especially when mitochondrial changes and cellular survival agree. It does not establish that the protein is the entire pore, or that deleting it protects every tissue under every insult. The relevant comparison is between defined stimuli in matched control and deficient cells. A parallel study by Nakagawa and colleagues also reported a distinction between susceptibility to some necrotic and apoptotic stimuli. These observations helped replace a purely morphological classification with a more causal understanding of cell death. The references give the original papers.

One can reconstruct the prediction without memorizing the name of the regulator. If the critical lethal event is a sustained leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane, reducing the likelihood of that leak should improve retention of the proton gradient under the appropriate stress. If death instead depends on outer membrane permeabilization and downstream caspases, changing an inner membrane regulator may have little effect. In either case, the actual survival outcome, rather than a single mitochondrial dye signal, is decisive.

7. Membrane failure and the transition to irreversible injury

As injury accumulates, several distinct membrane systems can fail. The plasma membrane is the boundary that retains cytoplasmic macromolecules and supports gradients across the cell surface. The inner mitochondrial membrane maintains the proton gradient. Lysosomal membranes isolate hydrolytic enzymes from the cytosol. Endoplasmic reticulum membranes define a compartment for protein processing and calcium storage. Losing the integrity of any one of these systems changes cellular function, and losing several together creates a particularly severe injury.

Plasma membrane disruption results from multiple converging events. Lipid oxidation and phospholipid degradation alter the bilayer; proteolysis weakens structural support; swelling can increase mechanical tension. ATP depletion can compromise repair. Small lesions may initially allow increased ion movement, whereas larger defects may permit leakage of soluble cytoplasmic proteins. These different sizes of permeability defects matter in experiments: a small fluorescent dye may gain access before a larger enzyme is released. Absence of enzyme leakage at an early time does not guarantee that the membrane is completely normal.

Lysosomal membrane permeabilization can release hydrolases, including proteases called cathepsins, into the cytosol. Lysosomal enzymes normally act in an acidic lumen, and their activities after release depend on the local environment and enzyme properties. Leakage is therefore not equivalent to all lysosomal enzymes instantly digesting the entire cell. Nevertheless, release of active hydrolases can contribute to damage, particularly in the context of abnormal pH and impaired repair. Breakdown of membranes and proteins exposes additional substrates to degradation.

Once a cell has undergone irreversible plasma membrane disruption or irrecoverable failure of the systems that support life, recovery is no longer possible. The timing of irreversible commitment and the timing of visible rupture are not necessarily identical. Some cells are already committed to death before they become permeable to the dye used in an assay. Conversely, certain transient membrane disruptions are repaired. The strongest experimental inference combines a terminal structural change with failure to restore sustained viability.

Necrotic degradation continues after the cell has died. Enzymes from the dying cell itself and, in tissue, enzymes from recruited inflammatory cells may digest the remains. Digestion after death should not be confused with the events that caused death. A severely injured cell can lose its ability to live well before all of its proteins and DNA have been broken down. This distinction explains why dead tissue can initially retain a recognizable outline and later lose that outline as clearance progresses.

8. Recognizing the necrotic pattern under a microscope

The morphological description of necrosis uses changes that can be observed in cells and tissues. During early reversible injury a cell may look enlarged, with pale or vacuolated cytoplasm due to swelling of organelles. Small protrusions of the plasma membrane may appear. Electron microscopy can reveal dilated endoplasmic reticulum, detached ribosomes and swollen mitochondria before the final disruption of the cell. Such observations indicate injury, but their reversibility cannot be decided from an isolated image.

In more severe injury the cytoplasm may become more homogeneous and eosinophilic in conventional hematoxylin and eosin staining. Eosin binds many cellular proteins and gives the cytoplasm a pink appearance; loss of RNA-associated basophilia and changes in cytoplasmic proteins contribute to the altered staining. Hematoxylin highlights nuclear material. Nuclear changes are conventionally described as pyknosis, karyorrhexis and karyolysis. Pyknosis is nuclear shrinkage and dense chromatin staining. Karyorrhexis is fragmentation of the nucleus. Karyolysis is fading and dissolution of nuclear staining associated with nucleic acid degradation.

These words describe recognizable changes, not three molecular pathways and not an inflexible clock. A given section of tissue may contain cells at different stages. Nuclear shrinkage and fragmentation can also be present during apoptosis, so pyknosis or karyorrhexis alone cannot identify necrosis. Karyolysis, extensive cell swelling, membrane disruption and the surrounding tissue response collectively support a necrotic pattern. A series of observations is more informative than one isolated feature.

There is a further distinction between cell morphology and the appearance of a region of dead tissue. After interruption of blood flow in many solid organs, denaturation of proteins may initially preserve the basic tissue architecture, even though individual cells have lost viability and nuclear detail. This is termed coagulative necrosis. In contrast, abundant enzymatic digestion can convert tissue into a softened or fluid region, as classically observed in many brain infarcts and abscesses; this is liquefactive necrosis. These are descriptions of tissue organization following death, not alternative universal molecular pathways of cellular death. They illustrate why preserved cell outlines do not necessarily prove that cells remain alive.

Microscopy therefore supports an important two-level interpretation. At the cellular level, ask about volume, nuclei and membrane integrity. At the tissue level, examine architecture, neighboring viable cells and the inflammatory infiltrate. At the mechanistic level, separate what the image demonstrates from what would require additional experiments. For example, a swollen cell with disrupted membranes is consistent with necrotic morphology, but a microscope image alone cannot reveal whether a regulated molecular process or overwhelming accidental injury initiated the death.

9. Why leakage from injured cells can promote inflammation

The interior of a cell contains molecules that normally remain separated from the extracellular environment. When severely damaged cells lose membrane integrity, some of these molecules enter the surrounding tissue. Molecules such as extracellular ATP and nuclear protein HMGB1 can act as damage-associated molecular patterns, often abbreviated DAMPs, in appropriate contexts. They are endogenous signals of injury, unlike pathogen-associated patterns that originate from microorganisms. Their effects depend on concentration, molecular state, local receptors and tissue conditions.

Neighboring cells and resident immune cells can detect damage signals through appropriate receptors. Their responses can include production of signaling molecules that influence blood vessels and attract leukocytes. Neutrophils and macrophages can remove dead cell material; their enzymes and reactive products may also injure adjacent cells in some settings. The overall response helps clear debris and initiate repair but can amplify injury if the inflammatory response is excessive or prolonged. Inflammation is thus a biological consequence of the interaction between damaged cells and their environment, not merely a property of the dead cell itself.

This explains why a necrotic cell in an isolated culture does not reproduce the full inflammatory response seen in a tissue. It may release intracellular components, but leukocyte recruitment and changes in blood vessels cannot occur in a culture that lacks these structures. Conversely, inflammation around cells can have many causes other than necrosis, such as infection. Therefore, inflammation is often associated with necrotic injury but is neither a necessary nor a sufficient marker of the mechanism of death.

Apoptotic cells ordinarily retain a plasma membrane barrier during early execution. Exposure of phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet can help phagocytes recognize and engulf them before their contents disperse. This is one reason orderly apoptotic removal often produces less inflammatory damage than uncontrolled lysis. However, apoptotic cells not promptly removed can later lose membrane integrity and become secondarily necrotic. Some regulated cell-death responses can also stimulate immunity. The scientifically useful comparison is between patterns of containment, clearance and release, not a rule that all apoptosis is silent and all necrosis always produces inflammation.

In an ischemic tissue, death of an initial group of cells can be followed by inflammation and stress in neighboring cells that survived the original insult. Distinguishing these two stages is important when interpreting a treatment: a compound could reduce inflammatory damage after necrosis without preventing the initial cell death. Conversely, a treatment that preserves cells may reduce inflammation indirectly by reducing leakage. Comparing the time of cell death with the time of inflammatory recruitment helps discriminate these explanations.

10. Distinguishing necrosis, apoptosis and reversible stress

An early apoptotic cell commonly becomes smaller, with condensed chromatin and characteristic blebbing. Its contents may be partitioned into membrane-bound fragments called apoptotic bodies. The classical apoptotic pathways use initiator and executioner caspases, proteases that cleave selected substrates to dismantle cellular structures in a coordinated manner. The plasma membrane generally retains its barrier function until later stages. Necrotic injury commonly shows early swelling, progressive loss of membrane integrity and leakage of intracellular molecules. The causal explanation in classical energy-depletion injury is failure of homeostatic systems rather than an obligatory executioner-caspase cascade.

Both patterns can include nuclear condensation, membrane blebs, mitochondrial abnormalities and DNA degradation. The same insult can produce different outcomes according to severity and cellular state. Moderate damage may activate apoptotic pathways if enough cellular machinery remains functional; severe stress can overwhelm energy metabolism and produce necrotic injury. This is a useful general tendency, not a quantitative ATP threshold that decides all death mechanisms. Some regulated necrotic deaths require intact signaling machinery and do not begin with catastrophic ATP depletion.

A third state is a viable cell under stress. It may arrest its cell cycle, reduce protein synthesis, mobilize stored substrates or increase intracellular recycling. Autophagy, which uses lysosomal degradation to recycle intracellular material, can help cells survive nutrient deprivation or organelle damage. An increase in autophagic structures does not prove either necrosis or death. Likewise, a cell that has ceased dividing may be quiescent, senescent or reversibly arrested rather than dead. The outcome must be tested through recovery of the relevant function or continued survival.

Suppose two cultures are exposed to different stresses. In culture A, cells stop proliferating, ATP falls modestly and plasma membrane exclusion of dyes persists. After removal of the stress, cells again proliferate. The evidence supports reversible injury or arrest. In culture B, ATP falls, cells swell and cytoplasmic enzymes accumulate in the extracellular medium; surviving cell counts fail to recover. The evidence supports lethal injury with necrotic features. In culture C, cells first shrink and show active executioner caspases, then become permeable later. That sequence favors apoptosis followed by secondary membrane breakdown rather than primary accidental necrosis.

The key distinction is temporal. A single observation made after all three cultures have been incubated for many hours may conceal the early features that distinguished the mechanisms. If B and C are both positive for a membrane-impermeant dye at the final time, they share a terminal endpoint but need not share a pathway. A well-designed experiment asks when the endpoint emerged, what preceded it, whether a relevant intervention altered it, and whether the cells ultimately recovered.

11. Necrotic morphology is not proof of an unregulated mechanism

For many years, textbooks used necrosis as the opposite of programmed cell death. This classification was based heavily on morphology and on the contrast between catastrophic injury and the orderly sequence of apoptosis. Research summarized by the Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death in 2018 separates accidental cell death from regulated cell death according to mechanism and susceptibility to intervention. A catastrophic mechanical disruption is an example of accidental death; a process requiring defined molecular effectors can be regulated even if it ends in membrane permeabilization.

Necroptosis provides a standard example. In some cellular contexts, particular receptor-associated signals can promote a regulated death pathway involving RIPK3 and the effector protein MLKL. Activated MLKL contributes to plasma membrane permeabilization. RIPK1 participates in some, but not all, initiating contexts. Caspase-8 normally restrains this pathway in certain receptor-associated settings. Therefore, blocking caspases can sometimes redirect rather than prevent death. It is not sufficient to observe that caspases are inhibited and conclude that any remaining death is necroptosis: pathway-specific evidence and appropriate controls are required.

Other regulated death mechanisms can also have a lytic or necrotic appearance. Pyroptosis involves gasdermin-dependent membrane pore formation and can be strongly inflammatory, while ferroptosis is associated with iron-dependent accumulation of damaging lipid peroxides. These examples are useful for the basic conceptual distinction; detailed signaling networks belong outside the present scope. All share the possibility of eventual loss of membrane integrity, but they do not require the same upstream molecules or the same initiating conditions.

To show that death depends on a regulated pathway, researchers need more than a morphological endpoint. A useful experiment compares a control cell with a cell lacking a candidate effector, exposes both to a matched stimulus, and measures both death and pathway activity. If the candidate-deficient cells survive and reintroducing the functional effector restores susceptibility, that supports a causal requirement in the tested model. If death is only delayed, or if the mutant cells were already metabolically different before treatment, the interpretation becomes weaker. Inhibitors can have off-target effects, so genetic and biochemical evidence are valuable independent checks.

The exam-level conclusion is precise: necrosis is a morphological and cellular outcome that can be reached by multiple mechanisms. Classical severe injury is frequently associated with passive collapse of essential systems, but some processes leading to necrotic morphology are actively regulated. The presence of membrane rupture establishes a common endpoint; the particular molecular route requires evidence about the preceding events and relevant dependencies.

Historical evidence from a death pathway that resisted caspase inhibition

Degterev and colleagues reported in 2005 that a death-receptor-associated stimulus could produce nonapoptotic death when intracellular apoptotic signaling was unavailable. They identified a small molecule that inhibited this response in their experimental systems. The significance for cell biology was that a process resembling necrosis could be interrupted pharmacologically, challenging the view that every nonapoptotic death was uncontrollable physical collapse. Later work defined molecular dependencies of necroptosis; the initial pharmacological observation by itself did not establish all of those dependencies.

A careful interpretation distinguishes the matched stimulus without inhibitor, the inhibitor-treated group and appropriate controls for inhibitor effects in otherwise viable cells. If a molecule suppresses death in one system, the result suggests a tractable process, but off-target effects, differences in metabolism or a mere delay in death must still be considered. This is why modern nomenclature emphasizes a combination of molecular and functional evidence.

12. Measuring viability, permeability and cellular leakage

A cell-death assay measures an observable feature rather than death as an abstract state. The choice of assay should follow the question. Is the plasma membrane permeable? Has cellular metabolism fallen? Are apoptosis-associated proteases active? Can the cell recover after removal of the stress? These questions are related but not equivalent. The experimental guidelines in the references emphasize combining complementary observations rather than identifying a mechanism from a single assay.

Trypan blue and propidium iodide (PI) are commonly used as indicators of membrane permeability. Healthy cells with an intact plasma membrane exclude these dyes under standard live-cell staining conditions. PI becomes strongly fluorescent when bound to nucleic acids. When the membrane becomes sufficiently permeable, PI can reach intracellular nucleic acids. In this live-cell assay a PI-positive event is evidence of loss of dye exclusion during the staining interval, not proof of the initiating mechanism or irreversibility. Deliberate fixation or permeabilization for DNA-content analysis makes the same dye unsuitable for inferring prior live-cell membrane integrity. Handling, timing and staining conditions matter.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme that catalyzes the interconversion of lactate and pyruvate coupled to NAD⁺/NADH. If membrane damage allows LDH to escape, its activity can be measured in extracellular culture medium using a coupled reaction. In a calibrated assay, the signal reflects extracellular enzyme activity within an appropriate working range. It does not count dead cells directly. Some cells contain more LDH than others; released enzyme can lose activity over time; medium or a test compound can affect the measurement. A small number of extensively lysed cells may produce a similar signal to a larger number of partially permeable cells.

An LDH experiment needs a cell-free background, a matched untreated control measuring spontaneous leakage, and a maximum-release control in which the total accessible enzyme is released under validated assay conditions. Equal initial cell numbers, similar volumes and suitable timing help make the groups comparable. A treatment that inhibits LDH catalysis could lower the measured signal even while membrane damage increases, so a cell-free enzyme-interference control is useful when interpreting an unexpected result. This illustrates how assay design separates a biological change from a measurement artifact.

Annexin V binds exposed phosphatidylserine in a calcium-dependent manner. Phosphatidylserine is normally enriched on the cytosolic leaflet of the plasma membrane. During early apoptosis its exposure can occur while the membrane still excludes PI. A population that is annexin-positive and PI-negative is therefore consistent with an early apoptotic pattern, but phosphatidylserine exposure can occur in other contexts too. A double-positive population can contain late apoptotic cells, secondary necrotic cells or primary lytic deaths with accessible phosphatidylserine. An annexin/PI plot must be interpreted together with timing, morphology and other evidence.

Metabolic assays, including ATP measurements and assays of reduction of indicator compounds, assess aspects of cellular metabolism. Their signal changes can reflect altered metabolism per cell or altered cell numbers. A drug that arrests proliferation may reduce the signal relative to rapidly growing controls without killing cells. An ATP assay also cannot distinguish a transient energy deficit from irreversible injury without complementary evidence. For cells capable of dividing, a recovery or colony-formation assay after removal of the stress provides information about sustained proliferative survival. For terminally differentiated cells, recovery should instead be assessed by appropriate functional and viability measures; inability to form colonies is normal for many such cells.

The strongest interpretation integrates at least two different kinds of evidence. Time-lapse morphology can establish whether swelling or shrinkage preceded membrane permeability. A caspase activity assay can help evaluate apoptosis, but inhibition must be verified. LDH leakage provides a measure of membrane-associated cytotoxicity. Recovery after stress removal distinguishes a transient response from persistent loss of survival. None of these alone identifies every possible death pathway.

Why a time-resolved panel is stronger than a single end point

In a flow-cytometry experiment, separate unstained and single-stained control samples help establish background fluorescence and the distinction between positive and negative signals. Where fluorescent emissions overlap, appropriate compensation is necessary to avoid assigning signal from one dye to another. An untreated viable population provides a practical reference for intact cells, while a known membrane-permeable population checks the ability of the dye to detect compromised cells. If one condition causes cells to detach, excluding the detached fraction would selectively remove the very cells most likely to be injured. Comparable sampling of attached and detached populations is therefore important.

Interpreting a change in the percentage of double-positive events also requires knowing the denominator. A gate that excludes fragments can be appropriate for measuring the fraction of recognizable cells, but the result then does not describe the entire starting population. An apparent fall in positive fraction at a late time may reflect disintegration and loss from the gate rather than a reversal of death. A total count, recovery test or extracellular leakage measure can reveal that problem. A student should always ask what population the plotted percentage represents.

13. Quantitative interpretation of leakage and time-course data

Consider an LDH release assay in which all absorbance readings have been measured within the linear range, with equal starting cell numbers. Let B be the cell-free background, S the spontaneous release signal from untreated cells, M the signal from the maximum-release control and T the signal from the treated culture. The background-corrected values are S − B, M − B and T − B. The treatment-related excess release above spontaneous release is (T − B) − (S − B) = T − S. The measurable range between spontaneous and maximum release is M − S. Therefore the percentage of this range occupied by treatment-related leakage is 100(T − S)/(M − S).

Suppose B = 0.05, S = 0.15, M = 0.95 and T = 0.47 absorbance units. The untreated corrected signal is 0.10 and maximum corrected signal is 0.90. The treated corrected signal is 0.42. Excess signal is 0.42 − 0.10 = 0.32 and the available range is 0.90 − 0.10 = 0.80. Thus normalized release = 100 × 0.32/0.80 = 40%. Absorbance is dimensionless, and the ratio is also dimensionless. This is 40% of the assay's available leakage range, not necessarily exactly 40% dead cells.

Why not simply divide the treated value by the maximum value? That would include spontaneous release in the treatment effect and, in this example, also include the optical background. Such a calculation answers a different question. The chosen normalization assumes matched controls, linear signal with enzyme activity, comparable total enzyme available and no differential interference. A value below zero or above 100% can arise through variability, interference or mismatched controls; it should be investigated rather than automatically treated as a physically meaningful death fraction. When M is equal to or below S, the available range is absent or invalid and the formula cannot be interpreted.

A separate counting experiment uses a membrane-impermeant dye. Suppose a sample of 400 identifiable cells contains 110 dye-positive cells. The positive fraction is 110/400 × 100 = 27.5%. If the same culture yields 40% normalized LDH release, the two values are not necessarily contradictory. The dye counts individually identifiable cells, whereas the LDH assay measures extracellular activity accumulated over the observation interval. Completely fragmented cells may be absent from the count but still contribute to the enzyme signal. Cell-to-cell enzyme differences and transient permeability further complicate direct conversion.

Time-course experiments add mechanistic information. In a hypothetical ischemia-like treatment, relative ATP is 100%, 55%, 30% and 15% at 0, 15, 30 and 60 minutes. Normalized LDH release is 0%, 3%, 15% and 60% at the same times. The largest early ATP decline occurs before extensive leakage, consistent with metabolic disturbance preceding widespread membrane injury. From 30 to 60 minutes the average increase in normalized LDH release is (60 − 15)/30 = 1.5 percentage points per minute. It is not a first-order rate constant, and it is not a prediction that every subsequent minute will show that same increase.

To investigate whether ATP loss is causally upstream, a researcher might restore conditions that support ATP production at an early time and compare the later leakage and recovery with a matched continuously stressed group. If early restoration preserves ATP and reduces subsequent leakage, the evidence supports a causal link in that experimental setting. If ATP falls at the same time as leakage, ordering is less clear. If the intervention reduces leakage but not sustained survival, the effect might be delay rather than prevention. These distinctions are frequently more important in Part C questions than recalling the name of the assay.

14. From experimental observation to a mechanistic conclusion

Consider a hypothetical tissue experiment in which a brief period of oxygen deprivation produces cell swelling but membrane exclusion of PI remains intact. When oxygen and suitable nutrients return, cell volume normalizes and ATP recovers. A longer deprivation produces swelling, extensive dye entry, extracellular LDH and failure of recovery. The short and long treatments share a starting disturbance but differ in reversibility. It is the prolonged loss of viable function, not merely the existence of swelling, that distinguishes the second outcome.

Now imagine a second experiment comparing cells under metabolic stress with and without an intervention that lowers sustained cytosolic calcium. Both groups undergo the same initial ATP fall. The treated group shows less membrane leakage at a later time and better survival after recovery. Because the initial ATP decline is similar, reduced downstream injury is consistent with calcium acting as an amplifier rather than the sole initial cause. The inference becomes stronger if intracellular calcium is independently measured and the intervention does not directly alter the LDH assay. It becomes weaker if the intervention changes oxygen consumption, baseline cell number or dye detection.

A third experiment examines a receptor-induced death that ends in cell swelling and PI entry. In the normal cells, caspase inhibition does not protect them, but removal of MLKL strongly reduces terminal permeability and restores sustained survival. Reintroducing functional MLKL restores susceptibility. In this model, the observations favor an MLKL-dependent regulated necrotic death rather than accidental physical damage. The comparison is meaningful because the genetic perturbation changes the outcome under matched conditions. It does not prove that all necrotic injuries require MLKL.

These examples illustrate the difference between correlation, temporal order and causation. Correlation means two observations vary together. Temporal order means one change precedes another. Causal involvement is supported when a carefully chosen intervention changes the predicted downstream event and alternative explanations are excluded as far as possible. A student encountering experimental graphs should identify the measured variables, the controls, the time axis and the actual endpoint before deciding which pathway is supported.

The final conceptual chain for classical energy-failure injury is now clear. Oxygen or substrate deprivation lowers ATP production relative to consumption; ATP-dependent homeostatic processes fail; ion gradients and cell volume become abnormal; calcium and oxidative injury compromise proteins and membranes; further mitochondrial damage intensifies the energy deficit; terminal membrane failure allows cellular material to escape. Tissue responses may then promote clearance, inflammation and repair. This is a causal model with interacting loops, not an obligatory sequence for every form of necrotic death. Direct membrane injury can begin near the end of the chain; regulated necrotic processes can enter through different molecular routes.

A cell is capable of restoring its state only while essential maintenance and repair systems remain functional. This principle links necrosis to the next chapter on autophagy. Recycling damaged components and obtaining metabolic substrates can improve survival during manageable stress. When stress exceeds the capacity of maintenance, recycling and repair, the cell may die. The presence of recycling activity does not prove that recycling caused death. The distinction between an adaptive response, an execution mechanism and a terminal appearance is the central skill that connects all three chapters on apoptosis, necrosis and autophagy.

Biological connections and a final mastery check

The membrane pump chapter explains why transport requires coupling to a favorable energy source; the osmosis chapter establishes the driving force for water movement. The mitochondrial organization chapter and oxidative phosphorylation chapter explain the membrane gradient that becomes vulnerable during hypoxia. The lysosome chapter explains why hydrolytic enzymes are compartmentalized. Together these mechanisms show that necrosis is a breakdown of coordinated cellular organization, not an isolated molecular event.

To reconstruct the injury cascade, start with a healthy oxygen-dependent cell and explain which changes follow when oxidative ATP synthesis falls. Then reverse the premise: start with a physical membrane breach while ATP is initially normal. The second reasoning route should reach ionic disturbance without requiring ATP depletion to precede the breach. Finally, compare two cultures with equal final LDH values but distinct time courses of caspase activity and swelling. If you can explain why each observation is compatible with more than one mechanism and propose a suitable additional test, you have moved from recognition of necrotic morphology to mechanistic examination reasoning.

15. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original practice questions; choose one best answer before opening the explanation.

1. Which is the best description of classical necrosis?

  1. Reversible metabolic slowing
  2. Lethal injury commonly ending in loss of membrane integrity
  3. Any cell-cycle arrest
  4. Any nuclear condensation
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B is correct because the terminal loss of cellular organization and integrity distinguishes lethal necrotic injury. A and C can occur in surviving cells. D is also found in apoptosis and is insufficient.

2. Which event directly follows inhibition of mitochondrial electron transport during severe hypoxia?

  1. Increased oxidative ATP synthesis
  2. Guaranteed membrane rupture at once
  3. Reduced respiratory proton pumping
  4. Universal activation of MLKL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. C follows because electron transfer drives proton pumping. A reverses the expected effect. B can occur later but is not instantaneous or inevitable. D is not a universal response.

3. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase normally transports per ATP:

  1. 2 Na⁺ out and 3 K⁺ in
  2. 3 Na⁺ in and 2 K⁺ out
  3. 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in
  4. Equal Na⁺ and K⁺ in opposite directions
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. C is the usual animal-cell stoichiometry. A reverses the counts, B reverses direction and D incorrectly treats the pump as an electroneutral equal exchange.

4. Which finding is most directly measured by PI uptake in a live-cell exclusion assay?

  1. Mitochondrial ATP synthesis
  2. Plasma membrane permeability
  3. Caspase cleavage
  4. DNA replication rate
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B is correct because PI reaches nucleic acids after loss of exclusion. A and C require different measurements. D cannot be inferred from dye entry in an unfixed viability assay.

5. Karyolysis describes:

  1. Nuclear fading and dissolution
  2. Cell swelling only
  3. Nuclear fragmentation
  4. Chromatin condensation only
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A is correct. C describes karyorrhexis; D describes pyknosis; B concerns cell volume rather than nuclear structure.

6. Which statement about inflammation is most accurate?

  1. Necrosis always recruits leukocytes in cell-free buffer
  2. Apoptosis can never stimulate immunity
  3. Leakage can promote inflammation in a responsive tissue
  4. Inflammation uniquely diagnoses necroptosis
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. C recognizes the role of extracellular injury signals and responding cells. A lacks the necessary tissue components. B is too absolute. D ignores infection and other mechanisms.

7. Assertion: reversible swelling alone does not establish necrosis. Reason: cells can restore volume if ion transport and structural integrity recover.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A is correct: recovery is precisely the reason swelling cannot by itself establish irreversible injury. B fails to recognize this causal relationship; C and D incorrectly deny true statements.

8. Which is a regulated death mechanism that can end in necrotic morphology?

  1. Quiescence
  2. Normal cytokinesis
  3. Necroptosis
  4. Physiological calcium transient
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. C is correct, because it can require RIPK3 and MLKL signaling. A and B are viable cellular states or processes; D is normally reversible signaling.

9. Which distinction is correct?

  1. Inner mitochondrial membrane maintains the proton gradient; outer membrane permeabilization can participate in apoptosis
  2. Both mitochondrial membranes are identical energy barriers
  3. Cytochrome c release always proves primary necrosis
  4. Mitochondrial swelling uniquely identifies MLKL activity
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A distinguishes two mitochondrial events. B ignores membrane specialization. C and D infer a mechanism from findings that are not specific.

10. Annexin-positive, PI-negative cells are best interpreted as:

  1. Definitely all necroptotic
  2. Consistent with early apoptosis, requiring corroboration
  3. Certainly dead by accidental rupture
  4. Definitely normal cells with no stress
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B is the cautious interpretation. A and C claim pathway certainty not justified by the markers; D ignores exposed phosphatidylserine.

16. Part C — Experiments and quantitative reasoning

All numerical and experimental data in these questions are hypothetical teaching examples.

1. A model cell equilibrated at 300 mOsm/L is moved to 200 mOsm/L, with fixed internal osmoles and water equilibrium. What is its predicted volume?

  1. 0.67 times initial
  2. 1.20 times initial
  3. 1.50 times initial
  4. 2.00 times initial
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Use C₁V₁ = C₂V₂, so V₂/V₁ = 300/200 = 1.5, a 50% increase. A inverts the ratio, B and D are not the equilibrium ratio. The model excludes active volume regulation and lysis.

2. LDH values: S=0.20, M=1.00, T=0.60. What is normalized treatment-related release?

  1. 40%
  2. 50%
  3. 60%
  4. 75%
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. (0.60−0.20)/(1.00−0.20) × 100 = 0.40/0.80 × 100 = 50%. A ignores the range, C uses raw treated reading as a percentage, D does not apply the background-relative normalization.

3. ATP begins at 90 units, production is 5 units/min and use is 11 units/min for 8 min. What is the remaining amount?

  1. 42 units
  2. 48 units
  3. 50 units
  4. 138 units
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Net rate = 5−11 = −6 units/min; 90−48 = 42. B is the amount lost, C is 90−40 and ignores consumption, D adds the loss.

4. An intervention prevents swelling during hypoxia but ATP remains depressed and no cells recover. What conclusion is strongest?

  1. Necrosis prevented
  2. Osmotic morphology altered without demonstrated rescue
  3. ATP is irrelevant
  4. All cells underwent apoptosis
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B separates an observable phenotype from sustained survival. A contradicts the recovery test. C is unsupported, and D cannot be determined from volume alone.

5. A compound lowers LDH signal in injured cells but also inhibits purified LDH activity. Which is the best next step?

  1. Declare it cytoprotective
  2. Increase concentration without controls
  3. Use independent membrane and recovery assays
  4. Classify the death as apoptosis
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. C addresses measurement interference. A confuses assay suppression with protection; B compounds the confounder; D is not established by reduced enzyme activity.

6. Culture A shrinks and activates caspases at 2 h, then becomes PI-positive at 12 h. Culture B swells and becomes PI-positive without detected caspase activation at 2 h. What is most plausible?

  1. Both are primary accidental necrosis
  2. A shows apoptosis with later membrane failure; B shows early lytic injury
  3. A is definitely viable at 12 h
  4. B is definitely ferroptotic
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B accounts for the temporal evidence. A ignores early apoptosis features, C disregards later permeability and D specifies an untested molecular mechanism.

7. A mutant lacking MLKL survives a receptor stimulus; re-expression restores death. What inference is justified?

  1. MLKL is required in this model
  2. All necrosis requires MLKL
  3. All apoptosis requires MLKL
  4. MLKL expression alone is sufficient in every cell
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A follows from loss and restoration of susceptibility under the tested stimulus. B and C overgeneralize to other mechanisms; D confuses requirement under stimulation with universal sufficiency.

8. During a time course, ATP falls at 10 min, calcium rises at 20 min, and LDH rises at 50 min. Which experiment best tests a calcium contribution to leakage?

  1. Measure LDH only at 50 min again
  2. Compare calcium-lowering intervention with matched stress controls and measure recovery
  3. Measure DNA sequence of an unrelated gene
  4. Infer calcium causation solely from order
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B tests an intervention and an outcome while accounting for matched controls. A only repeats an endpoint; C lacks a mechanistic link; D mistakes temporal precedence for sufficient causal evidence.

9. A count finds 72 dye-positive cells among 240 intact identifiable cells. LDH release is 55%. Which interpretation is sound?

  1. Dye-positive fraction is 30%; LDH measures a different endpoint
  2. Both assays must equal 55%
  3. Dye-positive fraction is 72%
  4. The data prove a calculation error
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. 72/240 × 100 = 30%. LDH can include activity from disintegrated cells and varies by enzyme content and time. B assumes unjustified equivalence, C confuses count with percentage, and D ignores distinct endpoints.

10. A culture stops growing, ATP signal drops relative to controls, and after washout it forms colonies normally. What is best supported?

  1. Irreversible necrosis
  2. Reversible metabolic or proliferative suppression
  3. Mandatory pyroptosis
  4. All cells permanently senescent
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B fits sustained proliferative recovery. A and C conflict with survival; D conflicts with normal colony formation.

11. In the hypothetical time series in the chapter, LDH rises from 3% at 15 min to 15% at 30 min. What is the average slope?

  1. 0.8 percentage points/min
  2. 1.2 percentage points/min
  3. 12 percentage points/min
  4. 5 percentage points/min
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. (15−3)/(30−15)=12/15=0.8 percentage points per minute. B, C and D do not divide the observed change by the correct interval. This is an average slope, not a kinetic rate constant.

12. An inhibitor reduces extracellular damage signals but does not alter the fraction of dead cells. What can be inferred?

  1. It necessarily prevents necrosis
  2. It may modify release or downstream inflammatory signaling, not demonstrated survival
  3. It necessarily increases ATP production
  4. It demonstrates all cells are apoptotic
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. B is consistent with changed signaling despite unchanged death. A contradicts the observed death fraction; C and D require additional evidence.
