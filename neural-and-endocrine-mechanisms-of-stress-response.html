1. From a disturbance to an organized physiological response

A resting animal must continually adjust the delivery of oxygen, nutrients and water to tissues whose demands change from moment to moment. A sudden disturbance makes this problem especially apparent. Imagine an animal that has detected an approaching predator. The animal has not yet started running, but its heart may already be beating faster. This change cannot be explained as a simple response to an increase in carbon dioxide production by contracting muscles, because the increase in contraction has not yet happened. The nervous system can predict a likely demand and adjust organs in advance. In contrast, a reduction in arterial pressure following a loss of blood can evoke compensation without the animal having consciously interpreted any danger. Both cases illustrate physiological stress, but they do not begin with identical sensory information.

A stressor is a condition that challenges physiological stability or predicts such a challenge. The stress response is the collection of changes generated by the organism in response to that condition. A stressor is therefore not a hormone; an increase in a hormone is one component of a response. A change in cardiovascular performance is an outcome, which may or may not have been caused by the measured hormone. These distinctions prevent an important error: observing that an animal has elevated cortisol does not establish what caused the increase, whether the increase is adaptive, or whether the animal is injured. Context and measurements before and after the challenge are necessary.

An effective response must solve at least three different problems. It must detect the challenge, route information to relevant control centres and change the function of distant tissues. The first problem is sensory, the second involves neural integration, and the third involves neural and endocrine effectors. In mammals, the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal medulla provide a particularly fast route to several effector organs. The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis provides a complementary endocrine route that can alter substrate availability and tissue responsiveness over longer time scales. These routes are coupled, but neither is simply an obligatory next step of the other. Both may be engaged by a common upstream challenge.

Negative feedback is a central organizing principle of homeostasis. If arterial pressure falls, appropriate changes in heart function and vascular resistance can help restore it. The restored pressure reduces the original sensory signal. Hormonal feedback is another form of regulation: glucocorticoids can restrain the neural and pituitary signals that promote their own secretion. The first loop senses an altered regulated variable, while the second senses the output of an endocrine axis. It is important not to conflate the two. Cortisol feedback can reduce ACTH release even while the original environmental challenge continues.

Stress physiology involves predictive control as well as feedback. The sight of a threat, a learned cue or an expected energetic challenge may change organ activity before the physical variable that eventually needs regulation has changed. The value of predictive control is speed, but prediction may also be wrong. A rapid response therefore needs to be adjustable rather than fixed. A system that responds strongly to every unfamiliar stimulus could consume excessive energy or interfere with unrelated functions; a system that responds only after severe disruption might act too late. The magnitude, duration and tissue distribution of the response must be considered together.

Distinguishing disturbance, response and performance

The idea of physiological regulation can be made more precise by separating three measurable quantities. The first is the intensity of a challenge, such as the magnitude of a temperature change or the duration of restricted oxygen availability. The second is an effector response, such as an increase in sympathetic activity. The third is the outcome, such as arterial pressure or locomotor performance. These quantities may be related but are not equivalent. A greater effector response at the same level of challenge could represent more effective compensation, a lower threshold for activation or a greater underlying disturbance in the regulated variable. Without independent measures, the direction of the relationship cannot be determined.

Suppose two animals experience the same moderate reduction in arterial pressure. One animal mounts a large increase in sympathetic activity and recovers pressure rapidly; the other displays a smaller increase and recovers more slowly. Under these simplified circumstances, stronger sympathetic recruitment may be beneficial. But change the experimental situation: suppose both animals recover equally rapidly, and the first requires a much greater effector response because its vascular smooth muscle is less responsive. In this case the large response may reflect inefficient compensation rather than better physiological capacity. Measuring hormone output and the outcome together is necessary to distinguish these explanations. The same principle holds for the HPA axis: a larger ACTH response can indicate a different central signal or an attempt to compensate for weaker adrenal responsiveness.

There is also an important difference between an effector whose activity changes the regulated variable directly and a mediator that changes the responsiveness of that effector. Sympathetic nerve activity in a vascular bed can alter its resistance within a short period. A glucocorticoid may help maintain the receptor or signaling environment in which the vascular response occurs. The immediate neural command and the endocrine preparation are complementary, but removing either may disturb the final outcome. This arrangement allows the organism to change performance quickly while retaining the capacity to respond again after the first challenge.

A comparison with simple feedback control helps clarify the concept of a target range. Many regulated variables do not have one immutable value. Heart rate, for example, is an output of several controls rather than a variable maintained at a single set point. Arterial pressure is controlled within limits, but its usual operating conditions can vary with activity and posture. The physiological target is not necessarily a return of every effector to its resting value during exertion. Increased heart rate and ventilation can be appropriate while the challenge continues, provided they support the more fundamental requirements of tissue oxygenation and cellular energy balance.

A response can also continue after a stressor has ended. This persistence may reflect residual circulating hormone, ongoing appraisal, delayed intracellular signaling or the time required to repair the disturbance. A researcher who measures output only at the end of exposure may miss the peak or the recovery phase. In a repeated-challenge experiment, an incomplete return to baseline can change the apparent magnitude of the second response. Defining response amplitude relative to the immediately preceding concentration is not the same as defining it relative to the original resting concentration. Both definitions can be informative if stated clearly.

There is no requirement that every stress response increase heart rate. Consider a vertebrate diving below the water surface. The need to preserve limited oxygen stores may favour slowed cardiac activity and selective perfusion, even though the animal is experiencing an environmental challenge. A response is adaptive when it contributes to an appropriate function under the conditions, not when it matches a stereotyped list of symptoms. The detailed diving response belongs to the comparative adaptation chapter, but this example establishes a general rule: identify the physiological problem before assigning a sign to the neural output.

The distinction between compensation and recovery also matters. During a challenge, an animal may maintain a regulated variable only by sustaining elevated effector activity. Recovery means that the supporting mechanism can relax as the challenge resolves or as underlying conditions improve. A stable measured variable is therefore not sufficient evidence that the organism is in the same physiological state as it was before exposure. Stable blood pressure, for example, could coexist with different combinations of cardiac output and vascular resistance. The stress response becomes understandable when we study how the same outcome can be supported by different internal states.

Stress and homeostasis are therefore related without being opposites. The response to a challenge is often one of the mechanisms through which stability is achieved. Homeostatic success may require a change in effector activity rather than a lack of change. This perspective prepares the distinction between the rapid nervous system and the slower endocrine axis: their value is measured through coordinated function, not by assuming that either is inherently beneficial or detrimental.

2. Sensory signals and central appraisal

Internal sensory receptors report conditions within the organism. Arterial baroreceptors provide information about stretch in arterial walls; chemoreceptor pathways respond to changes associated with oxygen availability, carbon dioxide and acidity. Visceral afferents communicate disturbances in internal organs to central structures. In contrast, sensory systems such as vision, hearing and smell provide information about events outside the body. The nervous system does not treat every detected event as equally important: the functional meaning of the event depends on context, previous experience and the physiological condition of the animal.

The hypothalamus is a small but crucial region of the vertebrate brain that coordinates neural, endocrine and behavioural aspects of physiological regulation. Its paraventricular nucleus contains several populations of neurons with different projections. Some neurons contribute to autonomic control by influencing brainstem and spinal networks, whereas others release regulatory peptides into the blood vessels that connect the hypothalamus with the anterior pituitary. These populations should not be imagined as a single neuron that releases every stress mediator. Their anatomical organization makes it possible to activate endocrine and autonomic components together or to vary one relative to the other.

Brainstem circuits integrate visceral inputs and help shape autonomic outputs. For instance, reduced stretch-sensitive afferent activity during a fall in blood pressure changes the activity of brainstem circuits governing sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow. A higher brain centre can also modulate those circuits in anticipation of exertion. The observation that two distinct types of input change the same effector does not imply that their initial neural pathways are identical. A useful examination approach is to identify where a stimulus enters the system before predicting which downstream mechanism is most directly affected.

Limbic and cortical networks contribute to the evaluation of environmental significance and remembered experience. The amygdala is commonly associated with processing salient or threatening information, while hippocampal and prefrontal circuits contribute to contextual evaluation and regulation. These descriptions are functional simplifications, not one-to-one mappings between structures and emotions. Many pathways act through intervening relays; inhibitory connections can produce net excitation when they suppress an inhibitory neuron. Consequently, removing a particular input does not necessarily reduce all stress responses. The direction of change depends on the net circuit effect.

This organization also explains how an apparently similar external event can produce different physiological responses in two animals. One animal may already be energy depleted, whereas another has recently fed. One may have learned that a sound predicts danger, while another has learned that the same sound is harmless. Central appraisal integrates such information with sensory input and existing physiological state. Experimental studies must therefore consider prior exposure, handling, time of day and the animal's baseline condition. Variation between animals can reflect biologically meaningful regulation rather than mere measurement error.

A helpful distinction is between the existence of a stressor and the decision to engage a specific effector. If a disturbance is predominantly osmotic, water-conserving mechanisms may be especially important. If it is a sudden threat requiring movement, rapid cardiac adjustments may dominate. In practice the categories overlap, because an animal may need to maintain arterial pressure, alter ventilation and mobilize fuel at the same time. A model that treats the hypothalamus as an all-or-none switch cannot explain this selective coordination.

How central pathways combine sensory evidence with context

Sensory transduction converts a physical or chemical change into a signal that can be transmitted by neurons. A stretch-sensitive receptor responds to mechanical deformation; a chemoreceptor responds to a chemical environment. Receptor activation changes the membrane properties of the receptor cell or sensory ending, and the resulting activity can be conveyed to the central nervous system. By encoding changes in a physical variable as neural activity, the sensory system makes it possible to combine information from many regions even though the original signals are very different in kind. The stress response does not require one special receptor that detects all threats.

A useful example is the detection of reduced circulating volume. As arterial pressure falls, stretch-sensitive endings can reduce their firing. This decrease is not itself a sympathetic command; it is sensory information. Brainstem networks interpret it within established reflex circuits. Reduced baroreceptor input changes the balance of inhibitory and excitatory influences on autonomic pathways, permitting increased sympathetic outflow and reduced cardiac parasympathetic influence in typical compensatory conditions. The underlying neural logic illustrates how decreasing sensory activity can produce increasing effector activity. Students should avoid assuming that all pathways transmit a signal with the same sign.

Different parts of the same central network may use different neurotransmitters. An excitatory input that increases activity in an inhibitory interneuron can ultimately reduce the activity of a target neuron. Conversely, reducing inhibitory activity can permit the target to become more active, a mechanism called disinhibition. To predict the net effect of a pathway in an examination problem, follow the sign of each synapse. Two inhibitory links in series can produce a positive net effect on the final target, provided the intervening cells are operating within a range in which their activity can change. This elementary rule helps explain why functional statements about a brain region are not sufficient to reconstruct the sign of every anatomical projection.

For example, imagine a hypothetical pathway in which a sensory input excites neuron A, neuron A inhibits neuron B, and neuron B inhibits an endocrine control neuron C. Increasing A tends to reduce B; reduced B removes some inhibition from C. The resulting increase in C is compatible with an inhibitory projection from A to B. If neuron B is already inactive, however, further inhibition of B may have little additional effect. The magnitude of an output therefore depends on the initial activity and dynamic range of each component, not merely on whether its connection is excitatory or inhibitory.

The hypothalamus is positioned to receive information about both external circumstances and the internal state. Inputs related to energy balance, daily rhythms, temperature and visceral conditions may influence its control of endocrine and autonomic output. These inputs do not require every hypothalamic neuron to sense all variables directly. Information can arrive through local and distant circuits. At a functional level the hypothalamus can be regarded as a collection of interconnected control populations rather than a single center whose activation automatically produces the same response in every situation.

An anticipatory response provides a particularly clear demonstration of central integration. Suppose a stimulus initially has no physiological significance, but repeatedly occurs before an energetic challenge. After learning, the stimulus itself may recruit preparatory responses. This change shows that the animal is responding to the predicted consequences of the cue, not only to a direct physical disturbance. The relevant evidence would be a response to the cue alone after training that differs from the response before training or from animals receiving an unpaired cue. A control group is essential because repeated exposure can itself alter responsiveness.

The same approach can distinguish habituation from an inability to respond. If an animal displays a smaller response after repeated presentations of a harmless cue, this may reflect a learned reduction in the cue's significance. To test whether its effector system remains capable of activation, an investigator could compare its response to a different meaningful challenge. A preserved response to the new challenge supports stimulus-specific changes in processing rather than a general failure of the endocrine or autonomic organs. The precise interpretation requires controls for baseline state and timing.

Central appraisal is not a purely psychological process in the human sense. A fish or other animal can integrate external sensory cues, internal physiological signals and prior experience without verbal thought. The word appraisal is used here to describe functional neural processing of significance. The relevant question is how available information changes the probability and magnitude of an effector response. This definition allows neural and endocrine stress regulation to be studied comparatively without attributing unmeasured subjective states to animals.

3. The sympathetic route and adrenal medulla

Autonomic efferent pathways control cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands. Most sympathetic pathways in mammals involve two neurons arranged in series. A preganglionic neuron in the spinal cord releases acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors of a postganglionic neuron in a peripheral ganglion. Many postganglionic sympathetic neurons release norepinephrine onto target cells. Important exceptions exist, including sympathetic cholinergic innervation of many human sweat glands. The response of the target is determined by its receptors and intracellular signaling machinery rather than by the label sympathetic alone.

The adrenal medulla has a different arrangement. Its chromaffin cells receive direct input from sympathetic preganglionic neurons. Acetylcholine released by these neurons activates nicotinic receptors on chromaffin cells, producing depolarization and promoting calcium entry. The increase in intracellular calcium triggers exocytosis of catecholamine-containing secretory granules. Chromaffin cells therefore function as an endocrine extension of the sympathetic system: instead of sending axons to individual peripheral organs, they release catecholamines into the bloodstream.

Epinephrine, also called adrenaline, and norepinephrine, also called noradrenaline, are catecholamines. Norepinephrine released from sympathetic nerve terminals can reach high concentrations in the immediate environment of a target cell. Circulating epinephrine can reach widely distributed organs, including cells that do not receive dense direct sympathetic innervation. The distinction is one of delivery and local concentration, not absolute exclusivity. Both catecholamines can act as circulating mediators and norepinephrine released by nerves can enter the blood. An increase in plasma norepinephrine cannot therefore be assigned solely to the adrenal medulla.

The fast endocrine route is possible because catecholamines are stored in vesicles before stimulation. Exocytosis releases preformed molecules without requiring the synthesis of each molecule at the moment of the challenge. Neural transmission and endocrine transport together nevertheless take time, and different tissues receive different concentrations as blood flow changes. The immediate sympathetic response is not an identical rise in every circulating catecholamine, nor is a circulating hormone required for every sympathetic effect. Local neural release can precede or occur without a large systemic concentration change.

A comparison with adrenal cortical secretion is instructive. Chromaffin-cell catecholamines are stored in granules, whereas glucocorticoid output from adrenal cortical cells depends largely on steroid synthesis in response to stimulation. This difference contributes to the characteristic timing of the two systems. The inner medulla and outer cortex are neighboring but functionally distinct tissues. Their anatomical proximity also permits local interactions: high local glucocorticoid exposure contributes to expression of the enzyme phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase, which converts norepinephrine to epinephrine. This relationship demonstrates that even the apparently rapid catecholamine response depends in part on the longer-term endocrine environment.

When the original neural drive subsides, catecholamine release may decline and the molecules are removed by uptake and metabolism. Termination of a local neural signal and disappearance of a circulating hormone are different processes. Sympathetic nerve terminals can recover norepinephrine through transport mechanisms, whereas circulating mediators are cleared by various tissues. Accordingly, the time required for heart rate or vascular tone to recover is not necessarily the same as the time required for a plasma catecholamine measurement to return to baseline.

Local neurotransmission versus circulating catecholamines

The distinction between a neurotransmitter and a hormone depends in large part on how it reaches its target. A neurotransmitter is released in close association with a neural effector pathway and acts locally across a specialized junction or within a nearby extracellular environment. A hormone is released into circulation and can act on distant cells with appropriate receptors. Norepinephrine illustrates that one chemical substance can participate in both forms of communication. Its identity alone does not tell us whether the physiologically important signal arrived from a local nerve terminal or from systemic blood.

In an autonomically innervated vascular bed, neurotransmitter release can be localized to the smooth muscle surrounding a particular vessel. Changes in sympathetic discharge can therefore alter resistance selectively. Circulating catecholamines, in contrast, expose many organs to a signal at once. The distribution is still not perfectly uniform: blood flow affects the rate at which molecules arrive, and receptor expression and local metabolism affect their actions. A blood concentration measurement cannot fully describe either the local synaptic concentration or the resulting tissue response.

The secretory process in chromaffin cells is a useful connection to the previously studied physiology of chemical synapses. Acetylcholine binding to nicotinic receptors opens ligand-gated ion channels. The resulting change in membrane potential promotes opening of voltage-dependent calcium channels. Calcium entering the cell participates in the machinery that permits secretory vesicles to fuse with the plasma membrane. The release of stored catecholamines is therefore an example of stimulus–secretion coupling. The immediate physical event is an electrical and calcium signal; the result is a chemical signal carried in blood.

A selective disruption of calcium-dependent exocytosis would be expected to impair secretion even if the cell contained a normal amount of catecholamine. Conversely, reduced synthesis might lower the stored quantity without abolishing the electrical response to acetylcholine. To distinguish these mechanisms experimentally, measure intracellular catecholamine content, the membrane or calcium response to stimulation, and the quantity released. A low released quantity by itself is compatible with several defects. This reasoning generalizes to the study of many secretory systems: synthesis, storage, stimulation and release are distinct steps.

Consider another experimental comparison. One preparation contains an innervated target tissue with its sympathetic nerves intact; another receives an equivalent circulating catecholamine concentration but lacks local neural input. If the first preparation produces a stronger response during nerve stimulation, local release may contribute to its behavior. However, the absence of nerves can also alter receptor responsiveness over time. An investigator should control for the sensitivity of the target tissue to an independently applied agonist. This additional comparison helps separate a difference in signal delivery from a difference in the target's ability to respond.

Adrenal chromaffin cells and sympathetic postganglionic neurons share features associated with their developmental origins and chemical communication, but they are not identical adult structures. A postganglionic neuron extends a process toward its targets. A chromaffin cell releases its product into the circulation. The common comparison of the adrenal medulla with a modified sympathetic ganglion is useful because both receive preganglionic cholinergic input. Its limitation is that the endocrine tissue does not simply consist of a set of ordinary postganglionic axons.

A change in catecholamine clearance can alter the duration of the response even when stimulus–secretion coupling remains normal. After a local release event, transporter-mediated uptake can contribute importantly to removal of norepinephrine from the extracellular environment. Enzymatic metabolism also contributes to catecholamine handling. The details vary with tissue and route of exposure. A short plasma half-life does not establish that every downstream physiological effect must end at the same moment, since intracellular signals and mechanical responses can persist after receptor stimulation has declined.

The medulla is also an example of how neural and endocrine mechanisms can interact through anatomical arrangement. Blood arriving from adrenal cortical regions can carry high local glucocorticoid concentrations to medullary tissue. These concentrations support aspects of catecholamine phenotype, including epinephrine synthesis. A temporary interruption of neural activity may reduce secretion without immediately changing the phenotype, while prolonged changes in local endocrine exposure may affect synthetic capacity. This difference is one reason to distinguish short-term secretion from longer-term regulation of the secretory apparatus.

4. Adrenergic receptor specificity and circulation

Catecholamines do not command all target cells to perform the same action. Their effects depend on the adrenergic receptor subtypes expressed in the target and on the pathways coupled to those receptors. Alpha-1 receptors generally signal through Gq-family proteins, promoting phospholipase C activity and calcium-dependent responses. In many vascular smooth muscle cells, increased intracellular calcium promotes contraction and increases vascular resistance. Alpha-2 receptors frequently couple to Gi-family proteins. Presynaptic alpha-2 receptors can reduce additional norepinephrine release, introducing local negative feedback into sympathetic transmission.

Beta-1 and beta-2 receptors commonly signal through Gs-family proteins, which activate adenylyl cyclase and increase cyclic AMP. In cardiac pacemaker and contractile cells, beta-1 stimulation supports faster pacemaker activity, increased contractile performance and other changes in excitation–contraction coupling. In many airway smooth muscle cells, beta-2 stimulation instead favours relaxation. This illustrates why identifying the second messenger alone is insufficient to predict the mechanical response. Cyclic AMP acts on different proteins and regulatory networks in different cell types. Tissue-specific machinery translates similar biochemical signals into distinct physiological effects.

Cardiac output is the product of heart rate and stroke volume. For example, if an animal's heart rate rises from 70 to 100 beats per minute while stroke volume rises from 70 to 80 millilitres per beat, cardiac output rises from 4.9 to 8.0 litres per minute. These values are hypothetical and illustrate the relationship rather than a universal stress response. If stroke volume instead falls because venous return is severely compromised, a higher heart rate may fail to restore output. Always account for both factors before predicting the direction of cardiac output.

A simplified circulation can be described using mean arterial pressure minus central venous pressure as the driving pressure for systemic flow. The relationship is pressure difference = cardiac output × systemic vascular resistance. Suppose output increases from 5 to 7 litres per minute while resistance decreases from 18 to 13 millimetres of mercury minutes per litre. The calculated pressure differences are 90 and 91 millimetres of mercury. Despite a large increase in total flow, the pressure difference changes only slightly. This result emphasizes that cardiac output and vascular resistance need not move in the same direction.

The distribution of flow is just as important as total flow. Local sympathetic vasoconstriction may reduce perfusion of some vascular beds, while metabolic products generated by working skeletal muscles can support local dilation. Circulating epinephrine can also promote beta-2-mediated dilation in suitable vascular beds. The final effect is the result of neural activity, receptor density, ligand concentration and local metabolic conditions. It is therefore incorrect to assert that stress necessarily constricts every blood vessel or necessarily increases flow to every tissue.

The baroreflex can continue to operate during a stress response. Central drive may alter the operating conditions of cardiovascular control, while arterial pressure and afferent feedback still influence the pattern of autonomic activity. The nervous system must combine a demand for greater performance with protection against excessive pressure variation. A strong response in the heart, in isolation, is not sufficient to guarantee appropriate tissue perfusion. Changes in resistance, venous return and tissue extraction all contribute to the actual availability of oxygen.

Receptor occupancy, tissue effects and hemodynamic predictions

A receptor acts as a molecular link between the extracellular signal and the response machinery of a cell. The magnitude of a response depends partly on how many receptors are occupied, but occupancy and effect are not always proportional. A small occupied fraction can produce a large response if downstream amplification is substantial. In contrast, a tissue with limited downstream capacity may reach a maximum effect even while additional receptors become occupied. The relation between ligand concentration and physiological performance therefore cannot be inferred solely from the amount of hormone present in blood.

For introductory quantitative reasoning, a simple equilibrium binding model can be written as fractional receptor occupancy = L/(Kd + L), where L is the free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant, both expressed in the same concentration units. The expression assumes one class of independent binding sites and an equilibrium condition. At a ligand concentration equal to Kd, half of the receptors are occupied. At a concentration three times Kd, occupancy is three quarters. Doubling ligand concentration does not always double occupancy because the available receptor population is finite.

Imagine two receptor populations with illustrative Kd values of 1 and 10 nanomolar for the same ligand. At a free ligand concentration of 1 nanomolar, the respective occupancies are 0.5 and approximately 0.091. At 10 nanomolar, they are approximately 0.909 and 0.5. These numbers are not measured adrenergic receptor affinities. They show why receptor populations with different apparent affinities can respond differently as a circulating mediator increases. In actual tissues, receptor abundance, coupling, competing ligands and local concentration must also be considered.

A further distinction concerns receptor selectivity and tissue selectivity. A ligand may preferentially activate one receptor subtype, but a physiological effect occurs only where the relevant receptor is expressed and coupled to an effector. If a receptor subtype is present in both the heart and another organ, selective activation of that receptor need not be organ-specific. Conversely, neural delivery of a less subtype-selective transmitter can produce a spatially selective effect if it is released principally in one vascular bed. This reasoning explains why receptor pharmacology and autonomic anatomy provide complementary information.

The circulatory consequences of receptor activation can be analysed using a simplified resistance model. In a network of parallel vascular beds, total systemic conductance is the sum of the individual conductances, where conductance is the reciprocal of resistance. For a teaching example, suppose three beds have resistances of 20, 30 and 60 arbitrary pressure-per-flow units. Their conductances sum to 0.05 + 0.0333 + 0.0167 = 0.10, giving an equivalent resistance of 10. If one bed increases its resistance from 20 to 40 while the other two remain unchanged, the conductance becomes 0.075, giving an equivalent resistance of approximately 13.33. The calculation illustrates why constricting one vascular bed influences total resistance without requiring every vessel to constrict.

At fixed perfusion pressure, the flow through a bed is the pressure difference divided by that bed's resistance. If resistance doubles, flow halves in the simplified model. But in an intact animal pressure need not remain constant. Cardiac output, sympathetic regulation and local mechanisms can all change simultaneously. Students should therefore state whether a problem assumes fixed pressure, fixed total flow or a fully regulated circulation. Predicting flow without specifying the operating conditions can produce apparently contradictory answers.

Adrenergic mechanisms also influence venous capacitance. A large portion of blood volume is normally contained in venous vessels, and changes in venous tone can alter the distribution of blood and the pressure driving venous return. The resulting changes in ventricular filling can affect stroke volume. Consequently, sympathetic support of cardiac output is not limited to direct stimulation of cardiac beta-1 receptors. The vascular response can modify the input to the heart. A question concerning cardiac performance may therefore require distinguishing heart contractility from the loading conditions under which contraction occurs.

Finally, there is a limit to the usefulness of strong stimulation. Very rapid heart rates can reduce the time available for ventricular filling, and elevated cardiac work increases energetic demand. Excessive vasoconstriction may compromise perfusion of some tissues even if it helps support systemic pressure. These are general physiological constraints, not fixed numerical thresholds that apply to all animals. A coordinated stress response balances the demands of perfusion and energy supply. Its success should be judged from the integrated result rather than from a maximal change in a single receptor pathway.

5. Ventilation, fuel mobilization and tissue coordination

A rapid increase in locomotor activity creates an immediate demand for ATP. Working muscle increases its use of available substrates, and the circulatory and respiratory systems must support this change. Neural mechanisms can increase ventilation in anticipation of movement and in response to sensory information from active tissues. Beta-2-mediated relaxation of airway smooth muscle can reduce airway resistance. However, ventilation depends on respiratory muscle activity, neural rhythm generation, lung mechanics and gas exchange. A wider airway alone does not demonstrate that oxygen delivery to tissues has increased.

Minute ventilation is the product of tidal volume and breathing frequency. Alveolar ventilation instead depends on the volume of each breath that reaches the gas-exchanging region. For a hypothetical adult with a tidal volume of 500 mL, anatomical dead space of 150 mL and a rate of 12 breaths per minute, alveolar ventilation is 4.2 L/min. Increasing the rate to 24 while reducing tidal volume to 300 mL produces 3.6 L/min of alveolar ventilation, despite an increase in minute ventilation from 6.0 to 7.2 L/min. The example shows why respiratory rate cannot alone quantify the effectiveness of ventilation. Constant dead space is an assumption, and real values vary.

A rapid stress response also changes the supply of metabolic substrates. Hepatic glycogenolysis can release glucose into the circulation because liver cells possess the machinery to generate free glucose from glucose-6-phosphate. Skeletal muscle glycogen, in contrast, mainly serves the muscle itself; skeletal muscle lacks significant glucose-6-phosphatase activity for direct export of free glucose derived from glycogen. Muscle can nevertheless export lactate, which may be used in other tissues or contribute to hepatic glucose synthesis. This separation of local and systemic glycogen functions is an important link between stress physiology and carbohydrate metabolism.

Catecholamines can stimulate lipolysis through adrenergic mechanisms in adipose tissue, making fatty acids available for oxidative metabolism. The relative roles of receptor subtypes differ across tissues and species, and inhibitory adrenergic effects can also occur. Rapid mobilization of fuel is valuable only if it is coordinated with the capacity of tissues to use it. An increase in blood glucose, for example, is not evidence that every tissue has increased glucose uptake. Contracting muscle can increase uptake through contraction-associated mechanisms even when the endocrine environment differs from that of the fed resting state.

The rapid neural response also reallocates activity. Gastrointestinal motility and secretion may be reduced in some contexts, while blood supply and energy are directed toward tissues needed for immediate performance. Such a description is a tendency, not an unqualified rule: stressor-specific autonomic responses can have different effects on regions of the digestive tract. The point is that organ function is adjusted in relation to the current challenge rather than uniformly activated or inhibited.

This coordination has costs. Mobilizing carbohydrate and lipid reserves provides resources now but reduces the reserves available later unless replenishment occurs. Redirecting perfusion may be useful for a brief challenge but problematic if prolonged. These immediate trade-offs explain why a stress response must have effective termination mechanisms. The next endocrine pathway helps sustain and regulate performance without requiring indefinitely intense catecholamine secretion.

Linking fuel supply to oxygen delivery and demand

Metabolic fuel must be available where ATP is being consumed. The amount of circulating glucose is influenced by entry into the blood from organs such as the liver and removal by tissues. In a simple accounting model, the change in the circulating glucose pool equals appearance minus disappearance. If both rates increase equally, the pool may remain nearly constant even though metabolic turnover has increased greatly. A glucose concentration that does not change is therefore compatible with a substantial mobilization response. This is analogous to a stable water level in a container through which water flows rapidly at equal input and output rates.

For a hypothetical example, suppose glucose appearance rises from 2 to 5 arbitrary mass units per minute and disappearance rises from 2 to 4. The net addition is 1 mass unit per minute. If the difference lasts for ten minutes, the simplified pool gains 10 mass units. This calculation cannot be converted to a concentration without specifying the effective distribution volume. It also assumes that the rates remain constant, which is unlikely in a real dynamic response. Its purpose is to show why a concentration measurement is the net consequence of multiple fluxes.

The liver can support systemic glucose availability because it has metabolic machinery that permits free glucose to leave hepatocytes. Glycogen breakdown is a rapid source of glucose equivalents, while gluconeogenesis can support production from substrates such as lactate, glycerol and amino acid-derived carbon skeletons. The stress response does not create energy from nothing: glycogen stores are depleted, substrate availability changes and gluconeogenesis itself requires energy. An animal's prior nutritional condition therefore affects its capacity to sustain a particular pattern of fuel mobilization.

Skeletal muscle stores glycogen for local use. During increased contraction, regulation of glycogen breakdown can supply intermediates to glycolysis. Some resulting pyruvate can be oxidized, while some can be converted to lactate depending on metabolic conditions. Lactate production and exchange are not restricted to complete absence of oxygen. A working tissue may produce lactate while oxygen remains available, and other tissues may oxidize it. The liver can also use lactate carbon to synthesize glucose. This exchange illustrates interorgan cooperation without implying that muscle glycogen directly supplies glucose to the circulation.

Hormonal mobilization cannot substitute for oxygen delivery. Oxygen delivery to the systemic tissues is cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content. In a hypothetical example, output is 5 L/min and arterial oxygen content is 200 mL O₂/L of blood. Delivery is therefore 1000 mL O₂/min. If output rises to 7 L/min but arterial oxygen content falls to 140 mL O₂/L, delivery becomes 980 mL O₂/min. The higher cardiac output has not completely compensated for the lower oxygen content. This simple calculation illustrates why cardiovascular, respiratory and metabolic responses must be evaluated together.

Oxygen consumption also depends on extraction. The Fick principle relates whole-body oxygen consumption to cardiac output multiplied by the difference between arterial and mixed venous oxygen content. If oxygen delivery rises but tissue demand remains unchanged, the fraction extracted may fall. If tissue demand rises, both flow and extraction may change. A measured increase in arterial oxygen delivery is not by itself evidence of an equivalent increase in tissue oxygen consumption. These relationships connect the stress response with circulation and respiratory physiology rather than treating catecholamine actions as isolated organ effects.

The distribution of energy is also important across time. Rapid mobilization can help an animal meet an immediate demand, but sustained use of reserves without replenishment changes its capacity to respond to further challenges. Glucocorticoids participate in maintaining the metabolic environment during longer demands, while insulin and other signals become important in subsequent regulation and replenishment. A full explanation of digestive and metabolic regulation belongs to later subunits. Here the critical principle is that the neural and endocrine responses adjust the availability and use of resources in relation to the challenge.

A useful experimental question is whether an observed rise in circulating fuel reflects increased production, reduced utilization or both. Comparing concentration alone before and after stimulation is insufficient. Information about substrate flux or tissue uptake is required. Isolated tissue preparations can help identify direct responsiveness to a mediator, whereas measurements across an intact animal reveal the integrated result. A student should practice distinguishing these levels of explanation: a receptor-mediated effect in one organ may contribute to, but does not uniquely determine, a systemic change.

6. The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal cascade

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, abbreviated HPA axis, is a hierarchical endocrine pathway. Hypophysiotropic neurons in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus synthesize corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and can release arginine vasopressin (AVP) into the portal circulation at the median eminence. Portal vessels carry these regulatory signals directly to the anterior pituitary, allowing relatively small amounts of hypothalamic peptide to regulate pituitary cells. The anterior pituitary and posterior pituitary must be distinguished: systemic AVP released from posterior pituitary terminals contributes importantly to water balance, whereas AVP in the hypothalamic–pituitary portal system can participate in ACTH regulation.

Corticotrophs in the anterior pituitary respond to CRH primarily through CRH receptor type 1. AVP acts through the V1b receptor and can enhance CRH-driven secretion. Corticotrophs process the precursor proopiomelanocortin to generate adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH is released into systemic blood and acts on melanocortin 2 receptors in the adrenal cortex. Receptor activation promotes signaling that increases steroidogenic activity, including cholesterol delivery to the mitochondrial steroid synthesis machinery. Detailed steroid biosynthesis belongs to the endocrinology subunit; the central issue here is how a neural signal becomes a change in adrenal hormonal output.

The adrenal cortex produces different steroid hormones in different zones. In a typical mammalian adrenal, the zona fasciculata is a major site of glucocorticoid synthesis. Aldosterone secretion from the zona glomerulosa is governed principally by angiotensin II and extracellular potassium, although ACTH can influence it transiently. An ACTH rise should not be interpreted as the principal physiological command for every adrenal steroid. This distinction becomes especially important when an exam question gives both abnormal pressure regulation and altered glucocorticoid concentrations.

The dominant glucocorticoid varies among vertebrates. Cortisol is the principal circulating glucocorticoid in humans, while corticosterone is the main glucocorticoid in commonly studied laboratory rats and mice and many birds. The names identify related steroid hormones with overlapping functional roles; they are not interchangeable measurements. An experiment that assays cortisol in a species whose dominant glucocorticoid is corticosterone may produce a misleading account of its stress response.

Glucocorticoid output requires time because activation of upstream neurons, secretion of pituitary ACTH, circulation to the adrenal cortex and steroid synthesis all contribute to the response. These processes overlap. An ACTH increase can begin quickly, and glucocorticoid effects are not exclusively delayed for hours. The distinction between the rapid sympathetic route and the HPA route describes their characteristic dynamics, not rigid nonoverlapping windows. Simultaneous activation of both systems is compatible with a delayed peak of one output.

An important consequence of the hierarchical design is that the same circulating hormone concentration can result from different changes. Increased hypothalamic drive may increase ACTH and glucocorticoids, but altered adrenal sensitivity can produce a different glucocorticoid response to the same ACTH input. Decreased clearance may increase circulating concentration without an increase in secretion. To identify the level of a perturbation, it is useful to measure at least two levels of the axis and observe their temporal relationship.

Why a three-level endocrine cascade is useful

A three-level cascade provides opportunities for integration that a direct neural command does not provide in the same way. At the hypothalamic level, inputs about the environment, internal state and biological timing can modify release of regulatory peptides. At the pituitary level, responsiveness to those peptides and inhibition by circulating glucocorticoids can modify the ACTH signal. At the adrenal level, the ability to synthesize steroid in response to ACTH can be regulated. The final output is therefore not simply a scaled copy of hypothalamic neuronal firing. A change at one level may produce a large response in another, and the gain of each stage can vary.

The hypothalamic portal circulation is important because it preserves the functional connection between two anatomically close but distinct parts of the brain–endocrine interface. A small quantity of peptide released into a general systemic circulation would become diluted throughout the blood volume and might be degraded before reaching its target. Delivery through portal vessels exposes anterior pituitary cells to hypothalamic signals more directly. An interruption of portal communication could impair several pituitary regulatory systems, whereas a selective loss of the receptor for one peptide may leave other inputs intact. This distinction is essential when interpreting a perturbation of endocrine anatomy.

CRH and AVP do not need to produce identical intracellular signals to cooperate. CRH receptor type 1 commonly engages cyclic AMP-dependent signaling in corticotrophs; AVP V1b signaling commonly involves phospholipase C and calcium-related pathways. Convergence on the cellular processes supporting secretion can enhance the output relative to either input alone. One way to examine the interaction is to compare responses to CRH alone, AVP alone and both together in an experimental preparation. If the joint response exceeds the sum of their increments above baseline, this supports a synergistic interaction under the tested conditions. The definition depends on the measurement scale and experimental context.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose an isolated pituitary preparation releases 2 units of ACTH per interval without added peptide. CRH alone produces 7 units, AVP alone produces 4, and their combination produces 13. The separate increments above baseline are 5 and 2, giving an additive prediction of 9 total units. The combined result exceeds that prediction by 4 units. It would be incorrect to compare the combined result directly with 7 + 4 without correcting for the duplicated baseline. Experimental interpretation requires a clear definition of the null model. At high concentrations, saturation of secretion may reduce the apparent additional effect of the second signal even if interaction exists at lower concentrations.

ACTH delivery to the adrenal cortex also demonstrates the difference between stimulation and capacity. A cell must possess the enzymes and substrates necessary for steroid production before stimulation can produce a full response. ACTH can rapidly promote the availability of cholesterol for steroidogenesis, while longer exposure can also influence the expression of relevant proteins. Therefore an adrenal gland exposed to altered ACTH patterns for an extended period may not respond in exactly the same way as one receiving an isolated ACTH pulse. The prior history of stimulation changes the meaning of a subsequent standardized test.

Steroid hormones and peptide hormones differ in their routes of production and release. ACTH is produced by processing a larger peptide precursor and released through regulated secretion. Cortisol is synthesized from a cholesterol-derived pathway and diffuses out of steroidogenic cells as it is produced. The absence of a large stored pool of ready-to-release glucocorticoid contributes to the dependence of output on steroidogenic activity. Yet adrenal cells already contain relevant enzymes, so an acute response does not require that all of those enzymes be newly produced. Confusing synthesis with new gene expression would lead to an exaggerated prediction of the delay in steroid release.

A hierarchical axis can also be investigated with a simple logical substitution. If endogenous CRH input is absent but an experimental CRH signal can stimulate ACTH, then the anterior pituitary retains at least some responsiveness to CRH. If an ACTH signal can stimulate glucocorticoid production despite poor responses to CRH, adrenal responsiveness is retained under the tested conditions. These observations narrow the possible site of dysfunction, but they are not absolute localizations: altered receptor sensitivity, feedback, delivery or hormone clearance can complicate the patterns. A student should treat such experiments as evidence for a mechanism rather than as proof that every other component functions normally.

Finally, the endocrine cascade is not a universal sequence for every hormonal adjustment during stress. AVP released into systemic blood, changes in insulin or glucagon, and the renin–angiotensin system can participate in challenge-specific adjustments. Their relation to the HPA pathway is context dependent. ACTH is not the controlling hormone for all of these responses. The advantage of learning the axis in its anatomically correct sequence is that it becomes possible to identify which signal belongs to the cascade, which participates in parallel, and which acts downstream as a consequence of the physiological change.

7. Glucocorticoid action, permissiveness and time scales

Glucocorticoids are lipid-soluble steroid hormones that can enter cells and interact with intracellular receptors. The glucocorticoid receptor is a ligand-regulated transcriptional regulator whose activity depends on cellular context. Binding of hormone changes receptor conformation and enables regulation of target genes through interactions with DNA and other transcriptional regulators. The resulting changes in RNA and protein can modify tissue function over relatively long time scales. Different tissues express different sets of regulatory proteins and genes; the same hormone can therefore cause different or even contrasting changes in different cell types.

The mineralocorticoid receptor also binds physiological glucocorticoids with high affinity in several tissues. In some epithelia, local conversion of cortisol to cortisone by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 protects mineralocorticoid receptor signaling from inappropriate glucocorticoid activation. In neural contexts, receptor occupancy and functional effects depend on the hormone concentration, receptor expression and local metabolism. At examination level the major point is that a receptor's name does not always uniquely identify every physiological ligand and that local hormone metabolism changes exposure at the target.

A permissive effect occurs when a hormone enables or sustains the full action of another signal rather than being its immediate trigger. Glucocorticoids contribute to vascular responsiveness to catecholamines. This means that the presence of adequate glucocorticoid action can be important for a normal response to sympathetic stimulation, even if the catecholamine is the more immediate signal for contraction. A permissive effect differs from simple addition: removing one background input can reduce the effectiveness of another despite an unchanged concentration of the latter.

Glucocorticoids also support hepatic glucose production during challenges that require continued substrate availability. Their longer-term actions affect enzymes, substrate mobilization and insulin responsiveness. The consequences depend on nutritional state and the duration of exposure. During a brief challenge, sustaining glucose availability can be adaptive. Prolonged or inappropriate exposure can have different metabolic consequences, which are considered in the later chapter on allostatic load. It is inappropriate to assume that every increase in glucocorticoid is harmful or that every decrease is beneficial.

Not all steroid-mediated responses require new transcription and translation. Rapid glucocorticoid effects, including some forms of feedback on neural and pituitary function, can occur on time scales shorter than expected for changes in protein abundance. These are often described as non-genomic effects. The term describes a mechanism not requiring the conventional transcriptional route for that immediate action; it does not mean that the steroid can never regulate genes in the same cell. A timing observation can motivate a mechanistic hypothesis but is not by itself definitive proof of a non-genomic mechanism.

Blood measurements are also affected by binding proteins. A substantial fraction of cortisol in plasma is bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin or albumin; free hormone is more immediately available for diffusion into many target tissues. Total hormone concentration includes bound and free fractions. For instance, if total cortisol is 300 arbitrary concentration units and the free fraction is 5%, the free concentration is 15 units. If total concentration remains 300 but the free fraction doubles to 10%, free concentration becomes 30 units. This hypothetical calculation does not assume that binding fractions are normally fixed; it illustrates why total concentration is not a complete description of tissue exposure.

Hormone availability, response duration and tissue-specific action

To understand why glucocorticoids can alter several organs at once without producing identical responses, distinguish exposure, receptor activation and cellular outcome. Exposure depends on circulating hormone, binding proteins, blood flow and local metabolism. Receptor activation depends on free ligand availability and on the receptor population in the target cell. The outcome depends on the genes and intracellular proteins that the activated receptor can influence. These levels are connected, but an experiment measuring one does not establish the state of the others.

A binding protein can act as a circulating reservoir. When free hormone leaves the blood or is metabolized, some bound hormone can dissociate. This dynamic equilibrium helps explain why binding affects distribution and kinetics as well as the immediately free fraction. Under a simplified one-binding-site model, the bound amount is proportional to the available binding-site concentration and the free hormone concentration at low occupancy. As the available sites become occupied, the relation becomes nonlinear. An increase in total hormone may then yield a disproportionately large increase in the free fraction. The real plasma environment also includes albumin binding, tissue uptake and metabolism, so this simple model is only a starting point.

Consider a hypothetical comparison between two animals with the same total glucocorticoid concentration, but different binding-protein concentrations. If the first animal has more available binding sites with the same binding affinity, the free hormone concentration may be lower than in the second. However, feedback may subsequently adjust hormone secretion to compensate for altered availability. An isolated measurement during this adjustment need not reflect the eventual steady state. This is why the interpretation of binding changes must specify both the time of measurement and which quantities are held constant.

The genomic route allows a relatively small steroid signal to alter the capacity of a cell to perform a function. A change in expression of a metabolic enzyme can affect the rate of a reaction long after the initial receptor activation. For example, a sustained signal can support hepatic glucose production by changing the expression or regulation of metabolic components. But a change in enzyme abundance is not sufficient evidence of a proportional change in metabolic flux. Substrate availability, enzyme activity, other regulatory pathways and energetic conditions also determine flux. The appropriate biological conclusion is therefore more limited than the simple statement that every induced gene produces a matching increase in its pathway.

The permissive role of glucocorticoids can be investigated by a factorial experiment. Suppose isolated tissues are studied under four hypothetical conditions: neither mediator, glucocorticoid exposure alone, catecholamine stimulation alone, and glucocorticoid exposure followed by catecholamine stimulation. If glucocorticoid exposure has little immediate effect on tension but substantially increases the tension evoked by catecholamine, the result supports a permissive or sensitizing interaction under those conditions. To determine whether this is due to altered receptor abundance, coupling or downstream machinery, additional measurements are needed. The organ-level observation establishes an interaction but does not identify every molecular step.

A distinction between genomic and rapid responses can similarly be investigated by measuring both time and dependence on gene expression. If a cellular change occurs within seconds or minutes, it may be unlikely to require synthesis of an entirely new protein population. This observation motivates a hypothesis of a rapid signaling mechanism. A more convincing test would examine whether the response persists when the specific required transcriptional or translational process is disrupted, while also checking that the perturbation has not broadly impaired cell function. Inhibition of protein synthesis, for example, can itself change cellular physiology. An experiment must therefore control for effects of the intervention that are unrelated to the proposed pathway.

A common conceptual error is to identify a receptor only with one cellular location. The conventional glucocorticoid receptor is an intracellular ligand-regulated transcriptional regulator, but rapid glucocorticoid effects can involve other modes of signaling. It is unnecessary at NET level to catalogue every proposed molecular variant. What matters is the experimentally supported distinction between changes that require new gene expression and changes occurring through more immediate regulation. A rapid effect is not incompatible with later genomic effects in the same tissue.

The duration of tissue response can differ from the duration of receptor occupancy. A protein produced during a period of hormone exposure may remain after hormone concentrations fall. Conversely, a cell may become less responsive during sustained exposure through changes in receptor signaling or other regulatory components. An experiment that measures glucocorticoids only when tissue performance is measured may therefore miss the hormone exposure that contributed to the response. The history of signaling must be considered. This principle also prepares the later study of repeated challenges, where prior exposure can change how tissues respond even after circulating concentrations appear to have recovered.

Glucocorticoid actions must be understood in relation to physiological context, not as a fixed list of desirable or undesirable effects. A shift toward maintaining glucose availability is useful in a short energetic challenge. The same shift over a very different duration or in the absence of increased demand can have other consequences. The focus here is the mechanism through which one mediator modifies many targets and supports coordinated responses. The later allostatic-load node examines the costs of sustained recruitment in greater detail.

8. Feedback, pulses and the daily rhythm

A simple endocrine cascade with three stimulatory links would risk sustained elevation of its output unless some mechanism limited continued activation. Glucocorticoids provide feedback at multiple levels of the HPA axis. They influence pituitary corticotrophs and hypothalamic neurons, as well as circuits that regulate those neurons. The result is generally a restraint on further ACTH and glucocorticoid secretion. Negative feedback can change the amplitude and duration of an endocrine response while allowing a substantial initial response to an important stimulus.

Consider a perturbation that selectively reduces adrenal glucocorticoid synthesis while leaving hypothalamic and pituitary function intact. Initially the output falls. The loss of feedback tends to increase the upstream drive and ACTH release. If the adrenal impairment persists, increasing ACTH may fail to restore normal glucocorticoid concentrations. Conversely, a sufficiently effective exogenous glucocorticoid signal can reduce endogenous ACTH through feedback. These predictions concern isolated perturbations under specified assumptions; simultaneous changes in multiple organs may yield more complicated patterns.

Feedback does not require that the cortisol concentration be constant. The HPA axis exhibits pulses even in the absence of an acute challenge. Baseline secretion also changes across the day, in part under the influence of circadian timing systems. The daily pattern is related to the species' activity phase: humans normally show relatively high cortisol near the beginning of the active day, whereas nocturnal rodents typically have a different clock-time profile. Comparing samples taken at the same civil clock time does not automatically match the physiological phase of different species.

Pulses and daily rhythms are distinct phenomena. The circadian rhythm describes changes over approximately twenty-four hours. Ultradian pulses occur on shorter time scales. A transient increase measured during a pulse might be interpreted incorrectly as a response to an experimental stressor unless the investigator has a suitable baseline and a control group. A single concentration is an observation of a dynamical system at one instant, not an estimate of its complete output over the day.

A simple model helps explain why concentration continues to change after upstream stimulation has ceased. Let C denote concentration, S the rate of input expressed as concentration per unit time, and k a first-order clearance constant with units of inverse time. Then dC/dt = S − kC. If input becomes zero, the remaining concentration declines as C(t) = C(0)e^(−kt). If k is 0.035 per minute, the half-life in the simplified model is ln(2)/k, approximately 19.8 minutes. This is an illustrative parameter, not a measured universal glucocorticoid half-life. Real secretion, distribution and binding may depart substantially from the model.

At a steady state with constant input, dC/dt equals zero and C = S/k. Doubling secretion at fixed clearance doubles the steady concentration. Halving clearance at fixed secretion has the same mathematical effect. A high steady concentration alone cannot separate these mechanisms. A time course after a controlled perturbation, together with upstream hormone measurements, can supply stronger evidence about why the concentration changed.

Interpreting feedback experiments and changing hormone profiles

The simplest graphical representation of the HPA axis contains stimulatory arrows from hypothalamus to pituitary to adrenal cortex, with inhibitory arrows from the glucocorticoid output to upstream regulation. This diagram captures the net sign of feedback, but its simplicity conceals several distinctions. Feedback can act on pituitary responsiveness, hypothalamic neuronal activity and the activity of intervening neural circuits. Some feedback occurs rapidly; some involves slower changes in gene expression or regulatory state. The output does not have to wait until all possible feedback mechanisms are active before it begins to decline.

Imagine that a short challenge increases hypothalamic drive. ACTH begins to rise and the adrenal responds by increasing glucocorticoid secretion. As glucocorticoid exposure increases, feedback can reduce upstream secretion. The ACTH peak may therefore occur before the glucocorticoid peak. When ACTH begins declining, the adrenal may still be receiving enough stimulation for its secretion to exceed clearance. Under these conditions circulating glucocorticoid concentration continues increasing. The peak in concentration occurs when the net rate of change becomes zero, not necessarily when the stimulating hormone reaches its maximum.

This distinction can be formalized using the input–clearance model introduced earlier. When S is greater than kC, concentration rises. When S equals kC, concentration is momentarily stationary. When S is less than kC, concentration falls. It is possible for S to be decreasing while still exceeding kC. Thus a falling secretion rate does not imply an immediately falling concentration. Conversely, a concentration that has returned to its original value does not prove the original secretion rate has returned; compensating changes in clearance can produce the same concentration.

For a numerical illustration, assume that an arbitrary glucocorticoid concentration is 40 units and the clearance constant is 0.05 per minute. At that instant clearance removes 2 concentration units per minute. If secretion contributes 3 units per minute, the concentration rises at 1 unit per minute. Later, if concentration has reached 60 and secretion is still 3, the net change is zero. If secretion then declines to 1 while concentration is 60, the instantaneous rate is minus 2 units per minute. These hypothetical values illustrate the sign of a derivative, not a measured endocrine response. A complete prediction over time would require updating concentration and secretion continuously.

Negative feedback changes the interpretation of upstream hormone concentrations in a particularly important way. A low downstream hormone concentration can increase upstream stimulation. Therefore high ACTH does not automatically imply an overactive adrenal cortex. If the adrenal cannot produce sufficient glucocorticoid, ACTH may be high precisely because the downstream output is low. Likewise, a reduced ACTH concentration can reflect strong inhibitory feedback rather than a primary inability of the pituitary to secrete. To distinguish these possibilities, examine both signals in relation to their expected baseline and timing.

A hypothetical feedback experiment compares the hormone response of two groups after the same brief challenge. Both groups show similar initial ACTH and glucocorticoid rises, but one group exhibits a much slower decline in ACTH while glucocorticoid remains elevated. This is consistent with impaired feedback sensitivity, sustained excitatory drive, or a combination of the two. An additional manipulation that changes glucocorticoid exposure independently of the original stressor can help distinguish them. If ACTH suppression in response to that signal is reduced in the affected group, the evidence for altered feedback strengthens. It is still important to examine hormone exposure and possible differences in drug delivery or clearance before making a receptor-specific conclusion.

Daily timing provides an alternative explanation for apparent differences in response. Consider two groups with identical acute stress responses measured as increments above their own baselines, but one has a higher baseline because it was sampled during the active phase. Their absolute peaks differ even though their challenge-induced increments are equal. Reporting both baseline and peak helps readers distinguish these interpretations. The area under a concentration–time curve can capture duration and magnitude, but its meaning depends on whether baseline is subtracted. Absolute area reflects overall exposure across the interval, whereas baseline-adjusted area emphasizes the additional response.

Pulsatile secretion means that the average of multiple samples may provide different information from one sample. Suppose samples are taken at times when one animal happens to be at a pulse peak and another at a trough. The resulting difference could be mistaken for a stable difference between animals. Repeated sampling over an appropriate interval, or a design that balances sampling phase across groups, reduces this risk. Sampling must itself be arranged to minimize disturbance. Otherwise the procedure intended to measure the response becomes part of the challenge.

One final complication is that feedback can interact with ongoing prediction and environmental input. If the original stressor persists, upstream neural drive may continue even as inhibitory hormonal feedback increases. The resulting concentration is an outcome of the balance between those influences. Removing the environmental stimulus may reduce the drive and allow feedback and clearance to dominate. Thus recovery from an acute challenge cannot be described solely as hormone degradation. It also involves withdrawal of stimulation, changes in feedback and restoration of the physiological state that initially required compensation.

9. Experimental evidence and causal interpretation

The study of stress physiology progressed from observations of rapid autonomic changes to experiments that identified the distinct endocrine components. Classical work on the sympathoadrenal response emphasized the ability of an organism to alter circulation and metabolism during a challenge. Later endocrine studies established that pituitary activity regulates the adrenal cortex and that adrenal steroid output feeds back onto upstream control. Modern measurements distinguish the temporal behavior of specific mediators rather than assigning every response to one generalized stress substance. The historical contribution of a model is not proof that it describes all species or challenges equally well.

A useful experimental strategy is to perturb one component while observing multiple downstream outputs. Suppose an experimental model has a selective reduction in CRH receptor signaling in pituitary corticotrophs. The prediction is that the ACTH response attributable to CRH will be reduced. However, AVP signaling and other inputs may preserve part of the response. A reduced ACTH peak accompanied by a reduced glucocorticoid peak is consistent with impaired pituitary stimulation, whereas an unchanged ACTH peak accompanied by reduced glucocorticoid would direct attention to adrenal responsiveness or clearance. Neither pattern alone proves that the perturbation has no other effects.

A separate experiment could compare the response to exogenous ACTH under carefully controlled conditions. This provides a signal downstream of hypothalamic and pituitary release. If the adrenal glucocorticoid response is retained despite a weak response to a natural stressor, the observation supports intact adrenal responsiveness under the test conditions. It does not establish that every upstream pathway is normal. If the glucocorticoid response remains weak, adrenal function or hormone handling deserves further examination. Appropriate controls include comparable baseline values, sampling time, dose conditions and a matched vehicle group.

Time-course measurements give a different kind of evidence. Imagine that a short standardized stimulus produces an immediate heart-rate increase, a later ACTH increase and a still later glucocorticoid peak. This order is consistent with the characteristic kinetics of the autonomic and endocrine pathways. Temporal precedence alone does not establish that the early heart-rate response caused the later hormone response: both may have been driven by a common neural input. An intervention that separates the outputs provides stronger causal evidence than their sequence alone.

An important methodological problem is that obtaining the sample can itself alter the system being studied. Handling, restraint, capture and unfamiliar environments may evoke autonomic and HPA responses. A sample collected after handling cannot be automatically treated as the undisturbed baseline. Comparisons should account for the method and interval between initial disturbance and sampling. Repeated collection may itself constitute repeated exposure to a stressor, changing the subsequent response.

Finally, an experimental result should be interpreted at the scale at which it was obtained. A cultured adrenal cell responding to ACTH establishes direct responsiveness under the culture conditions but does not establish the full response of an intact organism. An intact animal includes circulation, neural feedback, binding proteins, clearance and tissue interactions. A receptor-specific perturbation in isolated cells may identify a molecular mechanism, whereas whole-animal experiments are required to examine integrated consequences. The strength of physiological reasoning lies in connecting these levels without claiming that one experimental design answers every question.

Building a defensible experiment from response patterns

Experimental reasoning begins with specifying a hypothesis that could be contradicted by an observation. The claim that a stress response involves both neural and endocrine signals is broad. A more testable hypothesis is that the early cardiac response to a particular challenge requires beta-adrenergic signaling, whereas the later glucocorticoid response does not require the cardiac effect itself. The prediction is that selectively altering the cardiac receptor pathway could reduce the early cardiac response without necessarily abolishing the later endocrine response. Testing this prediction requires measurements of both outputs and evidence that the intervention acts at the intended site.

A suitable comparison would distinguish an experimental intervention from its accompanying conditions. If a receptor antagonist is administered, a matched comparison group should experience the same handling and vehicle exposure without the active agent. A baseline measurement can reveal whether the intervention alters resting organ function. A post-challenge measurement can then reveal how responsiveness changes. If resting heart rate already differs substantially, comparing only final heart rates may obscure the distinction between baseline and challenge-induced changes. Both measures should be reported in an illustrative data interpretation.

Suppose a hypothetical control group has a baseline heart rate of 80 beats per minute and reaches 120 after a standardized challenge. A group with altered beta-adrenergic signaling begins at 60 and reaches 75. The increments are 40 and 15, respectively, whereas the final rates differ by 45. This observation supports reduced cardiac recruitment in the second group, but it does not prove that the original challenge generated identical sympathetic neural activity in both groups. Altered feedback from the circulation could modify upstream drive. Measuring sympathetic activity or using a preparation in which the delivered agonist concentration is controlled could help address this uncertainty.

Now suppose that ACTH in the two groups rises by the same amount at the sampled times. This finding would be consistent with a preserved HPA response despite reduced cardiac recruitment. It would not prove the complete independence of the systems, because other sympathetic effects might remain intact and the intervention might not alter neural activity. The defensible conclusion is that the measured ACTH increase does not require the full measured cardiac response under these conditions. A carefully limited interpretation is stronger scientifically than an absolute conclusion unsupported by the experimental design.

An adrenal responsiveness experiment provides a complementary example. The investigator might compare a standardized ACTH challenge with a more complex stressor, measuring glucocorticoids before and after each. If the adrenal responds normally to ACTH but the natural stressor produces weak ACTH and weak glucocorticoid responses, an upstream explanation becomes more plausible. If endogenous ACTH rises normally but both the natural and standardized ACTH challenges produce weak steroid output, an adrenal explanation becomes more plausible. Different pharmacokinetic conditions and hormone binding can affect both tests, so the result should be described as supporting rather than uniquely proving one site of alteration.

The logic of controlled comparison is particularly important when interpreting an observational association. If animals with greater circulating glucocorticoid concentrations show greater glucose availability, several explanations remain possible. The hormone could contribute to glucose production; a shared upstream challenge could stimulate both signals; or glucose-related conditions could influence the endocrine response. Manipulating glucocorticoid signaling while measuring substrate flux under matched conditions provides stronger evidence of a causal role. Even then, one should consider indirect effects through other organs rather than assuming that all glucose changes arise from a direct liver action.

A physiological experiment also needs a clearly defined endpoint. A peak measurement addresses maximum observed concentration; a recovery-time measurement addresses the duration of response; a baseline-adjusted integral addresses cumulative additional exposure. Two responses can have the same peak but different durations, or equal integrated exposure despite different peaks. The choice of endpoint should follow the biological question. A study of rapid cardiovascular recruitment might prioritize the early change, whereas a study of prolonged exposure might require information over a longer interval. These are not interchangeable descriptions of stress intensity.

For example, consider hypothetical glucocorticoid increments above baseline measured at 0, 10, 20 and 30 minutes: 0, 20, 40 and 20 units. Using trapezoids over ten-minute intervals, the estimated incremental area is 100 + 300 + 300 = 700 concentration-unit minutes. A second profile of 0, 40, 20 and 20 gives 200 + 300 + 200 = 700. The areas are identical, but the timing of the peaks differs. The trapezoidal calculation assumes linear interpolation between samples; it cannot recover unobserved rapid fluctuations. This example shows why a summary statistic is useful but does not replace the time course.

Finally, confidence in a physiological explanation increases when distinct lines of evidence converge. An anatomical observation identifies a plausible route, receptor expression identifies a possible target, stimulation establishes responsiveness, and selective perturbation can test necessity. Each line of evidence has limitations. A strong examination answer recognizes which conclusion is justified by the particular observations rather than automatically claiming that an entire pathway has been proven. This approach is especially valuable in Part C questions, which often require choosing the explanation most consistent with multiple observations and explicitly stated assumptions.

10. Comparative physiology: teleosts and invertebrates

The requirement to coordinate responses to a challenge is not restricted to mammals. Other animals encounter changes in oxygen, salinity, temperature, food supply and predation risk, but they do not all possess mammalian adrenal anatomy. Comparative physiology asks whether the functional problem is similar, whether the control structures are evolutionarily related and whether particular mediators have corresponding roles. Functional similarity does not necessarily establish anatomical identity or a shared evolutionary origin for every component.

In many teleost fishes, the endocrine pathway is described as the hypothalamic–pituitary–interrenal (HPI) axis. Hypothalamic signals stimulate pituitary ACTH release, which in turn stimulates steroid output from interrenal tissue. Cortisol is a major glucocorticoid in many teleosts and participates in both stress-related responses and osmoregulatory physiology. Chromaffin tissue contributes to catecholamine release through sympathetic mechanisms. The two functional routes are comparable to mammalian sympathoadrenal and HPA responses, even though the tissue arrangement differs.

The anatomical location of interrenal and chromaffin tissue is often associated with the head kidney in teleosts. A question that asks where a teleost's stress glucocorticoid is produced should not be answered by describing the mammalian adrenal zona fasciculata. The appropriate functional term is interrenal tissue. Likewise, although cortisol is widespread in teleost stress physiology, its concentration and dynamics vary with species, life stage, salinity and temperature. Measurements need a species-appropriate context.

Environmental challenges may engage several physiological systems at once. A fish transferred to a different salinity faces changes in water and ion flux. Cortisol can participate in osmoregulatory adjustments as well as metabolic regulation. An increase in cortisol in this context cannot be interpreted solely as a generalized measure of distress, since a hormone's actions may have direct relevance to the challenge. To distinguish these roles, researchers can combine hormonal measurements with ion balance, gill function and performance data. The same logic applies when oxygen availability or food intake changes.

In many insects, octopamine acts as a biogenic amine with important neural and physiological functions. Adipokinetic hormones can mobilize energy stores from the fat body, with effects that depend on species and metabolic context. Together these mediators can contribute to responses to predation or energetic demand. Their functional resemblance to vertebrate rapid mobilization pathways does not imply that insects have a hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal cortex. Nor does it justify calling octopamine insect cortisol. Different signaling molecules and organ arrangements solve related physiological problems.

Comparing groups therefore requires a distinction between conserved functions and lineage-specific implementations. Vertebrate endocrine axes share many regulatory principles, but the dominant hormones and arrangement of endocrine tissues may differ. Invertebrate responses demonstrate that fast neural communication and longer-range metabolic regulation can arise in very different anatomical settings. A student who understands this distinction can answer a comparative question by tracing the functional chain from detection to effectors rather than memorizing a mammalian diagram as a universal template.

Comparative reasoning across animal lineages and environments

Comparative studies help separate features that are required for effective regulation from features specific to a lineage. A mammalian stress diagram contains a hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal cortex, adrenal medulla and peripheral target organs. Its functional logic includes sensory detection, integration, a rapid signaling route, a more sustained endocrine route and mechanisms limiting the response. When comparing a fish or insect with a mammal, the question is not whether every named anatomical structure can be identified. It is which mechanisms perform corresponding functions and whether the evidence supports a shared evolutionary relationship.

The teleost HPI system illustrates this distinction well. A pituitary ACTH signal can stimulate steroid output from interrenal cells. The interrenal tissue is functionally comparable to the glucocorticoid-producing component of the mammalian adrenal cortex, but it should not be described as an identically layered gland. The chromaffin tissue provides another endocrine component linked to rapid catecholamine signaling. Their location and arrangement vary across vertebrate groups. The common functional comparison is therefore more informative than forcing every comparative structure into a mammalian anatomical diagram.

The environmental context of an aquatic animal changes the demands placed on its physiological systems. Oxygen availability in water, the temperature of the environment and the salinity gradient between body fluids and the surrounding water can vary substantially. A stressor that changes ion balance may evoke hormonal responses that directly contribute to osmoregulatory adjustment. Consequently, a steroid measurement should be interpreted with reference to the environmental challenge. If cortisol increases after transfer to a new salinity, the relevant question is not merely whether the fish was stressed, but whether the change is associated with effective ion regulation, altered energy use or impaired physiological performance.

Imagine a hypothetical experiment in which two teleost populations are exposed to a standardized salinity change. One shows a large cortisol increase and maintains plasma ion concentration within a narrow range. The other shows a smaller cortisol response and a larger change in plasma ions. These observations suggest differences in integrated compensation, but they do not show that increasing cortisol would necessarily correct the second population's response. Gill transport capacity, hormone receptor sensitivity and prior acclimation could differ. A comparison of endocrine output, target response and regulated variables is required, just as in the mammalian examples.

Temperature can also affect the interpretation of temporal measurements in ectothermic animals. Reaction rates, circulation, metabolism and clearance may depend on temperature, and different species or acclimation histories can modify these relationships. A cortisol peak measured at a particular interval in one species should not be imposed as a universal timing standard for all fish. Comparative experiments should use justified sampling windows and record environmental conditions. A later peak can reflect differences in kinetics, not necessarily a stronger or weaker central response.

Birds and mammals provide another useful comparison. Many birds and standard laboratory rodents use corticosterone as the principal circulating glucocorticoid, while humans predominantly use cortisol. The identity of the dominant hormone matters when selecting a measurement. An assay that responds differently to closely related steroids can produce an apparent difference between species that reflects the analytical method rather than physiological activity. In an examination problem, first identify the expected principal hormone, then evaluate the response relative to the species' usual endocrine conditions. This is a straightforward but important example of matching a measurement to the biology being studied.

In insects, the fat body performs several metabolic and storage functions, including regulation of available fuels. Adipokinetic hormone signaling can promote mobilization of stored substrates during energy-demanding activities, while octopamine can modulate neural and physiological performance. An insect that needs rapid flight or escape faces a problem of coordinating energy supply and motor activity. These functional requirements resemble those faced by vertebrates, but the organs, mediators and pathways cannot simply be renamed as mammalian adrenal components. The comparison is useful because it shows that common physiological requirements can be satisfied through different biological implementations.

Suppose an experiment in an insect shows that a challenge increases an octopamine-related signal and a measure of fuel mobilization. If an intervention reducing the relevant signaling also reduces mobilization, the observation supports a role for the pathway under those conditions. It does not establish that the pathway is the sole insect stress response or that every invertebrate uses it in the same way. Invertebrates are a highly diverse collection of lineages; an observation in one insect should not be generalized to all molluscs, annelids or arthropods. Comparative physiology requires evidence at the relevant taxonomic scale.

A useful synthesis is to classify each mechanism by its role in the response. Sensory systems detect a challenge, integration networks determine the pattern of effector recruitment, chemical mediators communicate with targets, and target physiology produces the outcome. Termination mechanisms limit continued recruitment or restore previous function. This classification permits meaningful comparisons without assuming anatomical identity. It also keeps the current chapter distinct from the following node: whole-animal neuroendocrine coordination is one level of protection, while individual cells possess mechanisms that respond to oxidative, protein-folding and energetic disturbances even when the organism-level signals differ. Understanding both levels is necessary to explain adaptation fully.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Stress and stressor are synonyms

A stressor is a challenge; stress responses are the organism’s changes.

Every stressor activates identical responses

Input, appraisal and physiological context shape different outputs.

The adrenal medulla is a postganglionic nerve

Chromaffin cells are endocrine secretory cells receiving preganglionic input.

Sympathetic neurons always secrete norepinephrine

Preganglionic neurons release ACh; some postganglionic pathways are cholinergic.

All catecholamines constrict vessels

Receptor subtype and local signals determine vascular response.

Higher heart rate guarantees higher cardiac output

Stroke volume may change in the opposite direction.

Respiratory frequency equals alveolar ventilation

Tidal volume and dead space also matter.

Muscle glycogen directly supplies blood glucose

Muscle uses glycogen locally; liver can release free glucose.

ACTH originates in the hypothalamus

Anterior pituitary corticotrophs secrete ACTH.

AVP has only a water-balance role

Portal AVP can potentiate ACTH secretion.

Cortisol is the main glucocorticoid in every animal

Corticosterone predominates in many vertebrates, including standard laboratory rodents.

Glucocorticoids always act slowly

Rapid non-genomic actions can occur alongside genomic effects.

High cortisol always indicates higher secretion

Altered clearance or binding can change concentration.

Feedback abolishes hormone pulses

Feedback operates within a dynamic, pulsatile system.

Fish have the same adrenal zones as mammals

Teleost interrenal tissue has corresponding endocrine functions but different anatomy.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which cell type releases ACTH?

Answer and reasoning

Anterior pituitary corticotrophs CRH is released by hypothalamic neurons; glucocorticoids arise from adrenal cortical cells.

2. Part B — What receptor on chromaffin cells is activated by preganglionic acetylcholine?

Answer and reasoning

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor Activation supports depolarization and calcium-dependent exocytosis.

3. Part B — Which receptor commonly promotes cardiac contractile performance?

Answer and reasoning

Beta-1 adrenergic receptor Beta-1 signaling in cardiac cells increases cAMP-dependent responses.

4. Part B — Where is systemic cortisol primarily synthesized in humans?

Answer and reasoning

Adrenal cortex, principally zona fasciculata The medulla releases catecholamines.

5. Part B — What is the usual direction of glucocorticoid feedback on ACTH?

Answer and reasoning

Inhibitory Feedback restrains pituitary output.

6. Part B — Which glucocorticoid predominates in standard laboratory rats?

Answer and reasoning

Corticosterone Cortisol predominates in humans.

7. Part B — Which peptide can potentiate CRH-induced ACTH secretion?

Answer and reasoning

Arginine vasopressin AVP acts through corticotroph V1b receptors.

8. Part B — What is the primary effect of alpha-1 activation in many arterioles?

Answer and reasoning

Smooth muscle contraction Gq-mediated calcium signaling favours contraction.

9. Part B — What distinguishes an ultradian from a circadian rhythm?

Answer and reasoning

Ultradian periods are shorter than a day Daily timing and shorter secretory pulses can coexist.

10. Part B — What is a major function of insect adipokinetic hormone?

Answer and reasoning

Mobilization of energy stores It supports energetic demand without a mammalian adrenal axis.

11. Part C — Heart rate rises from 80 to 120 beats/min but stroke volume drops from 75 to 40 mL. Compare output.

Answer and reasoning

6.0 falls to 4.8 L/min Multiply rate by stroke volume; increased rate does not guarantee greater output.

12. Part C — Tidal volume is 350 mL, dead space 150 mL and rate 20/min. Calculate alveolar ventilation.

Answer and reasoning

4 L/min Only 200 mL per breath reaches the gas-exchanging region in this model.

13. Part C — An adrenal synthesis defect lowers glucocorticoid while pituitary feedback remains intact. Predict ACTH.

Answer and reasoning

ACTH tends to increase Reduced inhibitory feedback increases upstream drive.

14. Part C — After a stressor, ACTH is unchanged but steroid output is reduced. What level should be investigated?

Answer and reasoning

Adrenal responsiveness or steroid handling An unchanged measured upstream output does not explain the downstream decrease by itself.

15. Part C — Total cortisol is unchanged at 400 units but free fraction increases from 4% to 6%. What happens to free hormone?

Answer and reasoning

Increases from 16 to 24 units Multiply total by fractional unbound proportion.

16. Part C — Under a first-order model, clearance halves while secretion remains constant. Predict steady concentration.

Answer and reasoning

Doubles Steady concentration equals secretion divided by the clearance constant.

17. Part C — Two groups are sampled at different activity phases. What is the major confound?

Answer and reasoning

Circadian variation Basal hormone output varies independently of the experimental challenge.

18. Part C — A CRH receptor blockade only partially reduces ACTH release. Give a plausible explanation.

Answer and reasoning

Remaining AVP or other regulatory inputs Pituitary secretion integrates more than one signal.

19. Part C — A fish's cortisol rises after a salinity change. Why is this not conclusive evidence of tissue injury?

Answer and reasoning

Cortisol can support osmoregulatory adaptation The hormone response is not identical to a pathological outcome.

20. Part C — The cardiac response precedes the ACTH response. Does the first cause the second?

Answer and reasoning

Not established Both responses may arise from a shared central signal; intervention is needed to test causality.

21. Part B — Which hypothalamic structure contains major hypophysiotropic CRH neurons?

Answer and reasoning

Paraventricular nucleus Its relevant neuronal population releases CRH into portal vessels.

22. Part B — Which circulation directly conveys hypothalamic releasing peptides to the anterior pituitary?

Answer and reasoning

Hypophyseal portal circulation It supports direct communication without prior dilution throughout systemic blood.

23. Part B — Which receptor mediates ACTH stimulation of adrenal cortical cells?

Answer and reasoning

Melanocortin 2 receptor It couples ACTH to steroidogenic signaling.

24. Part B — What distinguishes catecholamine release from acute glucocorticoid release?

Answer and reasoning

Catecholamines are stored in secretory granules; glucocorticoids are largely synthesized on demand. This difference contributes to the characteristic response timing.

25. Part B — Which enzyme converts norepinephrine to epinephrine?

Answer and reasoning

Phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase Its expression is supported by local glucocorticoid exposure.

26. Part B — What is a common presynaptic effect of alpha-2 receptors?

Answer and reasoning

Inhibition of further norepinephrine release Gi-associated signaling can reduce transmitter release.

27. Part B — Which adrenergic receptor commonly relaxes airway smooth muscle?

Answer and reasoning

Beta-2 The tissue-specific cAMP pathway favours relaxation.

28. Part B — What is a permissive endocrine effect?

Answer and reasoning

A hormone supports the ability of another signal to produce its full response. It need not be the immediate trigger of the response.

29. Part B — Which hormone-binding protein is particularly relevant to plasma cortisol?

Answer and reasoning

Corticosteroid-binding globulin The total plasma concentration includes bound and free hormone.

30. Part B — Where is the major cortisol-producing endocrine tissue in many teleosts?

Answer and reasoning

Interrenal tissue It is functionally comparable to the glucocorticoid-producing adrenal cortex.

31. Part B — Which hormone is chiefly regulated by angiotensin II and potassium rather than the HPA axis?

Answer and reasoning

Aldosterone ACTH can influence it transiently but is not its principal physiological regulator.

32. Part B — Which intracellular messenger commonly increases following beta-1 receptor activation?

Answer and reasoning

Cyclic AMP Beta receptors generally couple to Gs and activate adenylyl cyclase.

33. Part B — What does disinhibition mean?

Answer and reasoning

Activation of a target by reducing an inhibitory influence. Two inhibitory links in series can yield a positive net effect.

34. Part B — What is the usual role of systemic posterior-pituitary AVP?

Answer and reasoning

Water conservation through regulation of renal water reabsorption Portal AVP has an additional role in corticotroph regulation.

35. Part B — Which feature distinguishes a neurotransmitter from a circulating hormone?

Answer and reasoning

Its mode of delivery to the target The same molecule can serve both roles in different contexts.

36. Part C — CRH alone gives 9 units of ACTH, AVP alone 5, baseline 3, and both 14. Does the response exceed the additive baseline-corrected prediction?

Answer and reasoning

Yes, by 3 units Additive total = 3 + (9−3) + (5−3) = 11; 14−11 = 3.

37. Part C — A hypothetical ligand concentration equals four times Kd. What fractional occupancy is predicted by the one-site model?

Answer and reasoning

0.8 L/(Kd+L) = 4/5, assuming equilibrium and independent sites.

38. Part C — Cardiac output changes from 5 to 6 L/min while arterial oxygen content falls from 200 to 160 mL/L. Compare delivery.

Answer and reasoning

Falls from 1000 to 960 mL/min Oxygen delivery equals flow times arterial content.

39. Part C — Three parallel vascular beds have resistances 10, 20 and 20. Find equivalent resistance.

Answer and reasoning

5 pressure-per-flow units Conductances sum to 0.1+0.05+0.05 = 0.2; reciprocal is 5.

40. Part C — A neural pathway inhibits an inhibitory interneuron that inhibits a CRH neuron. What is the predicted net sign?

Answer and reasoning

Excitatory, if the interneuron was active Removing inhibition can increase target activity; a silent interneuron cannot be further inhibited effectively.

41. Part C — Two groups show the same stress-induced increment but one starts with a higher baseline. What can be concluded about peaks?

Answer and reasoning

The higher-baseline group has the higher absolute peak Absolute and baseline-adjusted responses answer different questions.

42. Part C — Cortisol concentration is 50 units and k = 0.04/min. Secretion supplies 1.5 units/min. What is the instantaneous derivative?

Answer and reasoning

−0.5 units/min Clearance equals 2 units/min, giving 1.5−2.

43. Part C — An isolated chromaffin cell has normal calcium entry and normal stored catecholamine content but low secretion. Which process is a reasonable candidate?

Answer and reasoning

Vesicle fusion or another downstream release step Normal storage and entry narrow the possibilities but do not uniquely identify the molecular lesion.

44. Part C — A group has a normal adrenal response to experimental ACTH but low ACTH and cortisol after a natural challenge. What is supported?

Answer and reasoning

An upstream limitation of recruitment The adrenal retains responsiveness under the ACTH test; upstream drive or pituitary response deserves investigation.

45. Part C — Two tissues have identical receptor occupancy but different contractile responses. Give a plausible explanation.

Answer and reasoning

Different receptor coupling or downstream effector machinery Occupancy is not identical to response capacity.

46. Part C — Glucose appearance and disappearance both increase from 2 to 5 mass units/min. What happens to the simplified pool?

Answer and reasoning

No net change while rates remain equal High turnover can coexist with stable concentration.

47. Part C — An animal has higher plasma norepinephrine during a challenge. Can adrenal secretion alone be inferred?

Answer and reasoning

No Neural spillover and clearance can also influence circulating concentration.

48. Part C — A receptor blockade reduces cardiac recruitment but ACTH increases normally. What limited conclusion is justified?

Answer and reasoning

The full measured cardiac response was not required for the ACTH increase in that experiment. This does not establish total independence of autonomic and endocrine control.

49. Part C — A hormone returns to baseline while a target enzyme remains elevated. How is that possible?

Answer and reasoning

Protein persistence after the initiating signal has declined Tissue responses can retain a history of earlier hormone exposure.

50. Part C — At 0, 10 and 20 minutes a hormone increment is 0, 30 and 10 units. Estimate incremental area by trapezoids.

Answer and reasoning

350 concentration-unit minutes First interval: 150; second: 200; total: 350.

Revision and mastery

Trace the rapid sympathetic and adrenal-medullary response separately from the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal cascade, then explain how both cooperate to support organ function. Compare timing, receptor-dependent responses, feedback and hormone clearance. Use the same functional questions when examining a teleost or insect response without importing mammalian anatomy.
