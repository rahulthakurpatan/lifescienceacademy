1. From neural command to muscle force

A motor neuron has a remarkable task. Its electrical discharge must reach cells that may lie far from its cell body, and the cells receiving that discharge must generate an appropriate mechanical response. A movement such as picking up a pencil requires enough force to oppose gravity but not enough to break the pencil. Rising from a chair requires a substantially larger output. In either example, the nervous system must determine how many muscle fibres contribute and how often they are activated. The electrical signal of an individual motor neuron does not become larger simply because the task requires greater force. The solution is a hierarchy of control: transmission at each nerve–muscle contact is normally reliable, while the total mechanical output of the muscle is adjustable.

Skeletal muscle is composed of elongated, multinucleated cells called muscle fibres. A fibre contains numerous myofibrils, each of which contains an ordered series of contractile units known as sarcomeres. The membrane surrounding a muscle fibre is the sarcolemma. Although the fibre and its motor neuron are closely associated, the interior of one cell does not form a continuous electrical compartment with the interior of the other. At the neuromuscular junction, a chemical messenger crosses a narrow extracellular space to communicate between two separate excitable cells. The subsequent changes in muscle membrane voltage initiate a distinct intracellular calcium signal, which regulates the interaction of contractile proteins. It is important to separate these events experimentally as well as conceptually.

The term excitation denotes an electrical activation of a cell; contraction denotes development of active tension by its contractile apparatus. A contraction need not result in shortening. A person holding a weight motionless develops muscle tension despite the absence of visible limb movement. In an isometric preparation the endpoints of a muscle are held at fixed separation, whereas in a shortening contraction the muscle changes length against a load. The transmission mechanism described in this chapter can initiate either type of mechanical behaviour. External constraints determine whether the tension actually produces movement.

For the somatic motor pathway, the cell body of an alpha motor neuron lies in the ventral horn of the spinal cord or in an appropriate motor nucleus of the brainstem. Its axon extends towards skeletal muscle. Unlike the typical autonomic efferent pathway, there is no intervening peripheral autonomic ganglion between this neuron and its target muscle fibre. The terminal transmitter is acetylcholine, abbreviated ACh, and the postsynaptic receptor is a muscle-type nicotinic acetylcholine receptor rather than a muscarinic receptor. This distinction matters because the receptor determines the nature and time course of the response. A transmitter name alone does not specify its physiological action.

2. Organisation of the neuromuscular junction

The neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is the specialised chemical synapse between a motor nerve terminal and a skeletal muscle fibre. A single mature mammalian extrafusal muscle fibre is usually innervated by one alpha motor neuron at one end-plate region, but a motor neuron can innervate many fibres through axonal branches. An extrafusal fibre is an ordinary force-producing skeletal muscle fibre, as distinct from the specialised intrafusal fibres of sensory muscle spindles. The one-to-many branching arrangement allows an individual neuronal action potential to activate a distributed population of muscle fibres. The fibres belonging to one motor neuron together with that neuron form a motor unit.

The presynaptic terminal contains mitochondria, synaptic vesicles and specialised regions called active zones. Synaptic vesicles store transmitter; active zones bring the fusion machinery and voltage-gated calcium channels into appropriate proximity. This proximity is significant because free calcium becomes highly elevated locally when a channel opens, even if the change averaged across the terminal is relatively small. The arrangement therefore enables a brief electrical event to produce rapid vesicle fusion. Terminal Schwann cells cover and support the terminal without forming a continuous myelin sheath over the active junctional surface. Their supporting role should not be mistaken for the primary transmitter-mediated contact between the motor terminal and muscle.

The synaptic cleft separates the two cell membranes. Its basal lamina contains specialised extracellular proteins, including anchored acetylcholinesterase. The motor end plate, strictly the specialised postsynaptic membrane of the muscle fibre, has junctional folds. Nicotinic receptors are concentrated in the postsynaptic receptor-rich region, especially near the crests of folds in mammalian end plates; voltage-gated sodium channels are abundant in deeper fold regions and adjacent excitable membrane. This distribution supports efficient conversion of local receptor current into regenerative muscle excitation. A diagram showing a flat postsynaptic membrane is useful for depicting transmitter diffusion but conceals an important structural adaptation of the mature vertebrate junction.

A comparison with a central excitatory synapse clarifies the design. A single central excitatory postsynaptic potential commonly contributes only a fraction of the depolarisation needed to make a neuron discharge. At a normal adult mammalian neuromuscular junction, one motor impulse ordinarily produces an end-plate potential large enough to initiate a muscle action potential without a need for simultaneous input from other motor neurons. This high reliability allows the graded command to be established mainly upstream, through motor neuron recruitment and firing frequency. It also explains why synaptic failure may become mechanically evident when transmission is weakened enough to consume the normal reserve above threshold.

3. Biological variation in nerve and muscle organisation

The general account of the neuromuscular junction in this chapter describes the mature mammalian skeletal muscle synapse. That context matters. In many vertebrate twitch muscle fibres, a local end-plate potential is converted into a regenerative propagated action potential. Some other muscle fibres, including certain slow tonic fibres in non-mammalian vertebrates, receive multiple contacts and can exhibit graded local responses rather than behaving in exactly the same manner. In many invertebrates, the neuromuscular transmitter is not acetylcholine: glutamate commonly mediates excitatory neuromuscular transmission in arthropods, and inhibitory motor innervation occurs in several invertebrate groups. These examples should not obscure the mammalian mechanism. They establish the domain over which its conclusions are valid. A question that explicitly specifies a frog twitch fibre, an adult human skeletal muscle fibre or a crustacean muscle may require different assumptions.

The distribution of junctions also reflects developmental history. In developing mammalian skeletal muscle, a fibre can initially receive more than one motor axon. During maturation, competing innervation is refined, and most extrafusal fibres retain a single motor input at the end plate. In the mature arrangement an axon branches to supply a population of fibres, but a given ordinary fibre generally has one motor neuron as its functional input. The statement that one motor neuron supplies many fibres therefore does not imply that each of those fibres has several motor neurons contributing simultaneous excitation. It describes divergence from the neuron, not convergence onto the mature fibre. The distinction is particularly important when transferring concepts such as spatial summation from a central neuron to the ordinary mammalian motor end plate.

The postsynaptic apparatus is maintained by communication between nerve, muscle and supporting cells. During development, nerve-derived agrin acts through the muscle-associated receptor system involving LRP4 and MuSK to promote organisation of the end plate, including clustering of acetylcholine receptors. Rapsyn contributes to the clustering and stabilisation of receptors. These proteins are not the rapidly gating acetylcholine receptor itself. Their physiological role is to establish and maintain the local architecture that enables efficient signalling during repeated activity. Disturbance of the organisational machinery can reduce the quality of transmission even when transmitter synthesis and the intrinsic ability of acetylcholine to open an isolated receptor are unaffected. This is an example of a general cell-signalling principle: structural localisation of signal components can be as important as their total abundance.

The difference between total receptor abundance and local receptor abundance can be explored using a hypothetical receptor-labelling experiment. Suppose the total fluorescence associated with receptor protein in two muscle fibres is similar, but one fibre has most of the fluorescent signal concentrated beneath its nerve terminal while the other has a diffuse distribution across a larger surface. If the transmitter is released at a specialised local site, the fibre with diffuse receptors may expose fewer receptors to the brief high transmitter concentration. Consequently, similar total receptor abundance need not imply a similar end-plate current. This interpretation requires controls for labelling efficiency and optical geometry; brightness alone does not demonstrate that the labelled proteins conduct ions. Combining anatomical localisation with physiological recordings allows a stronger structure–function inference.

Myelination further distinguishes the long-distance and terminal functions of the motor axon. The proximal myelinated portion conducts efficiently by saltatory propagation, while the terminal branches are adapted for transmitter release. A myelinated internode is not itself a motor end plate, and the terminal Schwann cell covering the junction is not simply an extra node of Ranvier. The axon must maintain conduction into its branches, and each terminal must generate sufficient transmitter output. A single action potential in the parent axon can therefore produce multiple spatially separated muscle action potentials. A failure confined to one terminal branch could affect only part of a motor unit, whereas a failure proximal to the entire branching tree would affect all fibres supplied by that axon. This distinction provides a basis for understanding how changes in neural organisation influence the mechanical output measured from a whole muscle.

4. Acetylcholine synthesis, storage and release

Choline acetyltransferase transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to choline, producing acetylcholine in the motor terminal cytoplasm. A high-affinity choline transporter helps recover choline from the extracellular space. Acetylcholine is subsequently loaded into synaptic vesicles by a vesicular transporter that uses the proton electrochemical gradient generated by a vesicular ATPase. These are different transport processes: choline uptake supplies the precursor from outside the terminal, whereas vesicular transport concentrates the newly synthesised transmitter inside a membrane-bound compartment. Disruption of either can eventually reduce the amount available for release, but it does not follow that the immediate response to a single nerve impulse disappears in the same manner.

An action potential invading the terminal changes its membrane potential. Voltage-gated calcium channels then open, allowing extracellular calcium to enter down its electrochemical gradient. The incoming calcium activates calcium-sensitive components of the vesicle fusion machinery, including synaptotagmin. SNARE proteins help bring the vesicular and plasma membranes together and permit fusion. Transmitter is discharged into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis. A nerve impulse is therefore not itself a packet of acetylcholine, and acetylcholine does not flow down the axon as the propagating electrical signal. Rather, electrical propagation reaches a specialised terminal where a second process converts it into a chemical signal.

Several events can be experimentally dissociated. If extracellular calcium is substantially reduced, the terminal can still be electrically excited but evoked transmitter release is impaired. If postsynaptic receptor occupancy is reduced while terminal release is preserved, the same number of released vesicles may elicit a smaller end-plate potential. If muscle voltage-gated sodium channels are blocked without suppressing nerve excitability or receptor function, a graded local response can remain but the muscle action potential fails. These interventions give different results because they disturb separate links in the same causal chain. Successful identification of a defect therefore requires measurements on both sides of the junction or a way to bypass one of the intervening steps.

Vesicle membranes must be retrieved and reused during repetitive activity. A terminal does not release its entire supply of transmitter in a single impulse. Vesicle pools include readily available vesicles and additional vesicles that can be recruited during activity. If release temporarily exceeds replenishment, the amount released per stimulus may decline. Conversely, residual presynaptic calcium after an impulse can enhance release probability during subsequent stimuli. The net pattern during a train depends on the balance between facilitation, depletion and replenishment, among other factors. It is not adequate to infer the number of available vesicles from the size of the first response alone.

5. Transmitter handling, receptor state and ionic selectivity

The synthesis of acetylcholine provides a link between intermediary metabolism and neural communication. Acetyl-CoA contributes the acetyl group, while choline contributes the remainder of the molecule. ACh is electrically charged under physiological conditions and does not simply diffuse freely through the lipid core of vesicle membranes. Compartmentalisation therefore depends on transport proteins. The vesicle's proton gradient is an example of secondary active transport: ATP is consumed by the pump that creates the gradient, while the vesicular acetylcholine transporter uses the stored electrochemical energy. If vesicular acidification is impaired, synthesis in the cytoplasm may continue while effective vesicle filling declines. The existence of distinct biochemical compartments explains why measuring total ACh within a terminal would not necessarily predict its available quantal content during stimulation.

A neurotransmitter released into the narrow cleft encounters receptors over a short distance. Diffusion is essential, but concentration is not uniform in space or constant through time. Transmitter concentration near its release point rises briefly and then falls because of diffusion, binding and enzymatic hydrolysis. Acetylcholinesterase is positioned where released transmitter can encounter it. Its function helps preserve temporal resolution: two neuronal discharges close together must remain capable of producing separate postsynaptic electrical events. Without efficient termination, the receptor conductance produced by one discharge can persist into the next interval. Persistent conductance not only shifts the membrane voltage but also changes the membrane resistance, which affects the response to other currents.

Ligand-gated receptor activation is often described as a simple binding event, but a receptor has functionally distinct states. It can be closed and available for activation, open and conducting ions, or desensitised in a nonconducting condition despite the presence of agonist. A ligand can therefore remain bound without maintaining the same current indefinitely. The time course of receptor opening depends on ligand concentration and transitions among states. Desensitisation provides a molecular reason why continued exposure to ACh can produce an initial excitation followed by a diminished response. This is separate from inactivation of voltage-gated sodium channels in the muscle membrane. Both can contribute to impaired excitation during persistent depolarising stimulation, yet they concern different proteins and can be distinguished experimentally.

A brief washout after prolonged agonist exposure can permit recovery of receptor availability. If receptor current measured at controlled voltage is depressed even though the agonist concentration is restored to the same value, receptor state is a plausible contributor. If receptor current is relatively normal but regenerative muscle spikes are absent at depolarised membrane potentials, voltage-gated sodium channel availability deserves attention. The two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. An experiment that measures only loss of twitch force cannot distinguish them, because both ultimately diminish effective electrical activation. This illustrates why the NMJ is a useful system for teaching the general principle of measuring the closest possible intermediate to the proposed molecular defect.

The reversal potential of the receptor current is an emergent property of its ionic permeabilities and the concentrations of permeant ions. Imagine, as a first approximation, that the receptor permits sodium and potassium currents through conductances gNa and gK. Net current can then be written as gNa(V − ENa) + gK(V − EK). At reversal the sum is zero, so Erev = (gNa ENa + gK EK)/(gNa + gK). In a simplified example with equal conductances, ENa = +60 mV and EK = −90 mV, the calculated reversal potential is −15 mV. The actual channel's ionic selectivity and current–voltage characteristics need not satisfy this elementary conductance model. The calculation demonstrates why a channel permeable to two ions can reverse at an intermediate voltage rather than at either individual equilibrium potential.

A change in extracellular potassium can influence the resting potential of the muscle as well as the receptor's potassium component. Raising extracellular potassium generally makes the potassium equilibrium potential less negative. Moderate depolarisation may initially reduce the distance to spike threshold, but more sustained or severe depolarisation can reduce sodium channel availability. Therefore an increase in extracellular potassium should not be interpreted automatically as an increase in effective muscle excitability. The direction of change in voltage, the availability of regenerative channels and the state of the receptor conductance all contribute. In an analytical question, the duration of exposure and measured intermediate response can determine which effect dominates.

The receptor current can be separated from muscle sodium currents using their different activation mechanisms. The receptor channel opens in response to ligand, while the muscle sodium channel opens in response to depolarisation. A selective intervention that prevents the regenerative sodium current can leave a graded end-plate response visible. Conversely, stimulating an intact excitable sarcolemma directly can generate a muscle action potential without opening nicotinic receptors at the end plate. These observations demonstrate that the receptors and voltage-gated channels are arranged in series for physiological transmission, but neither is simply a subunit or mode of the other. The two forms of excitation are mechanistically distinct even when their voltage changes follow one another within milliseconds.

6. Nicotinic receptor currents and the end-plate potential

The adult mammalian muscle nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is a pentameric ligand-gated cation channel, commonly composed of two alpha1 subunits and one each of beta1, delta and epsilon. In fetal mammalian muscle, a gamma subunit occupies the position taken by epsilon in the adult receptor. Binding of acetylcholine at the appropriate extracellular binding sites promotes channel opening. The receptor permits passage of sodium and potassium ions. At the strongly negative resting voltage of a skeletal muscle fibre, the inward sodium current dominates the opposing outward potassium current, producing a net inward positive current and depolarising the postsynaptic membrane.

A useful expression for receptor-mediated current is I = g(Vm − Erev), where I is the net current in amperes, g is conductance in siemens, Vm is membrane potential and Erev is the current reversal potential, both in volts. By convention positive current is outward. For the muscle nicotinic receptor the reversal potential is near zero millivolts under ordinary ionic conditions, not equal to the sodium equilibrium potential. When Vm is negative, the calculated current is inward. As depolarisation approaches Erev, the net driving force falls even if the same number of channels remain open. At Erev, net current is zero, although individual sodium and potassium fluxes need not both be zero. This distinction is essential for interpreting current–voltage plots.

Consider a hypothetical voltage-clamp experiment with a receptor conductance of 20 nS and a reversal potential of 0 mV. At −80 mV the current is 20 × (−80) pA, or −1600 pA (−1.6 nA), since one nanosiemens multiplied by one millivolt equals one picoampere. At −20 mV the current is −400 pA, and at +20 mV it is +400 pA. This elementary ohmic approximation assumes a fixed conductance during comparison and ignores channel rectification and time-dependent gating. It predicts the sign and magnitude of net receptor current; it does not give the magnitude of the resulting voltage change without information about membrane resistance, capacitance and other currents.

The local depolarisation produced by evoked transmitter release is called the end-plate potential (EPP). Unlike a propagated action potential, its size varies with the number of transmitter quanta released, receptor function, driving force and electrical properties of the postsynaptic region. An EPP is not intrinsically all-or-none. Depolarisation spreads into surrounding membrane by local current flow. If voltage-gated sodium channels in the excitable membrane are sufficiently activated, their regenerative inward current produces a muscle action potential. After sodium channel inactivation and repolarising currents, the muscle membrane recovers excitability. The propagated action potential travels along the sarcolemma and enters transverse tubules; the end-plate potential itself is not the signal that spreads regeneratively through the whole fibre.

The difference between the normal end-plate depolarisation and the depolarisation needed for muscle action potential initiation is a reserve sometimes described in terms of a safety factor. Definitions of safety factor differ between experimental contexts, so a voltage margin or ratio should be identified explicitly rather than treated as a universal number. In a simplified teaching example, resting potential is −85 mV and threshold is −60 mV, requiring 25 mV of depolarisation. An EPP of 40 mV exceeds that requirement by 15 mV. If the EPP falls to 20 mV while threshold remains unchanged, the local voltage reaches only −65 mV and a muscle action potential is not initiated in the simplified model. Real thresholds are dynamic and the EPP measured at one position need not equal depolarisation at the spike initiation site. Nonetheless, this example illustrates why substantial physiological loss can be masked until a threshold is crossed.

Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses ACh into acetate and choline. This rapidly limits receptor activation and permits discrete impulses to be transmitted at short intervals. Choline can be recovered by the nerve terminal and used for new synthesis. Clearance is not primarily achieved by taking intact acetylcholine back into synaptic vesicles. If acetylcholinesterase is inhibited, transmitter persists longer and a weakened end plate may temporarily produce a more effective depolarisation. Excessive or sustained receptor activation, however, can lead to receptor desensitisation and failure of muscle excitability through persistent depolarisation and sodium channel inactivation. Thus, prolonging an excitatory chemical signal does not guarantee a sustained useful mechanical response.

7. Evidence for quantal transmission

Fatt and Katz reported spontaneous miniature end-plate potentials in 1952. Intracellular recordings from muscle showed brief small depolarisations even when deliberate nerve stimulation was absent. Their occurrence at innervated regions and resemblance to the time course of end-plate responses suggested that the junction could release small elementary packets of transmitter. Subsequent work on the statistical properties of evoked release supported quantal transmission. The historically important advance was that a large evoked response could be understood as the combined effect of many discrete elementary release events, rather than requiring a continuous variable amount of transmitter released by each terminal in every trial.

A miniature end-plate potential (MEPP) is normally the postsynaptic voltage response to spontaneous release of approximately one vesicle's transmitter content. Quantal size is the average postsynaptic response to an individual quantum, and quantal content is the average number of quanta contributing to the evoked response. A decrease in mean miniature amplitude may indicate reduced receptor function or reduced vesicular transmitter content; it is not unique evidence of a postsynaptic lesion. A change in miniature frequency can indicate altered spontaneous presynaptic release but does not directly quantify evoked release probability. In particular, spontaneous and evoked release need not be regulated identically.

Under an approximation of linear summation, quantal content m can be estimated by dividing mean evoked end-plate potential amplitude by mean miniature end-plate potential amplitude. Suppose a hypothetical recording gives an average evoked amplitude of 6 mV and a mean miniature amplitude of 0.5 mV. The estimated quantal content is 12, a dimensionless mean number of quanta. If an intervention reduces evoked amplitude to 3 mV without changing miniature amplitude, the new estimate is six. A presynaptic reduction in evoked release is a reasonable interpretation when muscle input resistance and recording conditions are held constant. At large responses the declining electrochemical driving force makes summation nonlinear; amplitude ratios then underestimate the number of released quanta unless a suitable correction is applied. End-plate currents measured at a controlled holding voltage can reduce this particular problem.

A second elementary model treats a terminal as having n independent release opportunities, each releasing with probability p on a stimulus. With identical probabilities and single-quantum opportunities, the expected quantal content is np. If n = 100 and p = 0.2, the expected release is 20 quanta per impulse. Reducing p to 0.1 with n unchanged reduces the mean to ten. The observations of the mean alone do not reveal whether n or p changed: halving n gives the same expectation. For independent identical release sites the variance of the quantal count is np(1 − p); heterogeneous probabilities, changing vesicle availability and nonlinear postsynaptic responses limit the application of this model. It is useful not because all synapses literally satisfy it, but because it demonstrates the difference between the number of potential events and the probability of each event.

In low-release conditions a Poisson approximation can sometimes be made when there are many possible events with small probabilities. The fraction of trials with no evoked release is P0 = exp(−m). Rearranging gives m = −ln(P0). In a hypothetical experiment with 25 failures out of 100 stimuli, P0 = 0.25, giving m approximately 1.386. The estimate is a population mean rather than a claim that a fractional vesicle fuses. A finite sample has statistical uncertainty; a trial with a very small response may be wrongly classified as a failure if measurement noise is substantial. Also, zero recorded mechanical response is not equivalent to zero vesicle release, because subthreshold EPPs may occur without muscle action potentials. Only direct synaptic recordings or an appropriately validated measure can distinguish these cases.

Checking the quantal model against two independent estimates

An important feature of the classical quantal experiments is that two independent kinds of information can constrain the same proposed mechanism. Suppose a low-release preparation gives a mean evoked end-plate potential of 0.75 mV when failures are included and a mean miniature amplitude of 0.25 mV. The amplitude ratio estimates three quanta per nerve impulse. Now suppose 20 of 400 stimuli fail to evoke a detectable response. The failure fraction is 0.05, and the Poisson estimate is −ln(0.05), approximately 3.00. The agreement is consistent with release of discrete packets of similar average postsynaptic effect under low-probability conditions. It is not proof that every vesicle contains an identical number of transmitter molecules. The analysis relies on a stable miniature response, linear summation at small depolarisations and sufficiently reliable detection of failures. When the evoked response is large, the changing driving force and membrane conductance reduce the validity of the simple amplitude ratio. In that case the recorded voltage is no longer proportional to the number of vesicles by the same factor across the full amplitude range.

Consider a second experiment after changing extracellular calcium. If miniature amplitude stays close to 0.25 mV but failures increase to 100 of 400 trials, the Poisson estimate becomes −ln(0.25), or 1.39. The corresponding predicted mean voltage under the same linear assumption is about 0.35 mV. This decrease in quantal content, with a relatively stable quantal size, is compatible with reduced evoked release. The conclusion is stronger if the terminal action potential is preserved and if receptor sensitivity is independently checked. It is weaker if small events have become harder to detect, because missed events would inflate the apparent failure fraction. The experiment therefore teaches not simply how to substitute numbers into a logarithm, but how to compare independent measures, identify assumptions and recognise systematic measurement error. The classical studies by Fatt and Katz and by del Castillo and Katz provide the experimental foundation for this reasoning.

8. Distinguishing the electrical and chemical stages experimentally

The general design of a nerve–muscle experiment makes this junction especially valuable in physiology. A peripheral motor nerve and its target muscle can be studied while electrical and mechanical events are recorded independently. The stimulating electrode establishes when the command is delivered; the recording electrodes reveal whether excitation actually arrives at the muscle; the force transducer reveals what the muscle accomplishes mechanically. A recording from only one of these locations cannot establish the state of the entire system. For example, a normal nerve action potential says that an impulse travels through the region of nerve in which it was recorded. It does not prove that transmitter is released, and an apparently normal muscle membrane response at one fibre does not prove that every fibre in a whole muscle has been activated.

A particularly useful distinction is between voltage recording and current recording. An intracellular recording of end-plate voltage measures the resulting change in membrane potential. Its magnitude depends on the receptor current and on how readily current can flow through other parts of the membrane. In voltage clamp, the experimenter instead uses feedback to maintain a specified membrane voltage and records the current required to maintain that voltage. Conceptually, this permits study of transmitter-gated conductance without allowing the driving force to change freely during the response. Neither technique automatically reveals the number of released vesicles. That inference requires additional assumptions or measurements, such as the mean response to spontaneous quanta and control of postsynaptic membrane properties.

Consider two hypothetical fibres with identical receptor conductance transients but different leak conductances. In a simple steady-state electrical model, the resting membrane conductance is gL with reversal potential EL, and receptor conductance is gA with reversal potential EA. During activation, ignoring capacitance and other voltage-dependent currents, the membrane voltage tends towards the conductance-weighted value V = (gL EL + gA EA)/(gL + gA). With EL = −85 mV, EA = 0 mV, gL = 20 nS and gA = 10 nS, the limiting voltage is approximately −56.7 mV. If gL increases to 40 nS while the receptor conductance is unchanged, the limiting voltage becomes −68 mV. The resulting local potential is smaller even though exactly the same receptor conductance was activated. The approximation is deliberately steady-state; a brief synaptic conductance may terminate before the voltage reaches its limiting value, and an action potential could invalidate the passive model. It explains how postsynaptic shunting can change an EPP independently of vesicle release.

Membrane capacitance supplies another distinction between current and voltage. During the early stage of a small response, some of the receptor current charges the membrane capacitance. A simplified passive model with resistance R and capacitance C has a time constant τ = RC. Resistance in ohms multiplied by capacitance in farads gives time in seconds. For a constant current step, the voltage approaches its new steady value exponentially, with about 63 percent of the total change achieved after one time constant. An actual EPP is not a constant current step, but the model explains why its time course reflects both the lifetime of the synaptic conductance and the electrical properties of the muscle membrane. Two fibres receiving identical chemical signals can exhibit somewhat different voltage waveforms even without different receptor kinetics.

In an experiment comparing miniature end-plate currents at different holding voltages, the direction of the current depends on the difference between the imposed holding voltage and receptor reversal potential. A smaller inward current at a less negative voltage does not by itself mean that fewer channels opened. A useful control is to compare the conductance estimated from current divided by driving force, except very close to reversal where division by a small number makes estimation unreliable. A reversal potential shifted substantially by changing the external sodium or potassium concentration supports the interpretation of a cation-permeable receptor channel. However, ionic changes may also affect other processes, so a good experimental question specifies which channels were isolated and which ionic conditions were changed.

It is instructive to think about thresholds at three levels. The motor neuron's axon initial segment must first reach the conditions for a regenerative spike. The nerve terminal's voltage-dependent calcium channels then open in response to its own electrical waveform. Finally, the local muscle depolarisation must activate the muscle fibre's spike-generating machinery. These thresholds are not identical physical quantities. Calcium channel activation is graded with voltage and duration; action potential threshold is a dynamic transition between competing inward and outward currents. Force threshold in a recording may additionally depend on the transducer's sensitivity. Therefore, an examination question mentioning an increase in threshold should be read carefully: does it refer to motor neuron recruitment, the muscle membrane's excitability, or the smallest detectable mechanical response?

9. Synaptic reserve, repetitive transmission and localisation

The normal neuromuscular junction solves a reliability problem by combining presynaptic and postsynaptic design features. A motor terminal releases numerous quanta during a typical successful impulse; the end plate presents many receptors; junctional structure helps organise receptor and voltage-gated channel populations. Each feature provides some reserve. A reduction in transmitter output does not always produce an immediately proportional reduction in force because a fibre continues to fire if its local EPP exceeds the spike threshold. Once transmission fails in a subset of fibres, however, whole-unit force can fall. This is one reason why the relationship between junctional function and mechanical output is neither a straight line nor adequately described by the amplitude of one electrically evoked response.

A hypothetical population of five fibres illustrates this point. Assume the depolarisation required for spike initiation is 20 mV in each fibre, with initial effective EPP amplitudes of 24, 28, 32, 36 and 40 mV. All five fibres are activated initially. An intervention reduces each amplitude by one quarter, leaving values of 18, 21, 24, 27 and 30 mV. Four fibres still fire. A further reduction to half the original amplitudes produces values of 12, 14, 16, 18 and 20 mV; only the last fibre reaches the stipulated threshold. This is an idealised deterministic threshold exercise, not a measured distribution. Its biological lesson is that variation in reserve creates a progressive loss of recruited fibres during impairment even if all fibres experience the same fractional synaptic change. In reality both transmitter release and threshold vary across trials, so firing probability can change gradually before complete failure.

A paired-pulse experiment separates some of the effects of repetitive stimulation. A first action potential releases transmitter and briefly raises presynaptic calcium. A second impulse arriving soon afterwards can encounter residual calcium and show enhanced release. At the same time, the first impulse has consumed some readily available vesicles. Depending on their relative effects, the second EPP may exceed or fall below the first. If the mean miniature amplitude is constant and electrical recordings are comparable, a change in evoked amplitude is consistent with a change in quantal content. But receptor desensitisation can also diminish a second response, especially when transmitter persists. Accordingly, paired-pulse ratios are evidence about the dynamics of transmission rather than unambiguous proof of one specific molecular mechanism.

Imagine that stimulation at a moderate frequency causes end-plate currents to fall from 10 nA to 7 nA across a short train, while miniature currents before and immediately after the train average 0.5 nA. In a simplified voltage-clamp interpretation, average evoked quantal content decreases from 20 to 14. If an intervention enhances recovery of the late response without changing miniature current, one possible explanation is improved replenishment of available quanta. Another is a different time course of presynaptic calcium. These cannot be distinguished from the amplitude sequence alone. A student should identify the hypothesis consistent with the data and then specify what additional measurement would separate the alternatives. A force trace alone would offer less specific evidence because the muscle calcium and mechanical stages could also change during the train.

Different interventions can also act at different temporal scales. Blocking a receptor can reduce the effect of acetylcholine immediately. Interfering with choline recovery or vesicle filling may initially leave previously loaded vesicles available, with greater effects emerging during repeated release. A defect of axonal transport may gradually undermine terminal maintenance, rather than prevent the first impulse after the intervention. Students sometimes group all presynaptic lesions as if they had the same immediate effect. Location is important, but the process affected and the amount of stored material determine how soon a functional consequence appears.

A fundamental experimental control is direct excitation of the postsynaptic membrane. If a drug prevents contraction after nerve stimulation but directly stimulated fibres retain normal action potentials, calcium release and force, the effect is compatible with impaired axonal or synaptic transmission. It does not, by itself, distinguish a nerve conduction defect from presynaptic release failure or receptor blockade. To separate those, one would record terminal excitation and the EPP. A preserved nerve terminal spike with markedly reduced evoked synaptic response suggests a junctional defect. A normal response to externally applied acetylcholine makes reduced postsynaptic receptor function less likely, although application conditions can differ from physiological release. The diagnostic principle is to test the suspected stage using an input that bypasses the earlier stages while monitoring appropriate outputs.

10. From the sarcolemma to intracellular calcium

A long muscle fibre has a large interior filled with contractile structures. If a surface electrical event depended only on diffusion of calcium from the external medium across the entire radius, activation of its interior would be delayed and nonuniform. Transverse tubules (T-tubules) are invaginations of the sarcolemma that bring membrane excitation close to the myofibrils. Their lumen is continuous with the extracellular space. The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), a specialised intracellular membrane system, stores calcium. Its enlarged terminal cisternae flank a T-tubule at a triad in typical mammalian skeletal muscle. A triad consists of one T-tubule and two SR terminal cisternae, not three tubules of the same membrane system.

Depolarisation of the T-tubular membrane changes the conformation of CaV1.1, also known as the dihydropyridine receptor or DHPR. In adult vertebrate skeletal muscle this protein acts as a voltage sensor coupled to the ryanodine receptor type 1 (RyR1) calcium release channel in the neighbouring SR membrane. The voltage-dependent conformational signal activates SR calcium release. Calcium rapidly enters the myoplasm from an intracellular store down its concentration gradient. The key distinction from typical ventricular cardiac muscle is that calcium influx through skeletal CaV1.1 is not normally required as the immediate trigger for each contraction. In cardiac muscle, CaV1.2-mediated influx activates RyR2 through calcium-induced calcium release. Both systems use calcium but the essential triggering step differs.

The immediate calcium sources at two points of neuromuscular function must be distinguished. The nerve terminal requires entry of extracellular calcium through presynaptic voltage-gated channels to release ACh. The activated skeletal muscle fibre obtains most of the calcium for rapid contractile activation from its SR. An experiment removing extracellular calcium from an entire nerve–muscle preparation may therefore suppress contraction indirectly by preventing neuromuscular transmission, even if a directly stimulated muscle fibre initially retains sufficient SR calcium to contract. To test the muscle mechanism, the presynaptic step should be bypassed and changes in muscle excitability, extracellular ionic conditions and SR loading should be considered.

Calcium in the myoplasm binds to troponin C on thin filaments. Through changes in the troponin complex and tropomyosin positioning, calcium increases access of myosin heads to actin binding sites. Troponin I is the inhibitory component of the troponin complex, and troponin T associates the complex with tropomyosin. The calcium-binding regulatory step should not be confused with the binding of calcium to calmodulin that activates myosin light-chain kinase in smooth muscle. Skeletal muscle is regulated mainly through calcium-dependent availability of actin interaction sites, whereas the smooth muscle pathway uses phosphorylation of myosin as an important regulatory mechanism.

The SR calcium transient terminates when release declines and calcium is removed from the cytoplasm, predominantly by the SR calcium ATPase (SERCA). SERCA uses ATP to move calcium back into the SR against its electrochemical gradient. Luminal calcium-binding proteins, including calsequestrin, help store calcium while limiting free luminal concentration. As cytoplasmic calcium decreases, calcium dissociates from troponin C and thin filament activation declines. Relaxation is therefore not merely passive disappearance of a neural command: ATP-dependent calcium handling and cross-bridge detachment are essential. Failure of calcium reuptake can prolong tension despite cessation of action potentials.

11. Evidence from perturbing calcium mobilisation

The SR is not an unrestricted reservoir. Its membrane must maintain a large difference between luminal calcium and the resting free calcium concentration of the myoplasm. This difference is established by active uptake and buffered storage. Calcium release through RyR1 is driven by the electrochemical gradient that has been built up, rather than by ATP hydrolysis at the release channel during every ion passage. ATP is nevertheless needed to restore the gradient after release. This is another example of a system in which a rapid passive flux is powered indirectly by previously established active transport. The distinction helps reconcile the apparent paradox that muscle activation can begin extremely rapidly while recovery and maintenance demand continuous metabolic support.

CaV1.1 is a member of a family of voltage-gated calcium channels, yet its essential excitation–contraction role in adult skeletal muscle is as a membrane voltage sensor. The conformational signal is communicated across the close junction between the T-tubule membrane and the SR membrane, activating RyR1. The precise structural organisation of these proteins is more elaborate than a simple pair of identical aligned channels. For NET/SET purposes, the essential distinction is that the membrane voltage sensor and intracellular release pore belong to different membranes and perform different operations. A defect in the voltage-sensing step can leave an intracellular calcium store present but inaccessible to normal electrical stimulation; a defect in calcium uptake can instead leave the voltage sensor intact while reducing the available store.

One way to investigate this distinction is to compare the ability of the fibre to respond to membrane depolarisation with its ability to release calcium in response to a suitable independent RyR-activating intervention. If depolarisation fails to release calcium but independent activation of the release machinery produces a substantial calcium transient, the store and release channel can still function under the tested conditions. A problem in the voltage-dependent coupling step is plausible. If neither method yields calcium release, a depleted store or impaired release channel becomes a stronger possibility. Neither observation is uniquely diagnostic without additional controls; the independent intervention may have secondary effects, and differences in the initial SR loading state can influence both measurements. The value of the comparison is that it narrows the possible location of failure.

An important experimental question is whether excitation–contraction coupling can operate when calcium passage through CaV1.1 is reduced. In adult skeletal muscle, changes that selectively diminish CaV1.1 ion conduction do not necessarily abolish voltage-induced SR calcium release if voltage sensing and coupling remain intact. By contrast, disruption of the conformational activation pathway can impair release even if some ionic current through CaV1.1 remains. These observations support a separation of ion conduction and signal transduction by the channel complex. It would be misleading to generalise that extracellular calcium is irrelevant to skeletal muscle physiology: extracellular calcium is essential for normal nerve-terminal release, and calcium entry can contribute to long-term calcium homeostasis and other functions. The specific conclusion concerns the immediate trigger for normal adult skeletal fibre contraction.

Skeletal muscle uses repeated cycles of activation and recovery over a broad range of frequencies. If each activation released calcium without recovery, the free cytoplasmic calcium concentration would remain elevated and SR content would eventually diminish. During repetitive stimulation, calcium removal occurs while new release episodes are also occurring. A sustained cytoplasmic calcium elevation can be understood as the dynamic outcome of a balance among fluxes and buffering, not simply as a channel being permanently open. As frequency increases, the recovery interval becomes shorter, favouring a higher average level of activation. Changes in the time constants of release and reuptake can shift the force–frequency relation even when the number of stimulated fibres remains constant.

The difference between free and total calcium is important when interpreting calcium-sensitive fluorescence. A fluorescent indicator generally reports a signal related to free calcium concentration in the compartment sampled. A substantial fraction of calcium may be bound to proteins or other buffers, and these bound pools do not necessarily produce an identical fluorescence response. Indicator calibration, affinity and response kinetics affect the quantitative relationship between signal and concentration. A larger fluorescence signal can be interpreted as a larger free calcium transient only if the measurement conditions support that inference. It is not automatically evidence of a proportionally larger total amount released from the SR. This qualification is sufficient for interpreting NET-level graphical questions without introducing specialist imaging methodology.

12. Calcium transients and the time course of force

The sequence between a muscle action potential and mechanical output can be represented by three qualitatively different time courses. First is a brief electrical spike. Second is a transient rise in free myoplasmic calcium. Third is a mechanical twitch that rises and falls more slowly. The peaks do not have to coincide. Even after free calcium has begun to decline, many cross-bridges can remain attached and tension can persist because of cross-bridge kinetics and elastic elements. Consequently, a delayed force peak does not imply that calcium release was delayed by an equally long interval. Direct calcium recording is needed to establish when calcium rose and fell.

In a fibre held at constant length, force develops when active sarcomeres pull against their neighbours and against internal and external elastic structures. The rate at which the force transducer records tension is partly influenced by stretching of series elastic elements. This is not an alternative mechanism of excitation–contraction coupling; it is a mechanical consequence of force transmission. A muscle may therefore have similar electrical and calcium responses in two loading conditions but different mechanical traces. To compare calcium sensitivity or contractile function, one should hold length and measurement geometry appropriately constant.

A conceptual description of free myoplasmic calcium balance uses the difference between release and removal rates. If C denotes free calcium concentration in an idealised well-mixed compartment, a schematic relation is dC/dt = Jrelease − Jremoval, with flux terms expressed in concentration per time. In a real fibre, binding to buffers, local concentration gradients, transport across the sarcolemma and the distinction between free and total calcium add further terms. The simplified relation predicts that calcium rises when release exceeds removal, reaches a peak when the net rate is zero, and falls when removal exceeds release. A peak calcium concentration does not mean that release must already have stopped; it means that the net rate of change of the measured concentration is zero.

Suppose two directly stimulated muscle fibres have the same initial calcium peak, but fibre A returns close to baseline in 50 ms and fibre B in 100 ms. If force-sensitive proteins and loading conditions are otherwise similar, the second fibre may exhibit a longer mechanical response. This could reflect slower calcium removal, different buffering, or a prolonged component of calcium release. An inhibitor of SERCA would be expected to affect removal, whereas prolonged RyR1 activity affects the source term. The time course of the free calcium signal alone does not uniquely establish which molecular process is changed. Combining measurements with knowledge of the specific intervention helps distinguish them.

A simple equilibrium binding relation illustrates why changes in calcium need not produce proportional changes in force. For a single class of independent binding sites, fractional occupancy θ = C/(Kd + C), where C and Kd are free calcium concentration and the dissociation constant in the same units. At C = Kd, occupancy is one half. At C = 3Kd, it is three quarters. Increasing calcium from Kd to 3Kd triples the concentration but increases occupancy only from 0.5 to 0.75. Skeletal muscle thin filament activation and force depend on cooperativity, cross-bridge kinetics and sarcomere conditions; the single-site equation is therefore an illustration of saturability, not a quantitative model for intact muscle force. The crucial inference is that the effect of an increment in calcium depends on the starting level of activation.

In an experimentally permeabilised fibre, the sarcolemma is made permeable so that the contractile apparatus can be exposed to controlled free calcium solutions. Under suitable conditions, a researcher can compare active tension over a range of calcium concentrations without requiring an intact nerve impulse or normal SR release. If the tension at a specified calcium concentration is lower than before but maximum tension at saturating calcium is unchanged, reduced calcium sensitivity is a plausible explanation. If maximum force is reduced as well, fewer functioning force-generating cross-bridges or altered force transmission may contribute. This is a conceptual explanation of the method, not a laboratory protocol. Its examination value lies in distinguishing the machinery that supplies calcium from the machinery that responds to it.

The calcium source distinction becomes particularly clear when comparing different muscle tissues. A cardiac cell uses extracellular calcium entry as a crucial trigger for its SR calcium release, whereas a typical adult vertebrate skeletal fibre uses voltage-dependent conformational communication between CaV1.1 and RyR1. Smooth muscle relies on calcium entry and intracellular stores in varying combinations and has a different regulatory pathway involving calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase. These contrasts are important because the phrase excitation–contraction coupling is used in all three contexts. The name denotes conversion from excitation to mechanical activation, not a single universal molecular sequence shared by every muscle cell.

13. Contractile machinery and mechanical response

A sarcomere lies between adjacent Z discs. Thin actin-containing filaments extend from Z discs towards the centre and thick myosin-containing filaments lie centrally. During shortening, the filaments slide past one another without needing to shorten individually. The A band corresponds to the length of thick filaments and remains approximately constant in the classical sliding-filament model; the I band and H zone decrease as overlap increases during shortening. The relationship between sarcomere length and force follows partly from the number and geometry of possible actin–myosin interactions, although passive elasticity and other structural effects also contribute to whole-muscle behaviour.

Myosin heads can bind actin and hydrolyse ATP. ATP binding promotes detachment of strongly bound myosin from actin. Hydrolysis prepares the head in a higher-energy conformation with ADP and inorganic phosphate associated. Binding to accessible actin, changes associated with phosphate release and the power stroke generate displacement and force. ADP is released and a new ATP molecule permits detachment and another cycle. This simplified cycle captures the essential causal role of nucleotide state and thin filament activation. It is incorrect to describe ATP as needed only for the power stroke; ATP binding is also needed to release strongly attached heads, and ATP hydrolysis is required to restore their capacity for further cycles.

Force depends on how many cross-bridges bear tension, their state and mechanical geometry, not solely on calcium concentration. With calcium present but no available ATP, cyclic force generation cannot proceed normally and attached cross-bridges may persist. In addition, impairment of SERCA limits relaxation. Thus a sustained increase in muscle stiffness does not necessarily indicate increased neural activity. Rigor is an example in which the normal cycle of attachment and detachment is interrupted rather than being driven by a train of motor neuron impulses.

A muscle twitch is the mechanical response to a single effective excitation. Its latent period includes the time needed for electrical propagation, calcium release, activation of cross-bridges and development or transmission of measurable force. The contraction phase reflects the rise of active tension, and the relaxation phase reflects decreasing activation and force. The action potential is much shorter than the complete mechanical twitch in ordinary skeletal muscle. A fibre may regain electrical excitability while mechanical tension from the earlier activation remains. This temporal mismatch makes repeated excitation capable of increasing tension even though separate electrical action potentials do not merge into one continuous larger action potential.

14. Mechanical behaviour beyond a single twitch

The word tension refers to force developed along the muscle's line of action. Mechanical work depends on displacement as well as force. For a constant force parallel to displacement, external work is W = Fd, with force in newtons, distance in metres and work in joules. Suppose a muscle produces an external force of 50 N while shortening by 0.04 m against a load. The external work is 2 J. If the same force is maintained isometrically with no displacement of the load, external work is zero, but the muscle still consumes metabolic energy. This result follows because cross-bridges cycle and calcium and ionic gradients require maintenance. It is not a contradiction to say that a muscle doing no external mechanical work can fatigue or generate heat.

Power is the rate of doing work. Under constant force during shortening, P = Fv, where v is shortening velocity in metres per second. A muscle exerting 40 N while shortening at 0.05 m/s performs external work at a rate of 2 W. Increasing load can reduce shortening speed even if activation is high. Consequently, maximum force and maximum power are not generally achieved under identical mechanical conditions. Isometric force can be substantial while external shortening velocity is zero, and unloaded shortening may be rapid but deliver little external force. The force–velocity relationship reflects contractile kinetics and is useful as a context for motor unit physiology: a neural command cannot specify movement speed or force independently of the mechanical load.

During an eccentric contraction, active muscle develops tension while being lengthened by an external load. It can absorb mechanical energy, for example when lowering an object in a controlled fashion. This does not imply that the motor neuron fires backwards or that calcium becomes an inhibitor. The same basic activation machinery is engaged, but mechanical conditions cause net lengthening instead of shortening. Active lengthening can involve distinct force behaviour compared with shortening, including contributions from elastic structures. In an exam problem, the direction of movement should not be used as the sole criterion for deciding whether the muscle is active. The relevant evidence may include neural discharge, muscle electrical activity and force opposing the imposed motion.

The length at which a muscle is activated also influences active tension. At an excessively short sarcomere length, filament geometry can interfere with effective interactions; at excessive length, overlap between thick and thin filaments is reduced. Between these extremes, effective overlap allows substantial active force. A passive length–tension component arises from elastic structures, including titin and connective tissue. The total force recorded during activation combines active and passive components. If a question asks which length produces maximal active force, it is not sufficient to choose the length at which total tension is largest; passive tension can rise markedly at long lengths. Subtracting appropriately measured passive force helps reveal the active component.

A numerical exercise makes that distinction concrete. Suppose a preparation has passive forces of 1, 3 and 12 N at three progressively increasing lengths, and the corresponding total forces during identical stimulation are 8, 15 and 20 N. Active force calculated by subtracting passive force is 7, 12 and 8 N, respectively. The middle length produces the largest calculated active force even though the longest preparation produces the greatest total force. These are illustrative values. The subtraction assumes that passive and active contributions can be treated additively for the stated measurement, a useful approximation rather than a universal description of muscle mechanics. This example shows why activation state, length and measured total tension must be specified together.

A whole muscle contains fibres arranged with different orientations and architectural relationships to tendons. Some fibres are aligned nearly parallel to the direction of force transmission, whereas others are arranged at an angle. The mechanical output measured at the tendon is therefore not always equal to a simple unweighted sum of every individual fibre's longitudinal force. Cross-sectional area, fibre length, pennation and tendon compliance influence the result. In the context of motor units, an increase in recruited fibre number generally increases force at comparable conditions, but the mapping between neural input and external joint torque includes muscle architecture and lever geometry. This explains why a change in limb angle can change the external force produced by a similar neural activation.

15. Motor units and graded control of tension

A motor unit consists of an alpha motor neuron and all of the extrafusal skeletal muscle fibres it innervates. A muscle consists of many motor units, whose fibres can be intermingled rather than arranged as isolated blocks. The innervation ratio is the number of muscle fibres supplied by one motor neuron. Muscles requiring very precise movement often contain units with small innervation ratios, permitting small increments in total force. Muscles that generate large forces can contain much larger units. However, force per unit depends on fibre dimensions, contractile properties and mechanical arrangement as well as fibre number. A unit with twice as many fibres does not invariably generate exactly twice the force of another unit.

Slow motor units typically contain slow oxidative fibres with relatively long twitch times, substantial oxidative metabolic capacity and good fatigue resistance. Fast fatigue-resistant units and fast fatigable units provide progressively different combinations of contraction speed, force and endurance. These are useful physiological categories, not immutable labels for every muscle or species. Human skeletal muscle fibre classification commonly distinguishes type I, IIa and IIx; type IIb expression is prominent in some other mammals but is not the usual major adult human limb muscle designation. A change in habitual activity or innervation can alter fibre properties. The neuron and the muscle fibres form a functionally coordinated system rather than independent static components.

Motor unit recruitment means bringing additional units into active discharge. For many gradually increasing voluntary and reflex contractions, recruitment tends to follow the size principle: smaller motor neurons with lower recruitment thresholds become active before larger neurons with higher thresholds. Henneman's classical work related recruitment susceptibility to motor neuron size. A simplified electrical explanation uses the relation ΔV = I Rin, where ΔV is the membrane voltage change, I is the imposed current and Rin is input resistance. A smaller neuron often has higher input resistance, giving a larger voltage response to the same current. Actual synaptic conductances, intrinsic membrane properties and network input complicate this simple model, and task-dependent departures from a strictly fixed recruitment order are possible.

For a hypothetical example, two neurons have input resistances of 100 MΩ and 40 MΩ. A sustained net input current of 0.15 nA gives approximate depolarisations of 15 mV and 6 mV, respectively, if the passive linear approximation holds. If each requires 10 mV additional depolarisation to reach threshold, the first is recruited and the second is not. Increasing current to 0.3 nA gives 30 and 12 mV, sufficient to bring both to threshold under the assumed conditions. These values are illustrative, not recorded measurements. The calculation explains a tendency towards recruitment order; once action potentials begin, the neuron cannot be treated as a passive resistor.

The second principal control mechanism is rate coding: variation of the firing rate of active motor neurons. Each discharge of a normally functioning motor unit produces activation of its fibres. If impulses occur far apart, individual mechanical twitches are separated. If another impulse arrives while tension from the previous twitch remains, calcium activation and the mechanical response overlap. Increasing stimulation frequency therefore increases mean force, eventually approaching a fused tetanus at sufficiently high frequency. The exact frequency depends on fibre type, temperature, fatigue and other conditions. A slower unit with longer twitches may reach fusion at a lower frequency than a faster unit.

Incomplete tetanus has visible oscillations in tension as mechanical responses partially relax between impulses; fused tetanus lacks discernible oscillations at the measurement resolution. Neither implies that action potentials become one prolonged depolarisation. In fact, repeated spike generation requires recovery of electrical excitability. The word tetanus in this context describes a sustained mechanically summated contraction, not the infectious disease. A cardiac muscle action potential, with its prolonged refractory period, generally precludes the corresponding physiological tetanic summation characteristic of skeletal muscle. This difference supports rhythmic filling and pumping in the heart.

Recruitment and rate coding usually operate together. Consider a simplified isometric muscle with three motor units whose forces at one fixed discharge rate are 2 N, 5 N and 10 N. If the first unit is active, its contribution is 2 N. If the first and second are active, their combined contribution is approximately 7 N. With all three active it is approximately 17 N. If a higher firing rate raises the first unit's force to 3 N while the other units remain inactive, force increases without recruitment. The simple addition assumes fixed muscle length, identical mechanical transmission and negligible interaction between the unit contributions. Actual whole-muscle force depends on tendon compliance, geometry and fatigue as well as the nervous command.

16. Motor unit diversity and experimental recruitment evidence

The physiological categories of motor units are best understood by examining their measured properties. A single motor axon can be stimulated while the force generated by its innervated fibres is recorded. The twitch duration, peak force and decline during repetitive stimulation provide complementary information. A slow, fatigue-resistant unit can have a long twitch, modest force and small decline during sustained activity. A fast fatigue-resistant unit can develop force more rapidly while retaining substantial endurance. A fast fatigable unit can generate a large initial response but show a pronounced decline during repeated stimulation. These properties form continua in real muscles, and the boundaries between categories are convenient descriptions rather than strict natural partitions.

A unit's fatigue resistance is influenced by metabolic organisation. Oxidative fibres contain machinery capable of sustaining ATP production through aerobic metabolism, and their oxygen delivery is supported by appropriate vascularisation. Glycolytic metabolism can provide ATP rapidly during demanding activity, while repeated high-intensity contractions alter intracellular metabolites and ionic homeostasis. Neither oxidative nor glycolytic metabolism operates as an exclusive pathway in a living muscle. The relative contribution depends on workload, duration, oxygen supply and fibre properties. A statement that slow fibres use only aerobic metabolism or fast fibres never use oxidative metabolism would be scientifically inaccurate. The difference is one of capacity, kinetics and typical functional use.

An innervation ratio can be estimated if the number of muscle fibres and motor neurons is known, but an average can conceal heterogeneity. Suppose a hypothetical muscle contains 12,000 fibres distributed among 100 motor neurons. The mean innervation ratio is 120 fibres per neuron. That value does not imply every unit contains exactly 120 fibres. If 20 small units each contain 30 fibres, they account for 600 fibres, leaving 11,400 for the remaining 80 units, which have a mean ratio of 142.5. Such an arrangement could allow low-force precision and large-force capacity within the same muscle. Fibre counts alone would still not reveal the force of each unit because fibre cross-sectional areas and physiological properties may differ.

The neural basis of recruitment was investigated through recording of motor axons during progressively increased reflex input. Henneman's 1957 study and later work related recruitment susceptibility to motor neuron size. The prediction was not simply that larger axons produce larger extracellular spikes, although extracellular spike size in a given preparation could help identify axons. The physiological inference was that neurons with different sizes have different susceptibility to discharge during common excitatory input. The later account integrated this neural property with motor unit force characteristics. This classical reasoning is important because the observations connected electrical properties of motor neurons with the organisation of graded mechanical output.

The electrical interpretation of the size principle is strongest when comparable net excitatory input reaches neurons with different input resistances. If a large neuron receives proportionally more excitatory synaptic current, or different patterns of inhibitory input, its activation may not follow a prediction based on input resistance alone. Intrinsic conductances can also amplify or sustain depolarisation. Consequently, the principle should be treated as a robust physiological tendency under many ordinary patterns of synaptic drive, not a theorem derived solely from the diameter of a cell body. A question describing selective stimulation of a particular descending pathway may require consideration of how that pathway distributes its synapses across the motor pool.

Motor-unit recordings during a ramp contraction can reveal both recruitment and subsequent rate changes. A plot of discharge events against time may show a low-threshold unit firing before a second unit joins. The first unit can increase its rate while the second begins at a relatively low rate. If the experiment also records force, it becomes possible to relate unit discharge to the mechanical task. But the external force includes the contributions of units that were not recorded; it is not appropriate to assign all force after recruitment to the newly identified unit. The most defensible inference from a single-unit spike train is when that unit discharged and at what rate, with mechanical attribution requiring additional information.

Suppose one motor unit produces six impulses during a 0.5 s observation interval and another produces ten impulses during the same interval. A simple average discharge count per second gives 12 and 20 Hz, respectively. The average rate does not describe the exact timing of individual impulses. The first unit might discharge regularly, while the second could produce a burst followed by a long pause. Two trains with the same average rate can produce different short-term tension profiles because the degree of summation depends on interspike intervals. This is why experimental force records are often interpreted alongside the actual time sequence of spikes rather than only the average number per second.

The concept of recruitment should also be separated from the concept of voluntary activation. A person may increase neural drive but fail to activate all available units or drive them at rates sufficient for maximal force. One approach to investigating this is to compare voluntary force with a superimposed evoked response. If an appropriately delivered stimulus produces additional force during a supposedly maximal voluntary effort, this suggests that the available muscle force was not fully expressed by the voluntary command under the test conditions. Interpretation requires care about stimulation, muscle length, fatigue and force summation. The logic is to probe reserve capacity rather than equate the size of an evoked increment with an exact count of silent motor units.

17. Force, time and the organisation of the motor pool

The nervous system can achieve a smooth whole-muscle contraction without requiring all active motor units to discharge at the same instant. Independent units typically discharge at different times, so their individual force fluctuations are partly averaged by summation of mechanical output. This property is particularly valuable when a person maintains a small steady force: the units need not be fused into a maximal tetanus. Instead, overlapping twitches from a modest number of active units produce relatively stable tension. Synchrony can occur because motor neurons receive shared inputs, but complete synchrony is not required and can increase fluctuations under some conditions.

To understand the contribution of rate coding, consider a hypothetical unit with a twitch that lasts roughly 100 ms. At a discharge rate of 5 Hz, the average interval between impulses is 200 ms, leaving substantial time for tension to decline. At 20 Hz the interval is 50 ms, shorter than the twitch duration; mechanical responses overlap. At 50 Hz the interval is 20 ms, and under otherwise appropriate conditions force can become more sustained. The conversion is T = 1/f, where f is frequency in hertz and T is interval in seconds. It does not determine a universal fusion frequency because twitch duration, calcium dynamics and force relaxation vary across fibre types. The calculation identifies whether intervals are long or short relative to the mechanical response, not whether fusion must occur at an exact frequency.

Motor unit forces commonly increase nonlinearly with discharge frequency and tend towards an upper region where further frequency increases have smaller effects. At low rates, each extra impulse can add substantially to the time spent under tension. As calcium activation becomes more continuous and active force approaches its attainable maximum at a fixed length, there is less room for further increase. Fatigue and transmission limitations can also change the relation at high rates. Thus rate coding is not a linear amplifier that can increase tension without limit. Recruitment extends the force range by adding contributions from previously inactive units.

A useful quantitative distinction is between unit recruitment threshold and unit force. In a hypothetical task, unit A first begins discharging at 1 N of externally measured force, unit B at 4 N and unit C at 9 N. These values describe the total force level at recruitment, not the force produced by the newly active unit. At 9 N, unit C may contribute a relatively small initial force while earlier units continue discharging. The threshold reflects the task and the distribution of drive across the motor pool, whereas unit force reflects the properties and activation of its fibres. Conflating these measures makes interpretation of motor-unit recording graphs difficult.

The size principle explains why low-force actions are often economical and precise. Small, fatigue-resistant units can supply a sustained low mechanical output while large, rapidly fatiguing units remain inactive until the required force is greater. This arrangement does not mean that slow units become silent whenever fast units are recruited. During a gradually increasing contraction, earlier recruited units generally remain active as new units join them. During force reduction, some units stop discharging, and the pattern can differ from the order observed during rising force because intrinsic motor neuron properties and synaptic drive vary. A simple strict reverse sequence should therefore be treated as an approximation rather than a universal law.

Electrically stimulating a peripheral nerve differs from physiological recruitment. An external electrode depolarises axons according to their geometry, location and electrical properties, and larger axons may be preferentially excited under some stimulation conditions. This does not invalidate the size principle because the stimulation bypasses the usual synaptic activation of motor neuron cell bodies. It does mean that an externally evoked whole-muscle force trace cannot be interpreted as if it directly represented natural voluntary recruitment. When an examination problem specifies stimulation intensity rather than voluntary drive, identify which anatomical structures receive the input before predicting order of recruitment.

A motor pool is the population of motor neurons supplying a muscle. Although individual motor units are defined by their peripheral targets, the pool receives descending commands, sensory input and spinal interneuronal influences. Some inputs are shared across many neurons; others are distributed selectively. The resulting recruitment pattern can depend on the direction of movement, joint position and task. The size principle is powerful precisely because it predicts a common orderly relationship under many conditions, not because every movement activates every motor neuron through an identical imposed current. Proprioceptive control and descending coordination will be considered in the following nodes rather than being expanded into a separate motor-system chapter here.

Why the same total force can conceal different neural strategies

Assume an isometric preparation in which two active motor units contribute 4 N and 6 N, respectively, for a total of 10 N. A small increase in firing rate raises their individual mean forces to 5 N and 8 N. The measured total increases by 3 N without recruitment of an additional motor unit. In a second trial the firing rates of the original units do not change, but a newly activated unit contributes 3 N. The same final total, 13 N, is obtained by recruitment. The simple summation is valid as an approximation at fixed length when force contributions are measured under comparable conditions. It cannot establish that the underlying patterns are identical. A recording that identifies individual motor-unit discharges can distinguish the two cases: recruitment introduces a new discharge train, whereas rate coding changes the interspike intervals of units already active. A surface electrical signal alone may be less decisive because waveform cancellation, electrode geometry and the changing number of contributing fibres influence its amplitude. This is why combined force and motor-unit recordings are particularly informative when the physiological question concerns the neural strategy, not merely the mechanical output.

18. Fatigue and interpretation of experiments

Muscle fatigue is an activity-dependent reduction in the capacity to generate force or power. A fall in measured force may arise from reduced neural drive, failure of excitation, impaired calcium release, changes in myofibrillar force production or several factors together. Peripheral fatigue refers to processes distal to the central nervous system, while central fatigue involves reduced ability to activate muscle voluntarily through central neural mechanisms. A normal neuromuscular junction often has a substantial transmission reserve, so failure of transmission is not the only or inevitable explanation for fatigue during ordinary activity.

ATP supply, phosphocreatine, glycolytic and oxidative metabolism support contraction and ion handling. During intense repeated activity, inorganic phosphate and other intracellular changes can alter cross-bridge function and calcium handling. Extracellular and T-tubular potassium accumulation can change membrane excitability. The relative importance of these mechanisms varies with the contraction protocol, fibre type and physiological condition. It is misleading to attribute all fatigue to accumulation of lactic acid, or to assume that all ATP must be depleted before fatigue occurs. A protective fall in force can precede a catastrophic failure of cellular energy homeostasis.

A nerve–muscle preparation can help localise a physiological defect. Indirect nerve stimulation tests the motor axon, synaptic transmission, muscle action potential generation, excitation–contraction coupling and mechanical output. Direct muscle stimulation can bypass nerve and synaptic transmission, although care is needed to ensure the stimulus actually excites muscle fibres rather than nearby nerve branches. If nerve stimulation produces no force but direct muscle stimulation produces a normal response, the defect is likely upstream of the muscle contractile apparatus. If both stimuli produce normal muscle action potentials but neither produces force, the defect lies after electrical excitation, such as impaired SR calcium release or contractile function. Recording a calcium transient provides an additional discriminating measurement.

Electromyography (EMG) measures electrical activity associated with muscle fibre excitation. Surface electrodes detect summed electrical signals affected by electrode placement, tissue conductivity, fibre orientation and cancellation of overlapping waveforms. A compound muscle action potential elicited by supramaximal nerve stimulation is a measure of the synchronised electrical response of activated fibres, not a direct measurement of their mechanical tension. An increased EMG amplitude during a voluntary contraction can be consistent with recruitment and changing discharge rates but cannot by itself determine how many units were active. Simultaneous force and electrical measurements distinguish electrical activation from mechanical efficiency.

As an illustrative paired-recording experiment, control nerve stimulation produces a muscle action potential, a normal calcium transient and a peak twitch force of 10 N. Intervention X preserves the muscle action potential but reduces the calcium transient to half its original peak and decreases force to 3 N. This points towards altered calcium handling or coupling, with a nonlinear relationship between free calcium and active tension. Intervention Y preserves both action potential and calcium transient but produces only 3 N, directing attention towards calcium sensitivity, cross-bridge performance or force transmission. The numerical values describe hypothetical teaching observations, not a specific published experiment. The purpose is to locate the first abnormal step rather than assume that equal force deficits have the same origin.

A few selective disturbances provide useful physiological contrasts. A competitive antagonist of muscle nicotinic receptors lowers the postsynaptic response to ACh. In myasthenia gravis, immune-mediated disruption of the postsynaptic apparatus commonly reduces the transmission reserve. In Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome, impaired presynaptic calcium-channel function reduces transmitter release, and transient improvement with repeated activity can reflect residual calcium-dependent facilitation. Botulinum toxin impairs vesicular release through cleavage of components of the fusion machinery. These examples are included as mechanism tests, not as diagnostic rules or treatment instructions. A given symptom of weakness does not uniquely identify the affected molecular process.

19. Integrating fatigue, recovery and physiological adaptation

Fatigue develops differently during a sustained low-force task and a brief maximal effort. During low force, relatively fatigue-resistant units may carry much of the demand. If their output declines or the task continues, additional units can be recruited and the discharge rates of active units can change. Maintaining the same external force can therefore require increasing neural drive. A constant force record is not evidence that the underlying muscle state is unchanged. Conversely, if the external demand is greater than the remaining attainable force, force may fall even when neural effort is substantial. The distinction between demand and capacity is central to interpreting a fatigue experiment.

The role of phosphocreatine provides a useful connection to bioenergetics. Phosphocreatine can donate a phosphate group to ADP via creatine kinase, helping buffer ATP concentration during rapid ATP utilisation. As activity proceeds, phosphocreatine can decline while ATP remains relatively preserved and inorganic phosphate rises. A fall in force under these conditions is compatible with altered cross-bridge performance and calcium handling, without requiring ATP concentration to have approached zero. Recovery depends partly on restoring metabolic conditions through energy supply, and the time required for metabolic recovery need not be identical to the time needed for membrane excitability or neural drive to recover. Multiple time courses may therefore be evident after repeated activity.

During repetitive muscle excitation, ions are redistributed across membranes. Potassium leaves during repolarisation and may accumulate in restricted extracellular spaces, including T-tubular regions. Sodium entry and pump activity also influence ionic gradients. A change in local potassium concentration can alter the resting voltage and availability of sodium channels. The sodium–potassium ATPase helps maintain the gradients that make repeated electrical responses possible. It is not responsible for the rapid repolarising current of each individual action potential in the same way as voltage-dependent channel currents. Distinguishing the fast electrical mechanism from the slower maintenance process connects muscle fatigue directly to the membrane physiology of the earlier nodes.

If repeated stimulation leads to a reduced calcium transient while the surface muscle action potential appears unchanged, altered SR release is a plausible contributor, but one must consider propagation into the T-tubules. A surface recording does not necessarily reveal electrical conditions throughout the fibre interior. Thus the phrase normal action potential should be qualified by where it was measured. In a carefully controlled preparation, complementary recordings can help distinguish a failure of excitation deep within the fibre from failure of the coupling machinery despite adequate local depolarisation. This refinement prevents overinterpretation of the simple sequential localisation model while preserving its usefulness for exam-level reasoning.

The relation between fatigue and lactate deserves particular care. Lactate formation is coupled to metabolic pathways that support rapid ATP production, and lactate can be transported and utilised by other tissues or oxidised under suitable conditions. Its presence during intense activity does not establish that it alone causes the observed decline in tension. Changes in pH, inorganic phosphate, calcium release and other factors can influence performance in different circumstances. To infer a causal role for any metabolite, one would need to distinguish its concentration from the many other changes accompanying exercise, for example by comparing contractile responses under controlled intracellular conditions. A correlation during exercise is insufficient to establish a single universal cause.

Motor units are adaptable over longer time scales. Regular endurance activity can improve oxidative capacity and fatigue resistance, whereas resistance activity can increase muscle force capacity through a combination of neural adaptation and muscular changes. A larger force after training need not imply that each individual action potential has increased in amplitude. The improvements can arise through greater effective neural activation, changes in muscle size and architecture, or altered properties of individual fibres. Conversely, after reduced activity or denervation, muscle properties can change even if the contractile proteins were initially intact. These examples place the neuromuscular system within the broader principle that cellular structure and function respond to patterns of use.

A useful concluding experiment would measure four quantities before and after a standardised repeated activation protocol: the amplitude of the evoked compound muscle electrical response, the free calcium transient, the isometric twitch force and the recovery of force with rest. If electrical response is stable but calcium and force both decline, the evidence favours a process beyond the surface electrical response. If calcium remains comparable while force declines, changes in myofibrillar response or mechanical transmission become more plausible. If all three electrical, calcium and mechanical responses decline, the earliest disturbance may be electrical or junctional, with downstream effects arising secondarily. The measurements cannot be treated as absolute proof of one molecular lesion, but they guide the formulation and testing of increasingly specific hypotheses. This causal approach is more useful than memorising a list of fatigue factors, and it applies equally to the control of muscle tone and posture developed in the subsequent chapters.

20. Reasoning from an integrated set of recordings

A particularly instructive approach to an examination problem is to define what each observable represents before attempting to name the affected mechanism. A miniature synaptic event represents the effect of spontaneous transmitter release on a small local region of the muscle membrane. An evoked end-plate current represents the collective receptor current at the imposed voltage, subject to the measurement conditions. A muscle action potential represents successful regenerative excitation at the recording site. A calcium transient represents a change in free calcium within the region sampled by the indicator. A force trace represents the mechanical consequences of activation and transmission of tension. They are related sequentially but do not measure the same phenomenon. The earliest abnormal observable can localise a defect more effectively than the magnitude of the final change in force.

Imagine an experimental series in which a control preparation has miniature currents averaging 0.4 nA, evoked currents averaging 12 nA and a normal muscle spike. After intervention A, miniature currents average 0.2 nA and evoked currents 6 nA. If linear summation is appropriate, both conditions give an estimated mean quantal content of 30. A change in the response to individual quanta is therefore consistent with the observations. The data do not identify whether there is less transmitter per vesicle or diminished receptor function; a controlled agonist response or an independent measure of vesicle content could help discriminate between these possibilities. After intervention B, miniature currents remain at 0.4 nA but evoked currents average 6 nA, suggesting an estimated mean release of 15 quanta. The same evoked amplitude occurs after A and B, yet the miniature measurements reveal that different causal explanations deserve consideration. These hypothetical values also show why a mean evoked response alone cannot identify a presynaptic or postsynaptic site of action.

Consider a second experiment in which three interventions each reduce a muscle's peak tension to 40 percent of its initial value. With intervention C, many fibres fail to generate muscle action potentials after nerve stimulation but remain mechanically responsive to direct excitation. With intervention D, the fibres generate action potentials under either form of stimulation but have reduced calcium signals. With intervention E, calcium signals are comparable to control but force is low. These observations support different regions of the causal sequence: before successful fibre excitation in C, between electrical excitation and calcium availability in D, and between calcium availability and external force in E. Nevertheless, the regions are not single proteins. A membrane conduction disturbance and receptor blockade could both contribute to C, for example. Correct Part C reasoning identifies the most specific conclusion justified by the observations and rejects answers that demand more molecular certainty than the experiment supplies.

An integrated control system also needs to distinguish force regulation from force production. A motor neuron may discharge because of descending excitation, a sensory reflex or a combination of inputs; the end plate does not label the origin of that discharge. Provided the spike reaches the terminal and transmission succeeds, it initiates the same downstream excitation–contraction pathway. Similarly, an individual extrafusal fibre is not instructed by the motor neuron to select a particular percentage of its maximum force independently of other conditions. Its output depends on the timing of excitation, calcium availability, contractile properties and mechanical state. The nervous system produces a graded command chiefly by distributing and timing activity across motor units. Feedback allows that command to be continually corrected when load changes or fatigue develops. This final distinction forms the link from cellular neuromuscular physiology to the control of posture.

The neuromuscular junction and the motor unit are the output stages of a much larger control system. The same recruitment and rate-coding machinery is used in voluntary movement and reflex activity. In the next chapter, the feedback signals generated by muscle spindles and tendon organs explain how changes in length and force can modify motor neuron discharge. The essential distinction is that an extrafusal motor unit produces force; proprioceptive receptors supply information used to adjust that output. Neither the mechanical response nor the sensory signal alone constitutes a complete control loop.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A motor neuron innervates just one muscle fibre. An alpha motor neuron can branch to many fibres, forming one motor unit. Students confuse the usual one motor input per mature fibre with the opposite direction of connectivity. In a branching diagram trace from the axon to all targets, not from a fibre to all nearby axons.
  2. The motor end plate is the nerve terminal. It is the specialised postsynaptic sarcolemma. The names of synaptic compartments are sometimes used loosely. Identify the cellular membrane bearing the muscle nicotinic receptors before choosing an option.
  3. Acetylcholine carries an impulse along the motor axon. The axonal action potential propagates electrically and triggers chemical release at the terminal. Students collapse propagation and transmission into one process. A normal terminal spike with absent release is possible.
  4. A nicotinic receptor is a sodium-selective voltage-gated channel. It is a ligand-gated cation channel permeable to sodium and potassium. The inward current at rest encourages the sodium-only misconception. Examine receptor gating and reversal separately from regenerative sodium-channel activation.
  5. An EPP is an all-or-none propagated action potential. It is a local graded response that can fail to reach spike threshold. Because transmission normally succeeds, the two events seem inseparable. A postsynaptic sodium-channel block can preserve the EPP while suppressing the spike.
  6. At the receptor reversal potential no ions pass through open channels. Opposing ionic currents can balance to yield zero net current. Zero net electrical signal is mistaken for zero movement of every ion. Distinguish net current from ion-specific flux.
  7. A small miniature amplitude proves a postsynaptic receptor defect. It can also reflect reduced transmitter content per vesicle or different recording properties. Students equate quantal size exclusively with receptor number. Seek a controlled agonist response and comparable electrical conditions.
  8. Calcium entering the motor terminal directly activates troponin in muscle. Terminal calcium triggers ACh release; SR calcium in the fibre activates troponin. Two calcium-dependent events occur in sequence in separate compartments. Identify the membrane crossed in each event.
  9. CaV1.1 and RyR1 are the same channel. CaV1.1 is the T-tubular voltage sensor; RyR1 is an SR calcium release channel. Both proteins appear together in coupling diagrams. Label the two different membranes and distinguish voltage sensing from calcium release.
  10. All muscle tissues require the same calcium-triggering mechanism. Adult vertebrate skeletal coupling differs from ventricular cardiac calcium-induced calcium release and smooth-muscle regulation. The common term excitation–contraction coupling hides molecular differences. Use the tissue specified in the question.
  11. Relaxation needs no ATP. SERCA requires ATP for calcium reuptake and ATP binding permits myosin detachment. Relaxation is mistaken for cessation of all energy use. Predict prolonged stiffness when ATP-dependent recovery fails.
  12. Filaments become shorter during shortening. Thin and thick filaments slide; the A band stays approximately constant. Sarcomere shortening is mistaken for molecular filament shortening. Compare distances and overlap on a diagram.
  13. Larger force requires a larger neuronal spike. Recruitment and rate coding increase whole-muscle force; spike amplitude is not the principal force code. Students confuse graded mechanical output with graded action potential size. Identify the number of active units and intervals between spikes.
  14. Tetanus is a fusion of action potentials. Tetanus is summation of mechanical responses during repeated electrical discharges. The smooth force trace hides discrete spikes. Compare electrical and mechanical timescales.
  15. The size principle predicts every form of electrical stimulation order. It describes physiological recruitment tendencies, not necessarily externally stimulated axons. Students transfer the rule to direct stimulation without considering the site of input. Distinguish somatic synaptic drive from an external electrode.
  16. Fatigue means all ATP has been depleted. Fatigue can arise from central drive, excitability, calcium handling and myofibrillar changes before complete ATP depletion. A single-metabolite explanation is tempting. Locate the earliest abnormal signal using electrical, calcium and force recordings.

CSIR-NET Part B

1. At a mature mammalian skeletal neuromuscular junction, which molecule is the principal transmitter?

  1. Glutamate
  2. Noradrenaline
  3. Acetylcholine
  4. GABA
Answer and explanation

C. Acetylcholine Motor axons release acetylcholine, which activates muscle-type nicotinic receptors. Glutamate is common at excitatory central synapses and many arthropod NMJs, but not the typical mammalian end plate.

2. Which structure is strictly the motor end plate?

  1. The specialised postsynaptic sarcolemma
  2. The motor neuron soma
  3. The entire motor axon
  4. The SR terminal cisterna
Answer and explanation

A. The specialised postsynaptic sarcolemma The end plate is the muscle-side specialisation of the junction. The axon terminal is presynaptic, and the SR is an intracellular calcium store.

3. Which immediate event triggers normal evoked transmitter exocytosis?

  1. SERCA inhibition in muscle
  2. Troponin activation
  3. Potassium entry into vesicles
  4. Calcium entry at the nerve terminal
Answer and explanation

D. Calcium entry at the nerve terminal Voltage-gated presynaptic calcium channels open during terminal depolarisation. The local calcium rise activates fusion machinery; troponin and SERCA operate downstream in muscle.

4. What is the adult mammalian muscle nicotinic receptor?

  1. A G-protein-coupled receptor
  2. A ligand-gated cation channel
  3. A voltage-gated sodium channel
  4. An ATP-driven pump
Answer and explanation

B. A ligand-gated cation channel ACh binding gates the receptor's ion-conducting pore. Regenerative muscle sodium channels are distinct voltage-sensitive proteins.

5. Which molecule hydrolyses acetylcholine in the cleft?

  1. Choline acetyltransferase
  2. Acetylcholinesterase
  3. SERCA
  4. Myosin ATPase
Answer and explanation

B. Acetylcholinesterase Acetylcholinesterase produces choline and acetate. Choline acetyltransferase synthesises ACh instead; the other ATPases have different substrates and functions.

6. Which statement distinguishes an EPP from a muscle action potential?

  1. Both propagate regeneratively
  2. Both are necessarily all-or-none
  3. An EPP is graded and local
  4. An EPP is a force measurement
Answer and explanation

C. An EPP is graded and local The EPP results from receptor conductance and varies with release and driving force. A propagated action potential arises through regenerative voltage-gated channel activation.

7. What is a miniature end-plate potential ordinarily attributed to?

  1. Spontaneous release of an elementary transmitter quantum
  2. Opening of all RyR1 channels
  3. A tetanic contraction
  4. Synchronous firing of all motor units
Answer and explanation

A. Spontaneous release of an elementary transmitter quantum A miniature potential commonly corresponds to the postsynaptic effect of one vesicle's transmitter. It need not trigger a muscle action potential or mechanical twitch.

8. Which enzyme synthesises acetylcholine?

  1. Acetylcholinesterase
  2. Creatine kinase
  3. Myosin light-chain kinase
  4. Choline acetyltransferase
Answer and explanation

D. Choline acetyltransferase Choline acetyltransferase combines choline and an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA. AChE hydrolyses the transmitter rather than synthesising it.

9. A skeletal muscle triad contains which structures?

  1. Three SR cisternae
  2. Two T-tubules and one SR cisterna
  3. Three motor terminals
  4. One T-tubule and two SR terminal cisternae
Answer and explanation

D. One T-tubule and two SR terminal cisternae A transverse tubule is flanked by two terminal cisternae of the SR. Their adjacent membranes support voltage-dependent communication.

10. What is the major immediate source of activating calcium in adult mammalian skeletal muscle?

  1. Extracellular influx through presynaptic channels directly into myoplasm
  2. Sarcoplasmic reticulum
  3. Nucleus
  4. Mitochondrial ATP alone
Answer and explanation

B. Sarcoplasmic reticulum RyR1 releases calcium from the SR into myoplasm. Extracellular calcium entry at the nerve terminal initiates ACh release, a different stage.

11. Which protein serves as the T-tubular voltage sensor in skeletal muscle excitation–contraction coupling?

  1. CaV1.1
  2. RyR2
  3. Troponin I
  4. Acetylcholinesterase
Answer and explanation

A. CaV1.1 CaV1.1 responds to membrane depolarisation and couples to SR RyR1. RyR2 is associated mainly with cardiac SR release; troponin I is a thin-filament regulatory protein.

12. Which protein binds activating calcium in the skeletal thin filament regulatory system?

  1. Troponin T
  2. Titin
  3. Troponin C
  4. Choline acetyltransferase
Answer and explanation

C. Troponin C Troponin C is the calcium-binding component. Troponin T associates with tropomyosin, while troponin I has an inhibitory role.

13. Which process directly returns calcium to the SR during relaxation?

  1. SERCA-mediated uptake
  2. Nicotinic receptor opening
  3. RyR1-mediated release
  4. Sodium channel inactivation alone
Answer and explanation

A. SERCA-mediated uptake SERCA uses ATP to restore SR calcium stores. RyR1 is a release pathway, and sodium channel inactivation alone cannot clear myoplasmic calcium.

14. Which sarcomere region remains approximately constant in width during classical shortening?

  1. I band
  2. H zone
  3. A band
  4. Distance between Z discs
Answer and explanation

C. A band The A band reflects thick filament length, which is approximately unchanged by sliding. Thin–thick overlap increases, narrowing the I band and H zone.

15. What is the defining composition of a motor unit?

  1. One fibre and all nearby sensory neurons
  2. One alpha motor neuron and its innervated extrafusal fibres
  3. All neurons in the spinal segment
  4. All fibres of any muscle regardless of innervation
Answer and explanation

B. One alpha motor neuron and its innervated extrafusal fibres The motor unit is a functional output group specified by one motor neuron's branching targets. It is smaller than a motor pool, which contains many motor neurons.

16. Which process recruits an additional motor unit?

  1. Increasing action potential amplitude in an already active neuron
  2. Opening troponin binding sites without neural activation
  3. Increasing the length of an inactive muscle
  4. Increasing force by bringing another motor neuron into discharge
Answer and explanation

D. Increasing force by bringing another motor neuron into discharge Recruitment adds a previously inactive unit. Rate coding changes firing frequency of units that are already active.

17. Which units usually enter activity first under a gradually increasing common physiological drive?

  1. Small low-threshold units
  2. Large fast fatigable units
  3. All units simultaneously
  4. Only gamma neurons
Answer and explanation

A. Small low-threshold units The size principle describes a tendency for smaller, low-threshold motor neurons to recruit first. Gamma neurons regulate intrafusal fibre contraction, not the ordinary extrafusal motor-unit population.

18. Which property is characteristic of fused skeletal muscle tetanus?

  1. A single prolonged neuronal action potential
  2. Complete absence of calcium signalling
  3. Sustained mechanical force without discernible twitch oscillations
  4. Absence of ATP use
Answer and explanation

C. Sustained mechanical force without discernible twitch oscillations Repeated discrete excitation causes mechanical summation. Fusion refers to the force waveform, not fusion of electrical spikes or absence of metabolism.

19. Which is typically a characteristic of slow oxidative units?

  1. Obligatory absence of mitochondria
  2. Very short twitch relative to all fast units
  3. Exclusive anaerobic ATP production
  4. High fatigue resistance
Answer and explanation

D. High fatigue resistance Slow oxidative fibres usually sustain repeated low-force activation well and have considerable oxidative capacity. No fibre type uses just one metabolic pathway in every condition.

20. Which statement correctly defines muscle fatigue?

  1. Complete ATP depletion in every fibre
  2. Activity-dependent reduction in capacity to produce force or power
  3. Any absence of visible movement
  4. An increase in action potential amplitude
Answer and explanation

B. Activity-dependent reduction in capacity to produce force or power Fatigue is loss of performance capacity during or after activity. It may involve central drive, excitability, calcium handling or contractile machinery, without complete ATP depletion.

21. At fixed holding voltage, control miniature current is 0.4 nA and evoked current is 12 nA. After X they are 0.4 and 6 nA. Assuming linear summation, what is most supported?

  1. Quantal size doubles
  2. Quantal content falls from 30 to 15
  3. Quantal content remains 30
  4. SERCA must be blocked
Answer and explanation

B. Quantal content falls from 30 to 15 The ratios are 12/0.4 = 30 and 6/0.4 = 15. Unchanged mean miniature current supports reduced evoked release; it does not distinguish fewer release opportunities from lower probability. Calcium reuptake is downstream of these currents.

  1. Mean quantal content is halved
  2. All vesicle fusion ceases
  3. A higher muscle firing rate is demonstrated
  4. Mean quantal content is approximately unchanged but quantal response is smaller
Answer and explanation

D. Mean quantal content is approximately unchanged but quantal response is smaller Both ratios equal 20 under linear summation. Reduced receptor responsiveness or reduced vesicular ACh content could reduce quantal size. The data alone cannot distinguish those alternatives.

  1. −900 pA
  2. +900 pA
  3. −4 pA
  4. 0 pA
Answer and explanation

A. −900 pA Using I = g(V − E), 15 × (−60) = −900 pA. Negative current is net inward; it is not zero because the holding voltage differs from reversal.

24. At the reversal potential of a nicotinic receptor current, which interpretation is correct?

  1. Every open receptor is necessarily closed
  2. Sodium and potassium flux must each be zero
  3. Net current can be zero while opposing ion currents persist
  4. The membrane necessarily initiates an action potential
Answer and explanation

C. Net current can be zero while opposing ion currents persist Reversal denotes balance of ionic currents, not absence of all ion flux. Receptor opening depends on ligand, and the membrane's regenerative threshold is a separate condition.

  3. The SR directly generates the EPP
  4. An EPP of 20 mV reaches −65 mV and fails

D. An EPP of 20 mV reaches −65 mV and fails Twenty millivolts added to −85 gives −65 mV, below threshold. A failed regenerative response is not a half-amplitude propagated action potential.

  1. 0.37
  2. 2.7
  3. 1.0
  4. 37
Answer and explanation

C. 1.0 m = −ln(0.37) ≈ 0.994. This is a mean across trials and assumes genuine zero-release events and a suitable Poisson distribution.

  2. Mean 20 to 10, variance always zero
  3. Mean 80 to 40, variance unchanged
  4. Mean 20 to 40, variance doubles

28. Two fibres have the same synaptic conductance but one has a greater leak conductance. What is the likely effect on its passive EPP?

  1. Larger EPP necessarily
  2. Smaller EPP under comparable driving force
  3. Identical EPP regardless of membrane properties
  4. No receptor current can flow
Answer and explanation

B. Smaller EPP under comparable driving force Greater leak conductance reduces effective membrane resistance and shunts the depolarisation. The receptor may still conduct normally; voltage and current are different observables.

29. Nerve stimulation no longer produces muscle force. Direct fibre stimulation restores normal action potentials, calcium signals and force. Which region is implicated?

  1. A step upstream of muscle excitation
  2. Actin filaments necessarily absent
  3. SERCA necessarily absent
  4. A compulsory reduction in myofibrillar force
Answer and explanation

A. A step upstream of muscle excitation Successful direct stimulation indicates that the downstream muscle machinery can function in the test. Axon conduction, transmitter release or receptor-mediated activation remain possible sites; the experiment does not distinguish them.

30. Nerve terminal action potentials are normal, evoked EPP is small, and direct application of ACh produces a normal receptor response. What is a reasonable next hypothesis?

  1. Loss of all muscle nicotinic receptors
  2. Inability of myosin to bind ATP alone
  3. Loss of the SR alone
  4. Impaired presynaptic release
Answer and explanation

D. Impaired presynaptic release A normal agonist response argues against severe receptor dysfunction in that test. The combination points towards insufficient transmitter delivery, but vesicle content and release probability need separate investigation.

31. A selective muscle sodium-channel block preserves nicotinic receptor currents. Which sequence is expected after nerve stimulation?

  1. EPP absent, muscle spike intact
  2. Both must be unchanged
  3. EPP present, propagated muscle spike impaired
  4. Force rises with no calcium dependence
Answer and explanation

C. EPP present, propagated muscle spike impaired Ligand-gated end-plate current can persist independently of the regenerative muscle sodium current. The absence of a muscle spike prevents the ordinary downstream excitation sequence.

32. With direct stimulation, muscle spikes remain normal but SR calcium release and force fall. What does the evidence best establish?

  1. A presynaptic ACh synthesis defect is proven
  2. A defect at or after electrical excitation and before effective calcium activation
  3. Motor unit recruitment must have increased
  4. A specific RyR1 mutation is proven
Answer and explanation

B. A defect at or after electrical excitation and before effective calcium activation The neural step was bypassed. Reduced calcium with preserved measured excitation implicates coupling, propagation to internal membranes or calcium handling. It does not identify a specific mutation.

33. An intact skeletal fibre is directly stimulated after a selective reduction of CaV1.1 ion permeation that leaves voltage sensing and coupling intact. Which initial outcome is plausible?

  1. No SR release is possible in any case
  2. SR release and contraction may persist
  3. ACh release must double
  4. RyR1 becomes a nicotinic receptor
Answer and explanation

B. SR release and contraction may persist The immediate skeletal muscle trigger depends on voltage-dependent conformational coupling rather than CaV1.1 calcium influx itself. Long-term calcium balance and presynaptic calcium requirements are separate issues.

34. Two fibres have similar peak calcium signals. One has prolonged calcium decay and prolonged force. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. SERCA inhibition is conclusively demonstrated
  2. ACh receptor abundance must have doubled
  3. Reduced removal or prolonged release is plausible
  4. Cross-bridges cannot contribute to the force time course
Answer and explanation

C. Reduced removal or prolonged release is plausible The prolonged calcium signal supports altered net calcium handling, but several source, sink and buffer changes could account for it. Force kinetics also depend on cross-bridge dynamics and mechanics.

35. In permeabilised fibres, the calcium required to generate half-maximal tension increases, but maximum tension at saturating calcium remains normal. What is supported?

  1. Reduced calcium sensitivity
  2. Complete loss of functional myosin
  3. Failure of motor nerve propagation
  4. Increased sensitivity
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced calcium sensitivity A larger calcium concentration for the same relative tension indicates reduced sensitivity under comparable conditions. Preserved maximum tension argues against a necessary loss of maximal contractile capacity.

36. Two motor neurons have input resistances 120 and 50 MΩ. Both receive 0.1 nA and require 10 mV additional depolarisation. Under a passive approximation, which reaches threshold?

  1. Only the second
  2. Both
  3. Neither
  4. Only the first
Answer and explanation

D. Only the first ΔV = IR gives 12 and 5 mV. Only the higher-resistance neuron reaches the stipulated threshold. This calculation does not model active spiking or unequal synaptic input.

  1. 14 N and 20 N
  2. 6 N and 8 N
  3. 8 N and 14 N
  4. 2 N and 8 N
Answer and explanation

C. 8 N and 14 N Initially 2+4=6 N; adding 8 gives 14 N. This simple addition assumes mechanically independent contributions under the specified conditions; rates and length must be held comparable.

38. A twitch lasts roughly 80 ms. What is the interstimulus interval at 25 Hz and what does this imply?

  1. 40 ms, overlap is likely
  2. 80 ms, no possible overlap
  3. 250 ms, fused tetanus is guaranteed
  4. 4 ms, no action potentials occur
Answer and explanation

A. 40 ms, overlap is likely T=1/25 s=40 ms, shorter than the twitch duration. Summation is plausible, but exact fusion depends on calcium and mechanical kinetics.

39. A slow and a fast unit have comparable excitation reliability, but the slow twitch lasts longer. At a common intermediate firing frequency, which is more likely to show pronounced summation?

  1. The fast unit necessarily
  2. Neither can summate
  3. Both must show identical tension
  4. The slow unit
Answer and explanation

D. The slow unit Longer mechanical responses overlap more at a given interval. Peak forces and exact fusion rates may still differ due to other contractile properties.

40. A muscle produces a normal evoked electrical response and calcium transient but less force at identical length. Which additional test is especially relevant?

  1. Counting cranial nerves
  2. Controlled calcium–force relation in permeabilised fibres
  3. Recording an unrelated sensory receptor
  4. Measuring ACh synthesis alone
Answer and explanation

B. Controlled calcium–force relation in permeabilised fibres Controlled calcium exposure helps assess the force-generating apparatus and calcium sensitivity independently of neural transmission and SR release. Mechanical transmission remains another possible contributor.

41. After fatigue, voluntary force falls but a suitably evoked muscle response remains relatively preserved. Which inference is reasonable?

  1. All peripheral fatigue is ruled out
  2. ATP is necessarily zero
  3. The voluntary force loss must reflect AChE absence
  4. Reduced voluntary activation may contribute
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced voluntary activation may contribute Preserved evoked capacity with reduced voluntary output is compatible with a central contribution, but stimulus selectivity, fatigue and mechanical conditions matter. It does not prove that peripheral changes are entirely absent.

42. At three lengths passive force is 2, 5, 11 N and total stimulated force 10, 19, 22 N. Which length has the greatest calculated active force?

  1. First
  2. Second
  3. Third
  4. All equal
Answer and explanation

B. Second Subtract passive force: 8, 14 and 11 N. The middle length has greatest active force even though total force is highest at the third length.

43. A unit discharges 12 times during 0.6 s. A second unit has the same average rate but clusters spikes into bursts. What follows?

  1. Both average 20 Hz but may have different tension fluctuations
  2. Both necessarily have identical force traces
  3. The first has 7.2 Hz
  4. The second produces no contraction
Answer and explanation

A. Both average 20 Hz but may have different tension fluctuations 12/0.6 = 20 impulses per second. Mechanical summation depends on individual intervals, so equal mean rates do not guarantee identical force profiles.

44. An external electrode recruits a large motor axon before a small one. Does this alone refute the size principle?

  1. Yes, because the size principle covers all stimulation methods
  2. Yes, because axons cannot be stimulated
  3. No, because direct axonal stimulation bypasses normal synaptic recruitment
  4. No, because the size principle concerns only muscle fibre length
Answer and explanation

C. No, because direct axonal stimulation bypasses normal synaptic recruitment Physiological motor neuron recruitment depends on synaptic input and excitability. Extracellular stimulation depends on axonal geometry and electrode arrangement and can follow a different order.

Mixed NET / SET

45. Assertion: An EPP can occur without a muscle action potential. Reason: The EPP is a graded receptor-mediated depolarisation that may remain below threshold.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true reason false
  4. Assertion false reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true and reason explains assertion A local EPP is not regenerative. If it does not produce sufficient depolarisation at the spike initiation region, the muscle spike fails.

46. Assertion: ATP is needed during relaxation. Reason: SERCA uses ATP to pump calcium back into the SR.

  1. Both true but unrelated
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion true reason false
  4. Assertion false reason true
Answer and explanation

B. Both true and reason explains assertion Calcium reuptake helps terminate thin-filament activation. ATP binding also supports myosin detachment, providing another reason relaxation is energy-dependent.

47. Which matched pair is incorrect?

  1. CaV1.1 — T-tubular voltage sensor
  2. RyR1 — SR release channel
  3. AChE — vesicular ACh loading
  4. SERCA — SR calcium uptake
Answer and explanation

C. AChE — vesicular ACh loading Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses transmitter after release. Vesicular ACh transport instead depends on the proton gradient generated by a vesicular ATPase.

48. Which sequence correctly follows normal skeletal excitation?

  1. SR release → nerve spike → EPP → ACh release
  2. Troponin activation → ACh synthesis → nerve spike
  3. SERCA uptake → motor neuron recruitment → ACh binding
  4. ACh release → EPP → muscle action potential → SR calcium release
Answer and explanation

D. ACh release → EPP → muscle action potential → SR calcium release The nerve terminal releases transmitter first, producing receptor-mediated depolarisation and a muscle spike. The propagated muscle excitation initiates SR calcium release.

49. Which is a distinguishing feature of adult ventricular cardiac coupling compared with ordinary adult skeletal coupling?

  1. Cardiac contraction never requires calcium
  2. Cardiac cells use motor end plates identical to skeletal muscle
  3. Skeletal cells lack SR calcium
  4. Cardiac CaV1.2 calcium entry triggers RyR2 release
Answer and explanation

D. Cardiac CaV1.2 calcium entry triggers RyR2 release Cardiac cells use calcium-induced calcium release, whereas skeletal muscle primarily uses voltage-dependent coupling from CaV1.1 to RyR1. Both tissues employ calcium-dependent contractile regulation.

50. A muscle generates constant 50 N force while shortening 0.04 m in the force direction. What external work is performed?

  1. 2 J
  2. 0 J
  3. 20 J
  4. 1250 J
Answer and explanation

A. 2 J W=Fd=50×0.04=2 J. The expression applies to constant force parallel to displacement; metabolic energy expenditure exceeds or differs from external work.

51. A muscle has 9,600 fibres and 80 motor neurons. What is its average innervation ratio?

  1. 80
  2. 960
  3. 120
  4. 768000
Answer and explanation

C. 120 9,600/80=120 fibres per neuron. This mean does not imply equal unit sizes or equal unit forces.

52. Which best explains why an isometric contraction consumes ATP although external work is zero?

  1. No action potentials occur
  2. Cross-bridge cycling and ion transport continue
  3. Force is necessarily zero
  4. ATP is consumed only by sensory neurons
Answer and explanation

B. Cross-bridge cycling and ion transport continue Zero external displacement gives zero external work, but active tension requires metabolic processes including myosin cycling and calcium handling.

53. Which statement about miniature-event frequency is most defensible?

  1. It directly measures the number of active motor units
  2. It can reflect spontaneous presynaptic release dynamics
  3. It uniquely proves postsynaptic receptor number
  4. It equals twitch frequency in every fibre
Answer and explanation

B. It can reflect spontaneous presynaptic release dynamics Miniature events arise spontaneously; their rate is not automatically a measure of evoked release probability or force. Mean event amplitude and evoked responses provide complementary information.

54. Which feedback structure is the focus of the following node rather than the primary motor-unit force-producing element?

  1. Muscle spindle
  2. Extrafusal fibre
  3. Alpha motor neuron
  4. Motor end plate
Answer and explanation

A. Muscle spindle The spindle is a proprioceptive receptor with specialised intrafusal fibres. Its feedback modifies motor output, whereas the ordinary alpha motor unit produces force.

Revision and mastery

Mechanistic summary

Motor axon spike → terminal calcium entry → acetylcholine release → graded EPP → propagated muscle action potential → CaV1.1–RyR1 coupling → SR calcium release → troponin activation → cross-bridge force. AChE terminates synaptic action, SERCA restores calcium stores, and ATP supports cycling and relaxation. Recruitment adds units; rate coding changes discharge frequency.

Quantitative relationships

I = g(V − Erev); m ≈ EPP/MEPP for linear summation; m = np under the independent binomial model; m ≈ −ln(P0) under low-release Poisson assumptions; ΔV = IR in the passive model; T = 1/f; W = Fd for constant parallel force. Use the assumptions and units explained in the teaching sections.

High-yield distinctions

EPP versus action potential; terminal calcium versus SR calcium; CaV1.1 versus RyR1; recruitment versus rate coding; mechanical tetanus versus electrical spikes; central versus peripheral fatigue. Connect these distinctions with membrane physiology and synapses, and then with the proprioceptive feedback of 7.D.09.
