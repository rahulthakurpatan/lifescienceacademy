1. Why a photosynthetic organism still needs nitrogen

A seedling emerging from a seed encounters a problem that light and water alone cannot solve. It can make new carbohydrates by photosynthesis, but a carbohydrate contains no nitrogen. To divide its cells, construct membranes, produce enzymes and expand its photosynthetic apparatus, the seedling must also obtain nitrogen. Initially it may draw on proteins and other nitrogen compounds stored in its seed. Those reserves are finite. Once it begins sustained growth, there must be an external supply of usable nitrogen or an association that provides it. The problem is not whether the environment contains nitrogen atoms, but whether those atoms occur in forms that the organism can access and use.

Consider two identical seedlings growing in nutrient solutions. Both receive light, carbon dioxide, water and the same amounts of phosphorus, potassium and other minerals. One solution contains a usable nitrogen source and the other does not. At first both seedlings can grow by consuming their stored reserves. As these reserves become exhausted, the nitrogen-deprived seedling generally forms smaller leaves, loses greenness and accumulates much less dry matter. More light cannot replace the missing atoms. Indeed, if the machinery needed to capture light is not maintained, the extra illumination may accomplish very little. Nitrogen is therefore a material prerequisite for the production and maintenance of the photosynthetic machinery itself.

A plant does not normally take proteins from the environment and attach them directly to its own growing tissues. It constructs proteins according to genetic information. Each new peptide bond requires an appropriate amino acid; each amino acid includes nitrogen in its amino group. Some amino acids have additional nitrogen atoms in side chains. A shortage of usable nitrogen restricts the supply of building blocks for ribosomes and metabolic enzymes even when carbon is abundant. Conversely, obtaining nitrogen without sufficient carbon compounds does not make a plant capable of synthesizing all the amino acids it needs.

The requirement extends beyond proteins. Purines and pyrimidines supply nitrogen-containing bases for DNA and RNA. DNA is required for genome replication before cell division; RNA carries genetic information and performs functions in ribosomes and other cellular complexes. The adenine group of ATP also contains nitrogen. Several electron-transfer cofactors contain nitrogen, including NAD and flavins. Chlorophyll contains a nitrogen-containing tetrapyrrole-derived ring system surrounding a magnesium ion. Magnesium and nitrogen are both necessary for normal chlorophyll formation, but neither can substitute for the other. A defect in either mineral supply can cause chlorosis through different physiological mechanisms.

The large investment in nitrogen within a leaf is not limited to chlorophyll. Much of leaf nitrogen is associated with proteins, including enzymes that participate in carbon fixation, electron transport and biosynthetic reactions. Rubisco, for example, is an abundant soluble protein in many photosynthetic tissues. A leaf can look green while already experiencing changes in its protein composition and carbon assimilation. Therefore, visible leaf colour is a useful physiological clue but not an exact measurement of leaf nitrogen concentration or the nitrogen content of a specific enzyme.

The term essential mineral nutrient describes an element without which an organism cannot complete normal development and whose function cannot be fully replaced by a different element. Nitrogen qualifies because it contributes directly to many fundamental biological molecules. It is called a macronutrient because plants require comparatively large amounts, not because nitrogen atoms are unusually large or because its functions are more important than those of micronutrients. A nutrient needed in tiny amounts can be equally essential. For example, molybdenum is needed for the nitrate reductase cofactor in plants, but providing additional molybdenum cannot compensate for a complete absence of nitrogen.

Nitrogen also illustrates the distinction between concentration and total content. A young leaf may contain more nitrogen per gram of dry material than a large mature organ, yet the mature organ may contain more nitrogen in total because of its greater mass. As a plant grows rapidly, new carbon-rich structural material can dilute tissue nitrogen concentration even while total nitrogen increases. A concentration measurement without information on biomass cannot distinguish growth dilution from a decline in absolute nitrogen acquisition. This distinction becomes important when comparing genotypes, nutrient treatments or developmental stages in experimental questions.

Nitrogen is often redistributed within the plant. During deprivation, nitrogen-containing compounds in older tissues may be broken down and mobilized towards young growing tissues. Older leaves consequently often become chlorotic before younger ones. This pattern reflects mobility and allocation, not an exclusive ability of old leaves to lose chlorophyll. At severe deprivation all leaves may be affected. Root growth, leaf expansion and the timing of reproductive development can also change, and these responses depend on species, development, environmental conditions and how rapidly nitrogen becomes limiting.

To make sense of nutrition, follow an individual nitrogen atom through successive boundaries. First it exists outside the plant, perhaps in nitrate dissolved in soil water. Then it crosses a membrane through a transport protein. It may be retained temporarily in an inorganic pool or transported to a distant organ. If it is nitrate, its oxidation state must change before incorporation into amino acids. Finally it becomes covalently associated with a carbon skeleton. Each stage is distinct and experimentally separable. A plant may be excellent at absorbing nitrate but poor at assimilating it, or have a functional assimilation enzyme but a limited ability to acquire the external nutrient. This separation is the foundation of the entire nitrogen metabolism subunit.

2. Chemical forms of nitrogen and the problem of availability

Atmospheric air contains a very large amount of molecular nitrogen, N₂. Two nitrogen atoms share a strong triple bond in this molecule. Although the atmosphere continually surrounds the leaves, the vast majority of plants do not possess the enzyme machinery needed to reduce molecular nitrogen. Photosynthesis fixes inorganic carbon from carbon dioxide; it does not confer the ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen. These two expressions use the word fixation in different chemical contexts and must never be treated as synonyms.

The inorganic nitrogen forms of greatest importance to the roots of most terrestrial plants are nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺). They are dissolved ions rather than diatomic gases. Their charges have opposite signs and their nitrogen atoms have different oxidation states. Nitrate contains three oxygen atoms and has one negative charge overall. In calculating nitrogen oxidation state, each oxygen is assigned minus two, so the sum of nitrogen and the three oxygen contributions must equal minus one. Nitrogen in nitrate is thus assigned plus five. In ammonium, each hydrogen is assigned plus one, giving a nitrogen oxidation state of minus three.

These oxidation states are formal electron-accounting conventions, not the actual partial charge on a nitrogen atom. They nonetheless identify a major metabolic distinction. Conversion of nitrate nitrogen from plus five to minus three requires a total of eight electrons per nitrogen atom. Ammonium nitrogen is already in the reduced state appropriate for entry into many organic nitrogen compounds. Both forms require further metabolic work for amino acid synthesis, but nitrate imposes an additional reduction requirement. The conversion of atmospheric nitrogen, whose formal oxidation state is zero, to ammonia represents yet another reduction process. The enzymes responsible for those conversions are not interchangeable.

Nitrite (NO₂⁻) is an intermediate whose nitrogen oxidation state is plus three. In biological nitrate assimilation, nitrate is first reduced to nitrite and then nitrite is reduced to ammonium. Nitrite can accumulate under circumstances where its production exceeds its consumption. It is normally maintained at relatively low concentrations because excessive accumulation is potentially damaging. Nitrite may be present in the rhizosphere, but it is generally not the dominant nitrogen source for plant growth. A question listing nitrate, nitrite and ammonium should be solved by identifying the exact transformation rather than assuming all three ions enter the same metabolic step.

Ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) are related by an acid–base equilibrium. Ammonia accepts a proton to form ammonium. The equilibrium depends on pH and temperature. Near neutral pH, ammonium dominates in aqueous solution; as pH rises, the fraction present as uncharged ammonia increases. The distinction is biologically relevant because neutral ammonia and positively charged ammonium do not behave identically at membranes or in soil. A loss of ammonia to the atmosphere is not the same chemical process as nitrate leaching. Both remove nitrogen from an accessible pool, but the mechanisms and environmental influences differ.

Plants also have the capacity to obtain some organic nitrogen. Urea and certain free amino acids can be taken up in suitable circumstances, and associations with microorganisms may extend access to nitrogen in organic matter. The relative importance of these routes varies with habitat and species. It is incorrect to claim that every plant obtains all of its nitrogen exclusively as nitrate or that all soil organic nitrogen is directly available to roots. Large proteins and nitrogen-containing residues usually need decomposition or other processing before their nitrogen becomes readily accessible. Roots and microorganisms interact in obtaining the resulting small compounds.

Total soil nitrogen, plant-available nitrogen and measured mineral nitrogen are not identical quantities. A soil may contain substantial nitrogen in stable organic matter, yet little nitrate or ammonium at the moment of sampling. Such a soil is not necessarily capable of meeting the instantaneous demand of rapidly growing seedlings. Conversely, a temporarily high nitrate concentration may disappear after heavy rainfall, microbial uptake or other changes. A nutrient concentration at one moment is a stock measurement. The supply over a growing season depends on rates of replenishment and removal as well as initial stock.

The concept of bioavailability combines chemical form, physical accessibility and biological capacity to acquire the compound. A nitrate ion in a water-filled pore immediately adjacent to an active root membrane is much more accessible than an identical ion deep below the root system. Similarly, ammonium held on an exchange surface may become available as it is released into solution, whereas ammonium trapped in inaccessible sites cannot necessarily be acquired immediately. Availability is a property of an interacting environment and organism, not just of the identity of a molecule.

3. Soil nitrogen transformations and root access

Soil contains living organisms as well as minerals, water, air and organic residues. Microorganisms use nitrogen for the same fundamental reason plants do: construction of proteins, nucleic acids and other nitrogen-containing molecules. Their metabolic activity can both replenish and diminish the inorganic nitrogen pools available to roots. The transformations need to be distinguished according to the reactants and products, rather than memorized merely as names in a cycle.

Mineralization is the conversion of organic nitrogen into inorganic forms. During decomposition, microorganisms break down nitrogen-containing organic matter, assimilate some of the released nitrogen and may release ammonium. The term ammonification commonly refers to the generation of ammonia or ammonium from organic nitrogen compounds. A nitrogen atom originally present in a fallen leaf can therefore return to a soil mineral pool. The soil does not create new nitrogen atoms in the process; it changes their chemical form and location.

Immobilization is the incorporation of inorganic nitrogen into microbial biomass and other organic pools. A growing microbe may take up ammonium or nitrate that would otherwise be available to roots. This does not necessarily remove nitrogen from the soil ecosystem permanently. After microbial turnover and decomposition, some nitrogen can be released again. The simultaneous occurrence of mineralization and immobilization explains why a small measured mineral nitrogen pool can be associated with rapid nitrogen cycling. The net change is the difference between multiple gross fluxes, not a measurement of any single reaction rate.

Fresh organic material rich in carbon relative to nitrogen can increase microbial demand for external nitrogen. Microorganisms must use part of the carbon as energy and construct biomass with a characteristic requirement for nitrogen. If the decomposing substrate supplies inadequate nitrogen, the microorganisms may acquire more from surrounding mineral pools. Adding carbon-rich residues can therefore temporarily reduce mineral nitrogen available to a crop. The actual effect depends on decomposability, microbial activity, temperature, moisture and initial nutrient status, rather than on carbon-to-nitrogen ratio alone.

Nitrification is the biological oxidation of reduced inorganic nitrogen to more oxidized forms. Ammonia oxidation yields nitrite, which is subsequently oxidized to nitrate. Diverse bacteria and archaea participate; some organisms can carry out complete oxidation of ammonia to nitrate. This process is generally supported by oxygen and can change the dominant form of soil nitrogen over time. An ammonium-based fertilizer does not guarantee that roots will acquire only ammonium, because the supplied nitrogen can be transformed before reaching a root.

One consequence of nitrification is acid generation. Written in a simplified balanced form, oxidation of ammonium by oxygen to nitrate yields two protons and water for each ammonium ion oxidized. The protons may react with soil buffering materials, and changes in bulk soil pH depend on those buffers and on many other chemical and biological processes. Thus nitrification can contribute to acidification, but a measurement of pH alone does not uniquely identify the rate of nitrification.

Denitrification reduces oxidized nitrogen compounds towards gaseous forms such as nitrous oxide and molecular nitrogen, especially in environments where oxygen availability is limited and suitable electron donors are available. This process can remove fixed nitrogen from a root-accessible soil pool. It is not biological nitrogen fixation. The directional contrast is useful: fixation supplies reduced nitrogen from N₂, whereas complete denitrification returns nitrate-derived nitrogen to N₂. In some low-oxygen environments microbial nitrate reduction instead yields ammonium, a pathway called dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium. These processes have different products even when nitrate is a common starting material.

Physical transport matters alongside microbial conversion. Nitrate is often relatively mobile in soil water because many soil surfaces carry net negative charge and do not strongly retain anions under typical conditions. Ammonium, being a cation, can associate with negatively charged exchange sites. This tends to restrict its mobility compared with nitrate, although the extent depends on mineralogy and soil chemistry. Anion retention can occur in some variable-charge soils. Nitrate mobility is therefore a useful generalization, not a universal rule stating that no soil ever retains nitrate.

Rainfall or irrigation may carry dissolved nitrate downwards. If that nitrate moves beyond the zone occupied by absorbing roots, its accessibility declines even though the nitrogen remains in the soil profile for a time. Waterlogged soils present a different problem: limited oxygen changes both microbial nitrogen transformations and the root's ability to sustain aerobic metabolism. Rice roots can transport oxygen internally through aerenchyma and create local oxygenated zones, so even a flooded paddy need not be an entirely nitrate-free environment. Broad environmental descriptions must not replace examination of local conditions at root surfaces.

A rhizosphere is the portion of soil influenced by a root and its activity. Roots change the composition of this region by absorbing ions and water, releasing organic compounds and protons, and supporting interactions with microorganisms. A concentration measured far from roots may not represent the concentration encountered by a transporter in the epidermis. If uptake is faster than supply, a depletion zone develops around the root. This changes the gradients that drive movement through soil water and may increase competition among neighboring roots or microbes.

Three routes contribute to nutrient delivery to root surfaces. Water flow can carry dissolved ions towards the root as the plant absorbs water. Diffusion moves solutes in response to concentration gradients. Root growth brings new absorbing surfaces into contact with previously unexplored soil. The contribution of each route depends on soil water, chemical mobility, root distribution and the rate at which transporters remove the nutrient. Arrival at the root surface is not the same as cellular uptake: a dissolved ion still needs an appropriate route across the plasma membrane to enter the symplast.

The distinction between delivery and absorption provides a simple experimental prediction. If delivery through soil is limiting, increasing membrane transporter abundance may yield little additional whole-plant nitrogen acquisition. If membrane capacity is limiting, maintaining a high external concentration may yield a plateau rather than a proportionate rise in uptake. Real plants can change root architecture, transporter regulation and metabolic demand together, which is why one measurement at a single nutrient concentration cannot identify the dominant limitation.

4. Crossing the root membrane: the energy problem

A root cell is enclosed by a plasma membrane built mainly from a lipid bilayer and associated proteins. A membrane interior has low polarity, whereas nitrate and ammonium carry electrical charges and are surrounded by water molecules. Movement of a hydrated ion directly through the lipid phase is energetically unfavorable. A high concentration of nitrate outside the root does not automatically ensure rapid entry. Specific transport proteins provide a pathway and determine the rate, selectivity and energy dependence of membrane crossing.

The term apoplast includes cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membranes; the symplast refers to the interconnected cytoplasmic system linked by plasmodesmata. A nitrate ion in the cell wall of an epidermal cell is still outside its cytosol. An ion must cross at least one plasma membrane to enter the symplast. Further inward, the endodermal barrier limits uninterrupted apoplastic movement to the vascular tissues, contributing to selectivity. Detailed radial water and ion transport is developed separately in the solute transport subunit; the key point here is that uptake requires regulated movement across biological interfaces.

To determine whether an ion tends to enter a cell, its concentration difference is not enough. A positively charged ion is attracted to an electrically negative interior, whereas a negatively charged ion experiences the opposite electrical effect. Plant root plasma membranes commonly maintain a negative cytosolic electrical potential relative to the outside. This favors entry of cations under appropriate concentration conditions but opposes entry of nitrate electrically. A concentration gradient can oppose or reinforce the electrical term.

For one mole of an ion with charge number z moving from outside to inside, the electrochemical free-energy change is ΔG = RT ln(a_in/a_out) + zF(ψ_in − ψ_out). R is the gas constant, T absolute temperature in kelvins, F the Faraday constant, a activity and ψ electrical potential in volts. The first term is chemical; the second is electrical. For dilute illustrative solutions, concentrations can replace activities approximately. A negative ΔG denotes thermodynamically favorable inward movement; a positive value means inward movement requires coupling to another favorable process. The equation does not specify which protein provides the pathway or whether the flux is fast enough to sustain growth.

For example, suppose the membrane interior is 120 mV negative relative to the outside at 298 K and the internal and external nitrate activities are equal. The chemical term is zero. With z = −1, the electrical term is (−1)(96485 C mol⁻¹)(−0.120 V), about +11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. Nitrate entry is electrically unfavorable. In the same situation a monovalent cation has a negative electrical term of equal magnitude. The negative potential does not by itself prove the mechanism of ammonium transport, but it clarifies why the energetic problems of nitrate and ammonium are different.

Root cells maintain electrochemical gradients partly through the activity of plasma membrane H⁺-ATPases. These pumps use ATP to move protons from the cytosol to the external space. The movement helps establish an electrical gradient and a pH difference. The proton can then return through a suitable coupled transporter. In a proton–nitrate symporter, downhill proton movement can provide energy for the coupled entry of nitrate. ATP is consumed by the pump, not necessarily directly by the nitrate carrier. This is secondary active transport rather than primary active transport by the nitrate transporter itself.

Consider an illustrative tightly coupled transporter bringing two protons and one nitrate into the cytosol. If the external pH is 5.5 and internal pH 7.0, the internal proton activity is about 1/31.6 of the external activity. At 298 K the chemical term per proton is about −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹; with a membrane potential of −120 mV the electrical term adds −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. Each proton therefore has approximately −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹ of free energy for inward movement. If cytosolic nitrate activity is one hundred times the external value, the nitrate chemical term is about +11.41 kJ mol⁻¹ and its electrical term +11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. The sum for coupled entry is 2(−20.14) + 22.99, or about −17.29 kJ mol⁻¹. This model shows how a proton gradient can drive uptake against a combined chemical and electrical gradient. The two-to-one coupling ratio is an illustrative assumption for this calculation, not a claim that all nitrate transporters have identical stoichiometry under every condition.

The model also explains why conditions that change cellular energy status may influence nitrogen absorption. If a root becomes oxygen deficient, mitochondrial ATP production can decrease. The proton pump may no longer maintain its usual gradients, reducing the energy available for coupled entry. However, the magnitude of this effect depends on alternative ATP supply, acclimation, transporter regulation and the external pH. Oxygen is not consumed directly by every nitrate transporter. An observed decrease in nitrate uptake under hypoxia is therefore consistent with an energetic limitation but is not sufficient by itself to prove a particular molecular mechanism.

5. Nitrate transport systems: affinity, capacity and regulation

Root uptake experiments reveal that changing the external nitrate concentration can change the relationship between concentration and influx. At very low concentrations, transporters with sufficiently high affinity contribute substantially to acquisition. When the external nitrate supply is abundant, systems with lower apparent affinity may make an important contribution to total influx. These are described physiologically as high-affinity and low-affinity transport systems, often abbreviated HATS and LATS. They are functional descriptions, not a simple classification in which every transporter protein belongs exclusively to one category.

Two major nitrate transporter families in plants are NPF, formerly known as NRT1/PTR, and NRT2. Different family members contribute to root uptake and nitrate movement between tissues. NRT2 proteins are often associated with high-affinity nitrate transport. Some high-affinity systems require an accessory protein, such as NAR2/NRT3, for full transport activity. NPF members have diverse substrate and transport roles, so the presence of an NPF gene sequence does not establish that it mediates nitrate influx at the root surface.

Arabidopsis NPF6.3, traditionally called NRT1.1 or CHL1, is an especially instructive example. It has been studied as a nitrate transporter and as a component of nitrate sensing. Its activity can exhibit dual-affinity behavior, and phosphorylation of a regulatory threonine residue has been associated with changes in transport properties. The significance for an examination is that the affinity categories describe observable physiology and do not correspond perfectly to two mutually exclusive gene families. A mutation affecting nitrate responses may alter transport, signaling, or both; the phenotype has to be interpreted using experiments that separate those functions.

Affinity should not be confused with maximum transport capacity. Consider the illustrative saturation model J = Jmax S/(Km + S), where J is the influx per unit root mass per time, S is external nitrate concentration, Jmax is the limiting influx in this model, and Km is the concentration at half-maximal influx. A low Km indicates half-maximal activity at a lower concentration and is often described as high apparent affinity. Jmax is a separate parameter that depends on the amount and properties of the transport machinery. A system with a lower Km can still have a smaller maximum influx than a lower-affinity system.

Suppose two independent systems have Jmax values of 6 and 24 micromoles per gram root fresh mass per hour, and Km values of 0.02 and 2 mM respectively. At 0.02 mM external nitrate the first system operates at half its maximum, contributing 3 units. The second contributes about 0.238 units. The high-affinity system dominates. At 2 mM nitrate, the first contributes approximately 5.94 units and the second 12 units. The lower-affinity system now contributes more. The example is hypothetical and assumes independent additive fluxes with fixed parameters. Living roots also regulate expression and activity, and some uptake components do not follow a simple saturation curve across the entire concentration range.

The apparent Km measured for an intact root should not automatically be equated with a microscopic binding constant of an isolated transporter. External diffusion, membrane potential, pH, multiple transport pathways, efflux and internal nitrogen status influence the observed curve. An experimentally fitted parameter can be extremely useful without being a direct measure of binding chemistry. If the data do not reach a plateau, a fitted maximum rate may also be uncertain. Questions that ask which genotype has higher affinity should specify or provide enough data to distinguish a changed half-saturation concentration from a changed maximum capacity.

A plant can regulate its nitrate acquisition system in response to both local and systemic signals. The presence of nitrate can induce expression of certain nitrate transport and metabolic genes. A nitrogen-replete plant can reduce some high-affinity uptake capacities through feedback. Nitrogen starvation may increase the capacity to acquire nitrate when nitrate reappears, but long-term severe starvation can also reduce growth and physiological resources. The net result is a dynamic response rather than a fixed on/off switch. Timing, nitrogen form, nitrate concentration, tissue type and previous nutrition determine the measured behavior.

A split-root experiment illustrates local and systemic control. Imagine a root system divided into two containers with the same shoot supplying both halves. In one treatment, both root halves receive nitrate. In a second, one half receives nitrate while the other receives no nitrogen. If the nitrate-fed half changes uptake relative to the corresponding half in the first treatment, despite having the same external nitrate concentration, a signal associated with whole-plant nitrogen status may be involved. If only roots directly exposed to nitrate change a particular response, local signaling is implicated. Appropriate controls must distinguish changes in root mass, external pH and resource distribution. The next step is to identify the relevant signal rather than to infer that any difference automatically proves a specific peptide or hormone pathway.

6. Ammonium acquisition and the need for controlled influx

Ammonium differs from nitrate in both chemical and physiological behavior. It does not need to be reduced through nitrate reductase and nitrite reductase before it can enter amino acid metabolism. Its positive charge means that the negative electrical potential of a root cell can favor inward movement of an intact ammonium ion. Nevertheless, the presence of this favorable electrical contribution does not make the plasma membrane freely permeable to ammonium. The lipid bilayer remains a selective barrier, and specific membrane proteins provide the pathways through which ammonium-derived nitrogen is acquired.

Ammonium transporters of the AMT family are major components of root ammonium acquisition. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, several AMT1 family proteins contribute to high-affinity uptake. Different family members are expressed in different cell types or under different nutritional conditions. They may have complementary roles rather than a single indispensable member accounting for all uptake. The presence of multiple transporters also explains why eliminating one gene sometimes produces only a modest phenotype: other transporters can sustain part of the flux. A multiple-mutant comparison can reveal contributions that are difficult to identify by studying one mutant alone.

The precise molecular mechanism by which an AMT protein conducts ammonium-derived nitrogen should be distinguished from its physiological function. Some transport mechanisms involve ammonium recognition, deprotonation and movement of ammonia or coupled charge; the details vary among proteins and remain a subject of mechanistic investigation. It is appropriate to describe AMTs as mediating ammonium uptake at the physiological level. It is not appropriate to assume that every member behaves as an ordinary aqueous pore through which intact hydrated NH₄⁺ moves unmodified. The electrical calculation for a free monovalent ion provides an energetic reference, not a complete molecular model of all AMT proteins.

An elementary acid–base calculation helps clarify this distinction. In dilute water at approximately 25 degrees Celsius, the pKa of ammonium is close to 9.25. The Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship states that pH equals pKa plus the logarithm to base ten of the ammonia-to-ammonium activity ratio. At pH 7.0, that ratio is approximately 0.00562. The fraction of total dissolved ammonia plus ammonium present as ammonia is therefore 0.00562 divided by 1.00562, or about 0.56 percent. At pH 8.5, the ratio becomes approximately 0.178, corresponding to about 15 percent ammonia. Even though total dissolved nitrogen could be unchanged, the relative quantities of the neutral and charged species have changed substantially.

The calculation assumes equilibrium, a specified temperature and dilute-solution behavior. It does not describe how rapidly ammonia crosses a particular membrane, how strongly ammonium binds to a transporter, or how a living root alters the pH around itself. An ammonium transport experiment must therefore distinguish nominal total ammoniacal nitrogen from the actual chemical conditions experienced by the membrane. Increasing external pH could change chemical speciation and the electrochemical gradient simultaneously. A larger nitrogen flux at higher pH would not, by itself, identify the route by which nitrogen entered the cell.

At high external ammonium concentrations, other transport components may contribute to measured uptake in addition to high-affinity AMTs. As with nitrate, an intact-root influx curve reflects several processes. Experimental evidence for AMT involvement can come from a reduced influx in a transporter mutant, restoration through genetic complementation, localization to appropriate root cells and activity measured when the protein is expressed in another biological system. No single observation answers every question. A transcript expressed in a root indicates that the gene is active at the RNA level, not that a specific amount of transporter protein is present or that its transport activity is unrestricted.

There is a strong physiological reason to regulate ammonium influx rapidly. Ammonium is needed for metabolism, but excessive free ammonium can disrupt cellular homeostasis. The cell has limited capacity to incorporate incoming nitrogen into organic compounds. If entry persistently exceeds assimilation and export, inorganic nitrogen accumulates or the plant spends resources cycling it across membranes. A large external supply can consequently inhibit growth instead of improving it. This is not a contradiction of nitrogen's essentiality. Many nutrients have an optimum range of supply, below which growth is restricted and above which disturbances become important.

AMT proteins can be regulated both by changes in gene expression and by rapid modification of the protein. Research on Arabidopsis ammonium transporters has demonstrated feedback regulation associated with phosphorylation of a conserved threonine in a cytosolic regulatory region. A rapid increase in external ammonium can be followed by inhibition of uptake through this type of regulation. The conceptual significance is that the plant does not have to wait for transporter proteins to be degraded and replaced. It can modify the activity of existing membrane proteins on a shorter timescale.

A hypothetical time course demonstrates how to distinguish the two levels of regulation. Suppose root ammonium influx falls within ten minutes of exposing previously nitrogen-limited plants to a high ammonium supply. During the same period, the quantity of AMT protein detected in isolated plasma membranes remains unchanged. A decrease in the number of AMT molecules is unlikely to be the immediate explanation. A change in the activity of those molecules, in their local environment, or in the driving force becomes more plausible. If a phosphorylation-resistant mutant fails to display the normal rapid decline while maintaining similar protein abundance, the evidence for regulation through that modification becomes stronger. The mutation alone, however, may affect protein structure, so localization and basal activity are useful controls.

It is important not to confuse physiological feedback with passive saturation. A carrier-mediated influx can approach a plateau simply because all available transport sites become occupied. Feedback inhibition is a regulated decrease in activity in response to a condition such as excessive nutrient supply. Suppose uptake initially increases when ammonium rises from 0.05 to 1 mM but subsequently falls even though the external concentration remains constant at 1 mM. Simple instantaneous saturation at a fixed substrate concentration cannot explain the later decrease; changes in regulation, energetics, nutrient pools or other time-dependent processes must be considered.

A root that acquires ammonium must balance the flow of positive charge and maintain a tolerable cytosolic pH. Proton extrusion can accompany ammonium-based nutrition in many conditions, contributing to acidification outside the root. Nitrate-based nutrition frequently has a contrasting tendency towards rhizosphere alkalinization, but neither direction is guaranteed for every plant and solution. The uptake of other cations and anions, organic acid metabolism, ammonium assimilation, buffering and microbial transformations all contribute. These relationships should be treated as physiological outcomes of integrated charge and acid–base balance rather than memorized as a universal equation linking the uptake of one nitrogen ion to the release of a fixed number of protons.

The choice of accompanying ion in a nutrient solution is consequently important. Calcium nitrate provides both nitrate and calcium. Ammonium sulfate provides both ammonium and sulfate. Replacing one salt with the other changes more than the form of nitrogen unless the other ions are adjusted. A growth difference in such an experiment could arise from nitrogen form, calcium supply, sulfate supply, ionic strength, pH or interactions among them. A convincing comparison requires matched nutrient composition as far as possible, controlled pH and equal amounts of nitrogen atoms rather than equal masses of salts.

The assimilatory capacity of a tissue places a further limit on ammonium nutrition. Roots receiving abundant carbohydrate can produce ATP and carbon skeletons that support assimilation. Roots deprived of photosynthetic products may lack sufficient substrates to process incoming nitrogen. The same ammonium concentration can therefore have different consequences for plants growing under contrasting light conditions. This connection explains why nitrogen toxicity cannot be interpreted solely from an external concentration without regard to the metabolic condition of the plant.

Ammonium is not an inherently unsuitable nutrient. Numerous plants grow successfully with ammonium as a substantial source of their nitrogen, particularly in environments where it is abundant. Some species and developmental stages respond favorably to mixed nitrate and ammonium nutrition. Others tolerate or prefer different mixtures. A general principle is that the plant must coordinate acquisition, assimilation, pH balance, charge balance and carbon metabolism. This coordination, rather than a universal superiority of one nitrogen form, accounts for many differences observed in nutrient experiments.

7. From the absorbing root to the rest of the plant

The entry of a nitrogen compound into a root epidermal cell is only one stage of acquisition by an entire organism. A plant is not a uniform solution in which all metabolites instantly reach equilibrium. Its tissues have different functions, membrane transport systems and metabolic capacities. A growing shoot may demand nitrogen while the absorbing root is some distance away. Nitrogen taken up at the root surface must therefore be retained, metabolized or transferred into long-distance transport pathways in a coordinated manner.

Nitrate entering root cells can follow several routes. It may undergo reduction within the root, be transported inward towards the vascular tissues, or enter a storage pool such as the vacuole. The relative proportions depend on species, external nitrate supply, root and shoot metabolism, developmental stage and environmental conditions. Storing nitrate temporarily can buffer fluctuations in external availability. A large nitrate pool does not necessarily represent a large rate of amino acid production at the same moment. It reflects the balance of input, metabolism, storage and output over time.

The vacuole is separated from the cytosol by a membrane called the tonoplast. Moving nitrate across this membrane is a regulated transport step, distinct from its initial entry across the plasma membrane. Vacuolar nitrate can also contribute to osmotic balance. However, calling the vacuole an inert reservoir would be misleading. It participates in dynamic exchange with cytosolic pools, and the energy gradients across its membrane differ from those at the root surface. When the external supply declines, stored nitrate may help sustain metabolism, provided that the plant can mobilize it. Measuring total tissue nitrate without separating compartments cannot reveal how much nitrate is immediately accessible to cytosolic nitrate reductase.

Some nitrate is loaded into the xylem and transported with the transpiration stream towards the shoot. The xylem is a long-distance pathway, but loading nitrate into it requires controlled exchange with adjacent living cells. Water transport through mature xylem conduits is driven by physical gradients rather than ATP consumption by the conduits themselves. Loading and retrieval of ions from the vascular apoplast may involve membrane transporters. The distinction between membrane transport and bulk flow prevents the misconception that one root uptake protein actively carries a nitrate molecule all the way from soil to a leaf.

A leaf supplied with nitrate through xylem may reduce it and incorporate the nitrogen into amino acids. The availability of light-derived reducing power and carbohydrates can make shoot assimilation physiologically advantageous under many conditions. Roots nevertheless also assimilate nitrate, and root assimilation can be important in particular species and nutritional environments. It would be inaccurate to assign all nitrate reduction to leaves and all ammonium assimilation to roots. The enzyme distribution and metabolic resources determine the location of conversion.

Ammonium-derived nitrogen often enters organic compounds relatively rapidly near the site of uptake. This helps avoid excessive accumulation of free ammonium. Nevertheless, nitrogen transport in the xylem is not restricted to a single chemical form. Nitrate, ammonium and organic nitrogen compounds may occur in xylem sap in varying proportions. The identification of glutamine or asparagine in a shoot does not prove that the root initially absorbed those amino acids; the molecules may have been synthesized after uptake of inorganic nitrogen.

Glutamine and asparagine are useful carriers of organic nitrogen because their structures include a side-chain amide nitrogen in addition to the alpha-amino nitrogen. Their transport allows the plant to distribute nitrogen in a form connected to central amino acid metabolism. Their relative contributions to long-distance transport vary among species and conditions. In many nitrogen-fixing legumes, amino acids or ureides can carry fixed nitrogen away from nodules. Ureides are nitrogen-rich compounds derived from purine metabolism. The detailed pathways of symbiotic nitrogen export belong to the later symbiosis chapter; here their significance is that the long-distance transport form can differ markedly from the chemical form acquired from the environment.

Phloem movement contributes to the redistribution of organic nitrogen between source and sink tissues. A source exports compounds; a sink imports and uses or stores them. The source–sink distinction changes over development. An expanding leaf may initially import nitrogen but later export nitrogen-containing compounds during senescence. A filling seed is an important sink. A root may be supplied with carbohydrates from photosynthetic tissues while simultaneously exporting nitrogen compounds or receiving organic nitrogen from elsewhere. It is therefore misleading to describe the entire phloem as carrying materials only downwards or the entire nitrogen supply as moving only upwards.

Suppose a young leaf retains green colour during the early stages of external nitrogen deprivation while an older leaf loses nitrogen. This pattern is consistent with redistribution from old to young tissues, but observations of concentration alone are insufficient to prove transfer. The old leaf may lose dry matter as well as nitrogen, or the young leaf may receive nitrogen from seed reserves or other tissues. A pulse of isotopically enriched nitrogen delivered before the deprivation treatment can help trace redistribution if the subsequent appearance of the label in other organs is measured. A full interpretation also requires the masses of the organs and the total nitrogen content in each.

The following simple mass budget is useful. Imagine a plant contains 20 mg nitrogen in its roots, 30 mg in older leaves and 10 mg in young leaves. After a period with no external nitrogen, the respective quantities become 18, 23 and 18 mg. Total nitrogen changes from 60 to 59 mg. The young leaves gained 8 mg even though external acquisition was zero. Other compartments together lost 9 mg, which is compatible with redistribution plus 1 mg of net loss or measurement discrepancy. This example does not identify the exact path of individual nitrogen atoms, but it demonstrates why increasing nitrogen content in one organ does not imply current root uptake.

The opposite situation is also possible. A root can show high uptake of nitrate in a short experiment while shoot nitrogen content changes very little. The acquired nitrate might be stored in the root, reduced locally, or returned partly to the external solution. Transport to the shoot might be limited. A high initial influx therefore should not be interpreted as an automatic increase in photosynthetic protein synthesis or yield. Uptake, retention, long-distance delivery and metabolic incorporation have separate constraints.

Water relations can change the supply of nitrate to leaves independently of root transporter activity. Reduced transpiration may alter xylem transport and the delivery of dissolved ions. Under such conditions, root nitrate might accumulate even if the root continues absorbing it. Root storage capacity and local assimilation can partially compensate. This is a reason to avoid diagnosing low shoot nitrate as evidence of a defective uptake transporter without examining root nitrate, xylem delivery, water status and the relevant metabolic processes.

At the whole-plant level, the most informative description is not a single direction of movement but a network of pools connected by fluxes. Soil nitrate enters roots; root inorganic pools exchange with vacuolar pools; nitrate or organic nitrogen enters long-distance transport; different organs assimilate, store and redistribute nitrogen; senescence releases nitrogen from existing macromolecules. Growth results from the coordinated operation of this network. The location of the largest pool need not be the location of the greatest flux, and the largest flux need not identify the rate-limiting step.

8. Metabolic entry: linking inorganic nitrogen to carbon skeletons

Nitrogen nutrition becomes nitrogen assimilation when the acquired nitrogen is incorporated into cellular organic compounds. This transition is the central biological challenge after uptake. A root cannot construct a protein by directly inserting a nitrate ion into a peptide chain. The nitrogen atom must be delivered to a chemical form that participates in amino acid synthesis. For nitrate, this requires reduction; for ammonium, incorporation into carbon-containing metabolites can begin without those preliminary reduction steps.

The basic sequence for nitrate-fed plants is nitrate uptake, reduction of nitrate to nitrite, reduction of nitrite to ammonium and incorporation of ammonium into organic nitrogen. In the usual assimilatory pathway, nitrate reductase functions in the cytosol and nitrite reductase in plastids, including chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues and non-green plastids in other cells. The spatial separation matters because nitrite must reach the appropriate compartment. It also means that uptake and reduction do not take place at a single protein or membrane. A mutation can interfere with any stage and produce a different pattern of metabolite accumulation.

The electron requirement follows directly from oxidation-state accounting. One nitrate nitrogen atom changes from plus five to plus three during the first reduction and from plus three to minus three during the second. The first step requires two electrons per nitrogen atom, and the second requires six. Together they require eight electrons. An ammonium-fed plant can avoid this particular eight-electron requirement per newly acquired nitrogen atom, although its total energetic expenditure on nutrition depends on transport, assimilation, ion balance and other processes. An exam question asking for the electrons required to reduce nitrate to ammonium is therefore narrower than a question asking for the complete energy cost of plant nitrogen nutrition.

Reduction of nitrate and formation of an amino acid are not the same transformation. Once ammonium is available, the major route for its incorporation in most plant contexts involves glutamine synthetase and glutamate synthase, often abbreviated GS and GOGAT. Glutamine synthetase uses ATP to add ammonium-derived nitrogen to glutamate, forming glutamine. Glutamate synthase transfers the amide nitrogen of glutamine to 2-oxoglutarate with a supply of reducing equivalents, yielding two glutamate molecules. One glutamate molecule can replenish the acceptor used by glutamine synthetase; the other represents net incorporation of an ammonium-derived nitrogen atom into a carbon skeleton. Details of enzyme isoforms, stoichiometry and regulation are developed in the dedicated GS–GOGAT chapter.

The carbon skeleton 2-oxoglutarate is produced through central carbon metabolism. The term carbon skeleton refers to the carbon framework of an organic molecule after considering separately the nitrogen-containing group that will be added or transferred. The nitrogen atom and the carbon framework have different origins: the nitrogen might come from soil nitrate, while much of the carbon in a photosynthetic plant ultimately comes from atmospheric carbon dioxide. Central metabolism must bring these two resources together in the right compartments and proportions.

Glutamate becomes a major donor of amino nitrogen through transamination reactions. In a transamination reaction, an amino group is transferred between an amino acid and a keto acid. The reaction does not add new nitrogen to the organism; it redistributes nitrogen already present in organic metabolites. For instance, transfer of an amino group from glutamate to oxaloacetate can generate aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate. This example shows how the amino acid network connects to carbon metabolism. It also demonstrates why an examination question must distinguish the first net incorporation of inorganic nitrogen from the subsequent transfer of organic nitrogen between metabolites.

Glutamine is important because its amide group supplies nitrogen to several biosynthetic pathways, including those leading to nucleotide synthesis. The ability to make glutamine is therefore linked not only to protein synthesis but also to DNA and RNA production. The organism must distribute nitrogen across these competing demands. A tissue undergoing rapid cell division may have high nucleotide and protein synthesis requirements. A mature leaf renewing damaged photosynthetic proteins has different allocation demands. These demands influence the rate at which imported nitrogen can be assimilated and incorporated into biomass.

Ammonium is generated internally as well as acquired externally. During photorespiration, the conversion of glycine to serine in mitochondria is associated with release of ammonium-derived nitrogen. This nitrogen is commonly reassimilated through GS–GOGAT-related metabolism. Protein turnover and other reactions can also release ammonium. Therefore, detecting ammonium inside a plant does not prove that the plant has absorbed ammonium directly from the soil. The measured pool reflects internal generation, external uptake, assimilation, compartmental exchange and losses.

This connection is particularly important when comparing plants grown under different atmospheric conditions. Elevated carbon dioxide can alter Rubisco oxygenation and the demand for photorespiratory nitrogen reassimilation in a C3 leaf. Such changes may affect measured nitrogen metabolism even when external nitrogen supply is constant. Conversely, changing external nitrogen can affect the investment in photosynthetic machinery. The two processes influence each other rather than forming an independent carbon pathway and an independent nitrogen pathway that never interact.

There is no requirement that every nitrogen atom entering a root be assimilated immediately. Nitrate can be stored and used later, allowing a temporal separation between acquisition and reduction. Ammonium is usually subject to tighter control because large free ammonium pools may be harmful. The ability to maintain inorganic pools can buffer variation in nutrient supply, but storage capacity is finite. If the rate of nitrate uptake exceeds the combined rate of assimilation and export for an extended period, the nitrate pool tends to increase unless uptake adjusts or additional loss routes operate.

A mass-balance model makes this reasoning explicit. Let Q represent the amount of nitrate nitrogen in a root. Let U represent gross nitrate influx, E efflux, R nitrate reduction and X net export from that root to other organs. Express all four rates in micromoles of nitrogen atoms per hour. Then dQ/dt = U − E − R − X, with other relevant fluxes added if present. If U is 18, E is 3, R is 9 and X is 4, the nitrate pool increases by 2 micromoles of nitrogen per hour under the stated assumptions. A steady nitrate pool would instead require that the total incoming and outgoing rates balance.

A pool that remains constant is not metabolically inactive. It can be maintained at a steady state despite rapid movement through it. Suppose influx and combined outputs each equal 100 micromoles per hour in one plant, while both equal 5 micromoles per hour in another. Both could have stable nitrate pools of the same size. Their fluxes and physiological demands are nevertheless very different. This distinction between pool size and flux underlies the interpretation of isotope-tracing experiments and prevents many errors in reading metabolic graphs.

Carbon availability can restrict nitrogen assimilation at several points. ATP is needed to sustain the proton pumps that support uptake and to drive glutamine synthetase. Reducing power is required for nitrate reduction and glutamate synthase. Carbon skeletons are required for organic nitrogen synthesis. A decline in photosynthesis or carbohydrate supply can thus limit nitrogen use even when nitrate is plentiful outside the plant. The converse is also true: insufficient nitrogen reduces the production of photosynthetic proteins and may eventually reduce carbon acquisition. A strong explanatory model includes both directions of this feedback.

A plant experiencing a sudden increase in external nitrate may respond first through transport and signaling, then through changes in enzyme activity and longer-term growth. A plant supplied with ammonium faces a different balance of reduction demand and homeostatic control. These changes need to be interpreted at the appropriate timescale. Measuring the first few minutes of ion influx answers a different question from measuring dry mass after several weeks. Both observations are biologically useful, but they cannot be used as substitutes for one another.

9. Biological nitrogen fixation as an additional route of supply

Most plants cannot directly utilize atmospheric N₂ because they lack nitrogenase, the enzyme system required for biological nitrogen fixation. Certain bacteria and archaea possess this ability and are termed diazotrophs. Some live independently, some associate with plant surfaces or tissues, and some establish highly integrated symbioses. In nitrogen-fixing plant associations, it is the microbial partner that performs the chemical reduction of molecular nitrogen. The plant benefits through access to nitrogen-containing products and may provide carbon and other resources to the partner.

A legume growing in a nitrogen-poor environment is a familiar example. When compatible rhizobia establish a functional symbiosis, the root can form nodules in which nitrogen fixation occurs. These nodules are specialized plant structures containing microbial symbionts. Their presence alone does not guarantee high fixation: nodules may be ineffective, immature, senescent or limited by environmental conditions. A functional nodule must support the metabolic requirements of the microorganisms while providing conditions compatible with nitrogenase activity.

Nitrogenase-mediated fixation is energetically demanding and sensitive to oxygen. Yet respiration can be important for providing the energy required to support it. A functioning nodule must therefore balance oxygen delivery for respiratory metabolism against protection of nitrogenase from oxygen damage. Leghemoglobin contributes to oxygen management in many legume nodules. The full mechanism of nitrogenase, the electron and ATP requirements of the reaction, and the physiology of oxygen control are treated in subsequent nodes. The present chapter uses these concepts only to explain how atmospheric nitrogen enters the plant's nutrition network.

Nitrogen fixation differs from both nitrate uptake and ammonium assimilation. Fixation reduces N₂ into ammonia-derived nitrogen. Uptake transfers a compound across a biological boundary. Assimilation incorporates inorganic nitrogen into an organic molecule. In a legume symbiosis, microbial fixation is followed by transfer and assimilation of nitrogen in the integrated host–microbe system. These processes can all occur in the same biological association but remain conceptually distinct. Confusing them makes it impossible to interpret whether a particular mutation affects nodulation, nitrogenase activity, transport or downstream assimilation.

External mineral nitrogen can alter the plant's investment in symbiotic nitrogen fixation. A plant that can acquire sufficient nitrate from the environment may reduce investment in the costly process of forming or maintaining nodules, although responses depend on species, concentration, developmental stage and environmental context. This illustrates that biological nitrogen fixation and mineral nitrogen acquisition are not necessarily mutually exclusive. A nodulated plant may obtain part of its nitrogen from fixation and another part from nitrate or ammonium in the soil.

Consider two plants of the same legume genotype grown in a nutrient solution lacking added nitrogen. One is inoculated with a compatible effective symbiont; the other is maintained without that symbiont. After the initial seed nitrogen is depleted, the inoculated plant may continue accumulating nitrogen if fixation occurs, whereas the non-inoculated plant may become nitrogen deficient. A third treatment with an appropriate mineral nitrogen supply provides a useful positive control for growth. This design distinguishes inability to obtain nitrogen in a given treatment from a general inability of the plant to grow.

A stronger test of the source of newly accumulated nitrogen would trace molecular nitrogen itself. If the atmosphere provided to the symbiotic system contains enriched ¹⁵N₂ and label subsequently appears in plant organic nitrogen, the result supports the transfer of nitrogen derived from N₂. Appropriate controls must account for labeled contaminants, nitrogen pools present at the start and background isotope abundance. Simply finding more total nitrogen in inoculated plants can be consistent with fixation but does not by itself identify the exact origin of every additional nitrogen atom.

Free-living nitrogen fixation contributes to nitrogen input in many ecosystems without forming a legume nodule. Fixed nitrogen may become available to plants through microbial turnover, transfer or transformations of soil nitrogen pools. Non-legume plants can also participate in other kinds of nitrogen-fixing associations, including actinorhizal symbioses involving Frankia. Mycorrhizal fungi, by contrast, often help plants acquire nutrients from soil but are not generally nitrogen-fixing organisms themselves. The benefit a plant obtains from an associated microorganism is not sufficient evidence that the association fixes N₂.

The input of atmospheric nitrogen by fixation and the recycling of existing fixed nitrogen by mineralization play complementary roles. Fixation introduces new fixed nitrogen into a biological system from the atmospheric N₂ pool. Mineralization converts organic nitrogen already present in the system into inorganic forms. A plant acquiring ammonium released from decomposing microbial biomass may ultimately use atoms that were fixed long before the current growing season. The immediate source and the ultimate source are not necessarily identical. This distinction clarifies nutrient budgets in both experiments and natural ecosystems.

In agricultural and ecological settings, the importance of fixation depends on the availability of appropriate microbial partners, plant genotype and the environmental conditions affecting both organisms. Providing mineral nitrogen can improve plant growth where the symbiosis is ineffective, but can also influence the plant's allocation to fixation. An experiment comparing legumes and cereals must control initial nitrogen reserves and external sources before attributing differences to fixation. The ability of a particular species to establish an association should not be inferred merely from its taxonomic membership or from the appearance of healthy leaves.
