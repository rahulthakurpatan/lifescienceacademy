Why magnetic resonance reveals molecular information

A solution of a purified enzyme may appear completely transparent, yet the molecules inside it are not motionless. They bend, rotate, exchange protons with water, bind substrates and move between conformations. A visible absorption measurement can sometimes identify a cofactor or establish concentration, while fluorescence may report the environment of a particular aromatic group. Neither measurement automatically identifies which individual atomic sites have changed. Magnetic resonance provides another route to molecular information. Certain nuclei and electrons possess magnetic moments, and their response to a magnetic field depends on both their intrinsic properties and their surroundings. Consequently, an experiment can distinguish sites within a molecule without first separating those sites into different samples.

Nuclear magnetic resonance, abbreviated NMR, uses the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei. Electron spin resonance, abbreviated ESR, uses the magnetic properties of unpaired electrons and is also known as electron paramagnetic resonance or EPR. The alternative names ESR and EPR refer to the same basic family of experiments. Their similarity is not accidental: in both cases a magnetic field creates energy differences between quantum states, and electromagnetic energy can induce transitions between those states. However, the two methods do not simply offer alternative instruments for measuring the same molecule. They differ in which particles contribute to the signal, how large their magnetic moments are, the frequency range employed and what molecular questions can be answered.

Consider two samples containing the same amount of a small protein. One contains a ligand that binds reversibly to the protein; the other does not. If the protein remains folded in both tubes, its far ultraviolet circular dichroism spectrum may change little. Nevertheless, NMR signals from nuclei close to the binding region may move because binding changes their local electromagnetic environment. Alternatively, consider a redox protein containing a metal centre that alternates between a diamagnetic state and an EPR-detectable paramagnetic state. Its change of electron configuration can be tracked by EPR even when a measurement of bulk protein concentration detects no change. These examples show why choosing the property that the instrument detects is an essential part of experimental design.

The word resonance describes the selective response of a system to an oscillating perturbation at a frequency appropriate to its energy separation. An analogy is a playground swing that responds efficiently when pushed at the right rhythm. The analogy explains frequency selectivity but not the discrete allowed states of a quantum spin, nor the fact that a macroscopic signal arises from an ensemble rather than a literal rotating object. In magnetic resonance, the applied oscillating field is magnetic, and the frequency condition is linked quantitatively to the energy required for a transition. The field strength can therefore be adjusted to change the resonance frequency.

The historical development illustrates the distinction between observing a phenomenon and constructing an analytical method. Isidor Rabi's experiments on molecular beams in the 1930s established magnetic resonance as a means of examining nuclear magnetic properties. Evgeny Zavoisky observed EPR in condensed matter in 1944. The independent work of the Purcell and Bloch groups in the mid-1940s demonstrated nuclear magnetic resonance in bulk samples. Their achievements were recognized by the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1952. Later developments in high-resolution and Fourier-transform NMR made it practical to separate closely spaced signals. Richard Ernst received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1991 for developments in high-resolution NMR methods, and Kurt Wüthrich shared the 2002 chemistry prize for work enabling three-dimensional structures of biological macromolecules in solution.

Early measurements established the magnetic nature of the transition by changing field and frequency and observing when the response appeared. The crucial inference was not that all nuclei in a material possessed one fixed resonance frequency independent of context. Rather, as measurement resolution improved, slight differences in resonance frequency became scientifically useful because they could be associated with different chemical environments. This development transformed nuclear resonance from a tool of physical measurement into a method of chemical and biochemical analysis. EPR followed its own route toward characterization of radicals, transition-metal sites and molecular motions.

The distinction between the observation and the inference remains central. A measured resonance frequency is an experimental property. Assigning that frequency to a particular nucleus or electronic species requires additional evidence. Interpreting a change as ligand binding, unfolding or a change in oxidation state requires further controls. A good examination answer therefore explains what the signal directly measures before using the signal to make a biological claim. The same logic applies when no signal is observed: absence of an observable signal may arise from physical insensitivity, broadening, unsuitable acquisition conditions or insufficient material, and cannot automatically establish absence of the species.

In this chapter the aim is to understand the signal itself, beginning with the quantum property that makes resonance possible and ending with comparative experimental interpretation. Detailed computation of three-dimensional macromolecular structures from multiple restraints belongs to the subsequent structural-analysis chapter. Here a student should learn enough about chemical shifts, interactions between spins and relaxation to understand why those restraints are informative and why a spectrum alone is not a complete structural model.

Nuclear and electronic spin: the measurable magnetic property

An electron has an intrinsic angular momentum called spin. Atomic nuclei can also possess net angular momentum arising from the combined angular momenta of their constituent protons and neutrons. The word intrinsic is important because quantum spin is not explained by imagining a small charged sphere physically rotating about an axis. The spin angular momentum is specified by a quantum number, S for an electron system and I for a nucleus. The magnitude of the angular momentum for a spin quantum number I is the square root of I(I+1) multiplied by the reduced Planck constant, ℏ. In a specified direction, its projection has the allowed values m times ℏ, where m runs from −I to +I in unit steps.

For an isolated spin-one-half nucleus, the allowed projections are +1/2 and −1/2. There are therefore two states for the projection along a selected axis. A spin-one nucleus has three projections, while a spin-zero nucleus has only one. A spin-zero nucleus has no ordinary nuclear magnetic dipole moment associated with spin and cannot generate conventional NMR transitions. The number of allowed projections is 2I+1, a result that applies to both integer and half-integer spin. This formula counts quantum states rather than the number of peaks that necessarily appear in an experimental spectrum.

The magnetic moment is related to angular momentum by the gyromagnetic ratio γ. This proportionality contains information about the charge and internal structure of the particle. A particle with nonzero angular momentum can interact with a magnetic field through its moment. For a nucleus described by the angular momentum vector J, the magnetic moment is μ = γJ. The sign of γ determines how the moment relates to the angular momentum. It matters, for example, for the ordering of the magnetic energy levels of nitrogen-15, whose gyromagnetic ratio is negative. In calculations of the ordinary positive resonance frequency the magnitude of γ is used.

A particularly useful nucleus is hydrogen-1, the proton, which has I=1/2 and a relatively large nuclear gyromagnetic ratio. It is abundant in organic molecules and water, so proton NMR offers a favorable combination of sensitivity and biological relevance. Carbon-12 has spin zero, whereas carbon-13 has I=1/2. An unlabeled biological sample therefore contains many carbon atoms but only a small naturally occurring fraction of carbon-13 nuclei that are accessible by ordinary carbon NMR. Nitrogen-15 also has I=1/2 and is naturally uncommon. Nitrogen-14 has I=1 and is magnetic, yet its quadrupolar interactions often cause rapid relaxation and broad signals.

Isotopes are nuclei of the same chemical element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number and, in the neutral atom, the same number of electrons. Their electronic bonding behavior can be similar even though their nuclear spin differs. This is why stable isotope enrichment is useful in protein NMR: it increases the number of detectable carbon-13 or nitrogen-15 nuclei without replacing carbon or nitrogen with a different chemical element. Isotope substitution can nonetheless affect molecular properties slightly through mass-dependent effects. A measured biological activity should not be assumed unchanged merely because an isotope has the same atomic number.

Phosphorus-31 has I=1/2 and a high natural isotopic abundance. It is particularly attractive for studying phosphorus-containing metabolites such as ATP and inorganic phosphate. Fluorine-19 also has I=1/2 and high natural abundance, and fluorinated labels can be valuable because most native biomolecules have little fluorine background. Deuterium, hydrogen-2, has I=1. It is often used in solvents and for isotopic substitution rather than treated as another proton signal. When a question asks which isotope is appropriate, the answer depends on spin, abundance, gyromagnetic ratio, molecular location and the nature of the proposed measurement.

Electrons require a separate line of reasoning. Two electrons in a closed-shell orbital pair have opposed spin projections and a net spin of zero in the simple pairing picture. A molecule with all electrons paired is normally diamagnetic and does not give ordinary electron spin resonance. It can still contain NMR-active nuclei. For instance, a solution of a closed-shell sugar contains magnetic protons but has no stable electron-spin signal of its own under ordinary conditions. A radical, on the other hand, contains at least one unpaired electron and can have a nonzero net spin. Many transition-metal ions also possess unpaired electrons.

The electron count alone does not establish the spin of every metal complex. The distribution of electrons among split d orbitals depends on ligand-field strength, metal identity, oxidation state and geometry. Iron in one environment can have a different spin state from iron in another. An even total electron count does not guarantee diamagnetism; two unpaired electrons can form a nonzero total spin state. Molecular oxygen provides a classic example of a paramagnetic molecule despite an even total number of electrons. Conversely, interactions between unpaired electrons can favor a spin-zero ground state, making the simple rule of counting unpaired electrons insufficient in coupled systems.

The difference between a paramagnetic species and a detectable EPR signal also deserves attention. Nonzero electron spin is a prerequisite for ordinary EPR, but not every such species yields an easily recorded line. Relaxation may be too rapid, transitions may lie outside the measured field or frequency range, or interactions can create complex spectra. Some integer-spin systems are difficult to observe at a conventional frequency because of zero-field splitting. These qualifications prevent the oversimplified inference that every EPR-silent sample is diamagnetic.

Electron and nuclear moments differ greatly in magnitude. The Bohr magneton, μB = eℏ/(2me), sets the natural scale for electronic magnetic moments. The nuclear magneton, μN = eℏ/(2mp), uses the proton mass instead of the electron mass. Because the proton is much heavier than the electron, the nuclear magneton is roughly 1/1836 of the Bohr magneton. The proton magnetic moment has an additional intrinsic proportionality factor, so the ratio of electron and proton gyromagnetic magnitudes is about 658 rather than 1836. Distinguish the ratio of fundamental magnetons from the ratio of actual magnetic moments or frequencies.

A biological sample contains many nuclear spins even when it has no unpaired electrons. EPR is selective for a smaller chemical population, while NMR commonly receives overlapping contributions from a large number of sites. Selectivity can be advantageous: a paramagnetic cofactor can be detected in the presence of considerable diamagnetic protein. It can also be limiting: an ordinary unlabelled membrane protein may be invisible to EPR. Magnetic nuclei and electron spins therefore supply complementary rather than competing windows into biology.

Zeeman splitting, populations and the resonance condition

Imagine a sample in the absence of an external magnetic field. For a simplified isolated spin-one-half system, the two magnetic projections are degenerate: they have equal energy. The application of a static field B0 defines an axis, conventionally the z direction, and removes that degeneracy through the magnetic interaction energy E = −μ·B. The dot product states that only the component of the magnetic moment along the field contributes to this simple energy expression. For a positive gyromagnetic ratio, the projection m=+1/2 has lower energy than m=−1/2. For a negative ratio the order reverses.

Writing the projection of the moment as γℏm gives Em = −γℏmB0. Adjacent allowed spin projections differ by one unit. Therefore the magnitude of the separation of neighboring Zeeman levels is |γ|ℏB0. This is called Zeeman splitting. In a real molecule, additional interactions alter the energies, which is precisely what produces the analytical information in magnetic resonance. For the moment the simplified expression isolates the role of the field.

The energy of a quantum of electromagnetic radiation is hν, where h is Planck's constant and ν is frequency in cycles per second. Equating this energy to the separation of the spin states gives hν = |γ|ℏB0. Because h=2πℏ, the resulting frequency is ν0 = |γ|B0/(2π). The angular frequency ω0 is |γ|B0 and is measured in radians per second. A common calculation error occurs when a gyromagnetic ratio expressed in radians per second per tesla is used as though it were in megahertz per tesla. Always examine the units of the supplied constant before deciding whether the factor of 2π is needed.

The free proton has γ/(2π) approximately 42.58 MHz/T. A nominal 400 MHz proton instrument therefore uses a field of about 9.39 T, since 400/42.58 = 9.39. The same field applied to carbon-13 produces a frequency near 100.6 MHz, using the approximate carbon-13 factor 10.705 MHz/T. A spectrometer called a 400 MHz instrument is normally named according to its proton resonance frequency, even though it can measure different nuclei at their own frequencies. This convention must not be mistaken for the operating frequency of every nucleus.

The Larmor frequency can also be understood using a vector model. A magnetic moment in a static magnetic field experiences torque when its direction is not parallel to the field. In the classical picture it precesses around the field axis, rather like the direction of a spinning top changes under gravity. The gravitational analogy is limited: the nuclear magnetic moment is not a tiny macroscopic gyroscope, and quantum spin measurements yield discrete projections. Nevertheless, the ensemble vector model correctly provides a convenient way to describe the time-dependent direction of net magnetization during many NMR pulse experiments.

At thermal equilibrium the populations of the two spin states follow the Boltzmann distribution. If the lower-energy population is Nl and the upper-energy population is Nu, their ratio is Nu/Nl = exp(−ΔE/kBT), with kB the Boltzmann constant and T the absolute temperature. At room temperature the nuclear Zeeman splitting is small compared with kBT. Thus the populations differ only slightly, even in a strong magnet. The net longitudinal magnetization arises from the small excess of moments in the lower-energy state, not from all nuclei becoming aligned in the same direction.

For two states of equal degeneracy, define the fractional population difference P=(Nl−Nu)/(Nl+Nu). Substitution of the Boltzmann ratio gives P=(1−exp(−x))/(1+exp(−x)), where x=ΔE/(kBT), or equivalently tanh(x/2). At high temperature relative to the splitting, x is much less than one, so P is approximately x/2. This approximation clarifies why increasing field or decreasing temperature increases equilibrium polarization. It is not valid when the energy separation approaches or exceeds the thermal energy.

As an illustrative calculation, at 298 K a proton resonating at 400 MHz has ΔE = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s)(4.00×10⁸ s⁻¹) = 2.65×10⁻²⁵ J. Thermal energy kBT is (1.381×10⁻²³ J/K)(298 K) = 4.12×10⁻²¹ J. The ratio x is 6.44×10⁻⁵, and the fractional population difference is approximately 3.22×10⁻⁵. Expressed as a percentage this is about 0.00322%. The number is small but still represents a substantial ensemble of spins in an ordinary macroscopic sample. It explains both the possibility of detection and the relatively poor intrinsic sensitivity of NMR.

For an electron spin with a resonance near 9.5 GHz, the corresponding energy separation is about 6.29×10⁻²⁴ J. At the same temperature, x is about 0.00153 and the fractional population difference is approximately 0.000765, or 0.0765%. Although the magnet used in this EPR example is much weaker than the NMR magnet, the electron's larger magnetic moment yields a higher transition frequency and greater thermal polarization. Actual instrument sensitivity additionally depends on the number of spins, line width, detector design and relaxation, so polarization alone is not a universal sensitivity ranking.

The static field B0 establishes the energy levels. An oscillating magnetic field B1 perpendicular to B0 can drive transitions when its frequency is close to resonance. In the simple magnetic dipole picture, the selection rule for the spin projection is Δm=±1. A longitudinal oscillating field parallel to B0 does not efficiently drive the ordinary transverse spin-flip transition in this model. In pulsed experiments, the B1 field manipulates the net magnetization, and its effect depends on the pulse amplitude, duration and frequency offset.

Resonance does not mean that only upward transitions occur. The field can also stimulate transitions from the higher-energy state to the lower-energy state. At equilibrium the net absorptive response reflects the excess of spins available in the lower state. If the populations become equal through strong or repeated excitation without sufficient relaxation, the net absorption decreases. This effect is called saturation. It is an especially important distinction in experiments intended to compare signal intensities quantitatively.

The energies involved in ordinary NMR and EPR are far below the energy of visible or ultraviolet photons and are not used to break chemical bonds or ionize molecules. The scientific response instead comes from changing spin-state populations and coherence. Strong magnets, radiofrequency or microwave systems and cryogenic equipment still require appropriate institutional safety controls; nonionizing radiation does not mean equipment is free of hazards. The discussion here concerns measurement principles, not practical operation of specialized instruments.

A graph of resonance frequency versus field is a straight line through the origin for the ideal isolated spin. Its slope is determined by the gyromagnetic ratio. When different nuclei are measured at the same field, they fall on lines with different slopes. When a particular chemical site differs slightly in shielding from another site of the same isotope, their slopes differ slightly. The large difference between proton and carbon frequencies and the tiny difference between chemically different proton frequencies must not be confused; both arise from magnetic interactions but represent different scales of information.

From pulse to spectrum: how an NMR measurement works

A high-resolution solution NMR spectrometer contains a magnet, a probe, radiofrequency electronics and a computer-based acquisition system. The magnet supplies a strong and highly homogeneous static field. The probe contains coils and circuitry that transmit radiofrequency energy and detect the resulting signal. The sample is located in a region of the magnet where the field is as uniform as practicable. A receiver converts the weak signal into data suitable for processing. Each component matters because the chemical differences that need to be resolved may correspond to only a few hertz.

The instrument does not determine the position of each nucleus in space by viewing it. It detects a collective response of spins. At equilibrium, the net magnetization lies along the z axis, even though individual spins have a distribution of quantum states and phases. A transverse magnetic field applied near resonance can rotate the net magnetization away from the z axis. The rotating-frame description makes this manipulation easier to understand: a coordinate frame rotating near the resonance frequency sees the applied field as approximately stationary during the pulse.

For an ideal on-resonance rectangular pulse, the flip angle is θ=|γ|B1tp when B1 denotes the effective rotating component of the radiofrequency field and tp is the pulse duration. A 90-degree pulse takes longitudinal magnetization into the transverse plane. A 180-degree pulse can invert longitudinal magnetization or refocus appropriately evolving transverse magnetization. The simple relation assumes a uniform B1 field, exact resonance and a rectangular pulse. Real pulse sequences can involve multiple frequencies, shaped pulses or offsets, but the basic relationship is sufficient to explain standard examination experiments.

Suppose an ideal pulse gives a 90-degree rotation at a particular amplitude and duration. Keeping the amplitude fixed and doubling the duration gives a 180-degree rotation on resonance. The reasoning fails if the pulse is far from resonance or if the amplitude is altered by a change in sample loading. A pulse described by its intended flip angle therefore has an experimental calibration behind it. The term flip angle describes the rotation of the ensemble magnetization vector, not the literal rotation of a nucleus.

After the excitation pulse, transverse magnetization precesses around the static field. Its time-varying magnetic flux induces an electrical signal in the receiver coil. This signal is known as the free induction decay, or FID. In an ideal sample containing one resonance, the FID can be represented by a decaying sinusoid. A molecule with several different chemical environments produces a signal composed of several frequencies. Their sum may look complicated in time, but the underlying contributions can be separated mathematically.

Fourier transformation converts the acquired time-domain signal into a frequency-domain representation. The frequency-domain spectrum displays resonances as peaks whose positions, areas, shapes and splittings can be analyzed. The transformation does not add chemical information absent from the time-domain measurement; it rearranges information into a form that exposes the constituent frequencies. An analogy is separating a musical chord into the frequencies corresponding to individual notes. Unlike an ideal chord, however, the NMR signals decay and can overlap, so peak widths and phase relationships are important.

A finite acquisition period limits the ability to distinguish nearby frequencies. The frequency spacing between discrete Fourier points is inversely related to the acquisition time under simple sampling conditions. For example, an acquisition window of 1 second produces a nominal frequency grid spacing of about 1 Hz; a 0.25-second window gives approximately 4 Hz. Zero filling can increase the density of displayed points but does not recover genuinely unmeasured time-domain information or improve intrinsic resolution beyond the line shape and acquisition constraints. Digital point spacing and physical spectral resolution are related but are not identical.

Sampling rate determines the frequency range that can be represented without aliasing. If a component lies outside the acquired spectral window, it can appear at an incorrect position. The result may resemble a legitimate chemical signal, so knowing the window and reference is part of interpretation. Increasing spectral width at fixed number of data points reduces acquisition time, creating a trade-off between coverage and point spacing. In examination problems, a change in sampling conditions should be distinguished from a change in the molecule itself.

Repeated acquisitions improve the signal-to-noise ratio when noise is uncorrelated between scans. The coherent signal adds predictably, whereas random noise grows more slowly, giving the approximate relationship SNR proportional to the square root of the number of scans. If 16 scans give SNR 10, then 64 scans under unchanged conditions give approximately SNR 20. To double the SNR again requires 256 scans, not 128. This square-root rule does not hold for coherent artifacts, sample degradation, instrument drift or changes in recovery between successive pulses.

A practical distinction exists between sensitivity and resolution. Sensitivity concerns how reliably a weak signal can be detected. Resolution concerns whether signals close together can be distinguished. More scans often improve sensitivity but do not necessarily separate two lines broadened by an underlying physical process. A stronger field may increase separation in hertz between two chemical shifts, while a field inhomogeneity may cause broadening that offsets that benefit. The experimental question is not merely whether a spectrum has more signal but whether the relevant features are distinguishable.

Field shimming improves magnetic-field homogeneity. If different regions of a solution experience different fields, spins of the same chemical type precess at slightly different frequencies, producing a broadened signal even when the molecular properties are uniform. A field-frequency lock helps compensate for slow drift of the magnetic field during measurement, often using a deuterium-containing solvent. Chemical-shift referencing is a different operation: it provides a consistent frequency origin for comparing spectral positions. Shimming, locking and referencing thus address three different problems.

Aqueous samples contain enormous numbers of water protons relative to most dissolved biomolecules. The water resonance can dominate the spectrum and obscure nearby signals or overload parts of the acquisition system. Water suppression exploits frequency or other magnetic properties to reduce the unwanted signal while preserving as much of the analyte information as possible. It is not equivalent to physically removing water from the sample. Exchangeable protons in the biomolecule can be affected by suppression through exchange or proximity to the water frequency, which is a reason to be careful when comparing their intensities.

Deuterated solvents are widely used in chemical NMR because replacing many solvent protons with deuterium reduces proton background. In protein experiments, a mixture containing mainly ordinary water may be required to observe exchangeable amide protons, with some deuterated water serving the lock. In predominantly D2O, solvent-exchangeable hydrogens may be replaced by deuterium and their proton signals can disappear. Such disappearance is informative about exchange under suitable conditions, but it is not evidence that the peptide bond itself has been lost.

Before interpreting an unusual spectrum, consider whether the instrument or the sample has changed. Field drift can shift many peaks together; incorrect phase correction can produce dispersive peak shapes; a distorted baseline can corrupt area measurements. Sample precipitation can lower detectable concentration; pH changes can move peaks; temperature changes can alter exchange and viscosity. Matching acquisition and processing conditions, inspecting a reference and checking sample integrity are therefore integral parts of scientific reasoning rather than merely technical housekeeping.

Chemical shift, shielding and site assignment

If every proton experienced exactly the field supplied by the instrument, all protons of an isolated isotope would have the same simple Larmor frequency. In a molecule, electrons respond to the applied field and generate local magnetic fields. The resulting field at a given nucleus differs slightly from the external field. The term shielding describes the reduction of the effective field associated with the electronic response in the simple isotropic case. For a site with shielding constant σ, the effective field is approximately B0(1−σ). Thus the resonance frequency of a proton depends on both the static field and its chemical environment.

The expression involving σ is a first-order simplified description for an isotropically averaged site. Magnetic shielding is in general directional, and the relevant quantity is a tensor. In a rapidly tumbling liquid molecule, orientations are averaged on an appropriate timescale, so the observed shift often corresponds approximately to an isotropic average. In rigid solids or oriented systems the directional contributions may remain visible, producing broader or more complex patterns. This is one reason solution NMR and solid-state NMR require different ways of obtaining and interpreting high-resolution spectra.

Greater shielding in ordinary proton NMR generally lowers the resonance frequency at fixed external field. Less shielding, often called deshielding, raises the frequency. Historical terminology calls low-frequency signals upfield and high-frequency signals downfield. Modern proton spectra conventionally plot increasing chemical shift toward the left, so downfield is normally leftward on the printed spectrum. Reading the numerical tick labels is safer than using the apparent visual direction when a graph has an unusual axis convention.

Chemical shift is expressed relative to a reference signal, rather than by writing the entire resonance frequency. The usual definition is δ = [(νsample−νref)/νref] × 10⁶, with both frequencies in the same units and δ expressed in parts per million. In routine calculations, the nominal operating frequency in megahertz is used as a close approximation to the reference frequency. Thus a shift difference in ppm equals the frequency difference in hertz divided by the operating frequency in megahertz. The word ppm here is a dimensionless frequency ratio scaled by one million, not a concentration of molecules.

At a nominal 400 MHz proton frequency, a site at 2.0 ppm is about 800 Hz above a zero-ppm reference. At 600 MHz, the same shift is about 1200 Hz above the reference. Both measurements give 2.0 ppm because the relative shift scales with the applied field to first order. A pair of signals at 2.0 and 2.1 ppm are 40 Hz apart at 400 MHz but 60 Hz apart at 600 MHz. A higher field therefore improves their separation in hertz, which is often valuable when the line widths do not increase proportionally.

Tetramethylsilane, TMS, is a common reference for organic solution proton and carbon NMR. Aqueous biomolecular NMR frequently uses an appropriate reference such as DSS. Referencing conventions and solvent conditions need to be consistent because small shifts can matter. A zero-ppm reference is a chosen origin, not the frequency of a nucleus in zero magnetic field. In an experiment measuring changes in chemical shift, the reference must remain stable enough that an apparent change is attributable to the analyte rather than a shift of the frequency origin.

Electron-withdrawing substituents can reduce shielding at nearby protons, but a simple rule based only on electronegativity is insufficient. Aromatic ring currents, carbonyl anisotropy, hydrogen bonding, conformation and local electrostatic environments can all influence shielding. In an aromatic system, the applied field induces circulation of electron density, which produces local fields with different directions in different spatial regions. A proton situated outside the ring plane may experience a different induced field from a proton positioned above the ring. Consequently, the same type of atom can exhibit an unexpected shift when its spatial surroundings change.

This spatial dependence helps make protein NMR informative. A side-chain proton in a compact hydrophobic core can experience shielding from nearby aromatic groups. A backbone amide proton participating in hydrogen bonding can shift relative to a corresponding site in a different conformation. Yet no universal one-to-one rule assigns a particular shift to a unique protein structure. Many combinations of local geometry, solvent exposure, charge and hydrogen bonding can yield similar values. Chemical shifts are best understood as site-sensitive observables that can constrain interpretations when combined with independent information.

A folded protein often displays greater chemical-shift dispersion than a highly disordered protein, especially for backbone amide proton signals. This pattern emerges because different residues in a folded structure occupy distinct environments, whereas many residues in a disordered chain sample more similar environments. It is a useful diagnostic but not a proof of one precise fold. A protein with some structured regions and some disordered regions can produce both well-dispersed and crowded signals. Aggregation or exchange may obscure peaks and alter the apparent pattern, so a broad conclusion about folding should take line widths, concentration and complementary data into account.

The number of resonances is also informative. Chemically equivalent nuclei have the same chemical shift under the experimental conditions. Symmetry or fast exchange can make sites equivalent, so a molecule containing six protons does not necessarily yield six proton resonance positions. For example, the three protons of a methyl group are often equivalent in an ordinary solution spectrum because rapid rotation and molecular symmetry average their environments. Distinct methyl groups elsewhere in a molecule may produce different resonances, even when each group individually contains three equivalent protons.

Equivalence can depend on the surroundings. Two protons on a methylene carbon may be equivalent in an achiral environment but become distinguishable when a neighboring stereocentre makes their environments different. In a protein, chiral residues and a folded asymmetric structure are common, so treating every pair of methylene hydrogens as equivalent is inappropriate. Chemical equivalence must also be distinguished from magnetic equivalence: chemically equivalent spins can have different couplings to other spins, leading to more complex spectra.

For a simple illustrative molecule, acetate has a methyl group whose three protons are ordinarily equivalent. Its proton spectrum therefore contains one dominant methyl resonance from the acetate ion, ignoring small isotope satellites and other sample components. Lactate has a methyl group attached to a methine group; these environments are different and their resonance positions and couplings provide additional information. An assignment based on the positions alone is strengthened by observing the expected proton ratios and spin-spin splitting, which will be developed in the next section.

The local environment may change without any covalent bond breaking. Consider a histidine-containing protein titrated across a pH range. Protonation of the imidazole group alters its charge distribution and can shift signals from nearby nuclei. In rapid exchange between protonated and deprotonated states, a single peak may move smoothly as their relative populations change. A plot of shift versus pH can sometimes be used to estimate an apparent pKa if a suitable equilibrium model applies. If other ionizable groups or conformational changes contribute, a simple one-site fit may not have a unique physical interpretation.

This example illustrates why controlling pH, ionic strength and temperature is essential in ligand-binding experiments. Adding a ligand solution can alter pH or salt concentration independently of specific binding. If the spectrum changes, a matched buffer addition without ligand helps separate the effect of dilution and buffer composition from the effect of the ligand. A systematic series of ligand concentrations can establish whether a change follows a saturating relationship. None of these controls alone establishes a particular atomic contact, but they narrow the possible explanations.

The use of chemical shifts in structure and interaction analysis follows a common chain of reasoning: first obtain a reproducibly referenced spectrum, next assign or track individual peaks, then quantify changes, and finally test a model against alternative explanations. A shift is neither a direct molecular distance nor a universal measure of binding strength. Its magnitude depends on both the difference in shielding between states and their populations. Two equally occupied sites can exhibit very different shift changes if the local shielding response differs. Understanding this distinction prepares the way for quantitative interpretation of binding and exchange.

Spin–spin coupling, integrals and molecular identification

A chemical shift reveals the magnetic environment of a nucleus, but it does not by itself identify the connectivity of that nucleus to its neighbors. A further source of information comes from interactions between nuclear spins. In ordinary high-resolution solution NMR, scalar coupling, also called J coupling, is often transmitted through bonding electrons. The coupled nuclei experience slightly different transition energies according to the spin state of their partners. This interaction creates a characteristic splitting of resonance lines. The frequency difference between the components of a simple first-order multiplet is measured in hertz and is called the coupling constant J.

Take two distinguishable spin-one-half nuclei A and X with a scalar coupling. Each nucleus can occupy either of two spin projections. When A undergoes a transition, the spin state of X remains unchanged in the simple allowed transition picture. There are therefore two possibilities for the transition of A, one with X in each of its states. Their energies differ slightly because of the coupling. The A resonance appears as two components separated by JAX. The same relationship applies when X is observed: its resonance is split by A, with the same coupling magnitude. This reciprocal splitting can help identify a pair of connected spins, although accidental similarity of coupling constants is possible.

A useful first-order approximation requires that the separation of the chemical shifts of the coupled nuclei in hertz be much larger than their coupling constant. Under these conditions each spin can be described largely by its own projection. For one neighboring spin-one-half nucleus, a doublet is obtained. For two equivalent neighboring spin-one-half nuclei, the neighbors may have projections both positive, one positive and one negative in either of two ways, or both negative. The resulting three groups have relative degeneracies 1:2:1, producing a triplet. For three equivalent neighbors, the degeneracies are 1:3:3:1, producing a quartet.

The familiar n+1 rule follows from grouping the 2ⁿ possible configurations of n equivalent spin-one-half neighbors according to their total projection. The binomial coefficients give the ideal first-order intensity pattern. This rule is a shortcut rather than a universal law. The neighboring nuclei must have the same coupling to the observed nucleus, the spin system must behave approximately in a first-order manner, and other couplings must be absent or unresolved. If there are two nonequivalent neighbors with different coupling constants, the observed resonance may become a doublet of doublets rather than a triplet.

As an illustration, consider a proton whose two neighbors produce coupling constants of 8 Hz and 3 Hz. The four components, relative to the center, occur at ±4 ±1.5 Hz, or −5.5, −2.5, +2.5 and +5.5 Hz. The spacings between adjacent components are therefore 3, 5 and 3 Hz. An observer who simply counts four peaks and calls them a quartet misses the unequal spacing and the different physical interpretation. A true first-order quartet from three equivalent spin-one-half neighbors would exhibit equal adjacent spacing and the 1:3:3:1 pattern.

The J coupling constant is approximately independent of field strength for the same molecular structure and conditions, unlike the separation in hertz associated with two different chemical shifts. A 7 Hz doublet remains about 7 Hz wide at 400 and 600 MHz. On a ppm axis it spans 7/400 = 0.0175 ppm at 400 MHz and 7/600 = 0.0117 ppm at 600 MHz. The chemical-shift separation in hertz between distinct sites increases at the higher field. Thus increasing field can improve the first-order approximation and make the multiplet structure easier to interpret.

Strong coupling becomes important when chemical-shift separation in hertz is comparable to J. The spectrum can then show unequal inner and outer intensities, additional complexity and apparent deviations from simple splitting rules. A student should not force the n+1 interpretation onto such a spectrum. Suppose two sites differ by 0.02 ppm and have J=8 Hz. On a 400 MHz instrument the separation is 8 Hz, giving a ratio of one. On an 800 MHz instrument the separation is 16 Hz, giving a ratio of two. Although the higher field moves the system toward the weak-coupling limit, a ratio of two remains far from the ideal condition that separation greatly exceed J.

Chemical equivalence also requires care. Three equivalent protons in a simple methyl group do not split one another into a triplet merely because they are close in space. For the ordinary first-order spectrum of an equivalent set, their mutual couplings do not resolve as separate multiplet components. They can split another chemically distinct group to which they are coupled. Magnetic nonequivalence, however, can make a set of same-shift spins produce complicated patterns through unequal couplings to other sites. This is why counting all neighboring hydrogens without considering equivalence is often misleading.

In a simple ethyl fragment, CH3–CH2–, the methyl protons are split by the two methylene protons into a triplet, and the methylene protons are split by the three methyl protons into a quartet, provided no additional resolved couplings are present and the methylene protons are equivalent. The triplet and quartet should share the same coupling constant. The ratio of their integrated areas is 3:2 in a fully relaxed quantitative proton spectrum. Those three features together provide stronger support for an ethyl fragment than either the shift or the multiplicity alone.

The lactate ion, CH3–CH(OH)–COO⁻, illustrates a different pattern. The methyl signal is split primarily by the adjacent methine proton into a doublet. The methine signal is split by the three methyl protons into a quartet under suitable conditions. Exchangeable hydroxyl protons often do not contribute a stable additional resolved coupling in aqueous solution because proton exchange can average or broaden their signal. The methyl-to-methine integrated area ratio is expected to be 3:1 if the measurement conditions permit quantitative interpretation. This example links a molecule encountered in metabolism to the basic rules of spectrum analysis.

The number of bonds separating the nuclei influences the coupling, but there is no universal monotonic rule for J. Two-bond couplings between hydrogens on the same carbon, three-bond couplings through a carbon skeleton and longer-range couplings can all occur. For many three-bond proton couplings, the magnitude depends on the dihedral angle between bonds. The empirical Karplus relationship expresses this dependence approximately as J = A cos²φ + B cosφ + C, with the constants chosen for the relevant chemical context. The angle is therefore constrained by the measured coupling, not necessarily determined uniquely.

Because cosine terms can yield similar values for more than one angle, a single measured J cannot generally establish a unique conformation. Substituents and electronic structure can alter the empirical constants. In a molecule that interconverts between conformations, the observed coupling may be an average if exchange is rapid on the appropriate timescale. A protein backbone coupling can thus supply information about preferred local geometry, but it must be combined with other observations to construct a reliable model.

Scalar couplings differ from direct dipolar interactions. A dipolar interaction acts through space between magnetic moments and depends on their separation and orientation. Rapid isotropic tumbling averages the direct dipolar splitting to zero in an ordinary solution spectrum, but fluctuating dipolar interactions still drive relaxation and cross-relaxation. Scalar coupling survives isotropic tumbling and contributes to resolved multiplets. This distinction becomes essential when comparing COSY correlations based on scalar coupling with NOESY correlations that often report spatial proximity.

An important application of spin coupling is heteronuclear decoupling. In a carbon-13 spectrum, protons attached to carbon can split the carbon resonance. Irradiating the proton spins in a manner that averages the relevant coupling removes the resolved proton-induced multiplets from the carbon spectrum. The resulting pattern is often easier to assign because each distinguishable carbon site appears approximately as a single line. Decoupling does not mean that carbon and hydrogen are no longer chemically bonded. It changes the way their magnetic interaction appears during the acquisition.

Routine proton-decoupled carbon spectra are not necessarily quantitative by peak area. Carbon sites can have widely different relaxation times, and proton irradiation can alter signal intensities through nuclear Overhauser effects. A protonated carbon may be enhanced differently from a quaternary carbon. The presence of a weak or missing quaternary carbon line is therefore not direct evidence that the molecule lacks that carbon. In quantitative carbon analysis, appropriate pulse timing, decoupling strategy and calibration are required.

Integration means determining the area of a resonance or multiplet rather than reading only its height. When proton sites are equally excited and sufficiently recovered, their areas can be proportional to the number of contributing nuclei. A broad line can have a smaller height than a narrow line even when both have the same area. In mixtures, overlapping peaks can make integration unreliable, and water suppression or baseline errors can selectively distort signals. A ratio of measured heights should never be substituted automatically for a ratio of proton numbers.

For an illustrative quantitative example, a reference compound at 2.00 mM has an isolated signal representing four equivalent protons with integrated area 80 units. A metabolite signal represents three equivalent protons and has area 60 units. Under conditions of equal response per proton, the ratio of molecule concentrations is (60/3)/(80/4)=1. The metabolite concentration is therefore 2.00 mM. If the metabolite line were partially overlapped with a different signal, this calculation would overestimate its concentration. If its longitudinal magnetization had not fully recovered, the area might instead be underestimated.

The purpose of combining positions, areas and couplings is to reduce ambiguity. A spectrum can contain two unrelated resonances with similar chemical shifts, and different molecular fragments can have similar multiplicities. A robust assignment combines expected connectivity, reciprocal coupling, stoichiometric proton ratios, and where available a two-dimensional correlation. In complex biological samples, comparison with authentic standards or established reference spectra provides additional support, especially when a metabolite occurs in a mixture of related compounds.

Relaxation, linewidth and molecular dynamics

After an NMR pulse, the spin system is no longer at its original thermal equilibrium. It must return toward equilibrium through interactions with its surroundings and through loss of phase coherence. These processes are described by characteristic times rather than by a single universal recovery constant. The longitudinal relaxation time T1 describes the return of the z component of magnetization toward its equilibrium value. The transverse relaxation time T2 describes the decay of transverse coherence due to intrinsic interactions under the assumed model. T1 and T2 are measured in seconds, and their reciprocals R1 and R2 are relaxation rates in inverse seconds.

Longitudinal relaxation is also called spin-lattice relaxation. The word lattice does not imply that a liquid sample is a crystal. It refers broadly to the surrounding degrees of freedom that can exchange energy with the spin system. Following an ideal 180-degree inversion pulse, the net longitudinal magnetization initially points opposite to its equilibrium direction. As the spin populations recover, Mz approaches M0. In a simple single-exponential system the time dependence is Mz(t)=M0[1−2 exp(−t/T1)].

The equation can be understood by considering the difference from equilibrium. Initially Mz=−M0, so Mz−M0=−2M0. If the rate of recovery is proportional to this difference, the differential equation is d(Mz−M0)/dt=−(Mz−M0)/T1. Its solution is the exponential expression above. At infinite time the exponential tends to zero and Mz approaches M0. At time zero it gives −M0, satisfying the initial condition. These checks are useful whenever an examination problem presents several possible equations.

At the inversion-recovery null point, Mz=0, so 1−2 exp(−t/T1)=0. Rearranging gives exp(−t/T1)=1/2 and hence t=T1 ln 2, approximately 0.693T1. If the measured null occurs at 0.832 seconds in an ideal experiment, T1=0.832/0.693≈1.20 seconds. The point of zero signal is not the time at which all spins have disappeared; it is the time at which the net longitudinal magnetization passes through zero.

An inversion-recovery experiment varies the delay between inversion and the observation pulse. A plot of signed signal amplitude against delay can be fitted to the recovery equation. The sign matters because a magnitude-only spectrum can conceal whether the magnetization is negative or positive. Imperfect inversion, incomplete initial equilibrium, exchange between environments and multiple populations can lead to deviations from a simple exponential. A null-point estimate is convenient for examination calculations, but fitting multiple delays provides more information about whether the assumed model is adequate.

After an ideal 90-degree pulse from equilibrium, the longitudinal component starts approximately at zero. Its recovery is described by Mz(t)=M0[1−exp(−t/T1)] in the simple model. After one T1 the recovered fraction is 1−e⁻¹, or about 63.2%. After three T1 it is about 95.0%, and after five T1 about 99.3%. These percentages are often used to reason about quantitative acquisitions. If two sites have different T1 values, identical short repetition delays can produce different degrees of saturation.

Consider two proton signals corresponding to equal numbers of nuclei with T1 values 0.5 and 2.0 seconds. With ideal 90-degree excitation and a one-second recovery period, their recovered fractions are 1−exp(−2)=0.865 and 1−exp(−0.5)=0.393. Their observed areas under otherwise identical conditions would be in the ratio about 2.20:1, even though their equilibrium areas are equal. Increasing the delay reduces this bias but increases the total experiment time. This trade-off matters in quantitative metabolite analysis and explains why a short experiment optimized for detection may not be suitable for concentration measurement.

Transverse relaxation concerns the loss of a coherent net magnetization in the xy plane. Immediately after a suitable pulse, many spins contribute transverse magnetization with a coordinated phase. Interactions between spins and fluctuating local fields cause their phases to become less coordinated. Even if the spin populations have not returned to equilibrium, the transverse components may cancel more completely, reducing the detected signal. Thus transverse coherence and longitudinal population are different properties.

For an ideal single-exponential transverse decay, Mxy(t)=Mxy(0)exp(−t/T2). At one T2, approximately 36.8% of the initial transverse amplitude remains. At two T2, about 13.5% remains. A shorter T2 means more rapid intrinsic loss of coherence and a broader homogeneous resonance. The loss of net transverse magnetization does not require every spin to lose energy to the surroundings. Dephasing can occur through interactions that affect relative phase without producing the same change in spin-state populations.

A simple Fourier argument connects time-domain decay and linewidth. The transform of a damped complex exponential gives a Lorentzian absorption component proportional to R2/[R2²+(2πΔν)²], where Δν is frequency offset from the line center and R2=1/T2. At half the maximum, (2πΔν)²=R2². The offsets at the two half-height points are therefore ±R2/(2π). Their difference is the full width at half maximum, Δν1/2=1/(πT2). The expression assumes homogeneous exponential decay and an ideal absorption line.

If T2=0.100 seconds, the ideal homogeneous linewidth is 1/(π×0.100)=3.18 Hz. If a second site has T2=0.020 seconds, the linewidth is about 15.9 Hz. The second signal is five times broader. At equal integrated area, its peak height is substantially lower for the same ideal line shape. This example explains why a broadened peak can become difficult to detect without implying that the number of molecules contributing to that site has changed.

The decay measured directly in a free induction experiment frequently includes static magnetic-field inhomogeneity in addition to intrinsic transverse relaxation. Its effective timescale is commonly called T2*. It is generally shorter than or equal to T2 in the simple picture where additional static frequency variation causes dephasing. A broad line may therefore be due to an imperfect magnet rather than a biological change. Under a simplified exponential model one may describe the effective rate as the sum of intrinsic and additional broadening rates, although real inhomogeneous line shapes need not be Lorentzian.

The spin echo demonstrates how some of the apparent dephasing can be reversed. Following a 90-degree pulse, spins with different static frequency offsets accumulate different phases over a delay τ. A 180-degree pulse about an appropriate transverse axis reverses their relative phase order. During a second delay τ, the static offsets cause the phase differences to retrace, and an echo forms at time 2τ. The spins have not reversed their physical motion or traveled backward in time. Rather, the pulse has changed the phase relationship in a way that compensates for static frequency differences.

Intrinsic transverse relaxation continues during both delays. The echo therefore does not restore all the original magnetization. In the simple model its amplitude is M0 exp(−2τ/T2). Measuring echo amplitude for different total times can estimate T2. The distinction between delay τ and total echo time 2τ is a frequent source of factor-of-two errors. Diffusion through field gradients, changing local environments and imperfect pulses can also attenuate an echo, so an experimental estimate must be interpreted in light of the pulse sequence and sample behavior.

The molecular origin of relaxation is linked to time-dependent magnetic interactions. Molecules in solution rotate, vibrate and undergo internal motions. These motions cause local magnetic fields to fluctuate. The efficiency with which a fluctuating field drives spin transitions or destroys coherence depends on the frequency content of those fluctuations. A rapidly fluctuating field and a slowly fluctuating field of similar amplitude need not cause the same T1 or T2. This is why relaxation can reveal molecular dynamics rather than merely being an unwanted loss of signal.

A rotational correlation time describes approximately how long a molecular orientation remains correlated with its initial orientation. Small molecules in dilute solution often rotate rapidly and have shorter correlation times than large complexes. Increasing viscosity or binding a small molecule to a much larger partner can slow its rotational motion. Such changes alter relaxation, although the direction and magnitude of the T1 change depend on field and the motional regime. A simple universal claim that slower motion always shortens both T1 and T2 is incorrect.

Large macromolecules frequently give broader solution NMR resonances because slower tumbling increases important transverse relaxation contributions. Binding, aggregation or oligomerization can likewise increase linewidths. Local flexible segments may remain relatively sharp because their internal motions partly decouple them from the overall tumbling of the molecule. Thus a protein spectrum with some sharp lines and some broad lines may contain information about heterogeneous local motion, not just a uniform molecular mass.

Chemical exchange provides another route to line broadening. If a nucleus alternates between two molecular environments with different resonance frequencies, its phase evolution changes during the observation period. Slow, intermediate and fast exchange are defined relative to the frequency separation of the states and the exchange rate. In the slow regime, separate resonances can be observed, each reflecting its corresponding environment. In the fast regime, a single resonance near the population-weighted mean can occur. Intermediate exchange often produces especially broad or weak signals.

For example, a nucleus in free protein has a shift of 8.0 ppm and in bound protein 8.4 ppm. If exchange is fast and the bound fraction is 0.25, the observed shift is (0.75×8.0)+(0.25×8.4)=8.1 ppm. The shift changes because the nucleus spends part of its time in each state and the experiment averages over rapid switching. If the two forms exchange slowly, a mixture at the same bound fraction instead may show separate signals near 8.0 and 8.4 ppm, with areas reflecting populations only if relaxation and detection effects are controlled.

The absolute difference in resonance frequency between the two states increases at higher field when their chemical-shift difference is fixed in ppm. As a result, the same molecular exchange rate may appear in a different spectral regime at different fields. A spectrum that looks fast-exchanging at one field might show appreciable broadening at another. This provides a conceptual reason to avoid treating the labels slow and fast as fixed molecular attributes independent of the measurement.

Paramagnetic species can strongly influence nuclear relaxation because the electron magnetic moment is large. A nucleus near a paramagnetic center may relax rapidly and show severe broadening or loss of intensity. This is called paramagnetic relaxation enhancement when compared with an appropriate diamagnetic reference. The effect can reveal proximity to the paramagnetic center, but intensity loss also depends on motion, electron relaxation, exchange and the detailed measurement. A broad or missing peak alone cannot establish an exact distance.

A well-controlled relaxation comparison distinguishes the effect of molecular properties from experimental variation. Compare spectra with matched temperature, sample concentration, buffer composition and field conditions. Check whether broadening affects all lines, only selected sites or a reference compound as well. If a reference peak broadens alongside the protein signals, poor field homogeneity becomes more plausible. If only particular protein sites change during ligand addition, local exchange or interactions become more plausible. The strongest interpretation arises when a model predicts several independent observables rather than explaining one peak after the fact.

Multidimensional NMR: connecting signals in crowded spectra

A small molecule may have only a few proton resonances, allowing the one-dimensional spectrum to be read by inspection. A protein containing many residues has far more distinct atomic sites, with many resonances occurring in a relatively narrow chemical-shift range. Overlap is not merely a visual inconvenience. Two unrelated sites can contribute to the same apparent line, obscuring their intensities and coupling patterns. Multidimensional NMR addresses this problem by encoding additional frequency information and displaying correlations between sites.

A two-dimensional NMR experiment generally contains preparation, an evolution interval, a mixing or transfer period and detection. During the first evolution interval, conventionally called t1, spin coherence evolves under a chosen set of interactions. The mixing period transfers or transforms the coherence according to the pulse sequence. The signal is then recorded during a second time interval t2. Repeating the experiment with systematically varied t1 encodes the first frequency dimension. Fourier transformation with respect to both time variables produces a spectrum with two frequency axes.

This description is deliberately general because not every sequence encodes the same physical property. A cross peak is a feature at the intersection of two frequency coordinates. Its interpretation depends on the mechanism that transfers magnetization or coherence during the sequence. A cross peak is not automatically a bond, a distance or a direct molecular contact. Identifying the type of experiment is the first step in interpreting a two-dimensional spectrum.

COSY, or correlation spectroscopy, is a homonuclear experiment commonly used to identify scalar-coupled proton sites. In a simple two-spin system, the spectrum contains diagonal peaks at the individual proton frequencies and off-diagonal cross peaks linking the coupled sites. In a conventional symmetric proton–proton COSY plot, a correlation between A and B appears at coordinates (δA,δB) and (δB,δA). Diagonal peaks represent self-coordinates rather than independent evidence of a connection. The cross peaks reflect the relevant scalar-coupling pathway.

For lactate with distinguishable methyl and methine signals, a COSY correlation between them supports the connectivity inferred from the reciprocal doublet and quartet. Suppose a mixture contains an unrelated methyl signal that happens to overlap the lactate methyl position in one dimension. The COSY cross peak at the methine coordinate can help separate the connected lactate signal from unrelated components. Nevertheless, severe overlap in both dimensions can still prevent unique assignment.

TOCSY, total correlation spectroscopy, can relay magnetization through a network of scalar-coupled spins. It can therefore reveal a group of connected sites belonging to a spin system even when two distant members do not show a direct COSY cross peak. For example, several protons within an amino acid side chain may be linked through successive couplings. A TOCSY correlation between two members of the network does not necessarily establish a direct coupling or a single-bond relationship between them. The distinction is analogous to knowing that two stations lie on the same connected rail network rather than proving that a direct track connects those particular stations.

Heteronuclear experiments correlate frequencies of different isotopes. HSQC, heteronuclear single quantum coherence, commonly correlates a proton with a directly bonded nitrogen-15 or carbon-13 nucleus through scalar coupling. Its two axes represent different nuclear chemical-shift scales, so there is no requirement for a symmetric diagonal like that of a homonuclear COSY spectrum. A proton–nitrogen HSQC of an isotopically enriched protein provides a characteristic pattern of many backbone amide correlations.

A simple count of HSQC peaks must respect protein chemistry. Most non-proline backbone residues contain an amide hydrogen that can produce a correlation when the nitrogen is suitably labeled and the signal is detectable. Internal proline residues ordinarily lack a backbone amide proton. Side-chain amides and other exchangeable groups may produce additional correlations. Overlap, solvent exchange, conformational heterogeneity or broadening can remove or multiply apparent peaks. Consequently, the total number of visible peaks is not automatically equal to the number of amino acid residues.

In an HSQC titration, peaks are followed as a ligand is added. Some move, some change intensity and some remain nearly unchanged. In fast exchange, continuous peak movement can report the changing fraction of bound protein. In slow exchange, separate free and bound cross peaks may coexist. In intermediate exchange, peaks may become difficult to follow because their intensity falls. A student interpreting a diagram should first identify the exchange pattern, then decide whether a simple shift-based binding fit is appropriate.

Consider a protein with assigned amide signals from residues 10, 24, 38 and 51. An illustrative ligand addition shifts residues 10 and 24 strongly, residue 38 slightly and residue 51 negligibly. This result is consistent with the environment around residues 10 and 24 being affected by binding. It does not establish that these two residues make direct ligand contact. A remote conformational rearrangement, altered protonation or changes in dynamics can also shift signals. Mapping the affected sites onto an independently established structure can support a hypothesis, but additional evidence is needed to establish the interaction mechanism.

A common analytical goal is to estimate the equilibrium dissociation constant Kd. For a simple one-to-one binding equilibrium P+L⇌PL, Kd=[P][L]/[PL]. If free ligand concentration is known, the fraction of protein bound is f=[L]/(Kd+[L]). In fast exchange, an observed shift can be related to f by δobs=δfree+f(δbound−δfree). If free ligand equals Kd, f=1/2, and the shift lies halfway between the free and bound limiting values.

This simple expression must not be applied blindly to the total added ligand concentration. When ligand is in large excess relative to protein, free concentration may approximate total concentration. Otherwise binding depletes the free ligand. Let total protein be Pt, total ligand Lt and complex concentration x. Then free concentrations are Pt−x and Lt−x. Substitution into the equilibrium expression gives Kd x=(Pt−x)(Lt−x). Rearrangement yields x²−(Pt+Lt+Kd)x+PtLt=0. The smaller physical root gives the bound concentration because it cannot exceed either total concentration.

In a hypothetical titration with Pt=20 μM, Lt=20 μM and Kd=10 μM, the quadratic becomes x²−50x+400=0 when concentrations are expressed consistently in micromolar units. The roots are (50±√(2500−1600))/2=(50±30)/2, giving 10 and 40 μM. Only 10 μM is physically possible because there is only 20 μM of each reactant. The bound fraction is 0.5. Treating all 20 μM of ligand as free would give 20/(10+20)=0.667, an overestimate.

A binding fit also assumes that the signal being followed belongs to the same molecular site throughout the titration and that the experimental response is appropriately modeled by the populations. A shift that curves with ligand concentration may reflect more than one equilibrium, including multiple binding sites or coupled conformational changes. If the final shift has not approached saturation, the limiting shift and affinity may be difficult to estimate independently. Fitting a plausible equation is therefore not by itself proof that the chosen binding model is correct.

NOESY, nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy, uses cross-relaxation between nuclear spins to obtain information that can depend on spatial proximity. The nuclear Overhauser effect originates in dipolar relaxation rather than requiring a continuous scalar-coupling path through bonds. In an appropriate simple regime, the initial cross-relaxation rate has a strong inverse sixth-power dependence on internuclear distance, with additional dependence on molecular motion and spectral density. This steep distance dependence explains why close nuclei can produce strong spatial correlations.

Suppose two otherwise comparable proton pairs are separated by 3 Å and 6 Å. Under the simplified initial-rate and same-motion assumptions, the closer pair has a cross-relaxation magnitude proportional to 1/3⁶, whereas the more distant pair is proportional to 1/6⁶. The ratio is (6/3)⁶=64. This result demonstrates sensitivity to proximity but is not a universal prediction of the ratio of measured NOESY peak heights. Mixing time, relaxation, indirect transfer, motion and experimental conditions can alter the observed intensity.

Spin diffusion is one reason a NOESY correlation can be misleading if treated as a direct distance measurement. Magnetization may transfer from A to B and subsequently from B to C. A correlation between A and C can then appear even when direct transfer between them is weak. Choosing an appropriate experiment and using multiple constraints can limit such ambiguity. Chemical exchange may also produce cross peaks in NOESY-type spectra. The interpretation requires attention to the timescale and mechanism, not just the presence of an off-diagonal spot.

The distinction between through-bond and through-space evidence allows complementary experiments to be combined. COSY and related scalar-transfer experiments help establish connected spin systems. Heteronuclear correlations help organize signals and assign nuclei. NOE-based measurements constrain relative spatial arrangement. Couplings can provide local angular information, while chemical shifts help characterize environments. A complete structural determination requires sufficiently many independent constraints, appropriate modeling and validation. That integrated process will be considered in the later node on structural analysis.

A two-dimensional plot is often shown as contours of peak intensity, analogous to a topographic map. The position of a contour island identifies a pair of frequencies, and the contour levels describe the signal magnitude. Some experiments display both positive and negative features, usually with different visual encodings. Increasing the contour threshold can hide weak signals, whereas lowering it can reveal noise. Therefore an apparent difference in peak count between two printed diagrams can reflect plotting settings instead of a biological difference.

When comparing spectra, consistent referencing, contour thresholds and sample conditions are important. Spectral overlap, incomplete assignments, signal loss and differences in relaxation limit the interpretation even when multidimensional data are available. The value of additional dimensions lies in encoding independent frequency relationships, not in guaranteeing that every molecule or conformation can be distinguished. The same caution about observation and inference that applied to one-dimensional spectroscopy remains essential.

Electron spin resonance: electron Zeeman and hyperfine interactions

The principles of nuclear magnetic resonance provide the starting point for understanding electron paramagnetic resonance. In both methods a static magnetic field separates spin energy levels and an oscillating magnetic field drives transitions. For EPR, the relevant magnetic moment belongs to an electronic spin system rather than to an atomic nucleus. This change is consequential: electronic moments are much larger than nuclear moments, and the detailed electronic environment of an unpaired electron strongly influences the spectrum. EPR is therefore particularly valuable for detecting paramagnetic species among many diamagnetic components of a biological sample.

In the simplest case of an isolated spin-one-half electron, the electronic magnetic moment is expressed as μ=−gμBS/ℏ, where S is its spin angular momentum vector, μB is the Bohr magneton and g is a dimensionless factor. The minus sign reflects the negative electronic charge. In a static field along z, the simple spin Hamiltonian is H=gμBB0Sz/ℏ. The two projections mS=−1/2 and +1/2 then have energies −gμBB0/2 and +gμBB0/2 for positive g. Their energy difference is gμBB0. The description is valid for a simple isotropic effective spin-one-half system; more complicated electron systems may need additional interactions.

Equating the energy difference to the energy of the incident microwave gives the fundamental resonance condition hν=gμBB0. For a free electron, the g factor is approximately 2.0023. The ratio μB/h is approximately 13.996 GHz/T. Thus a free electron has a resonance frequency near 28.0 GHz per tesla, whereas hydrogen-1 has a frequency near 0.04258 GHz per tesla. This enormous difference in gyromagnetic ratio explains why EPR commonly employs microwaves at fields where nuclear resonance involves radiofrequencies.

An X-band EPR experiment commonly operates near 9–10 GHz. At 9.50 GHz, a species with g=2.0023 resonates at B0=9.50/(2.0023×13.996)=0.3390 T, or approximately 339 mT. The field is far smaller than that of a high-resolution 400 MHz proton instrument. Comparing just the static field strengths of two instruments therefore gives a misleading impression of their transition energies. A smaller applied field can produce a larger electron-spin splitting because the electron moment is much larger.

The g factor is an observable that reflects the electronic environment, including the effects of spin–orbit coupling and orbital contributions to the magnetic moment. Organic radicals often give g values near the free-electron value, but small shifts and anisotropy can help distinguish their environments. Transition-metal centers can display much larger deviations and more complicated patterns. A g value near two is not by itself a molecular identity: many different radical species have similar g values. Conversely, a value substantially different from two is not evidence of an instrumental error if the electronic structure supports it.

At fixed microwave frequency, the field at resonance is inversely proportional to g. A hypothetical species with g=2.10 measured at 9.50 GHz resonates near 9.50/(2.10×13.996)=0.3232 T. A species with g=1.95 resonates near 0.3481 T. Thus the larger g value appears at the lower field in a field-swept EPR experiment. This is a classic analytical distinction from simply plotting NMR frequency at a fixed field. The direction in which a feature shifts cannot be interpreted correctly without identifying which quantity is swept and which is fixed.

Many paramagnetic molecules have anisotropic magnetic properties. The effective g value then depends on the orientation of the molecule relative to the magnetic field. In an appropriate principal-axis representation, three components gx, gy and gz describe the directional behavior of the g tensor. In a rapidly tumbling liquid, orientation-dependent contributions may be averaged to an approximately isotropic g value. In a frozen solution containing randomly oriented molecules, the spectrum can retain contributions from many orientations and show characteristic features associated with the principal components.

A powder-like spectrum is therefore not necessarily a mixture of different chemical species. It can arise from one anisotropic species distributed over many orientations. Comparing a liquid spectrum with a frozen spectrum can help distinguish motionally averaged properties from static anisotropy. Temperature changes can also alter relaxation and chemical equilibria, so interpretation should not attribute every spectral difference solely to a change of molecular rotation. The experimental history and appropriate reference samples matter.

A second major source of EPR information is hyperfine coupling, the magnetic interaction between the unpaired electron spin and nearby nuclear spins. The electron experiences different transition energies depending on nuclear spin projections. This interaction can include a contact contribution related to electron spin density at a nucleus and a dipolar contribution related to the spatial relationship between electron and nuclear magnetic moments. Hyperfine constants can consequently provide clues to the distribution of unpaired spin density and the local electronic structure.

For an ideal isotropic electron spin S=1/2 coupled to one nucleus with spin I, a simple high-field energy model includes a term A mS mI, where A is the hyperfine coupling constant expressed in energy units. Electron-spin transitions with ΔmS=±1 and ΔmI=0 then have energy gμBB0+A mI. The allowed nuclear projections range from −I to +I. Under suitable first-order conditions, each projection produces a separate EPR line, giving 2I+1 components.

A proton has I=1/2, so one coupled proton can split an EPR resonance into two components. A nitrogen-14 nucleus has I=1, so one coupled nitrogen-14 nucleus can produce three components. Unlike the proton NMR triplet from two equivalent neighboring protons, this triplet originates from the three nuclear projections of a single spin-one nucleus. If those projections have nearly equal populations and transition probabilities and the lines are fully resolved under nonsaturating conditions, the ideal relative areas of the three EPR lines are 1:1:1 rather than 1:2:1.

A nitrogen-15 nucleus has I=1/2 and can produce two hyperfine lines in the analogous simple spin system. Isotopic substitution can thus test whether nitrogen contributes to a radical spectrum. It may also alter the magnitude of hyperfine coupling because the nuclear magnetic properties differ. A student should not assume that replacing nitrogen-14 by nitrogen-15 changes only the number of components while leaving all their spacings identical. Isotopic enrichment is an experimental way to connect a spectral feature to a particular element without changing the basic chemical identity of that element.

Multiple equivalent nuclei give more complex degeneracy patterns. For two equivalent protons coupled to one electron, the possible total nuclear projections are +1, zero and −1, with degeneracies 1, 2 and 1. The three ideal hyperfine lines therefore have relative areas 1:2:1. Three equivalent protons yield four components with 1:3:3:1 degeneracy. For n equivalent nuclei of spin I, the maximum number of first-order groups associated with summed projections is 2nI+1, if all those groups are resolved. The relative intensities arise from the number of configurations with each summed projection, not automatically from the binomial coefficients unless I=1/2.

Nonequivalent nuclei can split each component further, with the maximum number of distinct first-order lines given by the product of the individual multiplicities when spacings do not accidentally overlap. An electron coupled to one nitrogen-14 nucleus and one proton can therefore show up to six components. If the two coupling constants have a particular ratio, some components may coincide. Additional effects such as anisotropy, quadrupolar interactions, electron–electron coupling and rapid relaxation can make the actual spectrum much more complex than the ideal pattern. The word maximum is therefore important.

Hyperfine constants are sometimes expressed in energy or frequency units and sometimes as equivalent field separations. For a simple isotropic spectrum, a hyperfine constant expressed as A/h in hertz corresponds to a field spacing approximately (A/h)/(gμB/h). For example, a coupling of 42 MHz for g≈2 corresponds to 0.042 GHz/(27.992 GHz/T)=0.00150 T, or 1.50 mT. An exam problem supplying a hyperfine spacing directly in millitesla does not require this conversion unless it asks for the equivalent frequency or energy.

The elementary EPR equation assumes a simple electron spin system. When the total electronic spin exceeds one-half, interactions can split sublevels even in the absence of the applied field. This phenomenon is termed zero-field splitting. It is relevant to some transition-metal and triplet-state systems and can create more complex resonance conditions. At examination level it is sufficient to recognize that the free-electron formula is not a universal description of all paramagnets and that nonzero spin does not ensure a simple g≈2 line.

EPR measurement, spectra and biological applications

A conventional continuous-wave EPR spectrometer uses a microwave source, a resonator containing the sample, a magnet and a sensitive detector. The microwave field interacts with the electron spin system, while the static magnetic field is swept through a range of values at approximately fixed microwave frequency. As the resonance condition is met, the spin system absorbs microwave energy. The instrument measures changes in the microwave response of the resonator. The resonator helps couple electromagnetic energy to the sample efficiently, but sample geometry and dielectric properties can affect its performance.

In many continuous-wave EPR instruments a small modulation is superimposed on the magnetic field and a phase-sensitive detector measures the corresponding modulated response. For sufficiently small modulation amplitude and a suitable detection phase, the displayed signal approximates the first derivative of the absorption spectrum with respect to field. This is why a standard EPR line is frequently drawn with both a positive and a negative lobe rather than as an ordinary upward absorption peak. The two lobes are features of the derivative of one resonance, not evidence of two independent species.

The center of a symmetric isolated absorption line is near the zero crossing of its first derivative between the two extrema. The peak-to-peak linewidth is the field separation between those extrema. It is not the same quantity as the full width at half maximum of the underlying absorption spectrum. Their relationship depends on the line-shape model. For a Lorentzian absorption line, the derivative peak-to-peak width is the absorption full width at half maximum divided by √3. For a Gaussian line, the corresponding relationship differs. A reported linewidth is interpretable only when its definition is stated.

The derivative signal also changes how intensity is measured. The area of an ideal derivative line integrated across the complete symmetric feature is approximately zero because its positive and negative contributions cancel. Integrating the derivative once reconstructs the absorption profile, up to baseline and scaling constants. Integrating that profile a second time gives a quantity that can be related to the number of detectable spins under appropriate conditions. This is called double integration. Reading the largest derivative peak height is not a reliable universal measure of spin concentration because broadening can strongly alter height even if the underlying absorption area remains unchanged.

Consider two spectra of a stable radical measured under comparable nonsaturating conditions. One has a larger peak-to-peak amplitude but a much narrower linewidth. The narrower line can produce a larger derivative amplitude without having more spins. A comparison of carefully corrected double integrals against a standard is a stronger approach to estimating relative spin populations. Even then, receiver gain, microwave power, resonator response, temperature, sample geometry and baseline must be accounted for. In a biological sample containing overlapping species, separating contributions may require additional evidence.

Microwave power affects intensity. At low power, increasing the applied oscillating field increases the response. At sufficiently high power, excitation can disturb the equilibrium spin populations faster than longitudinal relaxation restores them, producing saturation. The extent of saturation depends on relaxation properties and the applied field. Two radical species measured at the same power may respond differently if their relaxation times differ. A lower measured signal from one species cannot be interpreted as fewer spins without checking whether the comparison is made in an appropriate response regime.

Field modulation also influences resolution. If the modulation amplitude becomes too large relative to the intrinsic linewidth, the derivative approximation deteriorates and spectral features can broaden or distort. A spectrum acquired with strong modulation may have a favorable apparent signal-to-noise ratio but insufficient resolution to distinguish neighboring hyperfine lines. This parallels the distinction between sensitivity and resolution in NMR: acquiring more apparent signal does not necessarily improve the specific information needed for an assignment.

Pulsed EPR uses microwave pulses and time-domain detection rather than relying solely on continuous-wave field sweeps. Echo-based methods can probe electron spin coherence and relaxation. Two electron spins also interact through space; under an appropriate dipolar model the interaction scales with the inverse cube of the separation and depends on orientation. Double electron–electron resonance, often called DEER or PELDOR, exploits this interaction to estimate distance distributions between paramagnetic centers. The method is a useful conceptual bridge to biological structure analysis, but converting an observed modulation into a distance distribution requires modeling and appropriate controls. It should not be confused with the inverse sixth-power dependence associated with simple NOE cross-relaxation.

Biological EPR can detect intrinsic paramagnetic centers. A protein containing a redox-active metal cofactor may change its electron configuration when the cofactor is oxidized or reduced. Comparing spectra before and after a controlled redox change can reveal which EPR-detectable state is populated. For example, copper(II) commonly has a d⁹ configuration with an unpaired electron, whereas copper(I) is commonly d¹⁰ and diamagnetic. In a suitable protein system, changes in a copper(II) EPR feature can therefore help follow its redox state.

The interpretation is conditional, not absolute. The coordination environment affects g and hyperfine parameters, and spectral disappearance could result from changed relaxation, binding geometry or an alternative electronic state rather than from a simple reduction. Independent chemical or spectroscopic evidence for redox state, sample integrity and total metal content strengthens the conclusion. EPR can be highly selective for particular electron configurations, but it cannot generally count every metal atom in a sample regardless of its spin state.

Iron–sulfur centers in electron-transfer proteins offer another biological context. Different cluster types and oxidation states can exhibit different total spin configurations. Some states are readily observed by EPR; others have little or no conventional signal. The correct reasoning begins with the electronic state of the cluster, not simply the presence of iron. An EPR observation combined with optical absorption, chemical composition and functional measurements can help connect a paramagnetic intermediate to a biological electron-transfer process.

Short-lived radicals can be difficult to observe directly because their steady-state concentrations may be very low and their lifetimes brief. Spin trapping uses a reagent that reacts with a radical to form a paramagnetic adduct that is more amenable to detection. The EPR spectrum then reports properties of the adduct rather than an unchanged spectrum of the original radical. Adduct formation, competing reactions, adduct stability and background signals can influence the result. A spectrum compatible with a particular adduct is evidence that must be evaluated against suitable controls, not automatically proof of a specific radical-generation pathway.

Spin labeling is a distinct strategy. A stable paramagnetic group, frequently a nitroxide, is attached at a selected location of a biomolecule or introduced as a probe of its environment. A nitroxide containing nitrogen-14 can exhibit a three-line spectrum when rapid motion averages anisotropy. When the label is less mobile, the spectrum can become broader and more anisotropic. Comparing the lines can provide information about local motion or changes in the environment of the probe.

For example, a membrane-associated spin probe can sample different degrees of molecular mobility in a lipid environment. An increase in lipid ordering may reduce the probe's rotational freedom, altering the EPR line shape. A more mobile probe may exhibit narrower features. The signal represents the motion and surroundings of the particular label, not a universal direct measurement of the entire membrane's viscosity. Probe depth, orientation, partitioning and the molecular context can affect the result.

In site-directed spin labeling of a protein, the attached label can report local motion, accessibility or proximity to another labeled site. A comparison between the unbound and ligand-bound protein may reveal changes at a site even if the protein's overall conformation appears similar by another method. The label itself can perturb the system, however, especially if attached near a functional interface. It is therefore important to compare the behavior of labeled and unlabeled proteins with an independent functional or binding measurement. Changes attributed to protein motion may otherwise arise from the probe's own altered conformations.

EPR is useful precisely because many biological molecules have no intrinsic EPR signal. A small population of labeled molecules can be detected among many unlabeled components. This selectivity contrasts with ordinary proton NMR, where abundant signals from many sites can overlap. Yet the selective advantage has a cost: EPR does not provide a general site-by-site nuclear chemical-shift map of a diamagnetic macromolecule unless an appropriate paramagnetic strategy is available. Choosing between the techniques requires identifying the desired observable rather than ranking one as inherently superior.

Integrating magnetic resonance with experimental evidence

Suppose the research question concerns the binding of a small ligand to a soluble protein. NMR can reveal site-sensitive chemical-shift changes, potentially distinguish exchange regimes and, under suitable conditions, quantify affinity. EPR could be informative if the protein contains a paramagnetic center whose environment changes upon binding or if a validated spin label is available. Fluorescence can offer sensitive measurements of binding when an appropriate fluorophore responds. Circular dichroism can test for large changes in average secondary structure. Each method answers a related but different question.

Now consider an illustrative dataset from a ligand titration. An assigned protein NMR peak shifts from 7.80 to 8.10 ppm as ligand concentration increases, while several nearby peaks also shift. The CD spectrum remains approximately unchanged, and a spin label positioned in a distant region displays no detectable line-shape change. These observations are compatible with a local interaction or local conformational rearrangement without a large change in average secondary structure or in the mobility of that particular distant probe. They do not establish that the entire protein remains unchanged. The absence of a change in a selected observable is not evidence of absence of every possible molecular change.

The design of a useful control depends on the competing explanation. If ligand addition changes sample pH, a matched buffer addition and pH measurements help test whether the chemical shifts are due to protonation rather than binding. If a spin label might alter activity, an independent comparison of labeled and unlabeled protein addresses that problem. If NMR peaks disappear as concentration rises, checking solubility, aggregation and recovery after dilution helps distinguish exchange broadening from sample loss. No single control is appropriate for every uncertainty.

A second illustrative experiment compares an EPR-active metal-containing protein before and after a change of its redox environment. The derivative spectrum decreases markedly in amplitude while a UV–visible band also changes. If EPR line shape, linewidth and double integral are examined and independent redox evidence supports the same transition, the combined observations strengthen a redox interpretation. A decrease in derivative height alone would be much weaker because the number of detectable spins may not have decreased. Conversely, optical absorption may detect species that are EPR-silent, helping account for material that is not directly visible to the electron-spin experiment.

An important application of proton NMR is metabolite profiling. A mixture of small molecules contains overlapping resonances whose relative areas can sometimes estimate concentrations. The chemical shifts and scalar couplings establish potential identities, while reference standards and multidimensional correlations strengthen assignments. A concentration change in a metabolite pool does not directly establish a change in metabolic flux: a pool can remain constant while production and consumption both increase, or it can change because one process has changed. This distinction between measured amount and pathway rate is developed in the later metabolomics node.

Phosphorus NMR provides complementary access to phosphorus-containing compounds. The phosphorus environment in inorganic phosphate differs from those of nucleotide phosphate groups, giving characteristic resonance patterns under appropriate conditions. Changes in pH and metal binding can alter phosphorus shifts. Assigning a peak to a particular phosphate-containing metabolite therefore requires suitable context. The method can provide insight into energy metabolism, but the presence of an ATP signal is not itself a direct measurement of the rate at which ATP is synthesized or hydrolyzed.

The study of biological structure illustrates the value of combining restraints. An NMR chemical shift reports an environment; a J coupling can constrain a local dihedral angle; an NOE can support proximity under appropriate conditions; EPR dipolar measurements can constrain distances between paramagnetic centers. A structural hypothesis can be examined by predicting whether it is consistent with several such observables. A single signal generally cannot establish a full three-dimensional model. The later structural-analysis node considers how restraints and model validation are combined with information from X-ray diffraction and cryo-electron microscopy.

The major strengths of NMR are its site-specific information, the possibility of studying molecules in solution and access to interactions and dynamics. Its limitations include relatively low sensitivity, overlapping signals in complex samples, requirements for suitable concentration and sample stability, and broadening in many large or slowly tumbling systems. EPR offers selective sensitivity to paramagnetic species and access to electronic structure, mobility and distances between spin centers, but it is blind to ordinary closed-shell electron configurations and can require a suitable intrinsic or introduced paramagnet.

Experimental conditions should always accompany an interpretation. The same molecular sample may show different linewidths at different temperatures; a sample may change aggregation state with concentration; a paramagnetic signal may become difficult to detect at a different microwave power. Data acquired under different conditions can be compared only after deciding which differences are intentional variables and which are uncontrolled confounders. This reasoning is directly relevant to Part C problems that ask students to distinguish hypotheses rather than repeat definitions.

The central lesson is that magnetic resonance is not a direct photograph of a molecule. It is a quantitative measurement of magnetic interactions and their evolution. Nuclear or electron spin supplies the signal, the field and oscillating radiation create the conditions for resonance, and the molecular surroundings modify observable parameters. Interpretation connects those parameters to chemical identity, conformation, motion or electronic state through explicit physical models. Recognizing the assumptions of those models is what turns knowledge of spectroscopy into reliable biological reasoning.

Original examination practice

These original questions model CSIR-NET and State SET reasoning; they are not previous-year questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

Part B — conceptual foundations

1. Which isotope is spin-zero in its ground state?

  1. ¹H
  2. ¹³C
  3. ¹²C
  4. ³¹P
Answer and explanation

C. ¹²C

¹²C has I=0 and no conventional nuclear spin resonance. The other three have I=1/2 and are useful magnetic nuclei.

2. What creates Zeeman splitting in the simple NMR model?

  1. A static magnetic field
  2. A Fourier transform
  3. A solvent reference
  4. A chemical bond breaking
Answer and explanation

A. A static magnetic field

B₀ interacts with the magnetic moment and lifts spin projection degeneracy. Processing and referencing describe data; bond cleavage is unnecessary.

3. What is the conventional positive resonance frequency for a nucleus of gyromagnetic ratio γ?

  1. γ²B₀
  2. B₀/γ
  3. 2πB₀
  4. Magnitude of γ times B₀ divided by 2π
Answer and explanation

D. Magnitude of γ times B₀ divided by 2π

The magnitude of angular frequency is |γ|B₀; dividing by 2π converts radians per second to hertz.

4. Which statement describes NMR chemical shift?

  1. An absolute molecular concentration
  2. Relative frequency offset expressed in ppm
  3. A direct interatomic distance
  4. An electron count
Answer and explanation

B. Relative frequency offset expressed in ppm

Chemical shift is a frequency difference normalized to the reference frequency and scaled by 10⁶. It reports a local magnetic environment.

5. For two proton sites under otherwise identical conditions, greater shielding generally means:

  1. Larger δ
  2. Larger J always
  3. Smaller δ
  4. No NMR signal
Answer and explanation

C. Smaller δ

Shielding reduces the effective local field and the frequency relative to a less shielded proton. Coupling and detectability are different properties.

6. What is the expected first-order proton multiplicity with three equivalent spin-half neighbors?

  1. Quartet
  2. Triplet
  3. Doublet
  4. Septet
Answer and explanation

A. Quartet

The n+1 rule gives four groups with 1:3:3:1 ideal intensities. The other options do not match three equivalent neighboring spins.

7. Which property is approximately field-independent in hertz for the same molecule?

  1. Absolute Larmor frequency
  2. Chemical-shift separation in Hz
  3. Proton chemical shift in Hz
  4. Scalar J coupling
Answer and explanation

D. Scalar J coupling

J is a molecular interaction measured in hertz; Larmor frequency and shift differences in hertz scale with field.

8. What is the free induction decay?

  1. A spatial image of the protein
  2. Time-domain signal from transverse magnetization
  3. A plot of ligand concentration
  4. An ESR hyperfine constant
Answer and explanation

B. Time-domain signal from transverse magnetization

Precessing transverse magnetization induces a signal that decays. Fourier transformation resolves frequencies in that signal.

9. T₁ describes:

  1. Recovery of longitudinal magnetization
  2. Only static field inhomogeneity
  3. The chemical shift origin
  4. Number of resonances
Answer and explanation

A. Recovery of longitudinal magnetization

T₁ is the time constant for return toward longitudinal equilibrium. T₂ concerns coherence, and T₂* includes additional dephasing.

10. Which ideal relation connects homogeneous NMR absorption linewidth and transverse relaxation?

  1. Δν = πT₂
  2. Δν = T₁/T₂
  3. Δν = 1/(πT₂)
  4. Δν = 2πT₁
Answer and explanation

C. Δν = 1/(πT₂)

Fourier transformation of exponential transverse decay gives a Lorentzian whose full width at half maximum is 1/(πT₂), in Hz.

11. Which sequence refocuses static frequency offsets in the basic Hahn echo?

  1. 180° alone
  2. 90°–τ–180°–τ
  3. 90°–90° with no delay
  4. Continuous water irradiation only
Answer and explanation

B. 90°–τ–180°–τ

The refocusing pulse reverses relative phase order and produces an echo after total time 2τ. It cannot remove intrinsic relaxation.

12. A COSY cross peak primarily indicates:

  1. Molecular weight
  2. Electron spin
  3. A unique interatomic distance
  4. A scalar-coupling correlation
Answer and explanation

D. A scalar-coupling correlation

COSY transfer reveals coupled nuclear spins. NOE-based experiments provide different information about proximity.

13. A ¹H–¹⁵N HSQC commonly correlates:

  1. Directly bonded proton and nitrogen sites
  2. Two unpaired electrons
  3. Two photon wavelengths
  4. Unconnected proton concentrations
Answer and explanation

A. Directly bonded proton and nitrogen sites

HSQC uses heteronuclear scalar coupling to correlate nuclear chemical shifts, commonly through one bond.

14. The initial NOE cross-relaxation magnitude, for otherwise comparable pairs in a simple regime, depends strongly on:

  1. r²
  2. r
  3. r⁻⁶
  4. r⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. r⁻⁶

Dipolar cross-relaxation has an inverse sixth-power distance dependence under the stated assumptions. Actual NOESY peak intensities can be affected by other processes.

15. Which species is ordinarily EPR active?

  1. Closed-shell glucose
  2. A stable nitroxide radical
  3. ¹²C in a closed-shell compound
  4. Paired-electron water
Answer and explanation

B. A stable nitroxide radical

A nitroxide contains an unpaired electron. The other choices do not normally contain an EPR-active electron spin.

16. ESR and EPR denote:

  1. Two different types of fluorescence
  2. Different isotopes
  3. Different field units
  4. The same basic electron magnetic resonance method
Answer and explanation

D. The same basic electron magnetic resonance method

Electron spin resonance and electron paramagnetic resonance are alternative names for the same spectroscopy family.

17. For a simple electron spin-one-half system, the EPR condition is:

  1. hν=gμBB₀
  2. hν=kBT
  3. hν=J²
  4. hν=1/T₁
Answer and explanation

A. hν=gμBB₀

The electron Zeeman gap is gμBB₀. The other expressions do not describe this gap dimensionally or physically.

18. One coupled ¹⁴N nucleus with I=1 gives how many ideal EPR hyperfine lines?

  1. Two
  2. Four
  3. Three
  4. One
Answer and explanation

C. Three

Its three nuclear projections yield 2I+1=3 electron transitions in the first-order model.

19. Conventional field-modulated CW EPR commonly displays:

  1. An optical emission spectrum
  2. First derivative of absorption
  3. A DNA sequence
  4. Integrated proton concentration
Answer and explanation

B. First derivative of absorption

Phase-sensitive detection approximates the derivative for small modulation; the positive and negative lobes belong to one absorption line.

20. What distinguishes spin labeling from spin trapping?

  1. Both always detect the original radical unchanged
  2. Spin labeling measures only nuclear spins
  3. Spin trapping requires all electrons paired
  4. Labeling introduces a stable probe; trapping forms a detectable radical adduct
Answer and explanation

D. Labeling introduces a stable probe; trapping forms a detectable radical adduct

A label reports its environment or mobility; a trapping reaction yields an adduct whose spectrum must be interpreted with chemical controls.

Part C — analytical reasoning

21. A proton spectrometer operates at 600 MHz. Estimate its field using 42.58 MHz/T.

  1. 7.05 T
  2. 14.09 T
  3. 25.55 T
  4. 0.071 T
Answer and explanation

B. 14.09 T

600/42.58=14.09 T. Multiply rather than divide gives incorrect units; 7.05 T would correspond to roughly 300 MHz.

22. Two sites differ by 0.15 ppm. What is their separation at 400 and 800 MHz?

  1. 60 and 120 Hz
  2. 120 and 60 Hz
  3. 0.15 Hz at both fields
  4. 60 Hz at both fields
Answer and explanation

A. 60 and 120 Hz

Δν=δ difference × nominal MHz, giving 60 and 120 Hz. The ppm difference is unchanged, but the Hz separation doubles.

23. A 9 Hz doublet is measured at 300 and 600 MHz. What are its approximate widths in ppm?

  1. 0.015 and 0.030
  2. 9 and 18
  3. 0.030 and 0.015
  4. 0.030 at both
Answer and explanation

C. 0.030 and 0.015

Divide 9 Hz by 300 and 600 MHz respectively. J stays 9 Hz; its ppm representation halves.

24. A multiplet has four equal-area lines at offsets −6, −2, +2, +6 Hz. Which conclusion is best?

  1. It proves three equivalent proton neighbors
  2. It must be a triplet
  3. It is necessarily one electron resonance
  4. Equal spacing alone does not establish a binomial quartet
Answer and explanation

D. Equal spacing alone does not establish a binomial quartet

An ideal proton quartet has 1:3:3:1 intensities, not four equal lines. A doublet of doublets or coincident patterns can give four lines; additional connectivity evidence is needed.

25. An isolated metabolite signal represents two protons and has area 90. A 3 mM reference representing three protons has area 135. Estimate metabolite concentration assuming equal response per proton.

  1. 2 mM
  2. 3 mM
  3. 4.5 mM
  4. 6 mM
Answer and explanation

B. 3 mM

Area per proton is 45 for each signal; their concentration ratio is one. Directly comparing areas without normalizing proton count gives the wrong result.

26. A proton FID yields SNR 12 with 25 independent scans. How many scans are needed for SNR 36?

  1. 225
  2. 75
  3. 100
  4. 900
Answer and explanation

A. 225

Tripling SNR requires nine times the scans because SNR scales as √N. 25×9=225 under stable conditions.

27. An ideal inversion-recovery null is observed at 1.386 s. What is T₁?

  1. 0.693 s
  2. 1.386 s
  3. 2.00 s
  4. 4.00 s
Answer and explanation

C. 2.00 s

The null occurs at T₁ ln 2, so 1.386/0.693=2.00 s. It is not one full relaxation time.

28. An absorption line has homogeneous FWHM 6.37 Hz. Estimate T₂.

  1. 0.50 s
  2. 0.157 s
  3. 2.00 s
  4. 0.050 s
Answer and explanation

D. 0.050 s

T₂=1/(π×6.37)=0.0500 s. Inverting the frequency without π would overestimate T₂.

29. Echo amplitude is 25% of its initial value at total echo time 0.277 s. Estimate T₂ for a single exponential.

  1. 0.10 s
  2. 0.20 s
  3. 0.40 s
  4. 1.386 s
Answer and explanation

B. 0.20 s

0.25=e^(−0.277/T₂), so T₂=0.277/ln4≈0.200 s. The supplied time already includes both delays.

30. A reference and every analyte resonance broaden after a sample change; echoes substantially refocus the extra loss. Which explanation is most directly supported?

  1. Static field inhomogeneity
  2. Every molecule formed the same covalent adduct
  3. All T₁ values became identical
  4. All spins vanished
Answer and explanation

A. Static field inhomogeneity

Common broadening and refocusing support static dephasing. Molecular processes may also contribute, but the observations do not demonstrate a uniform chemical change.

31. Equal proton populations have T₁=0.5 and 2 s. With an ideal 90° pulse and 1 s repetition recovery, their apparent area ratio is approximately:

  1. 1:1
  2. 1:4
  3. 2.20:1
  4. 4:1
Answer and explanation

C. 2.20:1

Recovery fractions are 1−e⁻²=0.865 and 1−e⁻⁰·⁵=0.393. Dividing gives 2.20, despite equal equilibrium populations.

32. Free and bound sites resonate at 7.5 and 8.0 ppm. A single fast-exchange peak is at 7.65 ppm. What is the bound fraction?

  1. 0.15
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.70
  4. 0.30
Answer and explanation

D. 0.30

f=(7.65−7.5)/(8.0−7.5)=0.30. This assumes a two-state system and population-averaged shift.

33. A ligand produces two resolved protein peaks instead of one moving peak. Which model best fits, absent other evidence?

  1. Fast exchange only
  2. Slow exchange between distinguishable states
  3. Zero spin nuclei
  4. Identical chemical environments
Answer and explanation

B. Slow exchange between distinguishable states

Separate free and bound signals are consistent with slow exchange relative to frequency separation. Areas are quantitative only under suitable acquisition.

34. In a one-site binding experiment, free ligand is 30 μM and the bound fraction is 0.75. Estimate Kd.

  1. 10 μM
  2. 22.5 μM
  3. 40 μM
  4. 90 μM
Answer and explanation

A. 10 μM

f=L/(Kd+L); Kd=L(1−f)/f=30×0.25/0.75=10 μM. Total ligand cannot be substituted without considering depletion.

35. For Pt=Lt=20 μM and Kd=10 μM, what is the complex concentration?

  1. 20 μM
  2. 40 μM
  3. 10 μM
  4. 13.33 μM
Answer and explanation

C. 10 μM

The quadratic x²−50x+400=0 has roots 10 and 40 μM. Only 10 is physically possible. 13.33 would follow from incorrectly treating total ligand as free.

36. A 100-residue enriched protein has five internal prolines. Why might an HSQC show fewer than 95 backbone amide peaks?

  1. Every residue must yield two peaks
  2. ¹⁵N is spin-zero
  3. EPR absorbs the amide protons
  4. Overlap, exchange or broadening may hide peaks
Answer and explanation

D. Overlap, exchange or broadening may hide peaks

Internal proline lacks the usual amide H; other expected signals may be unresolved or broadened. Side-chain signals can also complicate counting.

37. Two otherwise comparable NOE pairs are 4 and 6 Å apart. What is the ideal initial-rate magnitude ratio (near/far)?

  1. 1.5
  2. 11.39
  3. 2.25
  4. 64
Answer and explanation

B. 11.39

(6/4)⁶=1.5⁶=11.39. This applies to the simplified cross-relaxation model, not arbitrary measured peak heights.

38. A protein signal shifts near a distant residue during ligand addition. What is the strongest defensible inference?

  1. Its local magnetic environment changes
  2. It must directly touch the ligand
  3. Its peptide bond broke
  4. The ligand covalently modifies every residue
Answer and explanation

A. Its local magnetic environment changes

Chemical-shift perturbation indicates an environment change, possibly direct or allosteric. Direct contact requires independent evidence.

39. At ν=9.50 GHz, a simple EPR line is at 0.339 T. With μB/h=13.996 GHz/T, estimate g.

  1. 0.50
  2. 13.996
  3. 2.00
  4. 28.0
Answer and explanation

C. 2.00

g=9.50/(13.996×0.339)≈2.00. Frequency and field units must agree.

40. At fixed microwave frequency, a radical's g increases from 2.00 to 2.10. Its resonance field will:

  1. Increase by 5%
  2. Stay fixed
  3. Become zero
  4. Decrease by approximately 4.8%
Answer and explanation

D. Decrease by approximately 4.8%

Bnew/Bold=2.00/2.10=0.9524. Higher g means lower resonant field at constant frequency.

41. An electron is coupled to one ¹⁴N and one nonequivalent proton. What is the maximum first-order hyperfine line count?

  1. Five
  2. Six
  3. Three
  4. Nine
Answer and explanation

B. Six

The multiplicities multiply: (2×1+1)(2×1/2+1)=3×2=6, provided all lines resolve and do not coincide.

42. Two equivalent protons cause an ideal EPR triplet. Relative line areas are:

  1. 1:2:1
  2. 1:1:1
  3. 1:3:1
  4. 2:1:2
Answer and explanation

A. 1:2:1

Two spin-half nuclear projections can sum to +1, 0 or −1, with two configurations for the middle group. A single ¹⁴N instead gives an ideal 1:1:1.

43. An EPR hyperfine spacing is 2.0 mT at g≈2. Estimate its frequency equivalent using μB/h=13.996 GHz/T.

  1. 2 MHz
  2. 28 MHz
  3. 56 MHz
  4. 560 MHz
Answer and explanation

C. 56 MHz

gμB/h≈27.992 GHz/T. Multiply by 0.002 T to obtain 0.055984 GHz≈56 MHz.

44. Two derivative EPR spectra have equal double integrals after calibration, but one has a larger peak-to-peak amplitude and narrower line. Which conclusion follows?

  1. The taller spectrum must contain more spins
  2. Both species must have the same g
  3. Both species must be identical
  4. The data support equal detectable spin amounts under the stated calibration
Answer and explanation

D. The data support equal detectable spin amounts under the stated calibration

Narrowing can increase derivative amplitude without increasing absorption area. Equal corrected double integrals support equal spin amounts, not chemical identity.

45. A nitroxide-labeled membrane protein loses activity after labeling. How should a ligand-induced EPR mobility change be interpreted?

  1. It conclusively proves native conformational change
  2. The label may perturb the system; validate with a less perturbing probe and independent assay
  3. It is impossible for a label to alter activity
  4. It proves the protein is diamagnetic
Answer and explanation

B. The label may perturb the system; validate with a less perturbing probe and independent assay

Loss of activity is evidence that the labeling intervention affects function. An independently validated label or assay is needed before generalizing the observation to the native protein.

Additional SET, assertion–reason and experimental questions

46. Assertion–reason: A high-field NMR spectrum can resolve two chemical shifts better in Hz. Reason: a fixed ppm difference spans more hertz at larger nominal frequency.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; unrelated
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion

The ppm-to-Hz relation explains why separation can improve, assuming comparable widths.

47. Assertion–reason: All EPR-silent metal centers are diamagnetic. Reason: rapid relaxation and zero-field splitting can hinder ordinary EPR detection.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; unrelated
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

C. Assertion false; reason true

Nonzero spin does not guarantee detectability. The reason identifies mechanisms that invalidate the universal assertion.

48. State SET: Which is most suitable for following a phosphate metabolite without introducing a paramagnetic label?

  1. CW EPR alone
  2. ¹²C NMR
  3. Fluorescence without a reporter
  4. ³¹P NMR
Answer and explanation

D. ³¹P NMR

Phosphorus-31 is magnetic and can distinguish phosphate environments; the other choices lack the specified direct observable.

49. Data interpretation: An NMR peak vanishes upon moving a sample from H₂O to D₂O. Which is plausible?

  1. Protein has necessarily degraded
  2. An exchangeable proton was replaced by deuterium
  3. All carbon atoms were removed
  4. The magnet has reversed field
Answer and explanation

B. An exchangeable proton was replaced by deuterium

Exchange of a labile hydrogen with solvent deuterium removes its proton signal. Protein integrity needs a separate check.

50. Numerical: At 400 MHz a reference-to-site offset is 1000 Hz. Calculate δ.

  1. 2.5 ppm
  2. 0.4 ppm
  3. 400 ppm
  4. 1000 ppm
Answer and explanation

A. 2.5 ppm

1000 Hz / 400 MHz = 2.5 ppm by the standard conversion.

51. Design: Which control best separates buffer-driven NMR shifts from a putative ligand effect?

  1. Increase contour threshold
  2. Measure unrelated DNA sequence
  3. Add matched ligand-free buffer while monitoring pH
  4. Change the field and temperature together
Answer and explanation

C. Add matched ligand-free buffer while monitoring pH

A matched buffer addition controls dilution, ionic composition and pH-related effects. Changing several variables simultaneously weakens causal interpretation.

52. Assertion–reason: A longer acquisition period improves nominal Fourier frequency spacing. Reason: nominal spacing is approximately the reciprocal of acquisition time.

  1. Assertion false; reason true
  2. Both true; unrelated
  3. Both false
  4. Both true; reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

D. Both true; reason explains assertion

Increasing acquisition duration reduces grid spacing. It cannot overcome all intrinsic linewidth limitations.

53. Graph interpretation: A conventional isolated CW EPR derivative has adjacent positive and negative extrema. Where is the center of a symmetric absorption line?

  1. At the positive maximum only
  2. At the zero crossing between extrema
  3. At the negative minimum only
  4. At twice the field of the positive maximum
Answer and explanation

B. At the zero crossing between extrema

The derivative is zero at the absorption maximum. The two extrema define the derivative peak-to-peak width.

54. Experimental inference: EPR intensity falls after redox treatment, but linewidth doubles. What should be checked first before inferring fewer spins?

  1. Corrected double integral, saturation and an independent redox control
  2. Only the derivative peak height
  3. Only the number of protein residues
  4. Whether all protons are spin-zero
Answer and explanation

A. Corrected double integral, saturation and an independent redox control

Broadening changes derivative height. Integration and control measurements distinguish changes in spin population from line-shape artifacts.

55. Integrated reasoning: CD changes little while assigned HSQC peaks move on ligand addition. What can be concluded?

  1. No binding can occur
  2. The entire protein must unfold
  3. Local environments change without evidence for a large average secondary-structure change
  4. The ligand necessarily contains a radical
Answer and explanation

C. Local environments change without evidence for a large average secondary-structure change

CD and HSQC report different observables. The results are consistent with local perturbation without large average secondary-structure changes, not proof of a unique model.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Spin is literal rotation

Correct concept: Spin is intrinsic angular momentum; classical arrows describe ensemble behavior.

Why the trap works: A spinning-top analogy is taken literally.

Examination reasoning: Use quantum projections to count states.

2. All isotopes are NMR active

Correct concept: Spin-zero nuclei such as ¹²C lack conventional spin transitions.

Why the trap works: Chemical abundance is confused with magnetic activity.

Examination reasoning: Check I before discussing signal.

3. All even-electron molecules are diamagnetic

Correct concept: Electron pairing and total spin, not parity alone, determine paramagnetism.

Why the trap works: Students count electrons rather than occupations.

Examination reasoning: O₂ has nonzero spin despite an even electron count.

4. A stronger field changes δ by the same factor

Correct concept: The shift in ppm is approximately field independent; offset in Hz scales.

Why the trap works: ppm and Hz axes are confused.

Examination reasoning: Convert using the nominal frequency.

5. Higher shielding means higher δ

Correct concept: Proton shielding usually reduces effective field and shift.

Why the trap works: Upfield and downfield labels are reversed.

Examination reasoning: Read the numerical ppm axis.

6. Every quartet is n+1 splitting by three protons

Correct concept: Four components can arise from different couplings; binomial intensities and spacing matter.

Why the trap works: Students count lines only.

Examination reasoning: Inspect reciprocal J and intensity pattern.

7. Equivalent protons split one another in the simple spectrum

Correct concept: Equivalent sets do not generate ordinary resolved mutual multiplets.

Why the trap works: All nearby nuclei are counted mechanically.

Examination reasoning: Determine chemical and magnetic equivalence.

8. Peak height equals number of nuclei

Correct concept: Area may be proportional under calibrated fully relaxed conditions.

Why the trap works: Height is visually easier to compare.

Examination reasoning: Account for linewidth, relaxation and overlap.

9. T₁ and T₂ are interchangeable

Correct concept: Longitudinal recovery differs from transverse coherence decay.

Why the trap works: Both are called relaxation.

Examination reasoning: Use the corresponding magnetization component.

10. Spin echo removes all relaxation

Correct concept: It refocuses static offsets but intrinsic T₂ loss remains.

Why the trap works: Refocusing is mistaken for reversing time.

Examination reasoning: Use total 2τ in exponential decay.

11. A single moving peak proves a single conformation

Correct concept: Rapid exchange can average distinct states.

Why the trap works: One line is equated with one microscopic state.

Examination reasoning: Compare exchange rate with frequency separation.

12. COSY measures spatial distance

Correct concept: COSY reflects scalar coupling; NOE often reports proximity.

Why the trap works: Both display cross peaks.

Examination reasoning: Identify transfer mechanism before inference.

13. HSQC cross-peak count equals residue count

Correct concept: Proline, side chains, exchange and overlap complicate counting.

Why the trap works: Idealized one-peak-per-residue rule is overextended.

Examination reasoning: Account for detectable bonded isotope pairs.

14. NOE peak intensity is a unique distance

Correct concept: Distance, dynamics, mixing time and indirect transfer influence intensity.

Why the trap works: The r⁻⁶ law is applied without its assumptions.

Examination reasoning: Use calibrated restraints and additional evidence.

15. Higher g means higher field at fixed EPR frequency

Correct concept: B=hν/(gμB), so larger g corresponds to lower field.

Why the trap works: Frequency-swept intuition is applied to field sweep.

Examination reasoning: Identify the fixed quantity.

16. Three EPR lines always mean two equivalent protons

Correct concept: One ¹⁴N can give three equal-area lines, versus 1:2:1 for two spin-half nuclei.

Why the trap works: NMR n+1 rules are applied indiscriminately.

Examination reasoning: Use the nuclear spin and relative intensities.

17. No EPR signal proves no paramagnetic species

Correct concept: Fast relaxation, low amount, spectral range and zero-field splitting can hide signals.

Why the trap works: Detectability is confused with existence.

Examination reasoning: Check sensitivity, conditions and complementary evidence.

18. Derivative EPR height measures spin concentration

Correct concept: Line shape and saturation influence amplitude; calibrated double integration is needed.

Why the trap works: A taller trace appears intuitively more concentrated.

Examination reasoning: Examine width and integrated absorption.

Revision, recall and mastery

Magnetic resonance begins with a magnetic moment, Zeeman splitting and a frequency-selective transverse field. NMR reads magnetic nuclei; EPR reads detectable paramagnetic electronic states. Shielding shifts nuclear resonances, scalar coupling splits lines, and relaxation and exchange alter signal evolution. Correlation experiments encode relationships between sites, and EPR g and hyperfine parameters probe the electronic environment.

Equations to reconstruct

  • ν₀ = magnitude of γ × B₀/(2π); ΔE=hν.
  • δ = 10⁶(νsample−νref)/νref; Δν (Hz) ≈ Δδ (ppm) × nominal frequency (MHz).
  • Mz/M₀=1−2e^(−t/T₁) after inversion; tnull=T₁ ln 2.
  • Mxy(t)/Mxy(0)=e^(−t/T₂); FWHM=1/(πT₂) for homogeneous Lorentzian absorption.
  • Fast exchange: δobs=(1−f)δfree+fδbound.
  • EPR: hν=gμBB₀; simple first-order hyperfine multiplicity for one nucleus is 2I+1.
