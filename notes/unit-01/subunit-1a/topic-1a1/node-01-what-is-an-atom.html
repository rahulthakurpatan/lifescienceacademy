1.A.1.01 — What Is an Atom?

Structure and Biological Relevance · Unit 1 / Sub-unit 1.A / Topic 1.A.1

1.A.1.01.1 Introduction: understanding the world through atoms

Look at a leaf, a spoonful of salt, a glass of water, and the air around you. These things look very different, yet each contains matter built from atoms. An atom is a very small unit, but the way atoms are arranged and interact gives different materials their properties. This is why a clear understanding of atoms helps us understand biology, even when the final subject is a living cell rather than a chemical substance.

This lesson starts with familiar observations and moves carefully toward scientific definitions. It explains the central nucleus, the region occupied by electrons, the three commonly discussed subatomic particles, atomic number, mass number, ions, and isotopes. It then connects these ideas to water, proteins, DNA, membranes, minerals, and research methods. The later parts include worked problems, common errors, a glossary, and questions with explained answers.

Learning objectives

By the end, you should be able to define atom, element, molecule, and ion; describe the nucleus and electronic region; identify subatomic particles; calculate proton, neutron, and electron numbers; distinguish isotopes from ions; explain why atom diagrams are models; and describe why atomic properties matter to living systems.

How to study: Read the sections in order if you are a fresher. Try to explain each idea in your own words before using its formula. For practice problems, write down what is given, what must be found, and the principle that connects them.

Scope: This is a complete introduction to atoms and their biological relevance. The history of Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, and Bohr is developed in later nodes. Electronic configurations, orbital quantum numbers, isotope abundance, nuclear decay, chemical-bond geometry, and chemical reaction energetics also receive dedicated later treatment. Only the foundation needed here is introduced, avoiding both gaps and unnecessary repetition.

1.A.1.01.2 What is matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. The air in our lungs, water inside a cell, mineral salts in the body, a tree, and a mountain are examples. Matter exists in different physical states, including solids, liquids, and gases. Changes of state, such as melting ice, do not normally change which elements are present.

1.A.1.01.2.1 Matter and energy are not the same thing

A beam of visible light carries energy, but it is not ordinary matter composed of atoms. Therefore, the beginner's statement “everything is made of atoms” is helpful for everyday substances but is not literally true of all physical phenomena. Heat and light can influence matter without being chemical atoms themselves.

1.A.1.01.2.2 Building matter from smaller units

Many familiar substances are made of molecules. Water consists of H2O molecules, each having two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Oxygen gas consists mainly of O2 molecules, each having two oxygen atoms. A sodium chloride crystal is different: it is an extended three-dimensional arrangement of Na+ and Cl− ions rather than a collection of discrete NaCl molecules. All of these cases illustrate that the manner of arrangement matters as much as the identities of the atoms.

Matter → atoms and ions → molecules or extended structures → cells and living systems

This is an organizational pathway, not a claim that all matter must pass through every stage. Metals and ionic crystals are examples of structures that cannot be described adequately as collections of separate molecules.

1.A.1.01.2.3 Conservation in biological chemistry

When an organism grows, atoms from food, air, water, and minerals are incorporated into its molecules. In ordinary chemical reactions, atoms are rearranged rather than being created from nothing. Photosynthesis, digestion, respiration, and protein synthesis are all examples of processes in which chemical bonds and molecular arrangements change while the elemental identities of participating nuclei remain the same.

1.A.1.01.3 What exactly is an atom?

An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element that retains the identity of that element. The identity of an element is determined by its number of protons. A nucleus with six protons belongs to carbon; a nucleus with eight protons belongs to oxygen. The number of neutrons or electrons may vary, but the proton number fixes the element.

Historically, the word “atom” is associated with the idea of something indivisible. Modern science has shown that atoms contain smaller particles and can participate in nuclear changes. The term survived, but its scientific meaning became more precise. In chemistry, an atom is a basic unit of an element, not the smallest physically possible object.

1.A.1.01.3.1 Atom versus element

An element is a category of matter defined by a particular proton number. An atom is one specific unit belonging to that category. Carbon is an element; one carbon atom is a particular physical system. The periodic table lists elements, each assigned a unique atomic number.

1.A.1.01.3.2 Atom versus molecule

A molecule is a discrete group of two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds. An oxygen atom (O) is different from an oxygen molecule (O2). A hydrogen atom (H) is different from the hydrogen molecule (H2) and from water (H2O). Molecules can contain atoms of only one element, as in O2, or of different elements, as in H2O.

1.A.1.01.3.3 Atom versus ion

A neutral atom contains equal numbers of protons and electrons. When an atom loses or gains electrons, the resulting charged species is called an ion. A sodium atom can lose an electron to form Na+. The sodium ion still has eleven protons, so it is still sodium. Ion formation changes charge, not elemental identity.

Remember

The number of protons tells us which element an atom belongs to. The number of electrons influences its charge and chemical behaviour. The number of neutrons distinguishes isotopes of that element.

One common trap is to call every molecule a compound. Oxygen gas, O2, is a molecule of the element oxygen, not a compound, because both atoms are oxygen. Another trap is to call every compound a molecule. Solid sodium chloride is an ionic compound arranged as a lattice; NaCl gives its simplest formula-unit ratio, not an isolated molecule in the crystal.

1.A.1.01.4.1 Reading chemical formulas

In a molecular formula, a subscript specifies how many atoms of the preceding element occur in a molecule. Water, H2O, contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Glucose, C6H12O6, contains six carbon, twelve hydrogen, and six oxygen atoms per molecule. Therefore, one glucose molecule contains 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms.

1.A.1.01.4.2 Scientific symbols and capitalization

Element symbols normally begin with a capital letter and may have a lowercase second letter. C means carbon, Ca means calcium, Co means cobalt, and CO means a combination of carbon and oxygen in a chemical formula. Capitalization is not decorative: changing it can change the meaning of the symbol.

1.A.1.01.5 How small is an atom?

An atom is so small that an ordinary light microscope cannot show its internal structure. A useful order-of-magnitude estimate for an atomic diameter is about 10−10 metre. This is one ten-billionth of a metre. The exact concept of atomic “diameter” depends on the type of atom and how it interacts with its surroundings, so it is best to treat this number as a scale rather than a universal fixed boundary.

A nanometre (nm) is 10−9 metre. An ångström (Å) is 10−10 metre. Thus 0.1 nm and 1 Å represent the same distance. Both units appear in molecular biology, chemistry, and structural studies.

1.A.1.01.5.1 An atom compared with its nucleus

A characteristic nuclear size is of the order of 10−15 metre, much smaller than the atom's overall scale. Dividing the two illustrative diameters gives 10−10 / 10−15 = 105, or about 100,000. This means the atom's size can be roughly one hundred thousand times the size of its central nucleus, using these approximate scale estimates.

10−10 m ÷ 10−15 m = 105 ≈ 100,000Equation 1.A.1.01-E1

1.A.1.01.5.2 Is the rest of the atom empty space?

The nucleus occupies a tiny part of the volume conventionally associated with an atom. Sometimes this is described by saying that atoms are “mostly empty space.” That phrase is helpful only in a limited classical sense. Electrons have quantum-mechanical probability distributions in the surrounding region, and electromagnetic forces and quantum rules explain why matter resists compression. An atom is not a miniature hollow shell containing tiny planets.

1.A.1.01.5.3 A useful link to biology

Molecular biologists often describe bond lengths and protein structures in ångströms or nanometres. DNA's double helix, water molecules, and enzyme binding pockets all involve arrangements of atoms on very small length scales. Understanding the units prevents errors when reading diagrams and structural-biology data.

1.A.1.01.6 The nucleus and the electron region

Modern atomic structure can be introduced using two major regions: a very small central nucleus and a much more extended region described by the behaviour of electrons. The nucleus contains positively charged protons and, in most atoms, electrically neutral neutrons. Electrons have negative electric charge and occupy quantum states associated with spatial probability distributions around the nucleus.

1.A.1.01.6.1 What is inside the nucleus?

Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons. These particles account for nearly all the mass of an ordinary atom. They remain bound in the nucleus through nuclear interactions. The positively charged protons would repel each other electrically, but nuclear forces can hold suitable combinations of nucleons together. Understanding nuclear stability requires additional physics and will be developed in later nodes.

Not all nuclei contain neutrons. The nucleus of ordinary hydrogen-1 contains a single proton and no neutron. Therefore, the common sentence “the nucleus always has protons and neutrons” is not universally correct. This exception is important for accurate basic science.

1.A.1.01.6.2 Why electrons matter

Electrons largely determine how atoms interact chemically. They contribute to chemical bonding, electric charge, light absorption and emission, and reactions in which electrons are transferred or shared. In cells, electron transfer is important in processes including respiration and photosynthesis. The detailed electronic theory belongs to later nodes, but the connection begins here.

1.A.1.01.6.3 Why diagrams use exaggerated sizes

In classroom pictures, the nucleus is normally drawn large enough for protons and neutrons to be visible. If a model used the true approximate size ratio, the nucleus would be almost invisible inside a reasonably sized drawing of the atom. Such diagrams can be helpful when the exaggeration is explained clearly.

1.A.1.01.7 The three familiar subatomic particles

At an introductory level, the three particles most frequently used to describe atoms are the proton, neutron, and electron. Their electric charges and approximate masses are different. For most examination calculations, these relationships are more important than memorizing every decimal digit of the measured masses.

1.A.1.01.7.1 Protons

A proton carries a positive electric charge of magnitude equal to the elementary charge, e. Each carbon atom has six protons; each oxygen atom has eight. Because proton number identifies the element, knowing the proton count is enough to identify the element using the periodic table. A proton is a composite particle containing quarks; the present lesson focuses on its nuclear and chemical role.

1.A.1.01.7.2 Neutrons

A neutron has no net electric charge. Most atoms contain one or more neutrons. Different numbers of neutrons give different isotopes of an element. Neutron number affects nuclear mass and nuclear stability but not the element's proton-defined identity. The neutron is also a composite particle made of quarks.

1.A.1.01.7.3 Electrons

An electron is negatively charged. Its mass is much smaller than the mass of a proton or neutron: the proton is about 1836 times as massive as an electron. Electrons are elementary particles in the Standard Model of particle physics. Although their total mass makes only a very small contribution to ordinary atomic mass, they are central to chemical reactions.

1.A.1.01.7.4 The elementary charge

The elementary charge has the exact SI magnitude 1.602176634 × 10−19 coulomb (C). In chemistry problems, it is often easier to express charges as +1, −1, +2, and so on, measured in units of e. A proton carries +e; an electron carries −e. The charge magnitudes are equal and the signs are opposite.

Mass versus chemical importance

Protons and neutrons account for nearly all atomic mass. Electrons account for most ordinary chemical interactions. Do not assume that the most massive component must be the main component involved in bonding.

1.A.1.01.8 Charge, neutrality and the formation of ions

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. The positive and negative charges cancel, giving a net charge of zero. Neutrons do not alter the electric charge because they are neutral.

For a neutral atom: electrons = protons = ZEquation 1.A.1.01-E2

Consider a neutral magnesium atom. Magnesium has atomic number 12, so it has 12 protons. A neutral magnesium atom also has 12 electrons. When it loses two electrons, its net charge becomes +2. The resulting magnesium ion, Mg2+, still contains 12 protons but now contains only 10 electrons.

Net charge (units of e) = number of protons − number of electronsEquation 1.A.1.01-E3

1.A.1.01.8.1 Positive ions, or cations

A cation has a net positive charge because it has fewer electrons than protons. Sodium ions, Na+, calcium ions, Ca2+, and magnesium ions, Mg2+, are biologically familiar cations. Their names identify the elements; their superscripts specify charge.

1.A.1.01.8.2 Negative ions, or anions

An anion has a net negative charge because it has more electrons than protons. A chloride ion, Cl−, has one more electron than the corresponding neutral chlorine atom. In a chloride ion, the proton number remains 17, so the species remains chlorine despite the altered charge.

1.A.1.01.8.3 Charge is not mass number

The superscript charge at the right of a symbol describes the electron deficit or surplus. The mass number at the upper left describes the total number of protons and neutrons. These are different kinds of information and should never be confused. For example, Mg2+ does not mean that magnesium has a mass number of two.

1.A.1.01.8.4 Why ion formation is important in biology

Cells create and maintain unequal concentrations of ions across membranes. For example, sodium and potassium ion gradients help establish membrane potential, calcium ions participate in signalling, and magnesium ions are important for many enzyme reactions. These processes depend on charged particles and their interactions, connecting elementary atomic principles with physiology.

1.A.1.01.9 Atomic number Z: the unique identity of an element

The atomic number, written Z, is the number of protons in an atomic nucleus. It is always a whole number. The periodic table is organized in increasing atomic number because proton number determines which chemical element is present.

Atomic number, Z = number of protonsEquation 1.A.1.01-E4

Hydrogen has Z = 1, helium Z = 2, carbon Z = 6, nitrogen Z = 7, oxygen Z = 8, phosphorus Z = 15, and sulfur Z = 16. These values apply to every isotope of the stated element. Changing a neutron number does not change Z. Gaining or losing electrons does not change Z either.

1.A.1.01.9.1 Why atomic number, not atomic mass?

Neutron number can differ among isotopes. Measured atomic masses can also differ slightly. The number of electrons may change when an atom becomes an ion. Proton number is the defining characteristic that remains constant for an element in ordinary chemical and biological processes, making Z the correct identifier.

1.A.1.01.10 Mass number A and the first idea of isotopes

The mass number, written A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in a particular nucleus. Both are called nucleons. Because it counts particles, mass number is a whole number, not a decimal measurement.

Mass number, A = protons + neutrons = Z + NEquation 1.A.1.01-E5
Neutron number, N = A − ZEquation 1.A.1.01-E6

Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons: A = 12. Carbon-13 also has six protons but seven neutrons: A = 13. Both are carbon because Z = 6 in both cases. The two are isotopes. Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons; unlike carbon-12 and carbon-13, carbon-14 is radioactive.

1.A.1.01.10.1 Isotopes and chemical identity

Isotopes of one element have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. They are usually very similar in their chemical identity and often share closely related chemical behaviour, although measurable isotope effects can occur. Isotopes are especially useful in biology because labelled atoms can help researchers track the movement of substances through metabolic pathways.

1.A.1.01.10.2 Mass number versus atomic mass

Mass number is a count of nucleons. The actual atomic mass is a measured physical quantity, often expressed in unified atomic mass units (u). The decimal value reported for an element on a periodic table is related to the isotopic composition of the material for which the atomic-weight value applies. A value such as approximately 35.45 for chlorine is not the mass number of one particular chlorine atom.

1.A.1.01.10.3 A useful exception

Hydrogen-1 is sometimes called protium. It has one proton and zero neutrons. Deuterium (hydrogen-2) has one proton and one neutron, while tritium (hydrogen-3) has one proton and two neutrons. All three are hydrogen isotopes because each has Z = 1. Tritium is radioactive. Their detailed nuclear behaviour belongs to later nodes.

Three facts to separate clearly

Z: number of protons and element identity. A: number of nucleons in one nucleus. Charge: difference between proton and electron counts. Different symbols answer different questions.

1.A.1.01.11 How to read atomic and ionic symbols

An atomic symbol can communicate the element, mass number, atomic number, and electric charge in a compact form. To interpret one correctly, always follow the same sequence. First identify the element symbol. Next read the atomic number at its lower left, if shown. Read the mass number at its upper left. Finally read any ionic charge at the upper right.

AZXcharge   Example:   2311Na+Notation 1.A.1.01-E7

In 2311Na+, the symbol Na indicates sodium. Atomic number 11 means eleven protons. Mass number 23 means 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons. A +1 charge means there is one fewer electron than proton: 10 electrons. All information is obtained from three separate rules, not from guesswork.

1.A.1.01.11.1 Reading a neutral isotope

For 126C without a charge, the number of protons is 6, neutrons is 12 − 6 = 6, and electrons is 6 if the species is understood to be a neutral atom. Carbon-13 has one additional neutron, while its proton and neutral electron counts remain six.

1.A.1.01.11.2 Reading a negative ion

For 168O2−, proton count is 8 and neutron count is 16 − 8 = 8. Its charge of 2− means it has gained two electrons relative to a neutral oxygen atom, so it has 10 electrons. Negative charge is associated with extra electrons, not fewer protons.

1.A.1.01.11.3 Reading a positive ion

For 2412Mg2+, protons = 12, neutrons = 12, electrons = 10. The element remains magnesium, because its proton count remains 12. This distinction is essential when studying metal ions in enzymes and cells.

1.A.1.01.12 The modern picture of electrons: not fixed circular orbits

Many introductory drawings show electrons moving in circles around a central nucleus, like planets orbiting the Sun. Such pictures belong to simplified or historical atomic models. They can be useful for understanding how scientific explanations developed, but they are not literal descriptions of an electron's path in a modern atom.

In quantum mechanics, an electron is described by a quantum state. A mathematical quantity called a wavefunction can be used to calculate a probability distribution for measurements of the electron's position. An atomic orbital is associated with a quantum state and its spatial distribution. It is not a road, shell made of matter, or a permanent track through space.

1.A.1.01.12.1 How to understand an electron cloud

Imagine a shaded drawing around the nucleus. Darker shading can represent regions where an electron is more likely to be detected. The shaded cloud is a way of displaying calculated probability density. It is not a physical fog, and it does not mean that an electron is literally a tiny ball randomly hopping along a drawn outline.

1.A.1.01.12.2 Why we do not assign a simple orbit

A definite classical circular trajectory is not the correct description of an electron in a stationary atomic quantum state. The modern treatment describes allowed energies and probability distributions, consistent with quantum theory and the limitations on simultaneously specifying position and momentum. More mathematical details are developed in later chapters on electronic structure.

1.A.1.01.12.3 Is an atom's outer boundary sharp?

Electron probability distributions do not generally end at a perfect hard surface. Therefore, atomic radii depend on the physical situation and on how the radius is defined—for example, through bonding or nonbonding interactions. This explains why atomic-size values may vary depending on the measurement method and context.

Scientific accuracy rule

A nucleus surrounded by electron probability regions is a better conceptual description than electrons following permanently known circular tracks. Diagrams are models; their legends should state exactly what they represent and what they do not.

1.A.1.01.13 Why atoms interact and form chemical substances

Most biological materials are built from atoms interacting with one another. These interactions depend strongly on their electronic arrangements and electric charges. Chemical bonds and weaker molecular interactions help establish the structures of water, sugars, proteins, membrane lipids, nucleic acids, and many other essential substances.

1.A.1.01.13.1 A first idea of covalent bonding

In a covalent bond, electron density is shared between bonded atoms. Water contains two oxygen–hydrogen covalent bonds. Many carbon-containing biological molecules contain carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen covalent bonds. The full quantum description of bonding is more advanced than this first idea; the next topic nodes will explain bond formation, bond order, polarity, and molecular geometry in detail.

1.A.1.01.13.2 Charged particles and ionic interactions

Positive and negative charges attract one another. Sodium and chloride ions can form an ionic crystal. In cells, metal cations can also interact with negatively charged groups of biological molecules. Such interactions help shape enzyme activity, membrane potentials, and molecular recognition.

1.A.1.01.13.3 Why molecular shape cannot be inferred from a formula alone

The formula H2O tells us that a water molecule contains two hydrogens and one oxygen. It does not by itself show the complete three-dimensional geometry or how charge is distributed within the molecule. Geometry and electron distribution are crucial for explaining why water is polar and why different molecules may behave differently despite having similar atom counts.

1.A.1.01.13.4 A bridge to biological specificity

An enzyme usually recognizes only particular molecular arrangements. At a chemical level, these arrangements depend on which atoms are present, their bonds, charges, and three-dimensional positions. A simple concept—atoms are chemically distinct—therefore becomes the foundation of a sophisticated biological concept: molecular specificity.

1.A.1.01.14 Biological relevance: atoms are the foundation of life

Every cell is composed of chemical substances, and those substances contain atoms. Proteins are built from amino acids, nucleic acids from nucleotides, and cell membranes largely from lipids and proteins. Their functions depend on precise atomic compositions and arrangements. The study of atomic structure therefore supports cell biology, biochemistry, molecular biology, genetics, physiology, and biotechnology.

1.A.1.01.14.1 Carbon and the diversity of organic molecules

Carbon is central to organic chemistry because it can form a wide range of covalent bonds with itself and other elements. The resulting chains, rings, and functional groups provide enormous chemical diversity. This diversity supports carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, vitamins, and many metabolites. Carbon's role is not magic: it arises from its chemical properties and bonding possibilities.

1.A.1.01.14.2 Hydrogen and oxygen in water

Water molecules contain hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Water participates in biological reactions and provides the medium in which many cellular processes occur. Its polarity and ability to form hydrogen bonds contribute to its behaviour as a solvent, its cohesion, and its influence on biomolecular structure. These properties require a deeper discussion of bonding and molecular interactions, but they begin with atoms and electrons.

1.A.1.01.14.3 Nitrogen in proteins and nucleic acids

Nitrogen occurs in amino groups, amino acids, proteins, and the nitrogen-containing bases of DNA and RNA. This is why nitrogen availability matters for growth and biosynthesis. Different nitrogen-containing compounds may have very different biological roles, even though all contain atoms of the same element.

1.A.1.01.14.4 Phosphorus and sulfur

Phosphorus occurs in the phosphate groups of ATP and nucleic acids, as well as phospholipids in cell membranes. Sulfur appears in amino acids including cysteine and methionine, and in many coenzymes. Certain sulfur-containing groups help shape the structure and activity of proteins.

1.A.1.01.14.5 Metal ions in cells

Sodium and potassium ions help maintain membrane potentials and support nerve and muscle function. Calcium ions act as signals in many cells. Magnesium ions are involved in numerous ATP-associated enzyme reactions. Iron is found in proteins involved in oxygen binding and electron transfer. A metal ion's proton-defined elemental identity remains the same when it becomes charged.

1.A.1.01.14.6 Isotope labels in biology

Researchers can use stable isotopes such as carbon-13 or nitrogen-15 to trace where atoms move during metabolism. For example, introducing a suitably labelled carbon substrate can help reveal which metabolic products receive its carbon atoms. Radioisotopes such as carbon-14 are also used in some tracing methods, subject to radiation-safety requirements. Labelled atoms remain the same chemical elements because their proton numbers do not change.

1.A.1.01.14.7 Link to life-science examinations

Questions about atom identity, ion charge, isotope labels, molecular composition, and chemical bonding can appear directly or within larger problems about metabolism, membranes, enzyme mechanisms, transport, and molecular biology. Recognizing the atomic foundation makes these later topics easier to reason through instead of memorizing them as disconnected facts.

1.A.1.01.15 Seven solved problems with complete reasoning

1.A.1.01.15.1 A neutral oxygen-16 atom

Problem: Find the proton, neutron, and electron numbers of neutral oxygen-16. Given: Z = 8 and A = 16. Step 1: protons = Z = 8. Step 2: neutrons = A − Z = 16 − 8 = 8. Step 3: neutrality means electrons = 8. Answer: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 8 electrons. This is a basic model for interpreting a nuclide.

1.A.1.01.15.2 Magnesium-24 as a 2+ ion

Problem: Determine particle counts for 2412Mg2+. Given: Z = 12, A = 24, charge = +2. Protons: 12. Neutrons: 24 − 12 = 12. Electrons: 12 − 2 = 10. Answer: 12 protons, 12 neutrons, 10 electrons. Note that ion formation does not change Z.

1.A.1.01.15.3 A chloride ion

Problem: Find the electron number in 3517Cl−. Chlorine has 17 protons and 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons. A single negative charge means one extra electron. Thus the ion has 18 electrons. The symbol gives the mass number and charge independently.

1.A.1.01.15.4 Comparing two carbon isotopes

Problem: One species has Z = 6 and A = 12; another has Z = 6 and A = 14. Are they different elements? Reasoning: Both have six protons, so both are carbon. Their neutron counts are 6 and 8, respectively. Answer: They are isotopes of the same element, not two elements.

1.A.1.01.15.5 Reconstructing a nuclide

Problem: An ion has 15 protons, 16 neutrons and 18 electrons. Determine element, mass number and charge. Solution: Z = 15 means phosphorus (P); A = 15 + 16 = 31. Charge = 15 − 18 = −3. Answer: 3115P3−.

1.A.1.01.15.6 Comparing atom and nucleus scales

Problem: Estimate the ratio of 10−10 m to 10−15 m. Solution: Subtract exponents when dividing powers of ten: −10 − (−15) = 5, so the ratio is 105 = 100,000. Meaning: atomic size is roughly five orders of magnitude greater than nuclear size in this simplified comparison.

1.A.1.01.15.7 How many atoms are in glucose?

Problem: Determine the total number of atoms in one glucose molecule, C6H12O6. Solution: Six carbon + twelve hydrogen + six oxygen = twenty-four. Answer: 24 atoms in one molecule. The numbers in the formula are subscripts counting atoms; they are not atomic numbers or electric charges.

1.A.1.01.16 Common misconceptions: what to avoid

When students first learn about atoms, they often memorize a familiar diagram without asking what the drawing means. Misconceptions may then appear in questions about atomic structure, ions, isotopes, and chemical identity. Each correction below follows from one of the scientific definitions developed earlier.

Table 1.A.1.01-T5. Common incorrect statements and scientific corrections
Incorrect statementCorrect understanding
Atoms are absolutely indivisible.Atoms contain subatomic particles and can undergo nuclear changes. The name reflects historical ideas.
Every nucleus has neutrons.Hydrogen-1 has one proton and no neutron.
Isotopes have different proton numbers.Isotopes have the same proton number and different neutron counts.
Electrons are tiny planets on exact circular tracks.Modern quantum theory uses states and electron probability distributions.
Mass number and atomic weight are identical quantities.Mass number is a whole-number nucleon count; atomic weight is associated with measured masses and isotopic composition.
When an atom loses an electron, it becomes another element.Electron loss creates a positive ion without changing the nuclear proton number.
A neutral atom always has no protons.It can have many protons; neutrality means equal positive and negative charges.
Oxygen gas O₂ is a compound.O₂ is a molecule of the element oxygen, not a compound of different elements.
NaCl must represent an independent molecule in every substance.Solid NaCl is an extended ionic lattice; the formula gives the simplest ion ratio.
Atomic diagrams must be drawn to true scale.Diagrams often exaggerate nuclei to make their structure visible. Their captions should explain the simplification.

1.A.1.01.16.1 How to reason through a confusing question

Before calculating, identify what type of object the question describes. Is it a neutral atom, ion, nucleus, isotope, molecule, or compound? Then choose the appropriate defining rule. Proton count identifies the element. Neutron count distinguishes isotopes. Electron count and proton count together determine net charge. Molecular subscripts describe atom composition. Keeping these rules separate is more reliable than attempting to recall an isolated answer.

1.A.1.01.17 CSIR-NET and State SET practice with explained answers

The following original practice questions are designed to strengthen understanding. They are not represented as official previous-year questions. Some test a single fact; others require combining definitions with calculations or biological interpretation. Attempt each question before opening its explanation.

1.A.1.01.17.1 Question 1

A neutral atom has 13 protons and 14 neutrons. What are its atomic number, mass number, and electron count?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Atomic number Z = 13; mass number A = 13 + 14 = 27; electron count = 13 because the atom is neutral. The three answers are found using three distinct rules.

1.A.1.01.17.2 Question 2

Two species have six protons but different neutron counts. What is their relationship?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: They are isotopes of carbon. Both have Z = 6, so their element identity matches. Different neutron counts change their mass numbers, not their elemental identities.

1.A.1.01.17.3 Question 3

A neutral atom loses three electrons. What happens to its charge and proton number?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: It forms a +3 ion. Electron loss increases net positive charge, but the number of nuclear protons stays the same in an ordinary chemical ionization process.

1.A.1.01.17.4 Question 4

How many electrons are in a Ca²⁺ ion?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Calcium has Z = 20, so its neutral atom has 20 electrons. A +2 charge indicates the loss of two electrons: 20 − 2 = 18.

1.A.1.01.17.5 Question 5

A nuclide has 7 protons and 8 neutrons. Identify its element and mass number.

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: It is nitrogen (Z = 7) with mass number A = 7 + 8 = 15, or nitrogen-15. Its electron count is not established unless neutrality or charge is given.

1.A.1.01.17.6 Question 6

Explain why an electron-cloud diagram is preferable to a literal circular orbit for a modern atom.

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Quantum mechanics describes allowed states and their probability distributions; it does not assign an electron a permanently known circular track. A schematic cloud represents measurement probabilities.

1.A.1.01.17.7 Question 7

Why is O₂ a molecule but not a compound?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: The O₂ molecule has two bonded atoms, but both are oxygen atoms. Compounds contain atoms of different elements, so O₂ is a molecular form of one element.

1.A.1.01.17.8 Question 8

Why do isotopes of one element share their chemical identity?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: They have the same proton number. Neutral isotopes also have the same electron count, so their electronic structures are closely related, although measurable isotope effects may exist.

1.A.1.01.17.9 Question 9

A species has 20 protons and 18 electrons. Identify it and determine its charge.

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Its proton number identifies calcium. Charge in units of e is 20 − 18 = +2, so it is Ca²⁺. Neutron number cannot be determined without additional data.

1.A.1.01.17.10 Question 10

Why are electrons so important for biology if they contribute little to atomic mass?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Electron distributions and transfer processes govern bonding and many chemical reactions. Enzyme chemistry, membrane ion interactions, respiration, and photosynthesis therefore depend on electronic behaviour.

1.A.1.01.17.11 Question 11

Why is solid NaCl generally described as a lattice rather than a collection of separate NaCl molecules?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: Its cations and anions are arranged in an extended ionic structure. The formula NaCl gives a one-to-one formula-unit ratio and does not imply a discrete molecule in the solid.

1.A.1.01.17.12 Question 12

A glucose molecule has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆. How many atoms does it contain?

Show the answer with reasoning

Answer and explanation: It contains 6 carbon + 12 hydrogen + 6 oxygen = 24 atoms. This is a molecular atom-count problem; the subscripts are not charges.

1.A.1.01.17.13 What good exam reasoning looks like

High-quality responses do not stop at the final number or statement. They show the identifying principle, the intermediate steps, and the reason alternatives are wrong. For example, in an ion problem, the proton count gives the element, while the sign and magnitude of the ionic charge explain the electron count. This approach is especially valuable when a question includes several unfamiliar symbols.

1.A.1.01.18 Final revision, glossary, concept links, and scientific sources

1.A.1.01.18.1 Essential points to revise

  • Matter has mass and occupies space. Ordinary biological substances are built from atoms, ions, and chemical structures.
  • An atom is the smallest unit of an element retaining that element's chemical identity.
  • The nucleus contains protons and usually neutrons, and nearly all ordinary atomic mass is concentrated there.
  • Electrons occupy quantum-mechanical states and are essential for chemical interactions.
  • Protons have +1e charge, neutrons have zero net charge, and electrons have −1e charge.
  • Z is the number of protons; A is the total of protons and neutrons; neutron count is A − Z.
  • A neutral atom has equal proton and electron counts. An ion has a net charge because the counts differ.
  • Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers.
  • Modern orbitals are not exact circular paths; they are quantum states associated with probability distributions.
  • Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and many metal ions help form and operate living systems.
