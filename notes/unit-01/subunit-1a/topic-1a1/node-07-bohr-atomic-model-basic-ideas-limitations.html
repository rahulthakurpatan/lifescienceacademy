Bohr’s Atomic Model: Basic Ideas and Limitations

Why the hydrogen atom has stable states, discrete energies and characteristic light — an evidence-led journey to the limits of the old quantum model.

Learning outcomes: Interpret emission and absorption lines; distinguish Bohr's postulates from derived predictions; calculate quantized radii and energies for hydrogen-like species; solve Rydberg and photon questions; identify model limitations; connect foundational spectroscopy to biological science.

1.A.1.07.1 The two mysteries Bohr had to solve

Imagine entering a physics laboratory in 1913. Hydrogen gas glows in a narrow glass tube. Its light is separated by a spectroscope, and a surprising pattern appears: sharp colored lines, with spaces between them. Why does hydrogen emit these particular wavelengths instead of all possible colors? Another problem is waiting. Rutherford's nucleus is supported by scattering, but an electron traveling in a classical curved orbit accelerates and should radiate energy. Why, then, do ordinary atoms persist?

1.A.1.07.1.1 The evidence was already real

Geiger and Marsden's scattering experiments helped establish that atoms contain a concentrated central charge. Independently, spectroscopists had observed hydrogen's distinctive emission lines. The difficult task was to build one atomic picture consistent with both the existence of a nucleus and the observed spectral pattern. A scientific model earns confidence by explaining observations; it cannot simply ignore one of them.

1.A.1.07.1.2 What Bohr chose to keep

Niels Bohr did not discard Rutherford's positively charged center. The electron was still attracted to it by the electric force. The innovative step concerned which electron states were permitted. Instead of allowing the electron to possess any classical orbit and continuously radiate, Bohr imposed special stable states with restricted energies. Radiation would be connected with transitions between these states.

1.A.1.07.1.3 The difference between an assumption and a prediction

Bohr's stationary-state rule was a postulate: it was not derived from the earlier classical theory of accelerating electric charges. But the model then led to testable predictions for the wavelengths of hydrogen's spectral lines. This distinction matters. A postulate is judged by the explanatory success and testable consequences of the theory it helps build, not by pretending that it was already a theorem of classical mechanics.

1.A.1.07.1.4 Our learning journey

In this node, we begin with measured spectra, explain the three familiar Bohr postulates, derive allowed radii and energies, calculate photon wavelengths, interpret the Lyman and Balmer series, and identify the model's limitations. We will also separate Bohr's historical circular orbits from modern quantum-mechanical orbitals. The goal is not to memorize formulas without context; it is to understand why each equation arose and what it can and cannot tell us.

Big question: How can the hydrogen atom remain stable yet emit discrete photon energies, when unrestricted classical electron orbits explain neither feature?

1.A.1.07.2 Spectral evidence: what hydrogen actually shows

Before looking at Bohr's theory, picture the observation that any convincing theory must explain. Put low-pressure atomic hydrogen in a discharge tube. Provide sufficient energy to excite the atoms, then send the emitted light through a prism or diffraction grating. A spectroscope separates different wavelengths, allowing us to see that some wavelengths are strongly represented while neighboring wavelengths may be absent.

1.A.1.07.2.1 Continuous light and line light are different

A broad continuous spectrum contains light across a range of wavelengths, like much of the rainbow produced by a white-light source. An atomic emission-line spectrum contains narrow features at characteristic wavelengths. In the visible hydrogen Balmer series, notable lines lie near 656 nm (red), 486 nm (blue-green), 434 nm and 410 nm (violet). The series also contains lines outside the most easily observed visible region. These wavelengths arise from distinct changes in the atom's electronic energy.

1.A.1.07.2.2 The screen tells us an observation, not an orbital photograph

A spectroscope reveals which wavelengths reach a detector. It does not directly show a tiny electron traveling from one painted ring to another. The connection to electronic energy states is a theoretical interpretation supported by the mathematical agreement between allowed energy differences and measured lines. This is a crucial difference between measuring a spectrum and drawing an atomic model.

1.A.1.07.2.3 Emission and absorption

In emission, an atom in an excited state can release a photon and enter a lower-energy state. In absorption, an atom with a suitable occupied initial state can take up energy and reach a higher state. The same energy differences are important to both processes, although actual strengths depend on populations, selection rules and experimental conditions. Saying every atom always absorbs every mathematically matching photon would ignore those qualifications.

1.A.1.07.2.4 What makes this evidence difficult for classical orbits?

In an unrestricted classical orbital picture, a bound electron could have a continuum of radii and energies. Continuous radiation during a changing orbit does not naturally yield the observed sharp list of characteristic wavelengths. A successful explanation must therefore explain why electron energies have specific permitted values and why emitted photons correspond to differences between them.

Evidence to remember: A hydrogen spectrum contains reproducible sharp lines. Their wavelengths must be explained quantitatively, not merely described as 'different colors'.

1.A.1.07.3 Why quantization entered atomic theory

Bohr's breakthrough was part of a larger change in physics. Long before the complete modern quantum theory existed, researchers had begun to discover that microscopic phenomena did not always fit the continuous assumptions of classical models. The idea of energy quanta appeared in Planck's treatment of thermal radiation, while Einstein's analysis of the photoelectric effect connected light energy to frequency.

1.A.1.07.3.1 Balmer and Rydberg found the patterns first

Johann Balmer described a mathematical relationship among hydrogen's visible spectral wavelengths in 1885. Other spectral series were organized through related Rydberg formulas. The striking feature was that simple positive integers could be used to describe measured wavelengths. These patterns were known before Bohr proposed his nuclear-atom theory in 1913. An explanation was needed for why those integers worked.

1.A.1.07.3.2 Energy carried by light

Planck's constant h connects photon energy E to frequency ν through E = hν. Because frequency and wavelength in vacuum satisfy c = λν, the equivalent expression is E = hc/λ. A larger photon-energy difference therefore corresponds to a higher frequency and shorter wavelength. This relationship becomes particularly useful once we know the energies of two allowed atomic states.

1.A.1.07.3.3 The staircase analogy, with its limits

Imagine a staircase rather than a ramp. A ramp lets you stand at essentially any intermediate height; the staircase offers a series of distinct steps. Bohr's model treats bound electronic energies like allowed steps. But an electron is not actually a person resting on an invisible stair, and the allowed energies are not equally spaced. The analogy is only intended to emphasize that every intermediate energy is not permitted.

1.A.1.07.3.4 What quantization does not mean

It does not mean every photon in nature has the same energy. It does not mean an atom has only two possible states. It means that a given bound one-electron system possesses particular permitted stationary states. Different transitions between those states produce different photon energies. The probability and intensity of a real optical transition require additional physics beyond simply writing its energy difference.

History check: Balmer and Rydberg described spectral patterns experimentally; Bohr supplied a 1913 atomic model that explained their hydrogen-state energy structure.

1.A.1.07.4 Bohr's three postulates in plain English

The familiar Bohr model can be organized around three related assumptions. These assumptions do different jobs, so it helps to learn them one at a time. Do not confuse a postulate with a formula derived later from it.

1.A.1.07.4.1 First: selected stable states

The model allows an electron to occupy certain circular states about the nucleus. While occupying one of these permitted stationary states, the electron does not continuously radiate electromagnetic energy. This was an explicit departure from the classical expectation for a point charge moving along a curved path. It addressed the problem that an unrestricted orbit would lose energy and fail to remain stable.

1.A.1.07.4.2 Second: angular momentum is restricted

For a circular Bohr orbit, the orbital angular momentum is restricted to integer multiples of the reduced Planck constant. This is written mₑvr = nħ = nh/(2π), where mₑ is electron mass, v its modeled orbital speed, r orbit radius and n is a positive integer. The condition does not follow from Coulomb's law alone; it is the extra restriction that selects a discrete set of classical-looking orbits.

me v r = nħ = nh/(2π), with n = 1, 2, 3, ...

1.A.1.07.4.3 Third: radiation accompanies transitions

If the atom changes between allowed electronic energies Eᵢ and E𝒇, the light exchanged has photon energy equal to the magnitude of the difference: hν = |Eᵢ − E𝒇|. A downward change can emit a photon; an upward change requires energy input, which may come from absorbing a photon. The photon's wavelength follows from λ = hc/|ΔE|, so a larger gap means a shorter wavelength.

Ephoton = |Ei − Ef| = hν = hc/λ

1.A.1.07.4.4 Why the postulates work together

Allowed states answer the stability question by assumption. The angular-momentum restriction determines which circular configurations the model admits. The photon rule connects differences among those states to observed spectral lines. Using one of these ideas in isolation would not reproduce the whole story; the model is a linked explanation.

1.A.1.07.5 Angular momentum and why only integers matter

Angular momentum describes rotational motion. In the old classical picture of an electron moving in a circular path, the magnitude of orbital angular momentum is the electron's mass multiplied by its speed and orbit radius: L = mₑvr. Without any additional law, this expression could accept a continuous range of results. Bohr's new condition permits only L = nħ for positive integer n.

1.A.1.07.5.1 What every symbol tells us

The symbol mₑ means electron mass, v means speed in the historical circular-orbit picture, and r means the orbital radius. The symbol h represents Planck's constant, while ħ = h/(2π) is the reduced Planck constant. The number n labels the state. In Bohr's model n = 1 gives L = ħ, n = 2 gives 2ħ, and n = 3 gives 3ħ. An n = 1.5 circular state is not part of the model.

1.A.1.07.5.2 Why this is an assumption rather than ordinary mechanics

Newton's laws and Coulomb attraction can balance the inward force of many hypothetical circular paths; they do not automatically require angular momentum to be an integer multiple of ħ. Bohr introduced that rule because the classical picture alone did not explain discrete spectral energies. Imposing the rule allows specific orbit radii and binding energies to emerge mathematically.

1.A.1.07.5.3 A later matter-wave interpretation

Louis de Broglie later proposed that matter can be associated with wavelength λ = h/p. If a circular path is treated as a standing-wave analogy, its circumference needs an integer number of wavelengths: 2πr = nλ. Substituting p ≈ mₑv gives mₑvr = nh/(2π). This is a useful *later* conceptual explanation of an integer restriction, but not Bohr's original 1913 derivation. Nor is it a literal picture of a modern electron circling like a vibrating string.

1.A.1.07.5.4 How modern theory corrects the picture

In quantum mechanics, orbital angular momentum is associated with the orbital quantum number ℓ and has magnitude √[ℓ(ℓ + 1)]ħ. The ground-state 1s orbital has ℓ = 0, which is not Bohr's classical n = 1 circular electron traveling with angular momentum ħ. Thus Bohr's quantization was historically valuable without being the final quantum rule for real bound electrons.

1.A.1.07.6 Stationary states are not an electron at rest

The word 'stationary' causes a surprisingly common misunderstanding. Students may imagine that Bohr's electron becomes motionless when it reaches an allowed orbit. Bohr did not mean that. His historical model assigns a circular motion to the electron, yet asserts that the atom does not radiate continuously while it occupies a permitted state. 'Stationary' refers to the persistence of the state's energy, not to zero speed.

1.A.1.07.6.1 Start from the classical contradiction

According to classical mechanics, an electron changing direction in a circular orbit has centripetal acceleration. According to classical electrodynamics, a charged particle undergoing acceleration emits electromagnetic radiation. Since radiation carries energy, that electron should lose energy, and its orbit should not remain unchanged. This is why the unrestricted Rutherford-style planetary sketch could not explain stable matter.

1.A.1.07.6.2 Bohr's bold interruption

Bohr's stationary-state postulate says that selected electronic states do not radiate as the electron 'goes around' in the model. That instruction is not a result of the earlier classical radiation equations. It is a new quantum assumption. It reconciled the model with the everyday observation that stable atomic hydrogen can exist without emitting light forever. The model then uses transitions between states, rather than continuous orbit radiation, to account for photons.

1.A.1.07.6.3 Imagine a light switch rather than a leaking container

An unrestricted classical orbit would behave like a container with a hole continually leaking energy. The Bohr atom behaves more like a system that can stay in one permitted state and exchange energy only in suitable changes between states. This analogy is limited because atomic transitions are not mechanical switches controlled at will, and real atoms can interact with surrounding fields, collisions and radiation in more complicated ways.

1.A.1.07.6.4 What modern quantum mechanics says

Quantum-mechanical stationary states are energy eigenstates. Their spatial probability distributions are time-independent in the absence of appropriate perturbations. They are not miniature balls making exact circular tracks and breaking an otherwise valid classical rule. Modern quantum mechanics explains stationary electronic states in a more coherent framework, including the wave nature of matter. Bohr's postulate was an important historical step toward that framework.

Exam reminder: 'A stationary state does not radiate continuously' is a Bohr-model assumption. 'The electron is stationary in position' is not the correct statement.

1.A.1.07.7 Photons, absorption and emission: follow the energy

Suppose an electron in a one-electron atom changes from one allowed energy to another. The energy difference cannot simply vanish. In a radiative downward transition, the atom can emit a photon carrying the lost energy. For an upward transition, energy must be provided, perhaps through absorption of a photon with a suitable energy. This is the connection between discrete electronic states and sharp spectral lines.

1.A.1.07.7.1 Why the signs matter

In a bound atom, energy is often defined to be zero for a free electron infinitely far from the nucleus. Bound electronic energy levels are negative. A level at −1.51 eV is actually higher than a level at −3.40 eV because −1.51 is closer to zero. A downward transition from −1.51 to −3.40 eV emits a photon with energy (−1.51) − (−3.40) = +1.89 eV.

Emission: Ephoton = Einitial − Efinal > 0
Absorption: Ephoton = Efinal − Einitial > 0
Ephoton = hν = hc/λ

1.A.1.07.7.2 Why wavelength changes when the energy gap changes

The equation hν = hc/λ shows that a larger positive energy gap corresponds to a higher photon frequency and shorter wavelength in vacuum. A smaller gap corresponds to a longer wavelength. This is why different transitions in hydrogen give lines in the ultraviolet, visible or infrared, rather than one fixed photon color.

Figure 1.A.1.07-F2. Original energy-ladder schematic illustrating the n = 3 → n = 2 hydrogen Balmer Hα transition. Physical energies are approximate; vertical spacing is illustrative.

1.A.1.07.7.3 Don't mistake possible energy differences for equal intensities

The energy equation predicts the energy of a photon for a specified transition. It does not by itself tell how frequently that transition occurs, whether other quantum selection rules restrict it, or how intense a measured line will look. Those questions require a more sophisticated quantum treatment and information about which states are populated. Bohr's achievement was providing the correct first-order energy-level pattern for hydrogen.

1.A.1.07.8 Deriving the permitted radii from force and quantization

We now turn two conceptual rules into a numerical prediction. One rule comes from classical electrostatics: the positively charged nucleus attracts the electron. The second is Bohr's quantized angular momentum. Combining them lets us calculate which ideal circular radii are allowed instead of simply asserting that a diagram contains several rings.

1.A.1.07.8.1 Step 1: Coulomb attraction provides an inward force

Consider a nucleus with charge +Ze and one electron of charge −e at a modeled circular radius r. The magnitude of their Coulomb attraction is kZe²/r², where k = 1/(4πε₀). For a circular trajectory, Newtonian mechanics requires inward force mₑv²/r. Therefore:

mev²/r = kZe²/r²

1.A.1.07.8.2 Step 2: impose the Bohr angular-momentum condition

Bohr restricts the motion by mₑvr = nħ, so the modeled electron speed can be expressed as v = nħ/(mₑr). Substitute this into the force equation. The left side becomes mₑ[n²ħ²/(mₑ²r²)]/r = n²ħ²/(mₑr³).

n²ħ²/(mer³) = kZe²/r²

1.A.1.07.8.3 Step 3: solve for r

Multiply both sides by r³ and rearrange. The result is rₙ = n²ħ²/(mₑkZe²). Writing the constant for Z = 1 and n = 1 as a₀ gives the familiar hydrogen-like expression:

rn ≈ a₀n²/Z, where a₀ ≈ 5.29177 × 10⁻¹¹ m = 0.529 Å

1.A.1.07.8.4 Step 4: understand rather than memorize the dependence

The radius grows with n²: at a fixed Z, the n = 2 radius is four times the n = 1 radius, and the n = 3 radius is nine times the n = 1 radius. A larger nuclear charge Z draws a one-electron system closer in this model, so the radius is inversely proportional to Z. For a hydrogen-like He⁺ ion at n = 1, the approximate radius is a₀/2, half the hydrogen value.

1.A.1.07.8.5 What assumptions were hidden?

This form assumes a single electron, an ideal circular orbit, a nonrelativistic speed and effectively a fixed, heavy nucleus. Greater precision replaces electron mass by the electron–nucleus reduced mass and includes other corrections. Most importantly, these circles are features of Bohr's historical model, not modern measured electron trajectories.

Reasoning summary: Electrostatic force by itself permits continuously variable paths. Adding L = nħ selects rₙ ∝ n²/Z. This is a derived consequence of Bohr's rule, not an independent postulate.

1.A.1.07.9 Deriving hydrogen-like energy levels step by step

The radius formula tells us where an electron is drawn in Bohr's model, but the most important experimental link is energy. Spectral wavelengths depend on differences between electronic energies, so we must calculate the allowed energies rather than simply memorize a list. We use the same single-electron, fixed-nucleus circular model, retaining the distinction between classical mechanical equations and Bohr's added quantization rule.

1.A.1.07.9.1 Step 1: identify kinetic and potential energy

The electron's kinetic energy is K = (1/2)mₑv². Since its charge is negative and the nucleus positive, the electrostatic potential energy is U = −kZe²/r, taking U = 0 at infinite separation. The total mechanical energy is E = K + U. The negative potential term is essential: it describes the attraction that binds the electron.

1.A.1.07.9.2 Step 2: use the circular-force condition

From the force balance mₑv²/r = kZe²/r², multiply both sides by r and divide by two to obtain K = kZe²/(2r). Substituting this into E = K + U gives E = kZe²/(2r) − kZe²/r = −kZe²/(2r). Therefore a smaller permitted radius corresponds to a more negative total binding energy.

E = K + U = −kZe²/(2r)

1.A.1.07.9.3 Step 3: insert the permitted radius

Our radius result was rₙ = n²ħ²/(mₑkZe²). Replace r with this expression and simplify: Eₙ = −mₑk²Z²e⁴/(2n²ħ²). All factors except Z and n are constants within the fixed-nucleus approximation. The familiar numerical form is:

En ≈ −13.6 eV × Z²/n²   (one-electron hydrogen-like species)

1.A.1.07.9.4 Step 4: understand the negative sign

Freeing the electron from the nucleus corresponds to taking it to the continuum reference E = 0. Thus the n = 1 hydrogen state at −13.6 eV is more tightly bound than the n = 2 state at −3.40 eV. To raise the electron from n = 1 to n = 2, the system needs an input of +10.2 eV. To remove it completely from n = 1, it requires approximately +13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.9.5 Why the equation matters

The Eₙ ∝ −Z²/n² rule links a historical electron model to reproducible spectral energies. It predicts that hydrogen-like ions with larger Z are more tightly bound and that neighboring energy levels approach one another as n becomes large. This compression of higher levels helps explain why a series of spectral lines can converge toward a limit.

1.A.1.07.10 Read the hydrogen energy ladder, not just the formula

The energy expression Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n² is easy to write, but interpretation is more valuable than rote memory. We can build a small table and ask what happens as n increases. The answer reveals why an electron can be excited, why it becomes easier to ionize from an excited level and why emitted wavelengths depend on the initial and final states.

1.A.1.07.10.1 Calculate the first four levels

For n = 1, E₁ = −13.6 eV. For n = 2, divide by four to get −3.40 eV. For n = 3, divide by nine to get about −1.51 eV. For n = 4, divide by sixteen to get −0.85 eV. The values become progressively less negative as n increases. A student sometimes reads the minus signs backward; −0.85 eV is a higher electronic energy than −13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.10.2 What does 'ground state' mean?

The ground state is the lowest-energy allowed electronic state. In the simple hydrogen Bohr model it is n = 1. It is not a situation where the electron has zero kinetic energy: the model gives it nonzero kinetic energy while the negative potential energy makes the sum negative. In modern quantum mechanics, hydrogen's ground state is a 1s orbital rather than a little circular track.

1.A.1.07.10.3 Why gaps become smaller higher up

Compare E₂ − E₁ = 10.2 eV with E₄ − E₃ ≈ 0.661 eV. The higher-state separation is much smaller. The n² denominator causes electronic energies to approach zero gradually, so adjacent levels become crowded near the ionization limit. Spectral lines representing transitions among high levels correspondingly approach one another in frequency or wavelength under the relevant series.

1.A.1.07.10.4 Be careful with a word like 'radius'

The tabulated rₙ values are radii of allowed circular orbits in the historical Bohr construction. They are useful for understanding n² scaling and calculating exam problems. They are not measurements of hard electron tracks or exact radii of all modern quantum orbitals. When an exam asks for the Bohr radius, answer using the Bohr model; when it asks about spatial probability density, use the quantum-orbital concept.

Practical reading rule: Larger n → larger ideal Bohr radius, higher (less negative) energy and weaker binding. The n → ∞ limit corresponds to ionization.

1.A.1.07.11 Hydrogen-like ions: why electron count matters more than element name

Hydrogen has one electron when neutral. But an atom does not need to be hydrogen to have only one electron. If helium loses one electron, He⁺ has a nucleus of charge +2e and one remaining electron. If lithium loses two electrons, Li²⁺ has a nucleus of charge +3e and one remaining electron. The Bohr formulas are a useful approximation for such hydrogen-like species.

1.A.1.07.11.1 Why the nuclear charge Z changes the result

An electron attracted to a +2e nucleus experiences a stronger Coulomb pull than an otherwise comparable electron attracted to +e. Within the one-electron Bohr model, the nuclear charge alters the permitted orbit sizes and their binding energies. The radius scales inversely with Z, while the energy magnitude scales with Z². This is an especially high-yield comparison for basic atomic-structure numericals.

rₙ ≈ a₀ n²/Z
Eₙ ≈ −13.6 eV × Z²/n²

1.A.1.07.11.2 Compare H and He⁺ at n = 1

For hydrogen, Z = 1, giving r₁ ≈ 0.529 Å and E₁ ≈ −13.6 eV. For He⁺, Z = 2, giving r₁ ≈ 0.529/2 = 0.2645 Å and E₁ ≈ −13.6 × 4 = −54.4 eV. Thus the ideal He⁺ ground-state orbit is half as large and four times as strongly bound in energy magnitude as the corresponding hydrogen orbit.

1.A.1.07.11.3 Now compare Li²⁺

Lithium has Z = 3. A Li²⁺ ion contains one electron, so the one-electron model applies approximately. The ground-state radius is about a₀/3 ≈ 0.176 Å, and the binding-energy magnitude is 13.6 × 9 ≈ 122.4 eV. These are model predictions with a heavy fixed nucleus; actual precise energies include finite nuclear mass and other corrections.

1.A.1.07.11.4 Why neutral helium is a different problem

Neutral helium has two electrons. They repel one another and their electronic states cannot be treated as two unrelated hydrogen-like circular orbits with only an independent nuclear attraction. The simple Bohr formula Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² does not correctly give the total neutral helium electronic structure. Later many-electron quantum methods are needed. Counting electrons before choosing a formula prevents this extremely common exam mistake.

1.A.1.07.12 Excitation energy is not ionization energy

Suppose a hydrogen atom begins in the ground state. You supply energy to the atom. There are two fundamentally different possibilities: you may raise its electron to another bound state, or you may supply enough energy to remove the electron completely. These are called excitation and ionization. Bohr's energy ladder makes their distinction mathematically clear.

1.A.1.07.12.1 Excitation: stay bound, move higher

To excite a hydrogen electron from n = 1 to n = 2, calculate E₂ − E₁ = (−3.40) − (−13.60) = +10.20 eV. The electron remains bound to the positive nucleus in the excited n = 2 model state. A suitable photon with 10.20 eV may be absorbed, or the state may be populated through other interactions such as collisions.

1.A.1.07.12.2 Ionization: reach the free-electron limit

In the standard energy convention, a free electron at infinite separation has E = 0. Removing a ground-state hydrogen electron therefore requires 0 − (−13.6) = +13.6 eV in the simple model. From n = 2 the required energy is only 0 − (−3.40) = +3.40 eV. The atom is more weakly bound in the excited state.

1.A.1.07.12.3 Do all collisions produce exactly one allowed excited state?

No. Real excitation depends on available energy, initial state, collision dynamics and transition probabilities. A collision can transfer various amounts of energy and may also ionize an atom if the threshold is exceeded. For a single photon and an isolated specified bound-bound transition, the photon energy must match the permitted level difference within the applicable physical linewidth. Larger or arbitrary photons do not simply place the electron at any forbidden energy.

1.A.1.07.12.4 Work through the wavelength of a ground-state excitation

The n = 1 → n = 2 hydrogen energy gap is about 10.2 eV. Using the convenient conversion hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm, the photon wavelength is λ ≈ 1240/10.2 ≈ 121.6 nm. This is ultraviolet light and corresponds to the Lyman-alpha transition when observed in reverse emission direction (n = 2 → n = 1).

Hydrogen: ΔE1→2 ≈ 10.2 eV; λ ≈ 121.6 nm
Ground-state ionization threshold: 13.6 eV

1.A.1.07.12.5 Three values with three meanings

Do not confuse the ground-state energy −13.6 eV, the first excitation energy +10.2 eV, and the ionization energy +13.6 eV. The signs and reference states differ: an allowed bound energy is written relative to zero; energy supplied to increase electronic energy is positive. A correct solution should explicitly identify both the initial and final states before subtracting.

Exam routine: Excitation = difference between two finite bound levels. Ionization = difference between a bound level and E = 0. Always state where the electron begins.

1.A.1.07.13 From Bohr energy gaps to the Rydberg formula

The hydrogen spectrum had been measured before Bohr developed his model. A formula involving integers described its lines, but nobody yet had a satisfactory atomic explanation for the integers. Bohr's bound-state expression provided the missing physical connection: the integers identify the initial and final allowed electronic energies. We can derive the wavelength formula by following the energy released in a downward transition.

1.A.1.07.13.1 Begin with a photon from two bound levels

For hydrogen, the energy of level n is Eₙ ≈ −13.6 eV/n². Suppose an electron begins at nᵢ and ends at n𝒇, with nᵢ greater than n𝒇. The atom releases a photon of positive energy Eᵢ − E𝒇 = 13.6 eV[1/n𝒇² − 1/nᵢ²]. The subtraction order matters, because the final level is more negative than the initial level.

1.A.1.07.13.2 Convert energy into a wavelength

Photon energy equals hc/λ. Dividing the electron energy difference by hc gives the celebrated Rydberg form: 1/λ = R_H[1/n𝒇² − 1/nᵢ²]. The hydrogen Rydberg constant is approximately 1.0968 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; many introductory exercises round it to 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Once the two state labels are known, the wavelength follows without memorizing each individual hydrogen line.

1/λ = RH[1/nf² − 1/ni²], for emission ni > nf ≥ 1

1.A.1.07.13.3 Interpret the constant carefully

The Rydberg constant associated with an infinitely massive nucleus, R∞, is approximately 1.09737 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Hydrogen's specific constant differs slightly because the proton and electron orbit their common center of mass. Introductory formulas neglect this small distinction, but precise spectroscopy considers reduced mass and further corrections. Keeping the distinction in mind prevents a student from claiming that every rounded classroom wavelength is an exact measured value.

1.A.1.07.13.4 What happens for a one-electron ion?

Within the ideal fixed-nucleus Bohr approximation, energy differences for a hydrogen-like ion are proportional to Z², where Z is nuclear charge number. Consequently the wavenumber 1/λ scales approximately as Z² for corresponding transitions. This result applies to one-electron He⁺ or Li²⁺, not directly to neutral multi-electron helium or lithium.

1.A.1.07.13.5 Why this derivation matters

Instead of simply describing hydrogen as colorful, the model predicts a numerical family of lines. It connects the older measured spectral formula to a physical idea—quantized electronic energies. This was a major success, even though Bohr's theory could not predict all intensities, fine splittings or spectra of complex atoms.

1.A.1.07.14 Lyman, Balmer and Paschen spectral series

Think of an electron dropping from a higher electronic level to a lower one. Its starting level controls the size of the energy release, but the named series is determined by the final level. Transitions ending at n = 1 form Lyman, those ending at n = 2 form Balmer, and those ending at n = 3 form Paschen. This simple rule is both easy to remember and useful when several possible transitions are listed in an exam.

1.A.1.07.14.1 The Lyman series: ultraviolet light

Lyman lines arise when a hydrogen electron falls to n = 1. Examples include n = 2 to 1, n = 3 to 1 and n = 4 to 1. Because the destination is strongly bound, these transitions generally release relatively large photon energies. Their wavelengths are in the ultraviolet region; the well-known 2 to 1 Lyman-alpha line is near 121.6 nm.

1.A.1.07.14.2 The Balmer series: characteristic visible lines

Balmer transitions end at n = 2. The n = 3 to 2 line lies near 656 nm and appears red. The n = 4 to 2 line is around 486 nm, and further lines occur near 434 and 410 nm. The sequence continues beyond the most obvious visible lines toward a limit near 364.6 nm. Johann Balmer found the famous numerical relationship for this family before Bohr's quantum model existed.

1.A.1.07.14.3 The Paschen series: infrared light

Paschen transitions end at n = 3, such as n = 4 to 3. They release smaller energies than transitions from comparable initial levels into n = 1, so their wavelengths are longer, in the infrared. Series ending at n = 4 and n = 5 are traditionally called Brackett and Pfund, respectively, and are also in infrared regions.

1.A.1.07.14.4 What if the direction is upward?

For absorption, the atom moves from a lower state to a higher one by taking in energy. The same pair of energy levels gives the same photon-energy magnitude, but the atom absorbs rather than emits. A mathematical transition energy does not by itself guarantee that a strong absorption line is visible under every condition; the population of initial states and quantum selection rules matter.

1.A.1.07.14.5 A method that prevents mistakes

Always write both n labels, check whether the electron is going up or down, and identify the final n for an emission-series name. A 5 to 2 emission is Balmer; 5 to 1 is Lyman; 5 to 3 is Paschen. It is the destination, not the initial n, that names the series.
