1.A.1.07.1 The two mysteries Bohr had to solve

Imagine entering a physics laboratory in 1913. Hydrogen gas glows in a narrow glass tube. Its light is separated by a spectroscope, and a surprising pattern appears: sharp colored lines, with spaces between them. Why does hydrogen emit these particular wavelengths instead of all possible colors? Another problem is waiting. Rutherford's nucleus is supported by scattering, but an electron traveling in a classical curved orbit accelerates and should radiate energy. Why, then, do ordinary atoms persist?

1.A.1.07.1.1 The evidence was already real

Geiger and Marsden's scattering experiments helped establish that atoms contain a concentrated central charge. Independently, spectroscopists had observed hydrogen's distinctive emission lines. The difficult task was to build one atomic picture consistent with both the existence of a nucleus and the observed spectral pattern. A scientific model earns confidence by explaining observations; it cannot simply ignore one of them.

1.A.1.07.1.2 What Bohr chose to keep

Niels Bohr did not discard Rutherford's positively charged center. The electron was still attracted to it by the electric force. The innovative step concerned which electron states were permitted. Instead of allowing the electron to possess any classical orbit and continuously radiate, Bohr imposed special stable states with restricted energies. Radiation would be connected with transitions between these states.

1.A.1.07.1.3 The difference between an assumption and a prediction

Bohr's stationary-state rule was a postulate: it was not derived from the earlier classical theory of accelerating electric charges. But the model then led to testable predictions for the wavelengths of hydrogen's spectral lines. This distinction matters. A postulate is judged by the explanatory success and testable consequences of the theory it helps build, not by pretending that it was already a theorem of classical mechanics.

1.A.1.07.1.4 Our learning journey

In this node, we begin with measured spectra, explain the three familiar Bohr postulates, derive allowed radii and energies, calculate photon wavelengths, interpret the Lyman and Balmer series, and identify the model's limitations. We will also separate Bohr's historical circular orbits from modern quantum-mechanical orbitals. The goal is not to memorize formulas without context; it is to understand why each equation arose and what it can and cannot tell us.

Big question: How can the hydrogen atom remain stable yet emit discrete photon energies, when unrestricted classical electron orbits explain neither feature?

1.A.1.07.1.5 A thought experiment that tests the model

Suppose two students invent competing atomic models. The first says the electron can occupy any radius and radiates continuously, while the second says only particular stable electronic energies are allowed. Both students might draw a positively charged nucleus at the center. Yet only the second has a route toward explaining a repeated collection of sharply defined hydrogen wavelengths. This thought experiment illustrates why locating the nucleus and explaining the electron's energy are different tasks.

Bohr's great strength was making the energy of a bound one-electron atom calculable and connecting that value to light. His great limitation was retaining the idea of definite circular electron trajectories. Keeping both observations in view gives a balanced evaluation: the model was a milestone, not the final answer to everything about the atom.

1.A.1.07.2 Spectral evidence: what hydrogen actually shows

Before looking at Bohr's theory, picture the observation that any convincing theory must explain. Put low-pressure atomic hydrogen in a discharge tube. Provide sufficient energy to excite the atoms, then send the emitted light through a prism or diffraction grating. A spectroscope separates different wavelengths, allowing us to see that some wavelengths are strongly represented while neighboring wavelengths may be absent.

1.A.1.07.2.1 Continuous light and line light are different

A broad continuous spectrum contains light across a range of wavelengths, like much of the rainbow produced by a white-light source. An atomic emission-line spectrum contains narrow features at characteristic wavelengths. In the visible hydrogen Balmer series, notable lines lie near 656 nm (red), 486 nm (blue-green), 434 nm and 410 nm (violet). The series also contains lines outside the most easily observed visible region. These wavelengths arise from distinct changes in the atom's electronic energy.

1.A.1.07.2.2 The screen tells us an observation, not an orbital photograph

A spectroscope reveals which wavelengths reach a detector. It does not directly show a tiny electron traveling from one painted ring to another. The connection to electronic energy states is a theoretical interpretation supported by the mathematical agreement between allowed energy differences and measured lines. This is a crucial difference between measuring a spectrum and drawing an atomic model.

1.A.1.07.2.3 Emission and absorption

In emission, an atom in an excited state can release a photon and enter a lower-energy state. In absorption, an atom with a suitable occupied initial state can take up energy and reach a higher state. The same energy differences are important to both processes, although actual strengths depend on populations, selection rules and experimental conditions. Saying every atom always absorbs every mathematically matching photon would ignore those qualifications.

1.A.1.07.2.4 What makes this evidence difficult for classical orbits?

In an unrestricted classical orbital picture, a bound electron could have a continuum of radii and energies. Continuous radiation during a changing orbit does not naturally yield the observed sharp list of characteristic wavelengths. A successful explanation must therefore explain why electron energies have specific permitted values and why emitted photons correspond to differences between them.

Evidence to remember: A hydrogen spectrum contains reproducible sharp lines. Their wavelengths must be explained quantitatively, not merely described as 'different colors'.

1.A.1.07.2.5 Measure a line before explaining it

When an experimentalist reports a spectral wavelength, the number depends on a calibrated optical measurement. A prism or grating changes the direction of light according to wavelength, and the instrument must distinguish true narrow features from its own finite resolution. The existence of a line is an observation; the assignment of that line to an electron transition is a theoretically supported interpretation. A serious scientific answer does not treat the two as identical.

The fact that hydrogen's lines appear at reproducible positions under varied suitable conditions makes them strong constraints on any model. Relative brightness can change with temperature, excitation and detector sensitivity, but the fundamental line energies remain characteristic of the species. That is why Bohr's agreement with the line positions was such a significant quantitative achievement.

1.A.1.07.3 Why quantization entered atomic theory

Bohr's breakthrough was part of a larger change in physics. Long before the complete modern quantum theory existed, researchers had begun to discover that microscopic phenomena did not always fit the continuous assumptions of classical models. The idea of energy quanta appeared in Planck's treatment of thermal radiation, while Einstein's analysis of the photoelectric effect connected light energy to frequency.

1.A.1.07.3.1 Balmer and Rydberg found the patterns first

Johann Balmer described a mathematical relationship among hydrogen's visible spectral wavelengths in 1885. Other spectral series were organized through related Rydberg formulas. The striking feature was that simple positive integers could be used to describe measured wavelengths. These patterns were known before Bohr proposed his nuclear-atom theory in 1913. An explanation was needed for why those integers worked.

1.A.1.07.3.2 Energy carried by light

Planck's constant h connects photon energy E to frequency ν through E = hν. Because frequency and wavelength in vacuum satisfy c = λν, the equivalent expression is E = hc/λ. A larger photon-energy difference therefore corresponds to a higher frequency and shorter wavelength. This relationship becomes particularly useful once we know the energies of two allowed atomic states.

1.A.1.07.3.3 The staircase analogy, with its limits

Imagine a staircase rather than a ramp. A ramp lets you stand at essentially any intermediate height; the staircase offers a series of distinct steps. Bohr's model treats bound electronic energies like allowed steps. But an electron is not actually a person resting on an invisible stair, and the allowed energies are not equally spaced. The analogy is only intended to emphasize that every intermediate energy is not permitted.

1.A.1.07.3.4 What quantization does not mean

It does not mean every photon in nature has the same energy. It does not mean an atom has only two possible states. It means that a given bound one-electron system possesses particular permitted stationary states. Different transitions between those states produce different photon energies. The probability and intensity of a real optical transition require additional physics beyond simply writing its energy difference.

History check: Balmer and Rydberg described spectral patterns experimentally; Bohr supplied a 1913 atomic model that explained their hydrogen-state energy structure.

1.A.1.07.3.5 Why earlier discoveries cannot be reduced to one invention

It is inaccurate to say Planck 'discovered the Bohr orbits' or that Einstein 'wrote the hydrogen level formula.' Their contributions involved energy quantization and light, respectively, while Rutherford established a nuclear center from scattering evidence and Bohr applied new restrictions to the atom's bound electron. Each result addressed a different experimental puzzle. Their eventual connection was powerful precisely because independent lines of evidence began to fit a coherent framework.

In an exam asking for chronology, first identify the observation under discussion. Hydrogen spectral regularities preceded the Bohr model; the nuclear interpretation preceded Bohr's quantization of electron states. This order also guards against the misconception that quantization was invented solely to justify an already known Bohr radius.

1.A.1.07.4 Bohr's three postulates in plain English

The familiar Bohr model can be organized around three related assumptions. These assumptions do different jobs, so it helps to learn them one at a time. Do not confuse a postulate with a formula derived later from it.

1.A.1.07.4.1 First: selected stable states

The model allows an electron to occupy certain circular states about the nucleus. While occupying one of these permitted stationary states, the electron does not continuously radiate electromagnetic energy. This was an explicit departure from the classical expectation for a point charge moving along a curved path. It addressed the problem that an unrestricted orbit would lose energy and fail to remain stable.

1.A.1.07.4.2 Second: angular momentum is restricted

For a circular Bohr orbit, the orbital angular momentum is restricted to integer multiples of the reduced Planck constant. This is written mₑvr = nħ = nh/(2π), where mₑ is electron mass, v its modeled orbital speed, r orbit radius and n is a positive integer. The condition does not follow from Coulomb's law alone; it is the extra restriction that selects a discrete set of classical-looking orbits.

me v r = nħ = nh/(2π), with n = 1, 2, 3, ...

1.A.1.07.4.3 Third: radiation accompanies transitions

If the atom changes between allowed electronic energies Eᵢ and E𝒇, the light exchanged has photon energy equal to the magnitude of the difference: hν = |Eᵢ − E𝒇|. A downward change can emit a photon; an upward change requires energy input, which may come from absorbing a photon. The photon's wavelength follows from λ = hc/|ΔE|, so a larger gap means a shorter wavelength.

Ephoton = |Ei − Ef| = hν = hc/λ

1.A.1.07.4.4 Why the postulates work together

Allowed states answer the stability question by assumption. The angular-momentum restriction determines which circular configurations the model admits. The photon rule connects differences among those states to observed spectral lines. Using one of these ideas in isolation would not reproduce the whole story; the model is a linked explanation.

1.A.1.07.4.5 Postulates versus predictions: an exam checklist

Three statements often appear beside one another in textbooks: 'stable orbits exist,' 'angular momentum is nħ,' and 'the radius is proportional to n².' They do not have the same logical status. The first two are central assumptions in the old Bohr formulation. The n² radius result follows by combining Coulomb attraction with the angular-momentum condition. Similarly, the bound energy's n⁻² behavior is derived from the model equations, while photon emission in a transition follows from the state-energy difference rule.

Knowing which statements are postulates makes it easier to answer 'Explain Bohr's model' without turning a derivation into a list of disconnected claims. It also helps explain why a successful theory may still need correction: one can test its consequences and identify which starting assumptions no longer survive later experiments.

1.A.1.07.5 Angular momentum and why only integers matter

Angular momentum describes rotational motion. In the old classical picture of an electron moving in a circular path, the magnitude of orbital angular momentum is the electron's mass multiplied by its speed and orbit radius: L = mₑvr. Without any additional law, this expression could accept a continuous range of results. Bohr's new condition permits only L = nħ for positive integer n.

1.A.1.07.5.1 What every symbol tells us

The symbol mₑ means electron mass, v means speed in the historical circular-orbit picture, and r means the orbital radius. The symbol h represents Planck's constant, while ħ = h/(2π) is the reduced Planck constant. The number n labels the state. In Bohr's model n = 1 gives L = ħ, n = 2 gives 2ħ, and n = 3 gives 3ħ. An n = 1.5 circular state is not part of the model.

1.A.1.07.5.2 Why this is an assumption rather than ordinary mechanics

Newton's laws and Coulomb attraction can balance the inward force of many hypothetical circular paths; they do not automatically require angular momentum to be an integer multiple of ħ. Bohr introduced that rule because the classical picture alone did not explain discrete spectral energies. Imposing the rule allows specific orbit radii and binding energies to emerge mathematically.

1.A.1.07.5.3 A later matter-wave interpretation

Louis de Broglie later proposed that matter can be associated with wavelength λ = h/p. If a circular path is treated as a standing-wave analogy, its circumference needs an integer number of wavelengths: 2πr = nλ. Substituting p ≈ mₑv gives mₑvr = nh/(2π). This is a useful *later* conceptual explanation of an integer restriction, but not Bohr's original 1913 derivation. Nor is it a literal picture of a modern electron circling like a vibrating string.

1.A.1.07.5.4 How modern theory corrects the picture

In quantum mechanics, orbital angular momentum is associated with the orbital quantum number ℓ and has magnitude √[ℓ(ℓ + 1)]ħ. The ground-state 1s orbital has ℓ = 0, which is not Bohr's classical n = 1 circular electron traveling with angular momentum ħ. Thus Bohr's quantization was historically valuable without being the final quantum rule for real bound electrons.

1.A.1.07.5.5 What the standing-wave analogy does and does not prove

The later de Broglie wave idea offers an intuitive explanation for why a circular length might contain a whole number of wavelengths. Imagine trying to join the ends of a repeating wave on a ring: if the pattern fails to line up at the joining point, it is not a consistent standing-wave pattern. Allowing complete repetitions leads to an integer condition and reproduces the old Bohr angular-momentum rule in that simplified argument.

This should not be overstated as the full quantum solution. A real electron's stationary state in an atom is not confined to a one-dimensional vibrating ring, and its allowed angular momentum is described by separate quantum numbers. Use the standing-wave story to understand the origin of discreteness, while remembering which parts belong to later physical theory.

1.A.1.07.6 Stationary states are not an electron at rest

The word 'stationary' causes a surprisingly common misunderstanding. Students may imagine that Bohr's electron becomes motionless when it reaches an allowed orbit. Bohr did not mean that. His historical model assigns a circular motion to the electron, yet asserts that the atom does not radiate continuously while it occupies a permitted state. 'Stationary' refers to the persistence of the state's energy, not to zero speed.

1.A.1.07.6.1 Start from the classical contradiction

According to classical mechanics, an electron changing direction in a circular orbit has centripetal acceleration. According to classical electrodynamics, a charged particle undergoing acceleration emits electromagnetic radiation. Since radiation carries energy, that electron should lose energy, and its orbit should not remain unchanged. This is why the unrestricted Rutherford-style planetary sketch could not explain stable matter.

1.A.1.07.6.2 Bohr's bold interruption

Bohr's stationary-state postulate says that selected electronic states do not radiate as the electron 'goes around' in the model. That instruction is not a result of the earlier classical radiation equations. It is a new quantum assumption. It reconciled the model with the everyday observation that stable atomic hydrogen can exist without emitting light forever. The model then uses transitions between states, rather than continuous orbit radiation, to account for photons.

1.A.1.07.6.3 Imagine a light switch rather than a leaking container

An unrestricted classical orbit would behave like a container with a hole continually leaking energy. The Bohr atom behaves more like a system that can stay in one permitted state and exchange energy only in suitable changes between states. This analogy is limited because atomic transitions are not mechanical switches controlled at will, and real atoms can interact with surrounding fields, collisions and radiation in more complicated ways.

1.A.1.07.6.4 What modern quantum mechanics says

Quantum-mechanical stationary states are energy eigenstates. Their spatial probability distributions are time-independent in the absence of appropriate perturbations. They are not miniature balls making exact circular tracks and breaking an otherwise valid classical rule. Modern quantum mechanics explains stationary electronic states in a more coherent framework, including the wave nature of matter. Bohr's postulate was an important historical step toward that framework.

Exam reminder: 'A stationary state does not radiate continuously' is a Bohr-model assumption. 'The electron is stationary in position' is not the correct statement.

1.A.1.07.6.5 A state can be stable and still interact with light

A stable stationary state is not an isolated object permanently forbidden from interacting with its environment. An atom can absorb a suitable photon, be excited by collisions or be perturbed by external electric and magnetic fields. If it enters an excited electronic state, it may eventually decay through a radiative transition or other processes. The crucial statement is narrower: the state does not continuously lose energy simply because its electron follows the assumed permitted orbit.

Modern quantum theory expresses this more precisely through stationary energy states and transition probabilities. The Bohr postulate was effective because it separated the persistent atomic state from episodic photon exchange, a distinction that classical planetary orbits did not support on their own.

1.A.1.07.7 Photons, absorption and emission: follow the energy

Suppose an electron in a one-electron atom changes from one allowed energy to another. The energy difference cannot simply vanish. In a radiative downward transition, the atom can emit a photon carrying the lost energy. For an upward transition, energy must be provided, perhaps through absorption of a photon with a suitable energy. This is the connection between discrete electronic states and sharp spectral lines.

1.A.1.07.7.1 Why the signs matter

In a bound atom, energy is often defined to be zero for a free electron infinitely far from the nucleus. Bound electronic energy levels are negative. A level at −1.51 eV is actually higher than a level at −3.40 eV because −1.51 is closer to zero. A downward transition from −1.51 to −3.40 eV emits a photon with energy (−1.51) − (−3.40) = +1.89 eV.

Emission: Ephoton = Einitial − Efinal > 0
Absorption: Ephoton = Efinal − Einitial > 0
Ephoton = hν = hc/λ

1.A.1.07.7.2 Why wavelength changes when the energy gap changes

The equation hν = hc/λ shows that a larger positive energy gap corresponds to a higher photon frequency and shorter wavelength in vacuum. A smaller gap corresponds to a longer wavelength. This is why different transitions in hydrogen give lines in the ultraviolet, visible or infrared, rather than one fixed photon color.

1.A.1.07.7.3 Don't mistake possible energy differences for equal intensities

The energy equation predicts the energy of a photon for a specified transition. It does not by itself tell how frequently that transition occurs, whether other quantum selection rules restrict it, or how intense a measured line will look. Those questions require a more sophisticated quantum treatment and information about which states are populated. Bohr's achievement was providing the correct first-order energy-level pattern for hydrogen.

1.A.1.07.8 Deriving the permitted radii from force and quantization

We now turn two conceptual rules into a numerical prediction. One rule comes from classical electrostatics: the positively charged nucleus attracts the electron. The second is Bohr's quantized angular momentum. Combining them lets us calculate which ideal circular radii are allowed instead of simply asserting that a diagram contains several rings.

1.A.1.07.8.1 Step 1: Coulomb attraction provides an inward force

Consider a nucleus with charge +Ze and one electron of charge −e at a modeled circular radius r. The magnitude of their Coulomb attraction is kZe²/r², where k = 1/(4πε₀). For a circular trajectory, Newtonian mechanics requires inward force mₑv²/r. Therefore:

mev²/r = kZe²/r²

1.A.1.07.8.2 Step 2: impose the Bohr angular-momentum condition

Bohr restricts the motion by mₑvr = nħ, so the modeled electron speed can be expressed as v = nħ/(mₑr). Substitute this into the force equation. The left side becomes mₑ[n²ħ²/(mₑ²r²)]/r = n²ħ²/(mₑr³).

n²ħ²/(mer³) = kZe²/r²

1.A.1.07.8.3 Step 3: solve for r

Multiply both sides by r³ and rearrange. The result is rₙ = n²ħ²/(mₑkZe²). Writing the constant for Z = 1 and n = 1 as a₀ gives the familiar hydrogen-like expression:

rn ≈ a₀n²/Z, where a₀ ≈ 5.29177 × 10⁻¹¹ m = 0.529 Å

1.A.1.07.8.4 Step 4: understand rather than memorize the dependence

The radius grows with n²: at a fixed Z, the n = 2 radius is four times the n = 1 radius, and the n = 3 radius is nine times the n = 1 radius. A larger nuclear charge Z draws a one-electron system closer in this model, so the radius is inversely proportional to Z. For a hydrogen-like He⁺ ion at n = 1, the approximate radius is a₀/2, half the hydrogen value.

1.A.1.07.8.5 What assumptions were hidden?

This form assumes a single electron, an ideal circular orbit, a nonrelativistic speed and effectively a fixed, heavy nucleus. Greater precision replaces electron mass by the electron–nucleus reduced mass and includes other corrections. Most importantly, these circles are features of Bohr's historical model, not modern measured electron trajectories.

Reasoning summary: Electrostatic force by itself permits continuously variable paths. Adding L = nħ selects rₙ ∝ n²/Z. This is a derived consequence of Bohr's rule, not an independent postulate.

1.A.1.07.9 Deriving hydrogen-like energy levels step by step

The radius formula tells us where an electron is drawn in Bohr's model, but the most important experimental link is energy. Spectral wavelengths depend on differences between electronic energies, so we must calculate the allowed energies rather than simply memorize a list. We use the same single-electron, fixed-nucleus circular model, retaining the distinction between classical mechanical equations and Bohr's added quantization rule.

1.A.1.07.9.1 Step 1: identify kinetic and potential energy

The electron's kinetic energy is K = (1/2)mₑv². Since its charge is negative and the nucleus positive, the electrostatic potential energy is U = −kZe²/r, taking U = 0 at infinite separation. The total mechanical energy is E = K + U. The negative potential term is essential: it describes the attraction that binds the electron.

1.A.1.07.9.2 Step 2: use the circular-force condition

From the force balance mₑv²/r = kZe²/r², multiply both sides by r and divide by two to obtain K = kZe²/(2r). Substituting this into E = K + U gives E = kZe²/(2r) − kZe²/r = −kZe²/(2r). Therefore a smaller permitted radius corresponds to a more negative total binding energy.

E = K + U = −kZe²/(2r)

1.A.1.07.9.3 Step 3: insert the permitted radius

Our radius result was rₙ = n²ħ²/(mₑkZe²). Replace r with this expression and simplify: Eₙ = −mₑk²Z²e⁴/(2n²ħ²). All factors except Z and n are constants within the fixed-nucleus approximation. The familiar numerical form is:

En ≈ −13.6 eV × Z²/n²   (one-electron hydrogen-like species)

1.A.1.07.9.4 Step 4: understand the negative sign

Freeing the electron from the nucleus corresponds to taking it to the continuum reference E = 0. Thus the n = 1 hydrogen state at −13.6 eV is more tightly bound than the n = 2 state at −3.40 eV. To raise the electron from n = 1 to n = 2, the system needs an input of +10.2 eV. To remove it completely from n = 1, it requires approximately +13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.9.5 Why the equation matters

The Eₙ ∝ −Z²/n² rule links a historical electron model to reproducible spectral energies. It predicts that hydrogen-like ions with larger Z are more tightly bound and that neighboring energy levels approach one another as n becomes large. This compression of higher levels helps explain why a series of spectral lines can converge toward a limit.

1.A.1.07.10 Read the hydrogen energy ladder, not just the formula

The energy expression Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n² is easy to write, but interpretation is more valuable than rote memory. We can build a small table and ask what happens as n increases. The answer reveals why an electron can be excited, why it becomes easier to ionize from an excited level and why emitted wavelengths depend on the initial and final states.

1.A.1.07.10.1 Calculate the first four levels

For n = 1, E₁ = −13.6 eV. For n = 2, divide by four to get −3.40 eV. For n = 3, divide by nine to get about −1.51 eV. For n = 4, divide by sixteen to get −0.85 eV. The values become progressively less negative as n increases. A student sometimes reads the minus signs backward; −0.85 eV is a higher electronic energy than −13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.10.2 What does 'ground state' mean?

The ground state is the lowest-energy allowed electronic state. In the simple hydrogen Bohr model it is n = 1. It is not a situation where the electron has zero kinetic energy: the model gives it nonzero kinetic energy while the negative potential energy makes the sum negative. In modern quantum mechanics, hydrogen's ground state is a 1s orbital rather than a little circular track.

1.A.1.07.10.3 Why gaps become smaller higher up

Compare E₂ − E₁ = 10.2 eV with E₄ − E₃ ≈ 0.661 eV. The higher-state separation is much smaller. The n² denominator causes electronic energies to approach zero gradually, so adjacent levels become crowded near the ionization limit. Spectral lines representing transitions among high levels correspondingly approach one another in frequency or wavelength under the relevant series.

1.A.1.07.10.4 Be careful with a word like 'radius'

The tabulated rₙ values are radii of allowed circular orbits in the historical Bohr construction. They are useful for understanding n² scaling and calculating exam problems. They are not measurements of hard electron tracks or exact radii of all modern quantum orbitals. When an exam asks for the Bohr radius, answer using the Bohr model; when it asks about spatial probability density, use the quantum-orbital concept.

Practical reading rule: Larger n → larger ideal Bohr radius, higher (less negative) energy and weaker binding. The n → ∞ limit corresponds to ionization.

1.A.1.07.11 Hydrogen-like ions: why electron count matters more than element name

Hydrogen has one electron when neutral. But an atom does not need to be hydrogen to have only one electron. If helium loses one electron, He⁺ has a nucleus of charge +2e and one remaining electron. If lithium loses two electrons, Li²⁺ has a nucleus of charge +3e and one remaining electron. The Bohr formulas are a useful approximation for such hydrogen-like species.

1.A.1.07.11.1 Why the nuclear charge Z changes the result

An electron attracted to a +2e nucleus experiences a stronger Coulomb pull than an otherwise comparable electron attracted to +e. Within the one-electron Bohr model, the nuclear charge alters the permitted orbit sizes and their binding energies. The radius scales inversely with Z, while the energy magnitude scales with Z². This is an especially high-yield comparison for basic atomic-structure numericals.

rₙ ≈ a₀ n²/Z
Eₙ ≈ −13.6 eV × Z²/n²

1.A.1.07.11.2 Compare H and He⁺ at n = 1

For hydrogen, Z = 1, giving r₁ ≈ 0.529 Å and E₁ ≈ −13.6 eV. For He⁺, Z = 2, giving r₁ ≈ 0.529/2 = 0.2645 Å and E₁ ≈ −13.6 × 4 = −54.4 eV. Thus the ideal He⁺ ground-state orbit is half as large and four times as strongly bound in energy magnitude as the corresponding hydrogen orbit.

1.A.1.07.11.3 Now compare Li²⁺

Lithium has Z = 3. A Li²⁺ ion contains one electron, so the one-electron model applies approximately. The ground-state radius is about a₀/3 ≈ 0.176 Å, and the binding-energy magnitude is 13.6 × 9 ≈ 122.4 eV. These are model predictions with a heavy fixed nucleus; actual precise energies include finite nuclear mass and other corrections.

1.A.1.07.11.4 Why neutral helium is a different problem

Neutral helium has two electrons. They repel one another and their electronic states cannot be treated as two unrelated hydrogen-like circular orbits with only an independent nuclear attraction. The simple Bohr formula Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² does not correctly give the total neutral helium electronic structure. Later many-electron quantum methods are needed. Counting electrons before choosing a formula prevents this extremely common exam mistake.

1.A.1.07.12 Excitation energy is not ionization energy

Suppose a hydrogen atom begins in the ground state. You supply energy to the atom. There are two fundamentally different possibilities: you may raise its electron to another bound state, or you may supply enough energy to remove the electron completely. These are called excitation and ionization. Bohr's energy ladder makes their distinction mathematically clear.

1.A.1.07.12.1 Excitation: stay bound, move higher

To excite a hydrogen electron from n = 1 to n = 2, calculate E₂ − E₁ = (−3.40) − (−13.60) = +10.20 eV. The electron remains bound to the positive nucleus in the excited n = 2 model state. A suitable photon with 10.20 eV may be absorbed, or the state may be populated through other interactions such as collisions.

1.A.1.07.12.2 Ionization: reach the free-electron limit

In the standard energy convention, a free electron at infinite separation has E = 0. Removing a ground-state hydrogen electron therefore requires 0 − (−13.6) = +13.6 eV in the simple model. From n = 2 the required energy is only 0 − (−3.40) = +3.40 eV. The atom is more weakly bound in the excited state.

1.A.1.07.12.3 Do all collisions produce exactly one allowed excited state?

No. Real excitation depends on available energy, initial state, collision dynamics and transition probabilities. A collision can transfer various amounts of energy and may also ionize an atom if the threshold is exceeded. For a single photon and an isolated specified bound-bound transition, the photon energy must match the permitted level difference within the applicable physical linewidth. Larger or arbitrary photons do not simply place the electron at any forbidden energy.

1.A.1.07.12.4 Work through the wavelength of a ground-state excitation

The n = 1 → n = 2 hydrogen energy gap is about 10.2 eV. Using the convenient conversion hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm, the photon wavelength is λ ≈ 1240/10.2 ≈ 121.6 nm. This is ultraviolet light and corresponds to the Lyman-alpha transition when observed in reverse emission direction (n = 2 → n = 1).

Hydrogen: ΔE1→2 ≈ 10.2 eV; λ ≈ 121.6 nm
Ground-state ionization threshold: 13.6 eV

1.A.1.07.12.5 Three values with three meanings

Do not confuse the ground-state energy −13.6 eV, the first excitation energy +10.2 eV, and the ionization energy +13.6 eV. The signs and reference states differ: an allowed bound energy is written relative to zero; energy supplied to increase electronic energy is positive. A correct solution should explicitly identify both the initial and final states before subtracting.

Exam routine: Excitation = difference between two finite bound levels. Ionization = difference between a bound level and E = 0. Always state where the electron begins.

1.A.1.07.13 From Bohr energy gaps to the Rydberg formula

The hydrogen spectrum had been measured before Bohr developed his model. A formula involving integers described its lines, but nobody yet had a satisfactory atomic explanation for the integers. Bohr's bound-state expression provided the missing physical connection: the integers identify the initial and final allowed electronic energies. We can derive the wavelength formula by following the energy released in a downward transition.

1.A.1.07.13.1 Begin with a photon from two bound levels

For hydrogen, the energy of level n is Eₙ ≈ −13.6 eV/n². Suppose an electron begins at nᵢ and ends at n𝒇, with nᵢ greater than n𝒇. The atom releases a photon of positive energy Eᵢ − E𝒇 = 13.6 eV[1/n𝒇² − 1/nᵢ²]. The subtraction order matters, because the final level is more negative than the initial level.

1.A.1.07.13.2 Convert energy into a wavelength

Photon energy equals hc/λ. Dividing the electron energy difference by hc gives the celebrated Rydberg form: 1/λ = R_H[1/n𝒇² − 1/nᵢ²]. The hydrogen Rydberg constant is approximately 1.0968 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; many introductory exercises round it to 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Once the two state labels are known, the wavelength follows without memorizing each individual hydrogen line.

1/λ = RH[1/nf² − 1/ni²], for emission ni > nf ≥ 1

1.A.1.07.13.3 Interpret the constant carefully

The Rydberg constant associated with an infinitely massive nucleus, R∞, is approximately 1.09737 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Hydrogen's specific constant differs slightly because the proton and electron orbit their common center of mass. Introductory formulas neglect this small distinction, but precise spectroscopy considers reduced mass and further corrections. Keeping the distinction in mind prevents a student from claiming that every rounded classroom wavelength is an exact measured value.

1.A.1.07.13.4 What happens for a one-electron ion?

Within the ideal fixed-nucleus Bohr approximation, energy differences for a hydrogen-like ion are proportional to Z², where Z is nuclear charge number. Consequently the wavenumber 1/λ scales approximately as Z² for corresponding transitions. This result applies to one-electron He⁺ or Li²⁺, not directly to neutral multi-electron helium or lithium.

1.A.1.07.13.5 Why this derivation matters

Instead of simply describing hydrogen as colorful, the model predicts a numerical family of lines. It connects the older measured spectral formula to a physical idea—quantized electronic energies. This was a major success, even though Bohr's theory could not predict all intensities, fine splittings or spectra of complex atoms.

1.A.1.07.14 Lyman, Balmer and Paschen spectral series

Think of an electron dropping from a higher electronic level to a lower one. Its starting level controls the size of the energy release, but the named series is determined by the final level. Transitions ending at n = 1 form Lyman, those ending at n = 2 form Balmer, and those ending at n = 3 form Paschen. This simple rule is both easy to remember and useful when several possible transitions are listed in an exam.

1.A.1.07.14.1 The Lyman series: ultraviolet light

Lyman lines arise when a hydrogen electron falls to n = 1. Examples include n = 2 to 1, n = 3 to 1 and n = 4 to 1. Because the destination is strongly bound, these transitions generally release relatively large photon energies. Their wavelengths are in the ultraviolet region; the well-known 2 to 1 Lyman-alpha line is near 121.6 nm.

1.A.1.07.14.2 The Balmer series: characteristic visible lines

Balmer transitions end at n = 2. The n = 3 to 2 line lies near 656 nm and appears red. The n = 4 to 2 line is around 486 nm, and further lines occur near 434 and 410 nm. The sequence continues beyond the most obvious visible lines toward a limit near 364.6 nm. Johann Balmer found the famous numerical relationship for this family before Bohr's quantum model existed.

1.A.1.07.14.3 The Paschen series: infrared light

Paschen transitions end at n = 3, such as n = 4 to 3. They release smaller energies than transitions from comparable initial levels into n = 1, so their wavelengths are longer, in the infrared. Series ending at n = 4 and n = 5 are traditionally called Brackett and Pfund, respectively, and are also in infrared regions.

1.A.1.07.14.4 What if the direction is upward?

For absorption, the atom moves from a lower state to a higher one by taking in energy. The same pair of energy levels gives the same photon-energy magnitude, but the atom absorbs rather than emits. A mathematical transition energy does not by itself guarantee that a strong absorption line is visible under every condition; the population of initial states and quantum selection rules matter.

1.A.1.07.14.5 A method that prevents mistakes

Always write both n labels, check whether the electron is going up or down, and identify the final n for an emission-series name. A 5 to 2 emission is Balmer; 5 to 1 is Lyman; 5 to 3 is Paschen. It is the destination, not the initial n, that names the series.

1.A.1.07.15 Calculate the Balmer H-alpha wavelength two ways

Consider a hydrogen atom in the third Bohr level. It makes a downward transition to n = 2 and emits light. Our aim is not merely to recall that H-alpha is red. We want to calculate the photon energy and wavelength, state the relevant assumptions, and explain what the number means physically. Two independent methods provide a useful consistency check.

1.A.1.07.15.1 Method A: electronic energy difference

The approximate hydrogen energies are E₃ = −13.6/9 ≈ −1.511 eV and E₂ = −13.6/4 = −3.400 eV. For emission the photon energy is the initial atomic energy minus the final atomic energy, so E_photon = (−1.511) − (−3.400) ≈ 1.889 eV. The result is positive even though the bound-state energies are both negative.

1.A.1.07.15.2 Convert the energy into wavelength

The useful conversion hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm gives λ ≈ 1240/1.889 ≈ 656.4 nm. With more accurate hydrogen values, the well-known H-alpha line is around 656.3 nm under ordinary reporting conventions. The small difference comes from rounded classroom constants and the details of precise spectroscopy, not from a different transition.

ΔE = 13.6(1/2² − 1/3²) eV = 13.6(5/36) ≈ 1.889 eV
λ = hc/ΔE ≈ 1240/1.889 ≈ 656 nm

1.A.1.07.15.3 Method B: the Rydberg equation

Start from 1/λ = R_H(1/2² − 1/3²) = R_H(5/36). Using R_H ≈ 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹, the reciprocal wavelength is approximately 1.524 × 10⁶ m⁻¹. Taking its reciprocal gives λ ≈ 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m. Since 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m, this is approximately 656 nm. Both routes agree to the intended precision.

1.A.1.07.15.4 What does the answer tell us?

The transition from n = 3 to n = 2 releases about 1.89 eV of atomic energy as one photon in the ideal radiative process. That photon has a red visible wavelength. It does not tell us how many photons are emitted by a whole gas sample per second: line intensity depends on how many atoms populate the states and on transition probabilities. Wavelength calculation and intensity prediction are different scientific problems.

1.A.1.07.15.5 How to avoid losing marks

Write initial and final levels first. Use Eᵢ − E𝒇 for emission. Keep photon energy positive. Convert meters to nanometers carefully if using the Rydberg constant, or keep electron-volts with hc in eV·nm. Finally, interpret the result as a hydrogen Balmer transition, not a general formula for arbitrary multi-electron species.

1.A.1.07.16 Series convergence and the ionization threshold

Look at a hydrogen series as the initial principal quantum number increases while the final one stays fixed. The emitted wavelengths approach a limiting value, and the lines crowd into a narrower range. The reason is that energy levels at large n lie ever closer to zero and to one another. The mathematical limit of a series carries a physical message about the energy required to free an electron from a particular bound state.

1.A.1.07.16.1 Take the Rydberg limit carefully

For a downward transition, 1/λ = R_H(1/n𝒇² − 1/nᵢ²). If the initial nᵢ becomes very large, the term 1/nᵢ² approaches zero. Therefore 1/λ_limit = R_H/n𝒇², giving λ_limit = n𝒇²/R_H. This is the shortest wavelength approached by that emission series, because it corresponds to the largest possible drop into its fixed final state.

ni → ∞: λlimit = nf²/RH

1.A.1.07.16.2 Compute the Lyman and Balmer limits

For Lyman, n𝒇 = 1. The limit is about 1/(1.097 × 10⁷) m, or 91.2 nm, within ultraviolet light. For Balmer, n𝒇 = 2, so the wavelength limit is four times greater, approximately 364.6 nm. This is near the ultraviolet end of the visible range. These numbers are not new arbitrary constants: they follow from the same Rydberg formula with different final levels.

1.A.1.07.16.3 Why higher lines become crowded

For large n, the difference between consecutive energies becomes small. A photon released from n = 20 to n = 2 has an energy not much different from one released from n = 30 to n = 2, since both initial energy values lie near zero. Thus their wavelengths approach one another as the series converges. The pattern is evidence of an energy-level structure with a finite ionization threshold.

1.A.1.07.16.4 What does infinity mean here?

In these equations, n → ∞ is a mathematical way to represent the boundary between bound electronic levels and the ionization continuum. It does not describe a real permanent circular orbit of literally infinite radius. Highly excited Rydberg atoms may exhibit very large spatial extent and special sensitivity to weak electric fields, but the limiting zero binding energy corresponds to an unbound electron.

1.A.1.07.16.5 One more exam trap: shortest versus longest

The series limit is the shortest wavelength for a fixed final n among transitions from successively higher bound initial states, because larger energy release produces shorter wavelength. The longest emission wavelength in a particular series is associated with the smallest allowed downward step into that final level (for example, n = 3 to n = 2 for the Balmer series in the ideal model). If an answer option swaps these, use E = hc/λ to correct it.

Remember: Lyman final n = 1, Balmer final n = 2, Paschen final n = 3; a series limit is the ionization-threshold edge of the corresponding emission series.

1.A.1.07.17 Why the Bohr model cannot describe many-electron atoms

Hydrogen's single electron makes it an ideal test for a simple nuclear model. But imagine moving from hydrogen to neutral helium. Now two electrons are attracted to the same positive nucleus while repelling one another. Their states influence each other, so an expression derived for one electron in a pure central Coulomb attraction cannot simply be applied to each electron independently.

1.A.1.07.17.1 Identify the missing interaction

The one-electron Bohr model keeps the electron–nucleus attraction but has no full treatment of electron–electron repulsion. For H, He⁺ or Li²⁺, there is only one electron, making the hydrogen-like formula useful. Neutral helium has two electrons, and neutral lithium has three. Those systems need additional physics. Electron interactions change the energies and organization of allowed states.

1.A.1.07.17.2 A dangerous calculation

Suppose a student substitutes Z = 2 and n = 1 into Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² and says each electron of neutral helium has energy −54.4 eV. This is not a correct complete prediction. The formula describes a one-electron ion with Z = 2, namely He⁺, under the simple Bohr approximation. Neutral He contains an additional electron, and the electron–electron repulsion cannot be ignored.

1.A.1.07.17.3 What is screening?

In atoms with multiple electrons, an outer electron is influenced by the positive nucleus and by other electrons. The others partly screen the nuclear attraction, so it does not behave like an isolated electron feeling the full bare nuclear charge in the same way as He⁺. Modern treatments use quantum orbitals, effective nuclear charge and electron correlation rather than independent fixed circular rings.

Always count electrons, including the ionic charge, before using Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n². He⁺ and neutral He are different electronic systems. The statement 'Bohr's model works for helium' is too ambiguous; it works approximately for hydrogen-like He⁺, not as a complete quantitative theory of ordinary neutral helium.

1.A.1.07.18 Fine structure, field effects and spectral intensities

A simple classroom drawing shows each spectral line as one sharp vertical mark. More precise measurements can reveal closely spaced components or shifts. The one-electron Bohr model predicts major hydrogen level differences but not every feature of high-resolution spectra. Understanding this limitation requires distinguishing the broad line pattern from the finer physical effects discovered through increasingly sensitive experiments.

1.A.1.07.18.1 Fine structure of atomic levels

The real electronic structure involves more than the principal quantum number n. Relativistic effects, electron spin and spin–orbit interactions contribute small corrections and splittings. The historical circular Bohr orbit does not include electron spin or a full relativistic quantum description. It consequently misses detailed spectral structure even for a one-electron atom such as hydrogen.

1.A.1.07.18.2 What a magnetic field can do

The Zeeman effect refers to the splitting or shifting of spectral lines in an external magnetic field. Atomic magnetic moments and angular momentum respond to that field. A single energy per n without additional state structure cannot generally account for the resulting multiplets. This does not make the original hydrogen energy calculation worthless; it shows that the energy picture requires refinement.

1.A.1.07.18.3 What an electric field can do

The Stark effect describes shifts or splittings associated with an applied electric field. Again, a theory that assigns a level only through Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² and ignores external-field interactions cannot explain the full observations. Modern quantum theory gives the appropriate framework for these interactions and for determining which transitions are allowed.

1.A.1.07.18.4 Why intensity is a separate prediction

Bohr's energy-difference rule tells us the photon's energy if a specified transition occurs. It does not provide the complete probability that a transition will take place, its polarization, or its relative intensity compared with other lines. These quantities depend on transition amplitudes, state populations and quantum selection rules. Predicting wavelength is therefore not the same task as predicting line brightness.

1.A.1.07.18.5 Do not confuse fine and hyperfine structure

Fine structure involves effects such as relativistic corrections and electron spin–orbit interactions. Hyperfine structure refers to smaller interactions involving nuclear spin and its coupling to electronic degrees of freedom. Neither is predicted completely by a simple Bohr circular-orbit picture. In foundational Life Science examinations, recognizing that such effects require more sophisticated theory is usually more useful than an advanced derivation.

Precise evaluation: Bohr predicted major hydrogen line energies successfully, but he did not supply a complete explanation of multi-electron spectra, fine details, external-field splitting, line intensities or modern electronic probability distributions.

1.A.1.07.19 Modern quantum orbitals replace classical circular tracks

Picture a drawing with an electron as a tiny bead moving along a perfect circle. Now picture a softly shaded spherical region around a nucleus. Those are two quite different kinds of models. Bohr's old atom drew permitted trajectories for its electron. Quantum mechanics describes electronic states through wavefunctions and probabilities. The older picture remains a helpful historical bridge but should not be confused with a literal microscopic electron track.

1.A.1.07.19.1 The wavefunction changes the description

A modern electron state can be represented by a wavefunction. Its squared magnitude is related to the probability density for finding the electron in a position measurement. For hydrogen's ground 1s state, the density is spherically symmetric, not concentrated on a thin ring at a single circular radius. The Bohr radius remains a useful fundamental length scale, yet a precise 'planetary orbit' is not present in the quantum 1s state.

1.A.1.07.19.2 Why exact position and momentum cannot both be assigned

Quantum states obey an uncertainty relation for position and momentum. The familiar form Δx Δpₓ ≥ ħ/2 relates their spreads in one direction. It is not simply a complaint about imperfect microscopes. It expresses a restriction built into the state description, and it undermines the assumption that an atomic electron is a miniature classical planet with a simultaneously definite track and momentum at every instant.

Δx · Δpx ≥ ħ/2

1.A.1.07.19.3 What became of angular momentum?

Quantum mechanics keeps orbital angular momentum but describes it using more than the principal quantum number. Its magnitude is √[ℓ(ℓ + 1)]ħ, associated with orbital quantum number ℓ. A hydrogenic 1s ground state has ℓ = 0, unlike a literal Bohr n = 1 circle with L = ħ. This difference provides a concrete example of why the old model cannot simply be declared the fully correct quantum atom.

1.A.1.07.19.4 Why the old model still deserves attention

Bohr showed how quantized electronic states could account for hydrogen's measured spectral lines and relate their wavelengths to fundamental constants. Modern theory retained quantized states while replacing classical circular trajectories with a richer electronic description. Learning the earlier model makes the historical and conceptual need for quantum orbitals much clearer.

1.A.1.07.20 Life Science connections: spectroscopy without overextending Bohr

Life Science students encounter photons in laboratory experiments long before they study specialized quantum physics. UV–visible spectroscopy, fluorescence microscopy and biochemical photoreactions all involve light interacting with matter. Bohr's historical model helps introduce energy differences and wavelength relationships, but it does not directly calculate the spectra of proteins, nucleic acids or complex dyes.

1.A.1.07.20.1 What the wavelength tells us

The relation E = hc/λ is broadly useful. In convenient units, photon energy in electron-volts is approximately 1240 divided by wavelength in nanometers. A 250 nm ultraviolet photon therefore carries about 4.96 eV, whereas a 500 nm visible photon carries about 2.48 eV. Shorter wavelengths correspond to higher photon energies. This is a reliable general idea beyond hydrogen, even though molecular absorption energies are not given by the Bohr hydrogen formula.

1.A.1.07.20.2 Why molecules are more complicated

Biomolecules contain many electrons, multiple nuclei and intricate chemical bonds. Their electronic states are molecular rather than one-electron hydrogenic states. Molecular spectra can also include vibrational and rotational contributions, solvent effects and interactions with surrounding proteins. The Bohr relationship Eₙ = −13.6/n² is therefore not a formula for DNA absorption or fluorescent protein emission.

1.A.1.07.20.3 Absorption and fluorescence

In fluorescence, a molecule typically absorbs light that promotes it to an excited electronic state. After some relaxation, it can emit a photon while returning to a lower state. The emitted wavelength is often longer than the excitation wavelength because energy can be lost to nonradiative processes. This illustrates the importance of discrete electronic energy changes while also showing why a molecular Jablonski diagram contains more information than a single Bohr ring.

1.A.1.07.20.4 Use the distinction on an exam

If a question asks the photon energy of 400 nm light, use E = hc/λ. If it asks the n = 3 to n = 2 hydrogen line, use Bohr energies or the Rydberg equation. If it asks why a DNA base absorbs ultraviolet radiation, discuss molecular electronic structure rather than pretending the base has one Bohr electron orbiting a single nucleus.

That conceptual boundary is a mark of deep learning: a useful model should be applied where its assumptions hold, and replaced where the problem requires greater physical detail.

1.A.1.07.21 Nine detailed worked numericals: from energy to spectra

Understanding a formula becomes easier when we use it in different circumstances. The examples below are deliberately varied: some involve energy, some radius, some spectral series, and some require deciding whether the Bohr formula is appropriate at all. Treat each one as a short scientific argument. Begin with the physical system, identify the valid approximation, substitute the numbers with units, and interpret the answer.

1.A.1.07.21.1 Worked example 1 — Radius of n = 3 in hydrogen

Question: Find the ideal Bohr radius for hydrogen in the n = 3 state. Given: a₀ = 0.529 Å and Z = 1. Formula: rₙ = a₀n²/Z. Substitute: r₃ = 0.529 × 9/1 = 4.761 Å (≈ 0.476 nm). Interpretation: The radius is nine times the n = 1 Bohr radius; it is an ideal circular-model radius, not an electron's measured planetary track.

1.A.1.07.21.2 Worked example 2 — Radius of He⁺ in its ground state

Question: Calculate r₁ for He⁺. It is a one-electron ion, and the nucleus has Z = 2. Thus r₁ = a₀/2 = 0.2645 Å. The stronger nuclear attraction gives a smaller predicted radius. Remember that neutral He has two electrons, so this exact one-electron procedure would not be appropriate for it.

1.A.1.07.21.3 Worked example 3 — Energy of hydrogen n = 4

Question: Determine E₄ for hydrogen. Use Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² eV with Z = 1 and n = 4. Calculation: E₄ = −13.6/16 = −0.850 eV. The negative sign means this is a bound level relative to E = 0 for an unbound electron. It is higher than E₁ = −13.6 eV, even though its numerical magnitude is smaller.

1.A.1.07.21.4 Worked example 4 — Ground-state ionization of He⁺

Question: Estimate the ionization energy of ground-state He⁺ in the simple model. Its n = 1 energy is −13.6 × (2²) = −54.4 eV. Ionization takes it to E = 0, so the energy required is 0 − (−54.4) = +54.4 eV. This is the binding-energy magnitude, not a negative amount of energy supplied.

1.A.1.07.21.5 Worked example 5 — Excitation from hydrogen n = 1 to n = 3

Question: Find the required energy. E₁ = −13.6 eV and E₃ = −13.6/9 ≈ −1.511 eV. The energy gain is E₃ − E₁ = −1.511 − (−13.6) ≈ 12.09 eV. This excites the atom while keeping it bound. It is smaller than the ground-state ionization threshold of 13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.21.6 Worked example 6 — The H-alpha emission photon

Question: Determine the approximate wavelength of the hydrogen n = 3 → n = 2 emission line. The energy difference is 13.6[1/4 − 1/9] ≈ 1.889 eV. With hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm, λ ≈ 1240/1.889 ≈ 656 nm. The final level is n = 2, so the line belongs to the Balmer series and lies in the red visible range.

1.A.1.07.21.7 Worked example 7 — A Lyman-alpha photon

Question: What wavelength is produced by hydrogen n = 2 → n = 1? Energy = (−3.4) − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV. Hence λ ≈ 1240/10.2 ≈ 121.6 nm. The final level is n = 1, identifying the Lyman series in ultraviolet light. The direction n = 1 → n = 2 instead describes absorption of energy of the same magnitude, subject to the relevant conditions.

1.A.1.07.21.8 Worked example 8 — The Balmer convergence limit

Question: Calculate the limiting wavelength for Balmer emission. Hold n𝒇 = 2 and let nᵢ approach infinity. The formula gives λ_limit = n𝒇²/R_H = 4/(1.097 × 10⁷) m ≈ 3.646 × 10⁻⁷ m = 364.6 nm. This is the short-wavelength limit of the Balmer emission series, not its longest wavelength.

1.A.1.07.21.9 Worked example 9 — Does the formula apply to neutral helium?

Question: A problem states 'neutral helium in its ground state' and offers E₁ = −54.4 eV as the energy of its electrons. Is this directly justified? No. Neutral helium has two electrons, which interact with one another. The formula −13.6Z²/n² is the basic one-electron hydrogen-like expression and can be applied approximately to He⁺, not used as a complete description of neutral He. This is a qualitative problem about assumptions, not merely arithmetic.

1.A.1.07.21.10 A reliable four-step approach for examinations

First, check electron count: hydrogen-like or multi-electron? Second, identify the initial and final states. Third, choose an appropriate formula with consistent units. Fourth, attach a scientific interpretation: emission or absorption, bound or ionized, and the correct spectral series. This procedure prevents many mistakes that occur when students apply a memorized equation before reading the physical description.

Numerical summary: rₙ grows as n²/Z; binding-energy magnitude grows as Z²/n²; photon energy is always the positive magnitude of the relevant state difference; λ varies inversely with photon energy.

1.A.1.07.22.1 Question 01 — Historical purpose

Which observation most directly motivated Bohr's permitted energy states? (A) Osmosis (B) Hydrogen's line spectrum and the instability of classical electron orbits (C) Dalton's law alone (D) Mitosis.

Answer: B. The nucleus had experimental support, but unrestricted classical orbits predicted radiation and could not explain particular hydrogen wavelengths. Quantization addressed those problems.

1.A.1.07.22.2 Question 02 — Stationary-state meaning

In the Bohr model, stationary states are those in which the electron (A) has zero speed (B) is outside the atom (C) does not continuously radiate while occupying an allowed orbit (D) has no mass.

Answer: C. Stationary describes a nonradiating allowed atomic state, not a motionless electron in the historical circular-orbit model.

1.A.1.07.22.3 Question 03 — Angular momentum rule

Which equation expresses Bohr's old quantum angular-momentum condition? (A) mvr = nħ (B) mvr = nh (C) mvr = n²ħ² (D) mvr = 0 for every n.

Answer: A. The circular Bohr condition is L = mvr = nh/(2π) = nħ, with n a positive integer. It is an additional old-quantum postulate.

1.A.1.07.22.4 Question 04 — Allowed n values

Which principal quantum-number value is forbidden in the standard bound Bohr model? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 1.5.

Answer: D. The label n is a positive integer. A noninteger n = 1.5 does not define a permitted Bohr stationary orbit.

1.A.1.07.22.5 Question 05 — Hydrogen ground energy

In the usual Bohr approximation, the ground-state energy of hydrogen is (A) +13.6 eV (B) −13.6 eV (C) 0 eV (D) −3.4 eV.

Answer: B. The conventional zero is the separated electron and proton. A bound ground-state electron lies at −13.6 eV; removing it requires positive energy.

1.A.1.07.22.6 Question 06 — Hydrogen n2 energy

The energy of hydrogen at n = 2 is approximately (A) −54.4 eV (B) −13.6 eV (C) −3.40 eV (D) +3.40 eV.

Answer: C. Use E₂ = −13.6/2² = −3.40 eV, a higher, less tightly bound level than n = 1.

1.A.1.07.22.7 Question 07 — Radius scaling

At fixed Z in the Bohr model, the ratio r₃/r₁ is (A) 3 (B) 6 (C) 9 (D) 1/9.

Answer: C. Because rₙ ∝ n², r₃/r₁ = 3²/1² = 9. The result refers to model radii, not exact measured electron tracks.

1.A.1.07.22.8 Question 08 — Nuclear charge scaling

At fixed principal n, the Bohr radius of a one-electron ion varies with Z as (A) Z² (B) Z (C) 1/Z (D) 1/Z².

Answer: C. The expression rₙ = a₀n²/Z shows that stronger nuclear charge pulls the single-electron model orbit inward.

1.A.1.07.22.9 Question 09 — Ground-state He plus energy

The approximate Bohr ground energy of He⁺ is (A) −13.6 eV (B) −27.2 eV (C) −54.4 eV (D) −3.4 eV.

Answer: C. He⁺ has one electron and Z = 2; E₁ = −13.6 × 2² = −54.4 eV under the fixed-nucleus approximation.

1.A.1.07.22.10 Question 10 — Neutral helium trap

Why cannot neutral helium be described completely by two independent hydrogen-like Bohr energy formulas? (A) It has no nucleus (B) Its electrons repel each other (C) Its electrons are positive (D) It contains no charge.

Answer: B. Neutral helium has two electrons with electron–electron interaction. The simple one-electron Coulomb model does not capture their combined quantum state.

1.A.1.07.22.11 Question 11 — Ground state ionization

Approximate ionization energy of hydrogen from n = 1 is (A) 3.4 eV (B) 10.2 eV (C) 13.6 eV (D) −13.6 eV.

Answer: C. Energy must be supplied to raise the electron from −13.6 eV to the continuum reference 0, requiring +13.6 eV.

1.A.1.07.22.12 Question 12 — First excitation energy

Energy required for hydrogen n = 1 → n = 2 is approximately (A) 1.89 eV (B) 3.40 eV (C) 10.2 eV (D) 13.6 eV.

Answer: C. Subtract E₂ − E₁ = −3.4 − (−13.6) = +10.2 eV. The atom remains bound in n = 2.

1.A.1.07.22.13 Question 13 — Photon sign

For emission from a higher electronic state Eᵢ to a lower E𝒇, the photon energy is (A) E𝒇 − Eᵢ if negative (B) Eᵢ − E𝒇, a positive amount (C) always zero (D) equal to the electron's charge.

Answer: B. Photon energy is positive and equals the energy lost by the atom. With Eᵢ greater than E𝒇, use Eᵢ − E𝒇.

1.A.1.07.22.14 Question 14 — Wavelength relation

For two photons, the one with the greater energy in vacuum has (A) longer wavelength (B) identical wavelength always (C) shorter wavelength (D) zero frequency.

Answer: C. E = hc/λ, so energy is inversely proportional to wavelength. Larger energy corresponds to shorter wavelength.

1.A.1.07.22.15 Question 15 — Balmer classification

The hydrogen transition 5 → 2 belongs to (A) Lyman (B) Balmer (C) Paschen (D) Brackett.

Answer: B. For emission series, name the series using the final level. Final n = 2 identifies Balmer.

1.A.1.07.22.16 Question 16 — Lyman classification

The hydrogen transition 4 → 1 belongs to (A) Lyman (B) Balmer (C) Paschen (D) Pfund.

Answer: A. A downward transition to n = 1 belongs to the ultraviolet Lyman series regardless of the higher initial n.

1.A.1.07.22.17 Question 17 — Paschen classification

Which transition is a Paschen-series emission? (A) 3 → 2 (B) 4 → 1 (C) 5 → 3 (D) 2 → 1.

Answer: C. The Paschen series consists of downward hydrogen transitions ending at n = 3, commonly producing infrared photons.

1.A.1.07.22.18 Question 18 — H alpha line

Approximate wavelength of hydrogen 3 → 2 emission is (A) 121.6 nm (B) 656 nm (C) 91.2 nm (D) 364.6 nm.

Answer: B. E = 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) ≈ 1.889 eV, giving λ ≈ 1240/1.889 ≈ 656 nm, the Balmer H-alpha line.

1.A.1.07.22.19 Question 19 — Lyman alpha

Approximate wavelength of hydrogen 2 → 1 emission is (A) 121.6 nm (B) 656 nm (C) 486 nm (D) 1875 nm.

Answer: A. The gap is 10.2 eV and λ ≈ 1240/10.2 = 121.6 nm, ultraviolet Lyman-alpha radiation.

1.A.1.07.22.20 Question 20 — Balmer series limit

The approximate short-wavelength limit of the Balmer series is (A) 656 nm (B) 121.6 nm (C) 364.6 nm (D) 0.529 Å.

Answer: C. At final n = 2 and initial n approaching infinity, λ_limit = 4/R_H ≈ 364.6 nm.

1.A.1.07.22.21 Question 21 — Series limit interpretation

In a hydrogen emission series with fixed final n, the series limit corresponds to (A) smallest energy drop (B) largest energy drop into that level (C) zero photon energy (D) electron rest mass.

Answer: B. The initial bound energy approaches zero at large n. The maximum release into a fixed lower level gives the shortest-wavelength limit.

1.A.1.07.22.22 Question 22 — Bohr model failure

Which phenomenon is not fully described by a one-integer Bohr orbit model? (A) Main hydrogen Balmer wavelengths (B) Approximate He⁺ binding (C) Fine structure and magnetic-field splitting (D) Hydrogen's ground energy.

Answer: C. The model lacks spin, detailed angular quantum numbers and modern field interactions, so it cannot completely explain fine and Zeeman structures.

1.A.1.07.22.23 Question 23 — Modern orbital contrast

A modern hydrogen 1s orbital is best described as (A) a thin classical circular wire (B) a stationary quantum probability distribution (C) a negative nucleus (D) a rotating solid shell.

Answer: B. The ground-state electronic wavefunction yields a spatial probability density, not a definite classical planet-like track.

1.A.1.07.22.24 Question 24 — Life Science boundary

For the energy of a 250 nm photon interacting with a biological sample, the generally valid starting formula is (A) E = hc/λ (B) E = −13.6Z²/n² for every molecule (C) A = Z + N (D) F = ma only.

Answer: A. E = hc/λ gives photon energy regardless of whether the absorbing species is atomic or molecular. Bohr's bound-energy formula does not directly describe multi-electron biomolecules.

1.A.1.07.23 Higher-order reasoning: explain Bohr's success and failure

A demanding examination question rarely asks students to reproduce only an isolated formula. It may combine an observation, a proposed model, a limitation and a numerical consequence. The short-answer challenges here are designed to develop that deeper reasoning. Try explaining each answer aloud in a few logical sentences. If the explanation relies only on a memorized number, return to the relevant derivation.

1.A.1.07.23.1 Challenge 1 — Why did Bohr keep Rutherford's nucleus?

Because the concentrated central charge had strong support from alpha-particle scattering. The problem with the classical nuclear atom was not that the nucleus had been disproved; the problem was that unrestricted classical electron trajectories were incompatible with atomic stability and discrete spectra. Bohr therefore preserved the nuclear center and changed the allowed electronic states and rules for radiation. The better theory retained successful evidence rather than discarding everything that came before it.

1.A.1.07.23.2 Challenge 2 — Why do line spectra imply special energies?

Measured hydrogen light contains sharp emission wavelengths. Each wavelength corresponds to a particular photon energy through E = hc/λ. If the emitted photon carries the difference between an initial and final atomic energy, particular observed photon energies suggest that allowed electronic energy differences are restricted. Bohr's model assigns permitted energies following −13.6/n² eV and thereby predicts many of the characteristic wavelengths.

1.A.1.07.23.3 Challenge 3 — Is the Bohr stationary-state idea classical physics?

No. A classical electron moving in a circle accelerates and would radiate energy according to classical electromagnetism. Bohr's assertion that certain allowed circular states do not continuously radiate was a new postulate. It addressed an observed stability problem but was not deduced from ordinary Newtonian or Maxwellian motion. Later quantum mechanics explains stable atomic states using a more complete mathematical framework.

1.A.1.07.23.4 Challenge 4 — How are excitation and ionization distinguished?

Excitation raises an electron between bound energy levels; ionization supplies enough energy to remove it from the bound system into the continuum. In hydrogen, the n = 1 → 2 excitation costs about 10.2 eV, while ionizing from the ground state requires about 13.6 eV. Both are positive energy inputs, but their final states and physical outcomes are different.

1.A.1.07.23.5 Challenge 5 — Why is He⁺ allowed but neutral He is not?

He⁺ has one remaining electron, which interacts with the +2e nucleus in the simplest Coulomb model. Neutral He contains two electrons that repel each other and form a many-electron quantum state. Applying one-electron Bohr energies independently to both electrons ignores their mutual interaction, so it cannot correctly predict the neutral atom's full electronic spectrum.

1.A.1.07.23.6 Challenge 6 — Why doesn't one successful spectral equation make the model complete?

Bohr's formulas predict major hydrogen and hydrogen-like transition energies, but real spectra contain finer details such as spin-associated structure, field-dependent splitting and intensity differences. Modern orbitals also do not have exact classical circular electron tracks. A theory can predict important experimental values while remaining incomplete for other measurable properties.

1.A.1.07.23.7 Challenge 7 — What is the difference between a photon wavelength and a spectral line's intensity?

Wavelength locates the energy of the photon associated with a specified transition. Intensity depends on the number of atoms taking part, their initial-state populations, rates and probabilities of transitions, detection response and experimental conditions. The old Bohr rule for an energy gap does not by itself determine line brightness.

1.A.1.07.23.8 Challenge 8 — Why are atomic models relevant to biology without being biological models?

Cells contain atoms, and light interacts with electronic states in biomolecules. Photon energy and the general concept of quantized electronic transitions are therefore foundations for UV–visible absorption, fluorescence and photobiology. But a protein or nucleic-acid base is a many-electron molecule, so its spectrum requires molecular quantum theory rather than the single-electron hydrogen energy formula.

1.A.1.07.24 Final revision map, key definitions and sources

Before leaving this node, organize the entire story into one connected explanation. Rutherford's scattering work provided a tiny positive nucleus. Classical electron orbits around that nucleus created an instability problem, and observed hydrogen spectral lines created a separate quantization problem. Bohr's 1913 model retained the nucleus and introduced allowed stationary states, quantized angular momentum and photon energies tied to transitions. The resulting formulas predicted important hydrogen wavelengths, but the theory remained incomplete for multi-electron atoms and the full quantum behavior of electrons.

1.A.1.07.24.1 The twelve most important things to retain

  1. Hydrogen produces characteristic emission and absorption spectral lines.
  2. Bohr retained Rutherford's compact positive nucleus.
  3. Bohr postulated stationary states without continuous radiation.
  4. Old-quantum circular angular momentum satisfies mₑvr = nħ.
  5. For a one-electron hydrogen-like ion, rₙ ≈ a₀n²/Z.
  6. Hydrogen-like energies are approximately Eₙ = −13.6Z²/n² eV.
  7. The negative sign represents binding relative to the E = 0 continuum.
  8. Emission and absorption exchange photons with energy |ΔE| = hc/λ.
  9. Lyman, Balmer and Paschen emissions end at n = 1, 2 and 3, respectively.
  10. Hydrogen ground-state ionization requires approximately 13.6 eV; first excitation requires 10.2 eV.
  11. The elementary model is useful for one-electron systems but not complete for neutral helium and other many-electron atoms.
  12. Modern quantum orbitals are probability-based states, not tiny measured planetary tracks.

1.A.1.07.24.2 Glossary: words you should explain without notes

Stationary state
An allowed electronic state that does not continuously radiate while occupied in Bohr's model; modern stationary states have fixed energy and stationary probability density.
Principal quantum number n
A positive integer labeling bound Bohr levels and, in modern hydrogenic theory, the principal electronic energy family.
Quantization
A restriction to particular allowed values of a physical quantity, such as energies of bound electronic states.
Angular momentum
A quantity describing rotational motion; Bohr postulated L = nħ for ideal circular orbits.
Bohr radius a₀
A fundamental atomic length scale near 0.529 Å; it is not the diameter of a literal electron ring in modern quantum mechanics.
Photon
A quantum of electromagnetic radiation with energy E = hν = hc/λ.
Excitation
Increasing an atom's internal energy, potentially to a higher bound electronic state.
Ionization
Removing a bound electron into the unbound continuum under specified physical conditions.
Rydberg equation
The spectral-wavelength relationship involving inverse squares of principal level numbers.
Spectral series
A family of atomic transitions with a common final level, such as Lyman or Balmer emission.
Hydrogen-like ion
A one-electron atomic species, such as H, He⁺ or Li²⁺.
Quantum orbital
A modern wavefunction-based electronic state rather than a definite classical trajectory.
