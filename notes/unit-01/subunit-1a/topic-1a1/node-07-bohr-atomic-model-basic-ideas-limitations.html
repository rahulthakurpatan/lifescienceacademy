Bohr’s Atomic Model: Basic Ideas and Limitations

Why the hydrogen atom has stable states, discrete energies and characteristic light — an evidence-led journey to the limits of the old quantum model.

Learning outcomes: Interpret emission and absorption lines; distinguish Bohr's postulates from derived predictions; calculate quantized radii and energies for hydrogen-like species; solve Rydberg and photon questions; identify model limitations; connect foundational spectroscopy to biological science.

Contents · 1.A.1.07

1. The two mysteries Bohr had to solve 2. Spectral evidence: what hydrogen actually shows 3. Why quantization entered atomic theory 4. Bohr's three postulates in plain English

1.A.1.07.1 The two mysteries Bohr had to solve

Imagine entering a physics laboratory in 1913. Hydrogen gas glows in a narrow glass tube. Its light is separated by a spectroscope, and a surprising pattern appears: sharp colored lines, with spaces between them. Why does hydrogen emit these particular wavelengths instead of all possible colors? Another problem is waiting. Rutherford's nucleus is supported by scattering, but an electron traveling in a classical curved orbit accelerates and should radiate energy. Why, then, do ordinary atoms persist?

1.A.1.07.1.1 The evidence was already real

Geiger and Marsden's scattering experiments helped establish that atoms contain a concentrated central charge. Independently, spectroscopists had observed hydrogen's distinctive emission lines. The difficult task was to build one atomic picture consistent with both the existence of a nucleus and the observed spectral pattern. A scientific model earns confidence by explaining observations; it cannot simply ignore one of them.

1.A.1.07.1.2 What Bohr chose to keep

Niels Bohr did not discard Rutherford's positively charged center. The electron was still attracted to it by the electric force. The innovative step concerned which electron states were permitted. Instead of allowing the electron to possess any classical orbit and continuously radiate, Bohr imposed special stable states with restricted energies. Radiation would be connected with transitions between these states.

1.A.1.07.1.3 The difference between an assumption and a prediction

Bohr's stationary-state rule was a postulate: it was not derived from the earlier classical theory of accelerating electric charges. But the model then led to testable predictions for the wavelengths of hydrogen's spectral lines. This distinction matters. A postulate is judged by the explanatory success and testable consequences of the theory it helps build, not by pretending that it was already a theorem of classical mechanics.

1.A.1.07.1.4 Our learning journey

In this node, we begin with measured spectra, explain the three familiar Bohr postulates, derive allowed radii and energies, calculate photon wavelengths, interpret the Lyman and Balmer series, and identify the model's limitations. We will also separate Bohr's historical circular orbits from modern quantum-mechanical orbitals. The goal is not to memorize formulas without context; it is to understand why each equation arose and what it can and cannot tell us.

Big question: How can the hydrogen atom remain stable yet emit discrete photon energies, when unrestricted classical electron orbits explain neither feature?

1.A.1.07.2 Spectral evidence: what hydrogen actually shows

Before looking at Bohr's theory, picture the observation that any convincing theory must explain. Put low-pressure atomic hydrogen in a discharge tube. Provide sufficient energy to excite the atoms, then send the emitted light through a prism or diffraction grating. A spectroscope separates different wavelengths, allowing us to see that some wavelengths are strongly represented while neighboring wavelengths may be absent.

1.A.1.07.2.1 Continuous light and line light are different

A broad continuous spectrum contains light across a range of wavelengths, like much of the rainbow produced by a white-light source. An atomic emission-line spectrum contains narrow features at characteristic wavelengths. In the visible hydrogen Balmer series, notable lines lie near 656 nm (red), 486 nm (blue-green), 434 nm and 410 nm (violet). The series also contains lines outside the most easily observed visible region. These wavelengths arise from distinct changes in the atom's electronic energy.

1.A.1.07.2.2 The screen tells us an observation, not an orbital photograph

A spectroscope reveals which wavelengths reach a detector. It does not directly show a tiny electron traveling from one painted ring to another. The connection to electronic energy states is a theoretical interpretation supported by the mathematical agreement between allowed energy differences and measured lines. This is a crucial difference between measuring a spectrum and drawing an atomic model.

1.A.1.07.2.3 Emission and absorption

In emission, an atom in an excited state can release a photon and enter a lower-energy state. In absorption, an atom with a suitable occupied initial state can take up energy and reach a higher state. The same energy differences are important to both processes, although actual strengths depend on populations, selection rules and experimental conditions. Saying every atom always absorbs every mathematically matching photon would ignore those qualifications.

1.A.1.07.2.4 What makes this evidence difficult for classical orbits?

In an unrestricted classical orbital picture, a bound electron could have a continuum of radii and energies. Continuous radiation during a changing orbit does not naturally yield the observed sharp list of characteristic wavelengths. A successful explanation must therefore explain why electron energies have specific permitted values and why emitted photons correspond to differences between them.

Evidence to remember: A hydrogen spectrum contains reproducible sharp lines. Their wavelengths must be explained quantitatively, not merely described as 'different colors'.

1.A.1.07.3 Why quantization entered atomic theory

Bohr's breakthrough was part of a larger change in physics. Long before the complete modern quantum theory existed, researchers had begun to discover that microscopic phenomena did not always fit the continuous assumptions of classical models. The idea of energy quanta appeared in Planck's treatment of thermal radiation, while Einstein's analysis of the photoelectric effect connected light energy to frequency.

1.A.1.07.3.1 Balmer and Rydberg found the patterns first

Johann Balmer described a mathematical relationship among hydrogen's visible spectral wavelengths in 1885. Other spectral series were organized through related Rydberg formulas. The striking feature was that simple positive integers could be used to describe measured wavelengths. These patterns were known before Bohr proposed his nuclear-atom theory in 1913. An explanation was needed for why those integers worked.

1.A.1.07.3.2 Energy carried by light

Planck's constant h connects photon energy E to frequency ν through E = hν. Because frequency and wavelength in vacuum satisfy c = λν, the equivalent expression is E = hc/λ. A larger photon-energy difference therefore corresponds to a higher frequency and shorter wavelength. This relationship becomes particularly useful once we know the energies of two allowed atomic states.

1.A.1.07.3.3 The staircase analogy, with its limits

Imagine a staircase rather than a ramp. A ramp lets you stand at essentially any intermediate height; the staircase offers a series of distinct steps. Bohr's model treats bound electronic energies like allowed steps. But an electron is not actually a person resting on an invisible stair, and the allowed energies are not equally spaced. The analogy is only intended to emphasize that every intermediate energy is not permitted.

1.A.1.07.3.4 What quantization does not mean

It does not mean every photon in nature has the same energy. It does not mean an atom has only two possible states. It means that a given bound one-electron system possesses particular permitted stationary states. Different transitions between those states produce different photon energies. The probability and intensity of a real optical transition require additional physics beyond simply writing its energy difference.

History check: Balmer and Rydberg described spectral patterns experimentally; Bohr supplied a 1913 atomic model that explained their hydrogen-state energy structure.

1.A.1.07.4 Bohr's three postulates in plain English

The familiar Bohr model can be organized around three related assumptions. These assumptions do different jobs, so it helps to learn them one at a time. Do not confuse a postulate with a formula derived later from it.

1.A.1.07.4.1 First: selected stable states

The model allows an electron to occupy certain circular states about the nucleus. While occupying one of these permitted stationary states, the electron does not continuously radiate electromagnetic energy. This was an explicit departure from the classical expectation for a point charge moving along a curved path. It addressed the problem that an unrestricted orbit would lose energy and fail to remain stable.

1.A.1.07.4.2 Second: angular momentum is restricted

For a circular Bohr orbit, the orbital angular momentum is restricted to integer multiples of the reduced Planck constant. This is written mₑvr = nħ = nh/(2π), where mₑ is electron mass, v its modeled orbital speed, r orbit radius and n is a positive integer. The condition does not follow from Coulomb's law alone; it is the extra restriction that selects a discrete set of classical-looking orbits.

me v r = nħ = nh/(2π), with n = 1, 2, 3, ...

1.A.1.07.4.3 Third: radiation accompanies transitions

If the atom changes between allowed electronic energies Eᵢ and E𝒇, the light exchanged has photon energy equal to the magnitude of the difference: hν = |Eᵢ − E𝒇|. A downward change can emit a photon; an upward change requires energy input, which may come from absorbing a photon. The photon's wavelength follows from λ = hc/|ΔE|, so a larger gap means a shorter wavelength.

Ephoton = |Ei − Ef| = hν = hc/λ

1.A.1.07.4.4 Why the postulates work together

Allowed states answer the stability question by assumption. The angular-momentum restriction determines which circular configurations the model admits. The photon rule connects differences among those states to observed spectral lines. Using one of these ideas in isolation would not reproduce the whole story; the model is a linked explanation.
