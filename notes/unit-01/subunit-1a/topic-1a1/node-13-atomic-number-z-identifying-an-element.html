1.A.1.13.1 The identity question: why six protons mean carbon

Imagine a researcher studying three tiny samples labeled carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14. Their masses differ; one is radioactive; one is especially useful as a stable isotope label. Nevertheless, all three are carbon. What common feature makes that statement scientifically justified? Each nucleus contains exactly six protons. That integer, the atomic number Z, identifies the chemical element. It does not depend on how many neutrons a particular isotope carries or how many electrons its atom has gained or lost.

1.A.1.13.1.1 Element identity is a nuclear fact

The modern definition of atomic number is the number of protons in the atomic nucleus. The symbol is capital Z, and for ordinary nuclei it is a nonnegative integer. Hydrogen has Z = 1, helium Z = 2, carbon Z = 6, nitrogen Z = 7 and oxygen Z = 8. Two nuclei with identical Z belong to the same element even when their neutron numbers, masses or radioactivity differ. If Z differs, the nuclei belong to different elements even if they happen to have equal mass numbers.

1.A.1.13.1.2 Why looking at electrons first can mislead

Neutral carbon normally has six electrons, but carbon can form charged species under appropriate conditions. The proton number remains six when electrons are removed or added in ordinary chemistry. A positive carbon ion does not become boron or nitrogen because it has gained or lost an electron. Electronic configuration and charge strongly affect reactivity, yet Z remains the nuclear identifier. For a neutral atom electron number equals Z; for an ion, that equality no longer holds.

1.A.1.13.1.3 What if the mass changes?

Add one neutron to a carbon-12 nucleus through a physically permitted nuclear process and retain it: the nucleus becomes carbon-13. Its mass number A increases from twelve to thirteen, but its atomic number remains six. In contrast, changing a nuclear proton into a neutron or vice versa can change Z and therefore transform the element, provided energy and conservation rules allow the process. This distinction underlies isotope analysis and nuclear transmutation.

1.A.1.13.1.4 One integer connects the periodic table with nuclei

The periodic table is organized in increasing atomic number, rather than increasing measured atomic weight. Chemical properties depend mainly on electrons, but the nuclear charge determined by Z influences electronic binding, energy levels and periodic organization. Thus a nucleus can determine element identity even though the electron distribution determines much of the element's ordinary chemistry. This is not a contradiction: the two descriptions concern different aspects of one atom.

1.A.1.13.1.5 Why atomic number matters to Life Science students

Biomolecules contain atoms such as carbon (6), nitrogen (7), oxygen (8), phosphorus (15) and sulfur (16), whose identity remains fixed as chemical reactions rearrange bonds. Stable isotope tracers can distinguish carbon-13 from carbon-12 while allowing carbon atoms to move through metabolic pathways. Ion channels distinguish species with different nuclear charges, electron counts, sizes and hydration environments. Understanding Z provides the common starting point for correct formula interpretation.

1.A.1.13.1.6 Our story will follow evidence, not only definitions

We will ask why scientists moved from atomic-weight order to nuclear charge, how Henry Moseley's X-ray experiments supported integer atomic numbers, and how the definition is used in notation and calculation. Then we will study isotopes, ions, nuclear transformations and biological measurements, with original diagrams and exam practice. No part of the lesson requires pretending that atoms are miniature solar systems whose exact electron tracks are known.

1.A.1.13.1.7 A question to carry forward

If a researcher receives an unknown ion containing eight electrons, can she identify the element from electron count alone? No. Oxygen as a neutral atom, fluorine as F⁺ and nitrogen as N⁻ can each be constructed as species with eight electrons in appropriate contexts, yet their nuclei have different Z. This simple ambiguity motivates the central rule: locate the proton number before naming the chemical element.

Fundamental rule: Atomic number Z = number of nuclear protons. It defines the element, whether the species is neutral, ionic, stable or radioactive.

1.A.1.13.2 The IUPAC definition: exactly what Z counts and what it does not

What does the phrase “atomic number” mean in a scientific definition rather than as an informal label on a periodic-table square? IUPAC's Gold Book defines atomic number as the number of protons in the atomic nucleus. That is the authoritative nucleus-based statement. The word number is a count of particles, not a measured mass, average atomic weight, electric charge in coulombs or ordinal position that can be assigned arbitrarily without physical meaning.

1.A.1.13.2.1 Counting protons, not adding masses

A nucleus has proton number Z and neutron number N. Its mass number A counts both kinds of nucleon, so A = Z + N. For nitrogen-15, Z = 7, N = 8 and A = 15. Only seven protons define nitrogen; eight neutrons distinguish the isotope. Actual nuclear mass differs from A multiplied by precisely one atomic mass unit because free nucleon masses and nuclear binding energy must be considered.

1.A.1.13.2.2 Atomic number and nuclear charge are related but not identical units

A proton carries +e, where elementary charge magnitude is exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C by the current SI definition. A nucleus with Z protons therefore has net electric charge +Ze, as long as its nuclear constituents are the ordinary protons and neutrons discussed here. Z is dimensionless, while nuclear charge Q_nucleus has units of coulombs. The atomic number six and nuclear charge +9.613059804 × 10⁻¹⁹ C describe related but dimensionally different quantities.

Z = Nprotons
Qnucleus = +Ze
A = Z + N

1.A.1.13.2.3 Why isotopes preserve Z

Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons; carbon-13 has six protons and seven neutrons; carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons. Their mass numbers differ but Z remains six. An isotope is therefore not a different element. Isotopes may differ in measured mass, nuclear stability, scattering behavior and sometimes reaction rates because of mass-dependent effects, despite sharing the nuclear positive-charge number.

1.A.1.13.2.4 Why ions preserve Z in chemical changes

A magnesium atom can lose two electrons to become Mg²⁺ without changing its twelve nuclear protons. The resulting charge of the whole ion is +2e, but nuclear charge remains +12e. If a question says “the atom has a charge of +2,” do not set Z = 2. Atomic number is not an ionic charge state and should not be inferred solely from the number of electrons in a charged species.

1.A.1.13.2.5 What the periodic table actually labels

Each square names an element and its proton-number identity. A listed atomic weight usually reflects the isotopic composition of a terrestrial sample, rather than the exact mass of one nucleus. A square may also show electron configuration, symbol and name. The atomic number is an integer even when the displayed standard atomic weight is not. Chemical symbols are labels whose identity is determined by proton number, not by a rounded mass.

1.A.1.13.2.6 A subtle distinction concerning atoms and nuclei

By convention the atomic number labels an element and its nuclei whether the atom is neutral, positive or negative, and even when a particular nuclear species is isolated. For most chemical discussions, a neutral atom has Z electrons, which makes it tempting to define Z as electron count. That shortcut fails immediately for ions. The proper definition starts in the nucleus and the electron count follows only after specifying net charge.

1.A.1.13.2.7 Why exact counts should not imply exact measurements of every property

The proton number of an identified nuclide is an exact integer. Its nuclear mass, atomic radius and transition energies are measured or model-dependent quantities that can have uncertainties. A precise scientific answer does not confuse an exact count with a claim of infinite experimental precision about size or binding energy. This distinction also explains why numbers like 1.00866 u for a neutron are not new definitions of atomic number.

1.A.1.13.3 Before atomic number: Mendeleev, masses and the ordering puzzle

Imagine arranging known elements in the nineteenth century without access to modern nuclear theory. Chemists could measure atomic weights and chemical similarities, so arranging elements approximately in increasing atomic weight was a natural strategy. Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic work recognized repeating chemical patterns, sometimes leaving spaces for undiscovered elements. Yet some pairs made the mass-order rule awkward. The later concept of atomic number provided a more fundamental ordering principle.

1.A.1.13.3.1 Why atomic weight once seemed attractive

Atomic weights were measurable from chemical composition and stoichiometry long before scientists could describe nuclei using protons and neutrons. Similarities in valence and compound formation helped group elements. Mendeleev's achievement was not simply putting symbols in a row; he used periodic chemical behavior to organize and predict relationships. The system worked remarkably well despite imperfect knowledge of subatomic structure.

1.A.1.13.3.2 The tellurium–iodine inversion

Tellurium has a standard atomic weight around 127.6, while iodine's is near 126.9. Yet tellurium belongs with chalcogens and iodine with halogens. Increasing atomic-weight order would appear to demand the opposite sequence. Modern atomic numbers resolve the ordering: tellurium is Z = 52 and iodine Z = 53, so iodine correctly follows tellurium despite its lower average atomic weight. Chemical identity follows proton count rather than the numerical order of isotope-weighted average masses.

1.A.1.13.3.3 The cobalt–nickel example

Cobalt has atomic number 27 and nickel 28. Their standard atomic weights are roughly 58.933 and 58.693 respectively, placing the higher-Z nickel slightly lower in average atomic weight. The chemical periodic order makes sense when based on Z. The apparent anomaly is no longer an exception demanding rearrangement; it shows why average mass is not the fundamental identity variable.

1.A.1.13.3.4 How isotopic composition complicates average atomic weight

Different isotopes of an element have the same Z but different masses. Natural isotopic abundances contribute to a weighted average atomic weight displayed on reference periodic tables. Consequently an element's listed standard atomic weight can be fractional and can vary within established intervals for certain elements because natural isotope ratios differ. Atomic number does not fluctuate with the sample's isotopic abundance.

1.A.1.13.3.5 Van den Broek's hypothesis before the decisive X-ray evidence

In 1913 Antonius van den Broek proposed a relationship between an element's serial order and nuclear positive charge. The idea pointed toward an integer property that increased by one between successive elements. A hypothesis, however, needed convincing measurements. Henry Moseley's subsequent characteristic X-ray studies supplied powerful evidence connecting element identity to a regularly increasing nuclear-charge-related integer.

1.A.1.13.3.6 Why Moseley's finding did not erase Mendeleev's contribution

Mendeleev recognized periodic chemical patterns and predictive gaps through empirical chemistry. Moseley's work gave a physical basis for a more exact organizing number. Both achievements matter: chemical regularities exposed the order, while X-ray spectra provided evidence for its underlying nuclear variable. The modern periodic table unites these historical approaches rather than treating one scientist's work as having instantly replaced all previous understanding.

1.A.1.13.3.7 What the early atomic number was not

It was not merely the number of letters in an element's name, the atom's measured mass rounded to an integer or an arbitrary textbook serial label. Its physical meaning emerged through the relation between nuclear charge and characteristic spectra. Today Z is defined directly as proton number. The historical pathway helps explain why this integer rather than atomic weight became the central organizing principle.

1.A.1.13.3.8 A useful historical examination strategy

If asked why the periodic table uses atomic number, refer to element identity by nuclear proton count, the regular succession of characteristic X-ray spectra and the resolution of mass-order anomalies. If asked who proposed periodic patterns, discuss Mendeleev. If asked whose 1913 X-ray experiments established the nuclear-charge sequence, identify Henry Moseley. This distinguishes discovery stages without oversimplification.

1.A.1.13.4 Henry Moseley's 1913 experiment: finding an integer in X-ray spectra

Imagine examining an X-ray spectrum from one metallic element, then repeating the measurement with the next element in the periodic sequence. The characteristic emission lines shift systematically. Henry Moseley investigated these relationships in 1913–1914 and found that the square root of characteristic X-ray frequency showed a remarkably simple pattern with the element's atomic number. This supported the idea that a fundamental integer related to nuclear positive charge determines element identity.

1.A.1.13.4.1 Why characteristic X-rays are produced

When an inner electronic vacancy is created in an atom through an appropriate energetic process, an electron from a higher-energy state can fill it. The energy difference may be emitted as a characteristic X-ray photon. Its frequency depends on the allowed electron energy difference and therefore on the effective nuclear attraction experienced by those inner electrons. Different elements have different nuclear charges and characteristic line energies.

1.A.1.13.4.2 How Moseley compared a sequence of elements

Moseley used X-ray spectroscopy techniques to examine characteristic spectra from multiple elements. The measured wavelengths or frequencies varied regularly with position in the periodic ordering. By assigning consecutive integers to elements, he found a strong linear relationship in a square-root-of-frequency representation for corresponding line families. It was not simply a visual impression that one sample glowed more brightly than another.

1.A.1.13.4.3 Why an X-ray spectrum can reveal nuclear charge indirectly

Inner-electron binding energies depend strongly on nuclear charge, with screening by other electrons. Increasing Z generally increases relevant characteristic X-ray energies in a systematic way. A simplified model gives √ν approximately proportional to Z minus a screening term, for a fixed spectral series. Real spectra require quantum and many-electron considerations, but the approximate relationship made the integer progression measurable.

1.A.1.13.4.4 The central conclusion was deeper than mass ordering

Moseley's result showed that neighboring elements are characterized by a fundamental quantity increasing in regular steps, consistent with units of nuclear positive charge. It supported van den Broek's identification of periodic serial number with nuclear charge and resolved ambiguities of atomic-weight ordering. The present definition Z = number of protons emerged within the developing proton–neutron picture of nuclear structure.

1.A.1.13.4.5 Why a given spectral line must be compared with the same kind

Atoms emit multiple line families and transitions. Moseley-type comparisons use corresponding characteristic lines rather than arbitrary peaks. Different transitions have different constants and screening behavior. If one compares unrelated transitions from different elements, a simple line through √ν versus Z may fail for reasons unrelated to element identity. This matters when applying the formula in numerical questions.

1.A.1.13.4.6 Historical precision and modern correction

The usual classroom expression of Moseley's law is a simplified screening model. It is powerful for explaining the trend but not an exact expression for every X-ray line across every element. Relativistic, spin–orbit and many-electron effects modify real line energies. An accurate lesson celebrates the discovery without claiming the simplified law predicts all experimental frequencies with zero error.

1.A.1.13.4.7 A scientist's reasoning in one paragraph

Moseley measured characteristic X-ray lines across elements, found a regular integer progression in the relationship between frequency and ordering, and connected that sequence to nuclear charge rather than average atomic mass. Modern chemistry identifies that integer with nuclear proton count Z. The distinction between recorded photon frequencies and inferred nuclear properties is what makes his work an excellent example of evidence-based inference.

1.A.1.13.5 Moseley's law: derive the trend without claiming an exact formula

Moseley's work is famous for the relationship between characteristic X-ray frequency and atomic number, but the physical reasoning deserves more than a single equation. Imagine increasing the number of protons in a nucleus while observing corresponding inner-electron transitions. The electrostatic attraction felt by inner electrons generally strengthens, raising the relevant binding energies and characteristic photon frequencies. This is the basic reason an experimentally measured spectrum can reveal the otherwise invisible ordering of nuclear charge.

1.A.1.13.5.1 Begin with characteristic transitions

An inner-shell vacancy can be filled by an electron from a higher-energy shell, releasing energy through an X-ray photon in an allowed radiative transition. For a simple Kα transition, an electron moves from an L-like state to a K-like inner state, roughly corresponding to n = 2 → n = 1 in a hydrogenic analogy. The photon energy is the difference between the initial and final electronic energies. Real atoms have electron–electron interactions, so this analogy is approximate.

1.A.1.13.5.2 Why a squared effective charge enters

In a hydrogen-like approximation, electronic binding energies scale with the square of the effective nuclear charge. Screening by other electrons means a given inner electron does not always experience the full bare charge Ze as if it were alone. Represent the screened effective nuclear charge by Z − σ. For the idealized Kα 2 → 1 transition, the Bohr-like energy difference is approximately (3/4)hcR∞(Z − σ)², where R∞ is the Rydberg constant and σ is an approximate screening factor.

ν ≈ (3/4)cR∞(Z − σ)² (simplified Kα-like relation)
√ν ≈ √[(3/4)cR∞] (Z − σ)

1.A.1.13.5.3 Why plotting square root of frequency matters

The approximate equation means that √ν versus Z is nearly linear for a corresponding family of X-ray lines over a suitable range. The vertical axis is the square root of frequency, not frequency itself. The horizontal axis is atomic number, an integer. A measured line trend can therefore support the existence of a smoothly ordered integer quantity rather than a sequence defined by average atomic weights.

Figure 1.A.1.13-F2. Original schematic Moseley-law trend. Dots are illustrative, not digitized historical data. The simplified law applies to comparable characteristic lines with screening approximations.

1.A.1.13.5.4 The familiar screening factor one is only a first approximation

Many school treatments use σ ≈ 1 for simple Kα estimates, giving ν ≈ (3/4)cR∞(Z − 1)². That is a useful trend formula for suitable heavier atoms, not an exact universal law. Screening is neither identical for every transition nor independent of details of the atomic electronic structure. Low-Z atoms, relativistic effects and spin–orbit splitting can require more sophisticated models.

1.A.1.13.5.5 Derive a safe relative-frequency relationship

If two elements have corresponding lines governed by approximately the same coefficient and screening parameter, ν₂/ν₁ ≈ [(Z₂ − σ)/(Z₁ − σ)]². For illustrative atomic numbers 20 and 30 with σ = 1, this yields (29/19)² ≈ 2.33. The result estimates a line-frequency ratio under the simplified assumptions; it is not an independently measured spectrum for those elements.

1.A.1.13.5.6 Why photon wavelength changes in the opposite direction

Because ν = c/λ in vacuum, higher characteristic frequency means shorter photon wavelength. The same line family will generally shift to higher photon energy and smaller wavelength as nuclear charge increases. A question that assumes frequency and wavelength rise together is incorrect. In spectroscopy, always identify whether the measured horizontal axis is energy, frequency or wavelength before interpreting the trend.

1.A.1.13.5.7 Why modern spectra contain more detail

Real characteristic spectra include multiple lines, finite widths, chemical shifts and effects of electronic structure. The approximate Moseley law captures a major trend but not every feature. Its historical power lay in connecting a systematic measurable progression to element identity and helping establish the atomic-number ordering of the periodic table. Knowing its assumptions is as important as knowing the square-root relationship.

1.A.1.13.6 Nuclear charge +Ze: separate an exact count from an electric field

Once atomic number Z is known, the total positive nuclear charge follows immediately. Each proton carries exactly one elementary charge magnitude +e, whereas neutrons have zero net charge. Thus a nucleus of oxygen-16 has Z = 8 and charge +8e, regardless of its eight neutrons. A nucleus of oxygen-18 also has charge +8e despite having ten neutrons. This simple relationship is the electrical meaning behind atomic number, but it must not be confused with an atom's net ionic charge.

1.A.1.13.6.1 The exact numerical relation

Under present SI definitions e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C exactly. Hence Q_nucleus = +Ze. For carbon Z = 6, nuclear charge is +9.613059804 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. For calcium Z = 20, nuclear charge is +3.204353268 × 10⁻¹⁸ C. These products use an exact elementary-charge conversion for an integer count; actual measurements of nuclear structure or radius involve different physical quantities and uncertainty.

Qnucleus = +Ze
For calcium (Z=20): Q = +3.204353268 × 10⁻¹⁸ C

1.A.1.13.6.2 The whole atom may still be neutral

A neutral calcium atom has twenty electrons contributing total negative charge −20e, so its net charge is zero even though the nucleus has +20e. Calcium ions Ca²⁺ have eighteen electrons, net charge +2e, and the same +20e nucleus. If the question asks nuclear charge, answer +20e; if it asks net ionic charge, answer +2e. Those two values describe different parts or wholes of the same species.

1.A.1.13.6.3 What nuclear charge does to electronic states

A more positively charged nucleus tends to bind electrons more strongly when other factors are held comparable. But real atoms contain multiple electrons that screen one another, and the spatial extent, subshell energies and electron correlations influence the result. A periodic trend cannot always be derived by comparing bare Z alone. The concept of effective nuclear charge helps account for screening in atomic-structure approximations.

1.A.1.13.6.4 Coulomb's law for a simplified point nucleus

Outside a spherically symmetric nuclear charge distribution, the ideal electrostatic field behaves as though the charge +Ze were concentrated at the center. The force magnitude between an electron and a simplified point nucleus separated by r is kZe²/r², attractive. Here k = 1/(4πε₀). This relation helps build hydrogen-like atomic models, but it omits screening from other electrons and the complexities of quantum states.

|Felectron–nucleus| ≈ kZe²/r² (ideal bare point nucleus)
k = 1/(4πε₀)

1.A.1.13.6.5 Why the nucleus is not a mathematical point in all experiments

Protons and nuclear charge occupy a finite spatial distribution, important at sufficiently small distances in high-energy scattering and nuclear studies. A simple point-charge field is an approximation useful outside the nucleus and in introductory atomic calculations. It does not imply electrons follow exact classical orbits or that nuclear sizes are determined solely by Z.

1.A.1.13.6.6 Proton number and electron count generate neutral charge balance

Let nₑ be the number of electrons in an isolated atomic or ionic species. Then Q_net = (Z − nₑ)e. The same Z can correspond to several ionic charge states, and different nuclei can sometimes produce isoelectronic species with equal nₑ. The charge arithmetic requires both numbers; atomic number alone identifies the element, while electron count determines whether that element's species is neutral or ionic.

1.A.1.13.6.7 Exam distinctions worth writing explicitly

A neutral fluorine atom with Z = 9 has nuclear charge +9e, electronic charge −9e and net charge zero. F⁻ has nuclear charge +9e, electronic charge −10e and net charge −e. The nuclear charge never becomes +8e merely because fluorine captured an electron. If the nucleus instead changed to Z = 8 through a permitted nuclear transformation, the new element would be oxygen.

1.A.1.13.7 Reading nuclide symbols: A, Z, N, charge and element identity

Imagine seeing the notation ³¹₁₅P³⁻ on a page of examination questions. The upper-left number 31, lower-left number 15 and upper-right 3− each answer a different question. If those numbers are treated as interchangeable, the result is confusion over protons, electrons and neutrons. A systematic reading method can extract the entire nuclear identity and ionic charge without any guesswork.

1.A.1.13.7.1 The four parts of a nuclide symbol

In the conventional representation ᴬ_ZX, X is the chemical symbol of the element, A is the mass number and Z is the atomic number. An optional superscript on the right indicates ionic charge. The neutron count is N = A − Z. If the species is neutral, its electron count equals Z. If it has net charge +ke, electron count is Z − k; for charge −ke, electron count is Z + k.

ᴬZXq: proton count Z; neutron count A − Z
Electron count = Z − q/e (q is the signed species charge)

1.A.1.13.7.2 Read phosphorus-31 step by step

For ³¹₁₅P³⁻, Z = 15 protons. N = 31 − 15 = 16 neutrons. Charge −3e means the species has three more electrons than protons, giving eighteen electrons. Its nucleus remains phosphorus. A neutral phosphorus-31 atom would have fifteen electrons, while the phosphide ion has eighteen. Neither version changes the isotope's proton or neutron counts.

1.A.1.13.7.3 Read sulfate's atoms separately from the molecular charge

Sulfate SO₄²⁻ is a polyatomic ion, not a single ⁴S atom carrying an isotope superscript. Sulfur has Z = 16, oxygen Z = 8, and the ion has net −2e as a whole. The ionic charge gives the total electronic excess relative to the nuclear proton sum, but electrons in covalent bonds need not be uniquely assigned to isolated atom-shaped containers. Nuclear atomic numbers remain those of sulfur and oxygen.

1.A.1.13.7.4 A comparison with chlorine-37

For ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻, chlorine has seventeen protons and twenty neutrons. Its chloride ion has eighteen electrons. For ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻, it still has seventeen protons and eighteen electrons but only eighteen neutrons. The difference between these species is isotope mass, not ionic charge or chemical element. The same reasoning works for many stable isotope chemistry problems.

1.A.1.13.7.5 When A is absent, what can you infer?

Writing 'Mg²⁺' tells us the element (Z = 12) and electron count (ten), but it does not specify an isotope or neutron count. Magnesium has multiple isotopes, so N cannot be determined uniquely without A or additional isotopic information. A typical exam distractor treats the rounded standard atomic weight as though it must be the exact mass number of an unspecified atom.

1.A.1.13.7.6 Nuclide, isotope and element are not synonyms

A nuclide refers to a particular nuclear composition, including Z and N. Isotopes are nuclides of the same element with different neutron numbers. An element consists of atoms having a given proton number, which may include different isotopes. Ionic charge is a further electronic property of a given atomic or molecular species. Precise vocabulary helps avoid scientific errors while reading mass spectra or isotope labels.

1.A.1.13.7.7 A reliable solution sequence

Identify X and its Z; read or determine A if given; compute neutrons with A − Z; then evaluate ionic charge to count electrons. If one of the needed numbers is missing, explain what cannot be determined rather than inventing a mass number. Showing the sequence in a written answer demonstrates understanding beyond memorizing a table.

1.A.1.13.8 Same Z, different neutrons: isotopes as an identity test

Imagine an experiment comparing two glucose molecules. In one, a particular carbon atom is carbon-12; in the other it is carbon-13. A mass spectrometer may distinguish them under suitable measurement conditions, but both are built from the element carbon. The isotope label alters the nuclear neutron count and mass signature while preserving nuclear proton number Z = 6. This is the clearest demonstration that atomic number is an identity property and not a synonym for atomic mass.

1.A.1.13.8.1 Carbon isotopes reveal the rule

Carbon-12 contains six protons and six neutrons, carbon-13 six protons and seven neutrons, and carbon-14 six protons and eight neutrons. All carry nuclear charge +6e. If neutral, they each have six electrons. Carbon-14 is radioactive and beta-decays to nitrogen-14 under the appropriate nuclear process, but being radioactive does not make it a different element before that transformation.

1.A.1.13.8.2 Hydrogen isotopes show the exception to 'every nucleus has neutrons'

Protium ¹H has one proton and zero neutrons, deuterium ²H one proton and one neutron, and tritium ³H one proton and two neutrons. Their Z remains one, so all are hydrogen isotopes. Neutral atoms each have one electron. Tritium is radioactive while protium and deuterium are stable under normal circumstances. The neutron count affects nuclear stability without changing the element name until a nuclear transformation changes Z.

1.A.1.13.8.3 Same atomic number, similar chemistry—with qualifications

Isotopes have the same nuclear charge and typically very similar electronic structure, giving broadly similar chemistry. But mass differences change vibrations, diffusion and sometimes reaction rates. Hydrogen-to-deuterium substitution can produce pronounced isotope effects. A careful Life Science answer should not claim perfect chemical identity in every kinetic measurement merely because Z remains fixed.

1.A.1.13.8.4 Atomic weight depends on a mixture of isotopes

Many samples contain more than one stable isotope. The displayed atomic weight is a weighted average of the masses of constituent atoms relative to the specified isotope distribution. For an element with two isotopes, a simplified average is x·m₁ + (1 − x)·m₂, using fractional abundances. That result can be non-integer even though every atom has an exact integer Z and A.

1.A.1.13.8.5 Why isotope labeling is so useful in biology

Metabolic tracing can follow ¹³C-labeled substrates through biochemical pathways while ordinary chemical transformations retain the carbon nuclei. ¹⁵N labels help investigate nitrogen metabolism or macromolecular structure. A label's presence does not change carbon into a new element; it changes which carbon nuclide is detected. Appropriate mass spectrometry, NMR or other analytical techniques then read the isotope-specific signal.

1.A.1.13.8.6 Radioactivity depends on nuclear energy, not merely a 'large Z'

Different isotopes of the same element may be stable or radioactive. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive. The difference is connected to nuclear energy and allowed decay pathways; Z alone does not specify whether every isotope of an element is stable. That is why isotope identity requires both proton and neutron information for many nuclear questions.

1.A.1.13.8.7 Exam-safe rule for isotope questions

If the element symbol and Z are fixed while A changes, the species are isotopes. If Z changes, the element changes. If only electron count changes, the ionization state changes. Keep these outcomes distinct and a broad range of atom-identity problems become straightforward.
