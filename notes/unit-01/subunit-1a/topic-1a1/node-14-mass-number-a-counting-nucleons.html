1.A.1.14.1 The number on a nucleus: what are we really counting?

Imagine two samples, both labeled carbon. One contains carbon-12 and the other carbon-13. Their atoms contain nuclei with the same six protons, which makes both samples carbon, yet the carbon-13 nucleus contains one additional neutron. Scientists give these two nuclei different mass numbers: twelve and thirteen. What does that number measure? Despite its name, mass number is not a weighing-machine reading. It is a count of the protons and neutrons—the nucleons—inside a particular nucleus.

1.A.1.14.1.1 Begin with a simple inventory

For carbon-12, six protons plus six neutrons give twelve nucleons. For carbon-13, six protons plus seven neutrons give thirteen. Nothing about adding an electron to make a negative carbon ion would change that nuclear count. Conversely, adding one neutron through a permitted nuclear process would alter the isotope's mass number even if the electronic charge state were restored to neutrality. Separating these operations is the key to understanding the word mass in this context.

1.A.1.14.1.2 A is an integer with no kilogram unit

The usual symbol for mass number is capital A. Its value is a nonnegative integer for a nucleus, and the important ordinary examples are positive integers. Carbon-12 has A = 12; nitrogen-15 has A = 15; iron-56 has A = 56. The number does not come with kg, grams or atomic mass units. It can be compared with a mass only approximately because each nucleon has a characteristic rest mass and nuclear binding modifies total system energy.

1.A.1.14.1.3 Why there are two nuclear counts

Atomic number Z counts protons and determines which chemical element the nucleus belongs to. Neutron number N counts neutrons and distinguishes isotopes at fixed Z. Mass number combines both: A = Z + N. Thus for a nucleus with Z = 8 and N = 10, A = 18 and the element is oxygen. If Z changes to nine while A remains eighteen, the nucleus belongs to fluorine, not oxygen. Different arrangements of proton and neutron counts can give an equal A.

1.A.1.14.1.4 Where are the electrons?

Electrons form quantum states associated with the nucleus in atoms and molecules, and their number can change during ionization and electron-transfer chemistry. Mass number counts none of these electrons. A neutral oxygen-18 atom contains eight electrons, while an oxide ion of the same oxygen-18 nuclide can have ten. Both retain A = 18 because the nucleus still has eight protons and ten neutrons. The electron's small rest mass matters to high-precision atom mass but not to A.

1.A.1.14.1.5 Why the idea supports Life Science research

Stable ¹³C and ¹⁵N isotope labels are useful because their mass numbers help distinguish isotopes while the nuclear proton count preserves the element's chemical identity. A labeled atom can follow a metabolic pathway without every ordinary chemical step changing the count of nucleons in its nucleus. Deuterium, ²H, adds a neutron compared with protium, ¹H, and can affect vibrational and reaction properties while remaining hydrogen. These examples make A more than an exam definition.

1.A.1.14.1.6 The question to keep asking

If a problem presents an atom, ion, isotope or nuclear reaction, first locate its nucleus conceptually. Count protons and neutrons; do not add electrons, charge units, photons or molecular subscripts. Next decide whether the nucleus changes during the specified process. Only then assign A or compare its before-and-after value. This simple sequence prevents confusion that even advanced students sometimes experience when multiple superscripts appear in one equation.

We will move from the IUPAC definition to notation, isotope families, isobars and nuclear structure. Then we will show why the measured mass of a nucleus does not equal the integer A in u, and how mass defect relates to binding energy. Central principle: Mass number A is the exact count of nuclear nucleons. For ordinary nuclides, A = proton count Z + neutron count N. It is not a measured mass.

1.A.1.14.2 IUPAC definition and the compact equation A = Z + N

Scientists sometimes use one letter for a physical count and a similar phrase for a measured quantity. Mass number is a classic example. The IUPAC Gold Book defines A as the total number of protons and neutrons, jointly called nucleons, in an atomic nucleus. This wording is more precise than saying that A is the mass of an atom. It names the objects counted, their location and the fact that the result is a discrete particle number.

1.A.1.14.2.1 Unpack all three symbols

Let Z be nuclear proton count, N nuclear neutron count and A mass number. The defining relationship is A = Z + N. Rearranging gives N = A − Z and Z = A − N. Every symbol is a dimensionless integer count in these equations. No factor of Avogadro's constant, elementary charge or atomic mass unit is needed to add the counts. Those constants describe other operations involving amount of substance, charge or mass.

A = Z + N
N = A − Z; Z = A − N
A, Z and N are counts, not masses in kg or u

1.A.1.14.2.2 Hydrogen makes the simplest test case

Ordinary protium ¹H has one proton and no neutron. Thus Z = 1, N = 0 and A = 1. Deuterium ²H has one proton and one neutron, so A = 2. Tritium ³H has one proton and two neutrons, so A = 3. All three are hydrogen because Z remains one. This example also disproves the absolute claim that every ordinary nucleus must contain both protons and neutrons.

1.A.1.14.2.3 Use a nucleus with many neutrons

For ²⁰⁸Pb, lead has Z = 82 and mass number A = 208. Therefore N = 208 − 82 = 126 neutrons. The atom may be neutral or ionic; neither condition changes its nuclear nucleon count. The arithmetic does not by itself prove which nuclear excited states are stable or the exact spatial distribution of its nucleons. Those properties need nuclear structure theory and data.

1.A.1.14.2.4 What does not belong in A?

Bound electrons are excluded. Photons, surrounding solvent molecules and free particles passing near the nucleus are excluded. In particle-physics language, proton and neutron are baryons, and a nucleus's baryon number in ordinary nuclear modeling corresponds to the total number of nucleons; we should not generalize the introductory count to every exotic strongly interacting system without specifying what is being studied. For common atoms, however, the nuclear proton-plus-neutron rule is direct and unambiguous.

1.A.1.14.2.5 What “nucleon” means physically

Protons and neutrons have comparable but unequal rest masses and each has spin one-half. They are composite particles built from quarks and gluon fields, yet nuclear mass number counts the nucleons as effective constituents, not the individual quarks inside them. Counting six proton constituents and six neutron constituents gives A = 12 for carbon-12, not thirty-six valence quarks or some number of gluons. Different levels of physical description answer different questions.

1.A.1.14.2.6 Why an integer count is different from an average

Every individual chlorine-35 nucleus contains exactly thirty-five nucleons. A natural sample, however, may contain both chlorine-35 and chlorine-37, producing an average atomic weight around 35.45 in the appropriate reference context. There is no single nucleus with 35.45 nucleons. An average over many atoms is useful for laboratory mass calculations; it is not an alternative nuclear mass number.

1.A.1.14.2.7 One formal limitation to remember

The IUPAC definition refers to the atomic nucleus. A neutral atom's mass is usually close to A unified atomic mass units because protons and neutrons each weigh about one u, but exact equality is false. The words close to and counts are not decoration; they mark the conceptual difference between discrete nuclear composition and measured atomic mass, which becomes crucial in binding-energy and isotope analyses.

1.A.1.14.3 Build the nuclide symbol rather than guessing its numbers

Imagine receiving a laboratory label written as ⁵⁷₂₆Fe³⁺. Four pieces of information are compressed into one expression: the element symbol Fe, atomic number 26, mass number 57 and ionic charge +3. Good exam reasoning does not read all the superscripts as one kind of number. Instead, it decodes the nuclear count and electronic charge independently, preserving what each numeral means physically.

1.A.1.14.3.1 Where A and Z sit in the symbol

The general nuclide format is ᴬ_ZX, with A at upper left of element symbol X and Z at lower left. The integer Z identifies the chemical element; A counts its nuclear nucleons. Thus ⁵⁷₂₆Fe has 26 protons and 57 − 26 = 31 neutrons. Because the element symbol already determines Z, scientific writing may abbreviate the isotope as iron-57 or ⁵⁷Fe when the context is unambiguous.

1.A.1.14.3.2 How charge appears without changing A

An optional upper-right superscript shows ionic charge, as in ⁵⁷Fe³⁺. Its nucleus still has fifty-seven nucleons and twenty-six protons. A simple isolated ion with charge +3e has twenty-three electrons, but those are not counted inside A. A neutral iron-57 atom would have twenty-six electrons and the same nuclear composition. Changing the charge superscript alone does not produce another isotope.

1.A.1.14.3.3 Work through an anion example

For ³⁷₁₇Cl⁻, Z = 17 tells us there are seventeen protons. A = 37 gives total nucleons, so neutron count is twenty. Charge −e means the species has eighteen electrons. An incorrect answer of thirty-seven electrons confuses mass number with electronic population; an answer of eighteen protons confuses ionic charge with element identity.

1.A.1.14.3.4 What if the problem omits A?

A symbol such as Ca²⁺ identifies calcium with Z = 20 and, for an ordinary monatomic calcium ion, eighteen electrons. It does not by itself reveal which calcium isotope is present, so its neutron count and mass number cannot be uniquely determined. Using a rounded periodic-table atomic weight to invent A would be an unjustified assumption unless the problem explicitly supplies the isotope or asks for an approximation based on stated data.

1.A.1.14.3.5 Difference between isotope, nuclide and ion labels

Nuclide language specifies nuclear composition (and may include nuclear energy state where relevant). Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts. Ions differ in electronic charge. Therefore ⁵⁶Fe²⁺ and ⁵⁷Fe²⁺ are different isotopes in the same ionic state, whereas ⁵⁶Fe²⁺ and ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ have the same nuclide but different electronic charge states. This two-dimensional distinction is central to spectroscopy and isotope-labeled chemistry.

1.A.1.14.3.6 A subtle difference in chemical formulas

In H₂O, the subscript 2 means two hydrogen atoms, not mass number two. If isotopic notation explicitly writes D₂O, D denotes deuterium ²H, and each deuterium nucleus has A = 2. Chemical formula subscripts count atoms, nuclide superscripts count nucleons within a nucleus, and ionic superscripts specify net charge. They can appear together in one expression while describing different aspects of the sample.

1.A.1.14.3.7 Exam method

Read the element symbol to establish Z; read the upper-left A; compute N = A − Z; only then use the charge superscript to find electrons if asked. If A is missing, identify the limitation. This sequence is fast, general and scientifically rigorous, and it prevents a correct-looking but unjustified guess about isotope identity.

1.A.1.14.4 A, Z and N: why the same mass number can label different elements

Imagine two nuclides that each contain fourteen nucleons: carbon-14 and nitrogen-14. Their mass numbers are both A = 14, but the first has six protons and eight neutrons, whereas the second has seven protons and seven neutrons. They are different chemical elements because Z differs. Equal A is therefore not a guarantee of equal chemical identity, measured atomic mass or stability. This comparison introduces isobars and reveals why atomic number and mass number must be kept separate.

1.A.1.14.4.1 Isotopes keep Z fixed and change A

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 both have Z = 6 but A = 12 and 13. They are isotopes of carbon. Atoms of these isotopes, when neutral, have the same electron count and broadly similar electronic chemistry, though mass-dependent isotope effects can be significant in some reactions. Their different A arises from differing neutron numbers. The defining property of isotope membership is shared proton count.

1.A.1.14.4.2 Isobars keep A fixed and change Z

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 both have A = 14, making them isobars, while their proton and neutron counts differ. Because their nuclear charges are different, their neutral atoms also have different electronic structures and chemistry. An isobar pair does not mean two elements have exactly equal measured atomic masses; binding energies and electron contributions differ. Equal mass number is an equality of integer nuclear counts.

1.A.1.14.4.3 Isotones keep neutron number fixed

Carbon-13 has N = 7 because 13 − 6 = 7. Nitrogen-14 has N = 7 because 14 − 7 = 7. They are isotones. Their A and Z differ, but neutron count is shared. The three words isotope, isobar and isotone describe three different ways nuclear counts can be held constant. Drawing a small Z–N table can resolve questions faster than memorizing word endings alone.

1.A.1.14.4.4 Isoelectronic is a separate electronic label

Na⁺ has ten electrons and neutral neon also has ten, making them isoelectronic in the usual introductory sense. That tells us nothing directly about equality of Z, N or A. Sodium has Z = 11 while neon Z = 10, and isotope numbers would still need to be specified. The term isoelectronic belongs to electronic structure, not to nuclear nucleon counting.

1.A.1.14.4.5 Why isobars can be related through beta decay

In a beta-minus nuclear process, a neutron can transform into a proton while an electron and antineutrino are emitted. The daughter nucleus has Z increased by one and N decreased by one, keeping A unchanged. Carbon-14 decaying to nitrogen-14 is an example. The parent and daughter are isobars, not isotopes; the process changes the chemical element even though total nucleon count remains fourteen.

1.A.1.14.4.6 A repeated trap in exams

Students sometimes call carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 isotopes because the number fourteen appears in both names. The correct comparison begins with Z rather than the spoken ending. Carbon and nitrogen have different Z, so these cannot be isotopes of one element. They are isobars because A matches. This is why Z determines the element, while A organizes nuclear counts within and across different elements.

1.A.1.14.4.7 Why biology researchers need the distinction

Stable isotope labels use nuclides such as ¹³C and ¹⁵N to follow element-specific atoms. Radioactive carbon-14 changes into nitrogen-14 through nuclear decay, an isobaric daughter relationship. Ordinary metabolic reactions transfer molecules and electrons without such nuclear conversion. Distinguishing the three nuclear relationships helps interpret both stable tracer experiments and radioactive decay schemes accurately.

1.A.1.14.4.8 One simple decision rule

First compare Z. If it matches, ask whether N differs: those are isotopes. If Z differs but A matches, they are isobars. If neutron numbers match, they are isotones. If only electrons match, they are isoelectronic. More than one descriptor can be relevant in specialized comparisons, but none should be inferred solely from a visually similar isotope superscript.

1.A.1.14.5 Hydrogen's isotopes: the smallest laboratory for nucleon counting

Imagine preparing three separate samples of hydrogen nuclei. Each nucleus has one proton, so all three belong to the same element. Yet one contains no neutron, another one neutron and the third two. The mass numbers are therefore A = 1, A = 2 and A = 3. This very small system is a remarkably clear demonstration of what A actually counts—and of why neutron number can influence nuclear stability, mass and experimental contrast without changing elemental identity.

1.A.1.14.5.1 Protium gives the baseline

Hydrogen-1, called protium, consists of one proton in its nucleus and no neutron. Its nuclear mass number is therefore one. The neutral atom also contains one electron, but that electron is not counted toward A. A free proton is the nucleus of isolated protium, while a neutral hydrogen atom includes electronic energy and mass as well. An aqueous H⁺ species is chemically solvated rather than a permanently isolated bare nuclear particle.

1.A.1.14.5.2 Deuterium adds exactly one neutron

Deuterium, ²H or D, has one proton and one neutron. This makes A = 2. The deuteron nucleus is a bound proton–neutron system with nuclear binding energy of about 2.2246 MeV. Its measured mass is not exactly twice the unified atomic mass unit: the free proton and neutron have different rest masses, and binding reduces total mass relative to separated constituents. This small system is frequently used as the simplest nontrivial example of nuclear binding.

1.A.1.14.5.3 Tritium adds a second neutron but is not simply 'more stable'

Tritium, ³H or T, contains one proton and two neutrons, so A = 3. It undergoes beta-minus decay to helium-3 with a half-life of about 12.3 years. This demonstrates that increasing neutron count is not automatically an improvement in nuclear stability. Tritium remains hydrogen before the nuclear transformation because its proton number is one. The daughter helium-3 has two protons and one neutron, preserving A = 3 while changing element identity.

1.A.1.14.5.4 What a stable isotope substitution changes in chemistry

Deuterated water and deuterium-labeled molecules differ in mass from their ordinary hydrogen analogues. Bond vibrational frequencies, diffusion and some reaction rates can change through isotope effects. Yet in ordinary chemical reactions, the nuclear proton and neutron count of a particular deuterium atom is preserved. A deuterium label can therefore help investigate a mechanism while continuing to behave as the element hydrogen in broad chemical terms.

1.A.1.14.5.5 Why deuterium helps neutron-based structure analysis

Hydrogen-1 and deuterium differ substantially in their neutron scattering properties despite sharing Z = 1. With suitable samples and experiments, isotope substitution can alter scattering contrast and make components of a macromolecular complex easier to distinguish. This does not mean that mass number alone determines scattering intensity; nuclear scattering lengths and isotope identity are important additional properties. It is an example of how simple nucleon counts connect to advanced life-science measurements.

1.A.1.14.5.6 Don't confuse mass number and isotope mass

The deuterium atom has A = 2 and an atomic mass of roughly 2.0141 u, not exactly 2 u. Tritium has A = 3 and measured atomic mass around 3.0160 u. These values reflect different constituent masses and binding energies, alongside electronic contributions. The integer A accurately tells you how many nucleons are in the nucleus; precise mass measurement answers a different quantitative question.

1.A.1.14.5.7 What to write in a long-answer examination

Explain the common Z, list proton and neutron counts for all three isotopes, discuss mass-number changes, and then identify the difference in nuclear stability and isotope-sensitive applications. Avoid the absolute claim that all hydrogen isotopes are radioactive or that every atomic nucleus has at least one neutron. This one family provides enough evidence to refute both misconceptions.

1.A.1.14.6 The central distinction: mass number A versus measured mass

Imagine reading “carbon-12” on an isotope-labeled vial and then opening a precision mass table. The label tells you A = 12, but it does not mean that the carbon-12 nucleus has a measured mass of exactly 12 unified atomic mass units. The reference definition of u assigns exactly 12 u to a free neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state, including the electrons and binding contributions. Its bare nucleus is a different physical system with a different measured mass.

1.A.1.14.6.1 How a count differs from a mass

Mass number A is an integer. A precise atomic mass is a physical quantity expressible in kg or u. Both can appear near the same element symbol, encouraging the mistaken equation M = A u for all nuclei. That equation is only a rough approximation, not a physical identity. Free proton and neutron masses differ from each other and from one u, electrons contribute additional mass and binding energies modify the total rest mass of a bound atom.

1.A.1.14.6.2 The unified atomic mass unit is a carefully chosen reference

One unified atomic mass unit u, also called a dalton Da, is one twelfth of the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom in its nuclear and electronic ground state, at rest. The 2022 CODATA estimate is approximately 1.66053906892 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, with measurement uncertainty in its conversion to SI kilograms. The carbon-12 atomic mass being 12 u by definition does not make a separated carbon-12 nucleus equal 12 u.

1.A.1.14.6.3 The nucleons themselves are not exactly one u

A free proton weighs approximately 1.00727647 u, a free neutron about 1.008664916 u and a free electron about 0.00054858 u. Summing the free-particle masses for a particular nuclear composition gives an unbound-constituent reference. A bound nucleus generally has a lower rest mass because binding energy changes the total energy of the system. An atom then adds the electronic mass-energy content with its own binding corrections.

A = Z + N (exact, dimensionless)
Mnucleus ≠ A·u (in general)
1 u = 1/12 × mass of a neutral ¹²C atom in its ground state

1.A.1.14.6.4 Why the difference matters to nuclear energy

Einstein's energy–mass relationship relates a change in total rest energy to a difference in mass. For a bound nucleus, the separated protons and neutrons have a greater combined rest mass than the bound system when the binding energy is positive. The mass difference corresponds to energy required to separate the nucleus into free constituents. Using A u instead of real masses would produce incorrect nuclear binding calculations.

1.A.1.14.6.5 What makes standard atomic weight another different number?

A naturally occurring sample can contain multiple isotopes of one element. Its standard atomic weight is based on representative isotope masses and abundances in normal material, potentially with intervals reflecting variability. The familiar chlorine value near 35.45 is not the mass number of an imaginary isotope with a fraction of a neutron. It is a population-level relative atomic-mass quantity.

1.A.1.14.6.6 Precision and exactness are different

The count A = 12 for carbon-12 is exact if the nuclide has been identified. The reference mass of the neutral carbon-12 atom is exactly 12 u because the unit is defined that way. The conversion of u into kg has a measured uncertainty. The mass of carbon-13 and any realistic reaction energy have experimentally determined precision. Being rigorous means saying which numbers are definitions and which are measurements.

1.A.1.14.6.7 A lesson for mass spectrometry

A mass spectrometer records mass-to-charge behavior of ions, and high-resolution work can separate isotopes whose measured masses differ slightly from integer mass-number expectations. In a mass spectrum, lowercase z denotes ion charge state, not nuclear proton number Z. Thus a formula like m/z cannot be interpreted simply as “mass divided by number of protons.” The terminology matters whenever atomic physics meets analytical biology.

1.A.1.14.7 Atomic masses, average atomic weight and isotope abundances

Imagine a flask of a naturally occurring element whose atoms are not all the same isotope. Every atom in it has a definite integer mass number, yet a periodic table prints a non-integer standard atomic weight. Where did the decimal come from? The answer is a population average over atoms with differing isotopic masses and abundances. This distinction matters in chemistry, in mass spectrometry and in biological tracer experiments.

1.A.1.14.7.1 A single atom has a definite nuclide identity

Take chlorine-35. The nucleus has seventeen protons and eighteen neutrons, so A = 35. Chlorine-37 instead has seventeen protons and twenty neutrons, so A = 37. These are individual nuclide descriptions. Their accurately measured neutral atomic masses are approximately 34.96885 u and 36.96590 u, respectively; neither equals exactly the integer A u. The difference arises from constituent masses and binding-energy contributions.

1.A.1.14.7.2 An average weights each isotope by its abundance

For isotopes with fractional abundances fᵢ summing to one, mean atomic mass is Σfᵢmᵢ, with measured isotopic masses mᵢ in common units. A simple two-isotope illustration is x m₁ + (1 − x)m₂. If we instead use approximate mass numbers 35 and 37 and abundances 75% and 25%, the elementary arithmetic gives 35.5 u. That is an explicitly rounded classroom estimate, not the official atomic weight.

Mean atomic mass = Σi fi mi, Σfi = 1
Approximate classroom illustration: 0.75×35 + 0.25×37 = 35.5

1.A.1.14.7.3 Why actual chlorine is more nuanced

NIST and CIAAW publish measured isotopic masses, compositions and standard atomic-weight information for chlorine. The isotope fractions and measured masses generate a value around the familiar mid-35 range, with CIAAW recognizing natural variations rather than insisting every sample has one unchanging mixture. The mass number of each isotope stays 35 or 37; environmental isotope variations alter their relative abundance and the resulting averaged quantity.

1.A.1.14.7.4 Relative atomic weight, molecular mass and amount of substance

Relative atomic weight is dimensionless by definition and historically called atomic weight. Atomic mass for a particular nuclide is a mass that may be expressed in u or Da. The mass of a mole of molecules involves amount of substance and molar mass. None of these quantities is identical to A, though A can help approximate a nuclide's atomic mass in introductory settings. Every calculation should specify which mass convention or quantity it uses.

1.A.1.14.7.5 Why two equal-A nuclides need not have equal measured mass

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 are isobars: both A = 14. Yet their different nuclear compositions and binding energies lead to different actual masses. The nuclear transformation carbon-14 to nitrogen-14 is energetically allowed in beta-minus decay because of the complete mass-energy balance. Equal A guarantees only equal nucleon count; it never guarantees identical rest mass or chemical element.

1.A.1.14.7.6 Biological tracer isotopologue patterns

When a carbon-13-labeled substrate is incorporated into a metabolite, a mass spectrometer may show an isotopologue distribution reflecting the number of labeled carbon atoms present. Interpreting those peaks requires accounting for natural abundance, tracer enrichment, ion species, charge states and possible adducts. The isotope mass difference is useful precisely because A differs; the metabolic interpretation requires more than recognizing a one-unit nominal shift.

1.A.1.14.7.7 Isotopic fractionation can change mixtures

Some physical, chemical and biological processes favor one isotope slightly over another, changing abundance ratios without changing nuclear Z for the surviving atoms. Such fractionation is important in ecology, geochemistry and some metabolic studies. The mass numbers of the participating nuclides remain fixed. A changing sample-average atomic weight is a statistical observation, not a conversion of one isotope into a nucleus with fractional A.

1.A.1.14.7.8 How to write a complete exam response

Define A exactly, then distinguish a particular isotope's measured atomic mass from a mixture's relative atomic weight. Show the weighted-average formula, including abundances as fractions rather than percentages unless converted. Note that official data use measured masses, not integer approximations. Finally mention that a mass spectrum observes ion m/z, so assigning an isotope signal requires chemical and instrumental context.

1.A.1.14.8 Mass defect: the 'missing' mass is a statement about energy

Imagine assembling one proton and one neutron into a deuteron. We are not adding an extra nucleon or removing one: before and after, there are two nucleons, so A = 2 remains the same. Yet the rest mass of the bound deuteron is smaller than the combined rest masses of a separated free proton and free neutron. Where did that difference go? Energy was released as the bound state formed under appropriate conditions. This is the concept of mass defect and nuclear binding energy.

1.A.1.14.8.1 Write the formula with every mass identified

For a bare nucleus containing Z protons and N neutrons, define Δm = Zmₚ + Nmₙ − M_nucleus, where mₚ and mₙ are free-particle masses and M_nucleus is the rest mass of the bound nucleus. Binding energy B = Δmc² in the usual separation-energy convention. If B is positive for a bound system, its mass is lower than that of the free constituents. A remains the same integer, and its value alone does not determine B.

Δm = Zmp + Nmn − Mnucleus
B = Δm c²
1 u·c² ≈ 931.494 MeV

1.A.1.14.8.2 Use the deuteron as a physical example

The deuteron contains one proton and one neutron. Using approximate bare free masses 1.00727647 u and 1.008664916 u and the bare deuteron mass 2.013553213 u, the mass defect is about 0.00238817 u. Multiplying by approximately 931.494 MeV/u gives around 2.2246 MeV of binding energy. Both before and after assembly, A = 2. The mass difference comes from energy, not from a fraction of one nucleon disappearing.

1.A.1.14.8.3 Why mixing atomic and nuclear masses is dangerous

A neutral deuterium atom includes an electron, and a free proton mass does not include a bound atomic electron. If the problem supplies neutral atomic masses, a formula involving the mass of neutral hydrogen atoms plus neutrons may be useful, but it must handle electronic binding differences consistently. Substituting a neutral isotope's mass into a bare nuclear mass equation without correcting for electrons can produce an incorrect binding energy.

1.A.1.14.8.4 Binding energy per nucleon

Dividing total nuclear binding energy B by A gives binding energy per nucleon, often used to compare the average binding scale of different nuclei. It does not mean every nucleon has exactly that individual separation energy. Proton and neutron separation energies can differ, and shell structure and pairing produce important variations. B/A is a useful summary measure but cannot replace detailed nuclear energetics.

1.A.1.14.8.5 How binding energy changes a nuclear calculation

If one estimated the mass of a bound nucleus simply by multiplying A by a constant nucleon mass, one would neglect both the proton–neutron mass difference and binding energy. Two nuclei with equal A but different Z and N may have different measured masses. Nuclear reaction Q-values therefore require appropriate actual masses, not only A and Z bookkeeping. Balancing counts is necessary but not sufficient for the energetics of a reaction.

1.A.1.14.8.6 Does mass defect violate conservation of mass-energy?

No. A closed physical description includes the energy carried away by radiation or kinetic products and any other relevant contributions. The combined energy of system plus emitted energy is conserved. Rest mass of a subsystem can decrease when its energy changes. Using E = mc² in the correct total-energy context reconciles the mass observation with conservation laws.

1.A.1.14.8.7 Connect to nuclear stability and biology

Differences in binding energy affect whether nuclear decays or reactions are energetically possible. Biological isotopic studies ordinarily rely on stable nuclei and measure their mass or spin characteristics, not on spontaneous conversion of nuclear binding energy into ordinary enzyme catalytic energy. This keeps nuclear physics and routine biochemical redox reactions conceptually separate while acknowledging that both obey fundamental energy conservation.

1.A.1.14.9 Nuclear size and density: why the cube root of A appears

Imagine comparing the characteristic size of a helium-4 nucleus with that of a much heavier nucleus. The heavier nucleus has many more nucleons, but its radius is not simply proportional to A. A widely used approximate nuclear-size rule says that a characteristic nuclear radius grows roughly with A to the one-third power. The logic is geometric: if nuclear density is approximately comparable across many nuclei, volume is roughly proportional to nucleon number, while radius is the cube root of volume.

1.A.1.14.9.1 Begin with a three-dimensional count

For a roughly spherical object of volume V = (4/3)πR³, doubling volume increases radius only by the cube root of two. If each additional nucleon contributes roughly comparable nuclear volume in a simple uniform-density picture, then V ∝ A and therefore R ∝ A^(1/3). This explains the phenomenological expression R ≈ r₀A^(1/3), where r₀ is a characteristic length on the order of 1.2 femtometres.

R ≈ r₀A1/3, r₀ ~ 1.2 fm (coarse nuclear-size model)
V ∝ R³ ∝ A; 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m

1.A.1.14.9.2 Make the approximation explicit

Nuclei do not have universally sharp rigid surfaces; charge and matter densities change with distance and nuclear state. Experiments may report root-mean-square charge radii, matter radii or parameters of a fitted density distribution. These are related but not identical definitions. The simple r₀ formula gives a useful order of magnitude across many nuclides; it should not be treated as a high-precision result for every light nucleus, deformed nucleus or halo nucleus.

1.A.1.14.9.3 Example: compare oxygen-16 and iron-56

In the same rough model, R_Fe/R_O ≈ (56/16)^(1/3) ≈ 1.52. Iron-56 has 3.5 times as many nucleons as oxygen-16 but an estimated radius only about one and a half times as large. The result does not depend on exact atomic electron configuration because it compares nuclei. A spectroscopically measured ionic radius would answer an entirely different question.

1.A.1.14.9.4 Atomic size is much larger

Characteristic atomic distances are often of order 10⁻¹⁰ m, while nuclear dimensions are typically of order 10⁻¹⁵ m. The electron distribution occupies much of the atomic spatial scale even though most ordinary atomic mass is nuclear. The nucleus is not simply a dense electron shell, and a nuclide's A does not reveal the exact radius of its entire chemical atom. This separation matters when interpreting X-ray, neutron and electron-based measurements.

1.A.1.14.9.5 Why density is roughly similar in the first approximation

If nucleon number scales with volume, average nuclear number density A/V can be approximately constant for many medium and heavy nuclei. The nuclear strong interaction and finite-range behavior contribute to the broad empirical regularity. But pairing, shell structure, deformation and weak binding can produce exceptions. A neutron-rich halo system can have an extended matter distribution compared with what a simple A^(1/3) estimate suggests.

1.A.1.14.9.6 How a question might try to trap you

If a model nucleus has eight times the nucleon count of another nucleus, the simple radius ratio is 8^(1/3) = 2, not eight. The volume ratio remains eight in that ideal model. If two nuclides have equal A, the simple formula gives equal radii, but their actual measured nuclear charge radii need not be identical because Z, N, deformation and nuclear structure can differ.

1.A.1.14.9.7 Biological research connection

Nuclear size is not the scale of a protein-binding pocket or ion channel. Biological chemistry usually depends on electronic states at far larger length scales. Nuclear measurements such as neutron scattering and isotope-sensitive spectroscopy can exploit nuclear properties, but their interpretation requires more than A alone. This distinction keeps atomic-scale biological reasoning and nuclear geometry in their appropriate domains.

1.A.1.14.10 Nuclear stability: neutron balance, pairing and shell structure

Imagine that a nucleus with a particular mass number stays stable for enormous periods, while another nucleus with the same A undergoes a radioactive transformation. If A only tells us the total nucleon count, why can stability differ? The answer is that the proton–neutron composition and the quantum state of the nucleus matter. Nuclear binding and decay are not calculated from A alone, even though A is necessary for labeling the nuclide and for many approximate trends.

1.A.1.14.10.1 Proton–neutron balance changes with element size

For many stable light nuclei, Z and N are similar. For heavier stable nuclides, N generally exceeds Z because increasing proton–proton Coulomb repulsion affects energetics. But there is no universal equation N = Z or N = some fixed multiple of Z that predicts every stable nucleus. Shell effects, pairing, strong-interaction dynamics and available daughter states influence stability. A high mass number does not alone guarantee that a nuclide is unstable or stable.

1.A.1.14.10.2 Why proton–proton repulsion remains real

Every nuclear proton has positive charge +e. Neutrons have no net electric charge, so they do not simply cancel the proton charge like negatively charged electrons would. Binding results from nuclear interactions and the full many-body quantum energy balance. Increasing neutron number can sometimes make a nuclide more favorable, but excess neutrons can create beta-instability. The phrase “neutrons act as glue” is an oversimplification, not a complete mechanism.

1.A.1.14.10.3 Pairing and even–odd patterns

Many nuclear properties show patterns depending on whether proton and neutron counts are even or odd. Nucleon pairing contributes to the relative energies of nuclear states and explains part of why some even–even nuclei are particularly well bound. Yet this is not a blanket rule saying every even-A nuclide is stable or every odd-A nuclide radioactive. A = Z + N alone cannot tell us which of Z or N is even, or whether the relevant pairing energy favors a given decay route.

1.A.1.14.10.4 Nuclear shell structure and magic numbers

The nuclear shell model organizes protons and neutrons in quantum levels under an effective nuclear potential. Certain shell closures—commonly associated with 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 and 126 for the relevant nucleon species—can provide extra stability in appropriate contexts. These are proton or neutron shell occupancy numbers, not a universal new sequence of allowed A values. A nucleus can have a particularly favorable N even when its Z differs.

1.A.1.14.10.5 What the chart of nuclides reveals

A chart with Z on one axis and N on another displays many points for a single element, each with a different A. Stable nuclei form a region whose position changes with Z. Beta-minus decay often shifts a neutron-rich nuclide toward greater Z at fixed A, while beta-plus decay or electron capture can shift suitable proton-rich nuclides toward smaller Z at fixed A. Other nuclear modes can change A as well.

1.A.1.14.10.6 Use carbon as a concise comparison

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable; carbon-14 is radioactive and undergoes beta-minus decay. All have Z = 6, with A values twelve, thirteen and fourteen. This shows that increasing A by one or two does not have a single predictable effect. The physical explanation depends on nuclear composition and allowed daughter energies, not on whether the mass number sounds “large.”

1.A.1.14.10.7 What biology students should retain

Stable isotope labels are chosen partly because the nuclide persists during the experiment, while radioactive tracers require decay-specific interpretation and safety planning. An isotope is not automatically radioactive merely because its A differs from the most abundant nuclide. Conversely, a beta-emitting isotope can change element identity after decay even while A remains unchanged. Accurate nuclear stability language therefore supports correct experimental design interpretation.

1.A.1.14.10.8 The exam reasoning sequence

Identify A and Z; compute N; compare the nuclide's position and, when needed, supplied decay data. If asked about nuclear stability, use known nuclear energetics or the stated information rather than guessing solely from A. If asked about a decay mode, apply appropriate conservation laws and energy conditions. This systematic reasoning is more rigorous than memorizing one fixed “stable neutron ratio.”

1.A.1.14.11 Binding energy per nucleon and the meaning of an A-dependent trend

Imagine plotting binding energy per nucleon across the chart of nuclides. Many medium-mass nuclei have relatively high average binding energy per nucleon compared with very light or very heavy systems. This broad trend helps explain why certain fusion or fission processes can release energy, but the curve has structure and exceptions. The mass number A locates the nuclide on the horizontal axis; it does not determine a unique binding energy without the composition and actual nuclear mass.

1.A.1.14.11.1 What is plotted in the standard curve?

Total binding energy B is the energy required to separate a bound nucleus into its free nucleon constituents under the appropriate reference conditions. Dividing B by A gives the average binding energy per nucleon. A curve of B/A versus A provides a rough comparative picture of nuclear energetic favorability. Peaks and variations reflect nuclear interactions, Coulomb effects, shell structure and pairing—not a uniform interaction between identical classical balls.

1.A.1.14.11.2 Why iron-region nuclei are often highlighted

The binding-energy-per-nucleon curve rises rapidly for very light systems and reaches a broad high region among medium-mass nuclei, with a maximum near the iron–nickel neighborhood depending on the specific nuclide and convention. A common classroom simplification says iron-56 has the highest binding energy per nucleon; the more precise statement recognizes that nickel-62 is slightly higher by that particular measure. Do not use a slogan as a numerical data table.

1.A.1.14.11.3 Fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei

Certain fusion reactions of light nuclei can produce products with greater total binding energy, releasing energy when the full mass–energy accounting is favorable. Similarly, fission of a sufficiently heavy nucleus may produce fragments with higher average binding and release energy. These general statements do not imply that every conceivable fusion or fission reaction is spontaneous, or that A conservation alone establishes an energy release. The complete initial and final masses matter.

B/A = binding energy per nucleon
Reaction Q = (Minitial − Mfinal)c² (complete consistent mass balance)

1.A.1.14.11.4 Why A counts do not give Q automatically

In many ordinary nuclear reactions, total nucleon number is the same on both sides, and therefore the sum of A labels balances. Yet the reaction may absorb or release energy because the total rest masses differ. This is not a contradiction: A counts nucleons, while mass-energy includes internal binding and kinetic possibilities. Energy conservation is satisfied by the full system, including emitted particles and radiation.

1.A.1.14.11.5 One smooth curve cannot predict every isotope

Two isobars with the same A may have different Z, N and binding energies. Nuclear shell closures and deformation can generate significant changes relative to a simple liquid-drop trend. A chart of average binding energy is a guide to broad nuclear behavior, not an exact algorithm for half-life, reactor feasibility or specific emission channels. This matters especially near the edges of nuclear stability.

1.A.1.14.11.6 Why the relationship with A matters mathematically

Dividing by A normalizes binding energy to a mean per nucleon, allowing nuclei of different size to be compared on a common rough scale. But the total energy B remains important for predicting energy required to separate a nucleus completely. A large nucleus can have high total B simply because it contains many nucleons, even when its B/A is lower than that of a medium-mass nucleus.

1.A.1.14.11.7 Academic scope and safety

Studying binding energies provides useful conceptual physics for understanding isotopes and radiation. It does not constitute instructions for building nuclear sources or performing reactions. Real nuclear experimentation requires specialist facilities and strict safety systems. For Life Science preparation, the needed skill is the correct distinction between nucleon count A, nuclear stability, nuclear mass and energy release.

1.A.1.14.11.8 A useful self-test

If two nuclides share A = 56, must their B/A values be exactly equal? No. Their proton and neutron counts, nuclear structure and masses may differ. If A is unchanged during beta decay, can energy still be released? Yes, because the daughter nuclear system and emitted leptons can have different total rest energies. Those answers show that A is a necessary bookkeeping number but not a complete energy description.

1.A.1.14.12 What a mass spectrometer actually measures: isotopologues, z and isotope mass

Imagine analyzing a peptide that has incorporated carbon-13 or nitrogen-15 labels. Its detected mass-related peaks may shift relative to an unlabeled sample. This is a practical application of different isotope masses, but interpreting the instrument does not mean reading A directly from the horizontal axis. Mass spectrometry involves ionization, charge states, measured mass-to-charge ratios, adducts, isotope distributions and sometimes fragmentation. Each has to be separated from the simple nuclear count.

1.A.1.14.12.1 Lowercase z is not atomic number Z

A mass spectrum often labels its horizontal axis m/z. The z refers to the charge-state number of the detected ion, while m is its relevant ion mass under the instrument's conventions. Capital Z is nuclear proton number and belongs to atomic identity. A protein ion can carry a charge state z = 10 even though it contains many different atomic elements with different Z values. That is not a nucleus with ten protons.

1.A.1.14.12.2 One heavy isotope changes the molecular ion mass

Substituting one ¹³C atom for ¹²C increases the molecular mass by approximately 1.003355 Da when comparing the relevant neutral isotopic atom masses; actual observed ion signals depend on charge and other details. For a singly charged molecular ion, the m/z separation is roughly that mass difference in Da per unit charge. For a doubly charged ion, it is approximately half as large. This is why nominal integer A differences should not automatically be equated with exact m/z shifts.

Approx. isotope mass difference: m(¹³C) − m(¹²C) ≈ 1.003355 Da
Same charge state z: Δ(m/z) ≈ Δm / |z|

1.A.1.14.12.3 How a labeled metabolite becomes an isotopologue set

A metabolite with several carbon atoms may contain zero, one, two or more ¹³C labels. Different numbers of labels lead to different isotopologues and characteristic peak patterns. Natural isotope abundance also contributes to the observed distribution. The instrument reports masses and signal intensities, which can support tracer incorporation estimates. It does not by itself reveal the exact biochemical sequence of reactions without a model and appropriate controls.

1.A.1.14.12.4 Why A still matters underneath

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are isotopes with mass numbers 12 and 13, reflecting six versus seven neutrons at fixed Z = 6. Their different measured masses are related to nuclear composition and binding. A is the bookkeeping label that identifies the isotope, while precise mass differences explain the fine spacing of peaks. A high-resolution mass spectrometer can exploit those non-integer differences in accurate analyses.

Even an unlabeled sample normally contains small amounts of naturally occurring heavy isotopes. A researcher who ignores isotope natural abundance may overestimate experimental incorporation, even though every individual nucleus retains an integer A.

1.A.1.14.12.7 Return to the physical distinction

Mass number A is exact counting. Measured isotopic mass, molecular mass and ion m/z are physical observables with units or instrumental conventions. Connecting them correctly allows biologists to exploit isotope labels without conflating a nuclear count with a decimal spectral coordinate. That is the point of including this analytical method in a mass-number lesson.

1.A.1.14.13 Balancing nuclear reactions: A is bookkeeping, not a Q-value

Imagine seeing a nuclear equation with one missing product. Chemical balancing teaches us to conserve atoms of each element, but nuclear reactions can transform one element into another. For the familiar introductory reactions, the sum of the mass-number labels A and the sum of the atomic-number labels Z can be used to identify unknown nuclear products. This is a powerful bookkeeping method precisely because nuclear charge and the count of nucleons are conserved in these ordinary cases. Yet the balance alone cannot prove the reaction is energetically possible.

1.A.1.14.13.1 Start with a known reaction

Consider ⁹₄Be + ⁴₂He → ¹²₆C + ¹₀n. On the left, total mass-number labels sum to 9 + 4 = 13; on the right, 12 + 1 = 13. Proton-number labels sum to 4 + 2 = 6 on the left and 6 + 0 = 6 on the right. The emitted ¹₀n is a neutron: it adds one to the nucleon count but carries zero net electrical charge. This is a nuclear reaction, not ordinary beryllium chemistry in a flask.

1.A.1.14.13.2 Balance a hydrogen-nucleus emission

The standard nitrogen reaction ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H has mass-number total 18 on both sides and atomic-number total 9 on both sides. The emitted ¹H nucleus is a proton. This modern notation helps represent historical proton-emission evidence, but an educational equation does not replace detailed experimental history or the requirement for suitable incident energies.

1.A.1.14.13.3 A missing particle as an algebra problem

If ²⁷₁₃Al + ⁴₂He → ³⁰₁₅P + X, mass-number balance gives A_X = 27 + 4 − 30 = 1. Charge balance gives Z_X = 13 + 2 − 15 = 0. Therefore X is a neutron. Writing a proton would fail Z conservation. This kind of example tests both A and Z; one equation alone may not identify a missing particle correctly.

In ordinary nuclide reaction bookkeeping: ΣAinitial = ΣAfinal
ΣZinitial = ΣZfinal (count nuclear and emitted charged particles consistently)

1.A.1.14.13.4 Why adding A values does not mean adding exact masses

Even when the sums of mass-number labels match, the actual rest masses of the initial and final systems may differ. That difference can appear as kinetic energy and radiation, or the reaction may require energy input. The reaction Q-value is calculated using a consistent set of actual nuclear or atomic masses, not by substituting the same sum of A values and concluding that Q must equal zero.

1.A.1.14.13.5 Conservation laws are broader than these two sums

Full nuclear physics also requires energy, momentum, angular momentum and relevant particle quantum-number conservation. For ordinary reactions involving nucleons, baryon number is conserved and A bookkeeping is convenient. For beta processes, emitted leptons have A = 0 in standard nuclear equations, and the nucleon count of the daughter remains A. The simplified method should not be assumed without qualification for arbitrary high-energy particle creation or exotic processes.

1.A.1.14.13.6 Why beta equations need extra care

Beta-minus decay of ¹⁴C to ¹⁴N emits an electron and electron antineutrino. The nuclear mass number stays fourteen while Z increases by one. If the electron is assigned nuclear charge notation ⁰₋₁e in introductory equations, the total charge balances, but the emitted electron has no nucleon count. The electron is produced by the weak process rather than removed from an ordinary atomic orbital during chemical ionization.

1.A.1.14.13.7 Exam method and limitation

Write A and Z for every nuclide; include emitted particles; solve the two integer equations and identify the particle. Finally ask whether the reaction is actually possible under stated energy conditions. Conservation of A and Z is necessary bookkeeping for the familiar reactions, not an independent guarantee of nuclear feasibility. That final question is what separates a complete solution from mechanical arithmetic.

1.A.1.14.14 Alpha, beta, gamma and electron capture: who changes A?

Imagine four radioactive nuclei that decay by different mechanisms. One emits an alpha particle, another undergoes beta-minus decay, a third releases gamma radiation, and the fourth captures an inner atomic electron. They may all produce measurable radiation or daughter states, but they do not all change mass number A the same way. This is a frequent examination topic because the numerical transformations are straightforward only when the physical particle emitted or transformed is identified correctly.

1.A.1.14.14.1 Alpha decay reduces nucleon count by four

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus containing two protons and two neutrons, so it has A = 4 and Z = 2. In alpha emission, the daughter nucleus has A four units lower and Z two units lower. For example, ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. Both nuclear mass-number sums and charge sums balance. The element changes because proton count drops by two.

1.A.1.14.14.2 Beta-minus decay changes Z but preserves A

At the nucleon-count level, a neutron becomes a proton, emitting an electron and electron antineutrino. The daughter nucleus has the same A but Z increased by one. Carbon-14 decays to nitrogen-14, making the parent and daughter isobars. Although a new electron appears among the products, its A = 0 as an elementary lepton in nuclear bookkeeping; it is not an additional nucleon.

1.A.1.14.14.3 Beta-plus decay and electron capture also preserve A

In appropriate proton-rich nuclear systems, beta-plus emission converts a proton into a neutron and emits a positron and neutrino. Electron capture uses an atomic electron in a weak process converting a nuclear proton into a neutron with a neutrino emitted. Both reduce nuclear Z by one while leaving A unchanged, subject to energy requirements. The two processes are physically different from simple atomic ionization, despite involving electron or positron language.

1.A.1.14.14.4 Pure gamma emission leaves nuclear counts unchanged

Gamma radiation can be emitted when an excited nucleus moves to a lower-energy state without changing its proton or neutron count. Thus A and Z remain the same for pure gamma de-excitation. The nuclear energy state changes, and an emitted photon carries energy and angular momentum. A chart showing only A and Z may therefore omit an important difference between a ground-state nucleus and an excited nuclear isomer.

Suppose a parent has A = 210 and Z = 84, and you are told it emits an alpha particle. Daughter A = 206 and Z = 82. Element 82 is lead, so the daughter is lead-206. If the same parent instead underwent a specified beta-minus process, the daughter would have A = 210 and Z = 85, a different nuclide. One must use the given decay mode and appropriate energy conditions; A alone cannot select the mode.

1.A.1.14.14.6 Neutron capture is not beta-minus decay

When a nucleus captures a neutron and retains it, its A increases by one while Z initially stays the same. The product may later decay, potentially changing Z. Students often collapse these two stages into one and say that capturing a neutron automatically creates a different element. It normally creates another isotope first, and subsequent transformations must be assessed separately.

1.A.1.14.14.7 The mass-number lesson hiding inside the decay chart

Decay equations teach exactly what A counts. An alpha particle removes four nucleons; beta conversion rearranges neutron and proton identity while preserving the nucleon count; gamma radiation removes energy but no nucleon; neutron capture adds a nucleon. Because electrons and photons are not nucleons, the simplified A bookkeeping is not a direct count of every physical particle in the event.

1.A.1.14.14.8 Safety and academic boundaries

This section explains nuclear reactions conceptually. It is not a procedure for producing sources or managing radioactive materials. Any laboratory use of radioisotopes requires appropriate authorization and radiation protection. For CSIR-NET or State SET, the goal is to interpret symbols, conserved quantities and daughter identities with scientific accuracy.

1.A.1.14.15 Neutron capture, activated nuclides and nuclear isomers

Imagine a stable nucleus receiving one extra neutron. If the neutron is captured and retained, its proton count is unchanged, so the element identity stays the same at that stage. Its mass number increases by one. But what happens afterward is not determined by this integer count alone: the resulting nucleus may be stable, excited, radioactive or able to release energy through further transitions. Neutron capture is therefore a particularly useful way to understand both the strength and the limitations of A.

1.A.1.14.15.1 A simple counting equation for capture

Represent a starting nuclide as ᴬ_ZX and a free neutron as ¹₀n. A simplified capture stage can be written ᴬ_ZX + ¹₀n → ᴬ⁺¹_ZX* where the asterisk indicates a possible excited state. The total nucleon count has increased by one for the bound product, but the proton count Z is unchanged. A subsequent gamma emission may remove excitation energy without changing A or Z.

1.A.1.14.15.2 Why a gamma photon is not a missing nucleon

In radiative neutron capture, one may write ᴬ_ZX + n → ᴬ⁺¹_ZX + γ for a suitable net transition. The photon carries energy, momentum and angular momentum, but its mass number is zero. The equation balances the nucleons, yet the measured gamma energy depends on actual initial and final masses and nuclear level energies. Simply multiplying A by a constant cannot predict the photon spectrum.

1.A.1.14.15.3 Some products later beta-decay

Capturing a neutron can produce a nuclide that is unstable to beta decay. In such a case, A may remain constant during the later beta transformation while Z changes. The nucleus thus passes through a heavier isotope of the original element before becoming another element. The two-step interpretation matters in activation studies and prevents the wrong statement that neutron capture itself always changes nuclear proton number.

1.A.1.14.15.4 Nuclear isomers introduce an extra state label

Two nuclear states can share identical A and Z yet differ in excitation energy, angular momentum and lifetime. A metastable nuclear isomer is often indicated by an m notation, such as a superscripted metastable label. The mass number remains the same because the same numbers of protons and neutrons are present. Yet the energy state may be sufficiently long-lived to require separate identification in nuclear data or medical-imaging contexts.

1.A.1.14.15.5 What IUPAC nuclide terminology reminds us

IUPAC's nuclide definition refers to mass number, atomic number and nuclear energy state, provided the state is sufficiently observable. Thus A and Z are central but sometimes not the complete state description. A student should not confuse a long-lived nuclear isomer with an isotope having a different neutron number, nor interpret gamma de-excitation as a loss of nucleons.

1.A.1.14.15.6 Capture cross sections vary substantially

The probability of neutron capture depends on target nuclide, neutron energy and nuclear structure. Two nuclides with the same A need not capture neutrons at the same rate, and a neutral neutron is not universally captured on contact. The role of resonance and nuclear energy levels illustrates why nucleon count is an input to nuclear modeling, not a prediction of reaction probability by itself.

1.A.1.14.15.7 Connection to elemental analysis and Life Sciences

Neutron activation analysis and some isotope-based measurements take advantage of nuclide-specific interactions and subsequent radiation signatures. Research may also use stable isotope substitutions without radioactive processes. The science is conceptually connected through A and Z, but experimental risks and equipment requirements differ greatly. Academic learning should focus on interpretation, not unsupervised nuclear experimentation.

1.A.1.14.15.8 Three checks when writing a reaction

First, verify the initial and final counts of nucleons using A. Second, verify nuclear charge with Z and any emitted charged particles. Third, examine whether the proposed energy and nuclear states make the reaction possible. A correct A sum alone is necessary but insufficient, just as a mathematically balanced chemical formula does not guarantee a fast or favorable chemical reaction.

1.A.1.14.16 How A stays constant in beta decay even as the element changes

Imagine a carbon-14 nucleus approaching a beta-minus decay event. Before decay it contains six protons and eight neutrons. After a weak transformation, the nitrogen-14 nucleus contains seven protons and seven neutrons. In both cases, the total nucleon count is fourteen. Yet the chemical element is different because the daughter nucleus has a different proton number. This is one of the cleanest examples of why A and Z describe different nuclear properties.

1.A.1.14.16.1 The physical reaction must include leptons

The simple beta-minus reaction is n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ. Inside a nucleus, energy conditions determine whether such a transition is permitted. The electron and electron antineutrino carry away energy and other conserved quantities, while the nucleon changes type. The emitted electron was not a pre-existing atom-shell electron removed through ordinary oxidation; it is produced through the weak interaction.

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̄e
Parent: Z=6,N=8,A=14; daughter: Z=7,N=7,A=14

1.A.1.14.16.2 Why carbon-14 is useful yet radioactive

Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope with a half-life of about 5730 years and is relevant in radiocarbon studies. It has mass number fourteen because the carbon nucleus contains fourteen nucleons. Its decay changes nuclear identity to nitrogen-14 while conserving A. The mass number alone cannot give the decay half-life; that depends on weak-interaction dynamics and the nuclear energy difference and transition properties.

1.A.1.14.16.3 Contrast with carbon-13 stable labeling

Carbon-13 has A = 13 and is stable. In a typical metabolic tracer experiment, a ¹³C-labeled atom can be incorporated into metabolites while retaining its nuclear identity. Carbon-14, by contrast, requires radiation-aware interpretation because its nucleus may transform over time. The two isotopes differ in neutron number and nuclear behavior, but both are carbon before any nuclear transmutation because each has Z = 6.

1.A.1.14.16.4 Isobars connect the parent and daughter

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 are isobars since both have A = 14. They are not isotopes of the same element; their Z values differ. This relationship is particularly useful for identifying daughter nuclides in beta processes. A problem that asks for the “isotope after beta-minus decay” may use casual language, but the scientifically correct statement is that the daughter can be a different element with the same mass number.

1.A.1.14.16.5 The energy difference is not zero because A is the same

Although the two nuclei have fourteen nucleons each, their rest masses differ because proton and neutron masses, binding energy and electronic components matter. Their complete mass-energy difference makes the beta transformation possible. If one mistakenly assumed that any nucleus of A = 14 had mass exactly 14 u, one would incorrectly predict no decay energy. This reveals the practical importance of distinguishing A from measured mass.

1.A.1.14.16.6 Mean lifetime and half-life are separate concepts

The decay constant λ relates to half-life through t₁/₂ = ln2/λ and to mean lifetime through τ = 1/λ for simple exponential decay. These relations describe statistical nuclear populations, not an exact timer assigned to every nucleus. A counts the nucleons in each still-existing parent or daughter; it does not count how many nuclei remain in a sample. A sample's changing activity needs decay kinetics as well as nuclide identification.

1.A.1.14.16.7 What a complete examination explanation includes

List the initial and final Z, N and A; identify the electron and antineutrino; state that beta-minus decay converts a neutron to a proton and leaves A fixed; and explain that the daughter is a different element. If the question concerns activity, supply the decay law separately. This explanation links nuclear composition, conservation and measured radioactivity without mistaking ordinary chemical electron transfer for a weak nuclear transformation.

1.A.1.14.17 Stable isotope tracing in biochemistry: fixed nuclei through moving molecules

Imagine following the fate of one carbon atom as glucose is converted into various metabolites. If that carbon is carbon-13 instead of carbon-12, researchers can detect a distinguishable isotope signal while the carbon atom ordinarily remains carbon throughout biochemical transformations. The nucleus has Z = 6 in both cases, but A differs because carbon-13 has seven neutrons rather than six. That small difference is the foundation of many stable-isotope tracer methods in metabolic research.

1.A.1.14.17.1 How an isotope label survives ordinary reactions

Most biochemical reactions redistribute electrons and change molecular bonds, not the nuclear count of protons and neutrons in each atom. A ¹³C label incorporated into a nutrient can appear later in a product molecule without changing its A = 13. A process that oxidizes a carbon atom changes its chemical environment, but it does not ordinarily convert a neutron into a proton. This persistence is why isotopically labeled atoms can trace the movement of elements through metabolic networks.

1.A.1.14.17.2 Carbon-13 in metabolic flux studies

Researchers may introduce labeled substrates and measure labeling patterns across metabolites using mass spectrometry or nuclear magnetic resonance, depending on the experiment. A compound containing several carbon atoms may show isotopologues with different numbers of ¹³C atoms. Mathematical models and appropriate time-course measurements can help infer pathway activity. The mass number identifies the nuclear label, but the full metabolic mechanism is not given by one peak or one labeled product alone.

1.A.1.14.17.3 Nitrogen-15 traces nitrogen atoms

¹⁵N has seven protons and eight neutrons, so A = 15. ¹⁴N has seven protons and seven neutrons, so A = 14. Both remain nitrogen through ordinary biosynthetic reactions. Stable ¹⁵N labels can aid studies of protein turnover, nitrogen movement or isotope-resolved NMR experiments. The instrument sees isotope-sensitive signals; the meaning of those signals depends on sample preparation and molecular context.

1.A.1.14.17.4 Deuterium and oxygen isotope tracers

Deuterium ²H has A = 2 and is widely used in suitable stable-isotope research, including some water-based physiological methods. Oxygen-18 has eight protons and ten neutrons, so A = 18; oxygen-16 has eight and eight, giving A = 16. Isotopically labeled water can be analyzed for the fate or exchange of hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Tracer interpretation requires understanding isotope abundance, equilibration and the specific measurement model, not just counting a superscript.

1.A.1.14.17.5 Biochemical isotope effects are real

Stable isotope substitution does not change the element, but it can affect vibrational energies, kinetic isotope effects and transport. Hydrogen versus deuterium substitution may be especially influential because the fractional mass change is large. Therefore a tracer is not automatically a perfectly nonperturbing reporter in every system. Good experimental interpretation considers whether the label could alter the process being measured.

1.A.1.14.17.6 Why labels need natural abundance correction

Unlabeled samples naturally contain small fractions of heavier isotopes such as ¹³C. A measured +1 nominal mass isotopologue may therefore appear even before intentional tracer addition. Instrument resolution, isotopic impurities, signal response and peak assignments matter. A data-analysis pipeline must distinguish naturally occurring patterns from introduced labeling to avoid overstating metabolic incorporation.

1.A.1.14.17.7 What the count A contributes—and what it cannot do

A specifies the number of nucleons in each labeled nucleus and helps name the isotope. It cannot alone tell where a label is located within a complex molecule, whether it arrived by a particular enzyme route or what the experimental flux is. Those conclusions require chemical structure, analytical signals and valid pathway models. The atomic physics supplies the label; biology supplies the interpretation.

Tracer principle: Stable isotope methods exploit different nuclear A at fixed element-defining Z, while ordinary biochemical reactions preserve those nuclei.

1.A.1.14.18 Nuclear magnetic resonance, neutron contrast and the role of isotope A

Imagine examining the same protein through two different physical approaches. One researcher collects an NMR spectrum of a suitable isotope-labeled sample, while another uses neutron scattering with deuterium contrast. Both experiments depend on nuclear properties, but neither can be reduced to the integer mass number A alone. Nucleon count identifies a nuclide; nuclear spin, magnetic moment and neutron scattering interactions determine its instrument response. This distinction is essential when connecting atomic structure to Life Science methods.

1.A.1.14.18.1 Why isotope identity affects nuclear spin

Some nuclei have nonzero quantum spin and magnetic moments, allowing nuclear magnetic resonance. Hydrogen-1 has spin one-half; carbon-13 also has spin one-half, whereas carbon-12 has nuclear spin zero in its ground state. Nitrogen-15 has spin one-half, while nitrogen-14 has spin one. These distinctions arise from nuclear quantum structure and are related to proton/neutron composition, not a rule that spin always equals A/2.

1.A.1.14.18.2 Carbon-13 NMR is not simply a heavier carbon signal

Carbon-13 is detectable by NMR because its nuclear spin and magnetic properties permit resonance under suitable conditions. Carbon-12, with spin zero, does not show the same direct nuclear magnetic resonance in the usual way. Both are carbon, and their mass numbers are twelve and thirteen, but A alone is not the full physical criterion for NMR. This is an important example where isotope counting must lead into a property-specific model.

1.A.1.14.18.3 How neutron scattering reads isotope-sensitive contrast

Neutrons interact with nuclei through nuclear scattering processes. Their scattering lengths may differ substantially among isotopes, so hydrogen/deuterium substitution can produce useful contrast when studying macromolecules, membranes or solutions. Neutron scattering can reveal information about structure and dynamics under appropriate experimental designs. The mass number distinguishes the isotopes but does not itself provide their scattering cross sections or predict all observed intensities.

1.A.1.14.18.4 What is meant by contrast variation?

Researchers can change relative scattering contributions by using deuterated components or adjusting solvent isotope composition in suitable controlled experiments. This may make one part of a complex assembly more visible against another in a model-based analysis. The result usually requires fitting and independent knowledge rather than directly photographing every nucleus. Label preparation, exchange and sample integrity influence the interpretation.

1.A.1.14.18.5 Mass spectrometry answers a third question

Mass spectrometry distinguishes isotopologues through ion masses and charge states. Unlike NMR, it does not principally measure nuclear spin; unlike neutron scattering, it does not directly map nuclear scattering contrast. The same ¹³C or ¹⁵N isotope can support different methods for different scientific questions. Understanding A helps identify which nuclide is involved, while instrument physics determines the observable.

1.A.1.14.18.6 Why an isotope with even A may still have spin

It is tempting to infer nuclear spin from parity of A alone. Such heuristics have limits because proton and neutron pairing patterns, quantum shell structure and nuclear state matter. Even-A nuclei can have nonzero spin in some cases, and odd-A nuclei have half-integer spins in ordinary nuclear systems, but the precise spin magnitude must be established by nuclear structure data. A alone is not a universal spin formula.

1.A.1.14.18.7 Compare this with electron paramagnetic resonance

EPR generally detects unpaired electron-spin behavior rather than nuclear-spin resonance. A protein radical may therefore provide an EPR signal even when the relevant nuclear isotope of carbon has spin zero. NMR and EPR share magnetic resonance ideas but study different quantum degrees of freedom. A scientifically complete answer identifies the signal-bearing particle rather than matching an acronym to a memorized isotope list.

1.A.1.14.18.8 The rigorous takeaway for Life Sciences

Nuclear isotopes differ in A, but their experimental fingerprints also depend on mass, spin, magnetic response and nuclear interactions. The number of nucleons is an essential identifier, not a complete predictor of every analytical technique. This insight helps a student choose between isotope mass labeling, NMR and neutron contrast without making an unsupported claim about a particle count.

1.A.1.14.19 Radioisotopes, activity and time: A labels the nuclide, not the clock

Imagine two radioactive samples with equal numbers of nuclei but different isotopes. Their radiation activity might differ enormously, even if their mass numbers happen to be close. Why? Mass number labels nucleon count; decay rate depends on nuclear energy states and transition probabilities. A tells us which nuclide is under discussion when combined with Z, but it is not a timer showing when each nucleus will transform. Understanding this distinction is central to radioactive tracer interpretation and radiological safety.

1.A.1.14.19.1 Define half-life as a population concept

For a sample undergoing simple exponential radioactive decay, the number of remaining parent nuclei satisfies N(t) = N₀e^(−λt), where λ is the decay constant. Half-life is t₁/₂ = ln2/λ, and mean lifetime is τ = 1/λ. Here N(t) means number of radioactive parent nuclei, not the neutron count N used earlier in A = Z + N. The same letter can represent different quantities in different equations, so context must always be stated.

t1/2 = ln2/λ; activity R = λn

1.A.1.14.19.2 Activity is not mass number

Activity measures the number of decays per unit time, commonly in becquerels where 1 Bq = 1 decay/s on average. It depends on both the decay constant and the number of radioactive nuclei. A substance with a larger A does not necessarily have greater activity. To predict activity, one needs nuclide-specific decay data and the population of the isotope, not merely its nucleon count.

1.A.1.14.19.3 Carbon-14 provides a familiar example

Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons, A = 14, and undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14. Its half-life is approximately 5730 years. Carbon-13 has A = 13 and is stable. The smaller difference of one nucleon does not predict the vast difference in decay behavior; nuclear stability and allowed weak transitions explain it. A radiocarbon calculation uses decay kinetics separately from A.

1.A.1.14.19.4 A short educational half-life problem

If a simple isolated population of one radionuclide starts with 8000 nuclei and experiences three half-lives without replenishment, the expected remaining parent population is 8000 × (1/2)³ = 1000 nuclei. Each surviving parent nucleus still has its original A. The number of nuclei in the sample decreases; the mass number of an undecayed nucleus does not become one eighth of its original value.

1.A.1.14.19.5 What about gamma and alpha emissions?

Some nuclear states de-excite by gamma emission without changing A, while alpha decay decreases daughter A by four. A specified decay mode determines how the daughter nuclear count changes. The decay rate is a separate probability property. A student should not infer the mode solely from how large the parent mass number is, although broad nuclear trends can suggest plausible modes when combined with actual nuclear data.

1.A.1.14.19.6 Biological experiments and ethical safety

Radioactive tracers have been used in many scientific and medical contexts, but their use requires institutional authorization, trained personnel, appropriate dose management and controlled waste practices. Stable isotopes such as ¹³C or ¹⁵N avoid introducing radionuclide decay and may be preferable for certain questions. A scientific teaching note can explain half-life and nuclear identity without providing operational source-handling procedures.

1.A.1.14.19.7 Why isotope identity must be complete

To describe a radionuclide reliably, Z and A are central, but nuclear state may also matter. Metastable states can share the same A and Z yet differ in half-life and gamma transitions. This reinforces why the word nuclide may require more than an integer mass number when nuclear energy states are important. Full nuclear identification, not merely A, underlies accurate activity data.

1.A.1.14.19.8 Exam boundary worth remembering

When asked for N neutrons, use N = A − Z. When asked for surviving radioactive nuclei after time t, use a clearly distinguished population variable and radioactive decay law. When asked for the daughter A, use the specified nuclear process. Those are three different operations even when textbook authors reuse the letter N. Precision in symbols prevents accidental equation mixing.

1.A.1.14.20 Integrating mass number into biology without confusing chemical mass

Imagine comparing a DNA molecule, a protein complex and a single atomic nucleus. Each has a mass, but only the atomic nucleus is assigned mass number A as a count of its nucleons in the standard nuclide notation. A DNA molecule contains many different atomic nuclei, each with its own Z and A. Its molecular mass can be measured in daltons, but that is not a single nuclear mass number. This distinction becomes essential when biology scales from isotopes to macromolecules.

1.A.1.14.20.1 A protein's daltons are not one giant isotope A

A protein with molecular mass near 50 kDa contains many carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen and sometimes sulfur atoms, each represented by individual nuclides. The value 50 kDa is a mass scale, not a claim that one nucleus contains fifty thousand nucleons. The mass includes electrons, binding-energy contributions, isotope distribution and the chemical composition of the molecule. Treating molecular mass as a single A would be physically meaningless.

1.A.1.14.20.2 What isotopic substitution changes in a peptide

When one atom of ¹²C is replaced with ¹³C, the total molecular isotope composition and accurate mass change slightly while the molecular connectivity can remain the same. In a high-resolution mass spectrum, the resulting ion m/z shift depends on charge state and isotope mass difference. Mass number supplies the isotope label, but molecular charge, adducts and isotope abundances affect measurement. A +1 nominal label is therefore not an exact universal +1.0000 Da signal.

1.A.1.14.20.3 Stable labels in protein turnover research

Researchers may introduce stable isotope-labeled amino acids or nutrients and track their incorporation into proteins under controlled conditions. Mass-number differences identify isotope substitutions, while measurements over time can provide data relevant to synthesis or turnover. Interpretation requires modeling precursor enrichment, degradation and sampling. The nuclear count A of a particular labeled carbon or nitrogen remains fixed during ordinary protein synthesis.

1.A.1.14.20.4 Why water isotope labeling can probe physiology

Heavy water containing deuterium or oxygen isotope labels can support suitable physiological measurements, with analysis of isotope enrichment and time courses. The chemical behavior is similar in broad terms but not identical in every kinetic respect. The exact protocol, dosage and medical context require professional oversight. From a physics perspective, the crucial observation is that isotope-specific signals stem from different nuclear compositions at fixed elemental identity.

1.A.1.14.20.5 Phosphate and ATP emphasize electronic rather than nuclear changes

ATP contains phosphorus nuclei each with Z = 15 and a particular isotope composition. Hydrolysis or phosphate transfer changes bonds and electronic energy relationships but ordinarily does not change the mass numbers of phosphorus, oxygen or hydrogen nuclei. In biological energetics, proton transfer and electron flow can be coupled to energy storage without transmuting the elements. Nuclear binding energy is not the direct energy source of routine ATP hydrolysis.

1.A.1.14.20.6 Where a question might hide the trap

A problem saying “a peptide has a mass of 15,000 Da” does not specify A = 15,000 for any nucleus. A problem saying “one nitrogen-15 label” specifies A = 15 for that nitrogen nucleus. A question saying “the protein carries a +3 charge state” supplies ion charge, not its nuclide isotope counts. These three phrases can occur together in analytical biochemistry but belong to different variables.

1.A.1.14.20.7 Reconcile atomic, molecular and nuclear models

At the nuclear level, A = Z + N. At the atomic level, measured mass includes nucleus, electrons and binding contributions. At the molecular level, masses combine across many atoms and electronic chemical binding, while isotope mixtures and charge states affect analytical measurements. Each level has useful approximations, but a complete explanation labels which object and which physical quantity are being discussed.

1.A.1.14.20.8 Why this chapter is not merely revision of atomic number

Node 13 identified elements using proton number Z. Here, mass number A enables isotope composition, binding-energy comparisons, nuclear transformations and isotope-specific measurements. The two integers work together but answer different questions. Knowing the difference unlocks a wide range of Life Science and nuclear-chemistry reasoning that a definition-only page would miss.

Scale distinction: A belongs to one nucleus. A molecular mass in Da or kDa is the mass of an entire chemical assembly, not its nuclear mass number.

1.A.1.14.21 Five scientific thought experiments that make A unforgettable

The most useful way to test understanding is to imagine changes to a nucleus and predict what its mass number should do. A student who only memorizes A = Z + N may answer a simple subtraction question, but a student who understands the physical objects counted can distinguish ionization, neutron capture, beta decay, alpha emission and binding-energy release. The following thought experiments create a continuous story through those processes without implying that they are practical laboratory instructions.

1.A.1.14.21.1 Experiment one — remove an electron from carbon-13

Begin with a neutral carbon-13 atom: six nuclear protons, seven nuclear neutrons and six electrons. Remove one ordinary bound electron through an idealized ionization step, leaving a positive carbon ion. Its net charge changes from zero to +e; the electron count changes from six to five. But the nucleus still contains thirteen nucleons. Therefore A remains thirteen and Z remains six. The electronic charge state is not an isotope transformation.

1.A.1.14.21.2 Experiment two — add a neutron to carbon-12

Begin instead with carbon-12, whose nucleus contains six protons and six neutrons. Imagine a neutron being captured and retained under conditions permitting that nuclear reaction. The nucleus now contains six protons and seven neutrons, becoming carbon-13 with A = 13. It is still carbon because Z remains six. Whether capture is probable and whether other radiation is released are separate nuclear questions that cannot be answered from A alone.

1.A.1.14.21.3 Experiment three — beta-minus decay of carbon-14

Now begin with carbon-14, whose six protons and eight neutrons give A = 14. During the permitted beta-minus decay, one neutron converts to a proton, producing nitrogen-14, an electron and an antineutrino. The daughter has seven protons and seven neutrons. Its element identity has changed, yet the nucleon count has not: A remains fourteen. If two students disagree about whether this is an isotope change, ask them to compare Z. Parent and daughter are isobars of different elements.

1.A.1.14.21.4 Experiment four — alpha emission from a heavy nucleus

Take a nucleus with A = 226 and Z = 88, and suppose it emits one alpha particle. The emitted helium-4 nucleus takes two protons and two neutrons away. Daughter mass number becomes 222 and Z becomes 86, identifying radon-222. The parent lost exactly four nucleons, so A decreased by four. The daughter also has a different chemical element identity. Nothing like this occurs when a calcium atom merely loses two valence electrons.

1.A.1.14.21.5 Experiment five — form a bound deuteron

Finally, place one free proton and one free neutron into a physically allowed process forming a bound deuteron with suitable energy release. Before binding, the system contains two nucleons. After binding, the deuteron contains two nucleons. A stays two, yet the rest mass of the bound nucleus is less than the sum of free constituents by the binding-energy mass defect. This demonstrates that A is conserved while rest mass of a subsystem changes due to energy redistribution.

1.A.1.14.21.6 One unexpected exam twist: same A, different actual mass

Compare carbon-14 and nitrogen-14. Both have mass number fourteen, yet their nuclear binding energies, proton–neutron composition and total atomic masses differ. Carbon-14 can beta-decay to nitrogen-14 with energy release. If A meant precisely measured mass rather than nucleon count, it would be difficult to explain why two A = 14 systems are not energetically equivalent. The distinction is therefore not a semantic technicality but a fundamental physical fact.

1.A.1.14.21.7 Which experiment belongs in a biology laboratory?

Stable ¹³C or ¹⁵N labeling can be used in authorized biochemical experiments to follow atoms through metabolites and proteins. Ordinary enzyme-catalyzed reactions generally preserve those nuclei. By contrast, neutron-capture and nuclear decay involve specialist nuclear physics and radiation considerations. The five thought experiments explain conservation laws; they are not recommendations for operating radiation sources or high-energy instruments.

1.A.1.14.21.8 Turn the five examples into a universal method

First identify Z, N and A of the starting nucleus. Next decide which physical objects are added, removed or converted. Recalculate the two nuclear counts. If only electrons changed, A stays fixed; if one neutron is captured, A usually increases by one; if beta changes a neutron into a proton, A stays fixed; if an alpha particle leaves, A falls by four. Then separately consider energy, decay probabilities and the measured mass of each state.

Whole-chapter insight: Count nucleons to obtain A, use Z to identify the element, and use measured masses and nuclear physics—not A alone—to decide reaction energetics and stability.

1.A.1.14.22 Twelve fully solved numerical problems: nucleons, binding and isotope data

These twelve original examples test far more than the ability to rearrange A = Z + N. They use nuclide notation, electronic charge, isotope comparisons, reaction bookkeeping, real-mass distinctions, nuclear-size approximations and the analysis of labeled molecules. Each solution states the physical system, applies a suitable equation and interprets its limits. The numbers are illustrative where marked, and actual mass or reaction-energy calculations always use consistent physical inputs.

1.A.1.14.22.1 Worked example 1 — Find neutrons in iron-56

Question: How many neutrons are in ⁵⁶₂₆Fe? Known: A = 56, Z = 26. Calculation: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30 neutrons. The nucleus also has twenty-six protons. A neutral iron-56 atom has twenty-six electrons, but they are not included in A. This is a count, not a measured nuclear mass.

1.A.1.14.22.2 Worked example 2 — Interpret iron-57(III)

Question: For ⁵⁷₂₆Fe³⁺, find proton, neutron and electron numbers. Calculation: Protons = Z = 26; neutrons = 57 − 26 = 31; charge +3e means electrons = 26 − 3 = 23. The nuclide remains iron-57 in the 3+ ionic state. The electron count differs, but nuclear A remains 57.

1.A.1.14.22.3 Worked example 3 — Hydrogen isotope puzzle

Question: One hydrogen nuclide contains a proton and two neutrons. Name it and determine A. Method: Z = 1, N = 2, so A = 1 + 2 = 3. It is tritium, ³H, not helium-3 because the proton number remains one. Tritium is radioactive; this fact requires nuclear stability data rather than an arithmetic conclusion from A alone.

1.A.1.14.22.4 Worked example 4 — Isobars with the same mass number

Question: Compare ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N. Calculation: Both have A = 14. Carbon-14 has N = 14 − 6 = 8; nitrogen-14 has N = 14 − 7 = 7. They are isobars, not isotopes. Carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay into nitrogen-14 under the physical weak process, illustrating fixed A with changing element identity.

1.A.1.14.22.5 Worked example 5 — Deuteron mass defect

Question: Approximate deuteron binding energy using free proton 1.00727647 u, neutron 1.008664916 u and bare deuteron 2.013553213 u. Mass defect: Δm = 1.00727647 + 1.008664916 − 2.013553213 = 0.002388173 u. Binding energy: B ≈ Δm × 931.494 MeV/u ≈ 2.2246 MeV. A remains two; the difference is energetic, not a fractional nucleon loss.

1.A.1.14.22.6 Worked example 6 — Approximate nuclear radius ratio

Question: Using R ∝ A^(1/3), compare the radii of A = 56 and A = 16 nuclei. Calculation: R₅₆/R₁₆ ≈ (56/16)^(1/3) = 3.5^(1/3) ≈ 1.52. The heavier nucleus has 3.5 times the nucleon count but only about 1.52 times the radius in this approximate model. Actual nuclear radii require consistent experimental definitions.

1.A.1.14.22.7 Worked example 7 — Eightfold change in A

Question: In a uniform-density nuclear-size approximation, nucleus B has eight times nucleus A's nucleon count. Estimate radius and volume ratios. Method: R_B/R_A = 8^(1/3) = 2; V_B/V_A ≈ 2³ = 8. Do not predict an eightfold radius increase, and do not treat the formula as a precise statement for diffuse or halo nuclear distributions.

1.A.1.14.22.8 Worked example 8 — Chlorine isotope weighted mass (simplified)

Question: Hypothetical chlorine sample has 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl. Estimate average using nominal mass numbers 35 and 37. Calculation: 0.75×35 + 0.25×37 = 35.5. If masses were approximated as u values, this is an approximate mass in u, not A of a new isotope. Accurate chlorine atomic weight uses measured isotope masses and appropriate abundances.

1.A.1.14.22.9 Worked example 9 — Alpha daughter identification

Question: A ²²⁶₈₈Ra nucleus undergoes alpha emission. Identify its daughter. Calculation: Subtract alpha's A = 4 and Z = 2: daughter A = 222 and Z = 86. Element Z = 86 is radon, so daughter is ²²²₈₆Rn. Mass-number bookkeeping is independent of the exact kinetic energies carried away by products.

1.A.1.14.22.10 Worked example 10 — Balance a missing neutron

Question: Complete ⁹₄Be + ⁴₂He → ¹²₆C + X. Calculation: A_X = 9 + 4 − 12 = 1; Z_X = 4 + 2 − 6 = 0. Particle X is ¹₀n. A proton would have Z = 1, so charge conservation rejects that alternative. A fully physical reaction also has energy and momentum constraints.

1.A.1.14.22.11 Worked example 11 — Three radioactive half-lives

Question: A sample initially contains 8000 identical radioactive parent nuclei. How many are expected after three half-lives if no parents are added? Calculation: n = 8000×(1/2)³ = 1000 parent nuclei. This is an expected population count. The mass number A of every surviving undecayed nucleus stays the same; it is not divided by eight.

1.A.1.14.22.12 Worked example 12 — A doubly charged ¹³C mass shift

Question: A molecular ion with charge state +2 has one ¹²C atom replaced by ¹³C; estimate m/z separation, assuming identical adduct state and using isotope mass difference 1.003355 Da. Method: Δ(m/z) ≈ 1.003355/2 = 0.5016775 Th (where Th denotes Da per elementary-charge number in common mass-spectrometric usage). This is not the integer change A = +1 read directly as an exact 1.0000 m/z step.

1.A.1.14.22.13 Connect the solutions instead of memorizing their answers

Examples 1–4 use exact nuclear counts. Example 5 introduces measured mass defect, and examples 6–7 use an approximate geometric scaling law. Example 8 distinguishes a mixture average from one nucleus's A. Examples 9–10 use conservation in nuclear reactions, example 11 uses decay population kinetics, and example 12 uses analytical ion mass-to-charge. These are different models, each appropriate to a different scientific question.

Solution standard: Write what A counts, keep measured mass and A separate, check charge and nuclear balance independently, and state approximations for binding, geometry and isotope signals.
