1.A.1.15.1 A label on a laboratory vial: the puzzle that starts our journey

A laboratory technician receives two vials: one labelled 146C and the other 147N. Both have fourteen nucleons, but they are not the same element. In a separate experiment, a vial marked 136C has a carbon atom with a different neutron count. The notation is therefore not decoration; it is a compact scientific statement about nuclear identity. Reading it correctly lets you connect chemistry, biochemistry, isotope tracing, radioactivity and examination questions without guessing.

This chapter builds on Node 13 (atomic number) and Node 14 (mass number). We will work from first principles, practise decoding symbols before attempting transformations, and mark the limits of what any label alone can establish. The aim is fluent interpretation rather than memorisation of isolated examples.

Learning contract: At the end you should identify the element, count protons and neutrons, calculate electrons from ionic charge, classify isotopes/isobars/isotones, balance simple nuclear equations and critically interpret common biological isotope labels.

The scene: two identical-looking glass vials

Imagine that a research student arrives in a biochemistry laboratory. One vial contains ordinary glucose. Another contains glucose whose first carbon atom is enriched in carbon-13. Both look the same, taste is not a safe or useful test, and both take part in related chemical reactions. Yet a mass spectrometer can tell the two samples apart. A tiny label changes what the scientist can follow through a metabolic pathway. The label does not say how fast glucose will be consumed, which enzyme works on it, or whether the sample is radioactive. To answer those questions, we must first learn to read the label correctly.

Why the story begins with a nucleus

A chemical formula tells us which elements and how many atoms are in a molecule. A nuclide symbol goes one step deeper and tells us which nuclear version of an element is present. The element symbol identifies the proton number. The left superscript tells the total nucleon count. The left subscript makes the proton number explicit. With this information we can calculate neutrons. A separate charge mark can indicate how many electrons have been gained or removed by a monatomic ion. Each mark answers a different question.

Our route through the chapter

We will begin with the historical puzzle that made isotope notation necessary. We will then build the symbol from evidence, decode it slowly, compare related species, and learn to recognise information that the symbol cannot provide. Only after this foundation will we use it to read nuclear transformations and life-science experiments. A final workshop will ask us to solve realistic, mixed-concept problems rather than merely copy an equation.

One sentence to remember: A nuclide symbol is a compact description of nuclear identity, not a complete description of the atom's chemistry or the sample's history.

1.A.1.15.2 How scientists reached nuclide notation: a chronological story

The emergence of atomic-number and isotope concepts in early twentieth-century chemistry changed how scientists classified atoms. Atomic identity came to be linked to nuclear charge rather than apparent atomic mass. Once distinct nuclides of the same element were recognized, mass spectrometry, nuclear spectroscopy and radiotracers offered ways to study chemistry and biological processes at the level of individual isotopic species.

Modern notation encodes that history: X retains chemical identity, Z expresses the nuclear charge that defines it, A counts nucleons and supplementary charge or state indicators add context. The notation is a small example of a larger scientific principle: a standard symbol should preserve the relevant information while avoiding unsupported claims.

Stage 1: Dalton gave chemistry atoms, but not a nuclear address

Early atomic theory explained why elements combine in simple ratios. It treated atoms of one element as having the same basic identity. That was a powerful starting point for chemistry, but scientists did not yet know about protons, neutrons or a nuclear label. Modern isotope knowledge later refined the idea that atoms of the same element always have exactly the same mass.

Stage 2: electrons and the nucleus changed the picture

Thomson's electron work showed that atoms contain a light negatively charged constituent. Rutherford's scattering evidence showed that positive charge is concentrated in a small nucleus. These discoveries explain why a scientific label sometimes needs both nuclear information and electron charge information. The nucleus determines Z and A; ordinary chemical ionization changes the electronic count.

Stage 3: isotopes and atomic number solved two classification puzzles

Frederick Soddy introduced the isotope concept while studying radioactive substances whose chemical behaviour placed them in the same periodic-table position despite different masses. Henry Moseley's X-ray work showed the importance of nuclear charge and atomic number in arranging elements. Francis Aston's mass-spectrometric work demonstrated isotopes among many elements. The lesson is chronological as well as conceptual: same chemical element came to mean same proton number, not necessarily the same mass number.

Stage 4: the neutron completed the counting rule

When the neutron was established experimentally in 1932, scientists could express nuclear inventory clearly. A nucleus with Z protons and N neutrons has A = Z + N. This is why the two left-side numbers of modern notation are sufficient to deduce the third count. The mass number does not need to tell us the exact measured mass in atomic mass units.

Stage 5: standardized notation entered the life-science laboratory

Nuclear chemistry, radiotracing, mass spectrometry and modern biochemical isotope-labelling all use standard nuclear symbols. A laboratory record may show ¹³C, ¹⁴C, ²H or ³H, while an equation might show complete symbols with A and Z. The purpose is shared across disciplines: specify exactly which nuclear species is being discussed while keeping the notation brief.

1.A.1.15.3 What is a nuclide? The exact meaning behind the label

In IUPAC terminology a nuclide is an atomic species characterized by atomic number, mass number and nuclear energy state, provided the state's lifetime is long enough for observation. In ordinary introductory notation we usually identify it through A and Z; when energy states matter we add a metastable-state designation such as technetium-99m. Thus equal A and Z alone do not always distinguish a nuclear ground state from a nuclear isomer.

An element is defined by its proton number Z. An isotope is a nuclide of that element with a different number of neutrons. A nuclide is not identical in meaning to a neutral atom: the nucleus may be represented while the electron population is separately described through ionic charge.

Nuclide, isotope, radionuclide, ion

A radionuclide has an unstable nucleus that undergoes radioactive transformation. An ion is an atom or group carrying net electric charge due to electron gain or loss. These terms refer to different aspects of matter and can overlap: radioactive iodine-131 may be present chemically as iodide, I⁻.

Why ‘same element’ does not mean ‘same nuclide’

Place ¹²₆C beside ¹³₆C. Their proton number is identical. Therefore each belongs to carbon, and a neutral atom of either has the same number of electrons. Their neutron counts are six and seven, so they are different nuclides of the same element. The word isotopes describes the relationship between these nuclides. It does not erase their distinct identities.

A practical distinction between a nucleus and an atom

When we write ¹⁴₆C, we are specifying the nuclear species. If a problem says ‘neutral carbon-14 atom,’ the electron count is six. If the problem says ‘carbon-14 ion with charge +1,’ the electron count is five. The label itself does not force the atom to be neutral in every possible context; the actual electronic condition must be supplied or stated as an assumption.

A note on IUPAC precision

For most beginner calculations, A and Z identify the nuclear species well enough. Strictly, IUPAC's definition also mentions the nuclear energy state when it is observable. That is why ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc may need distinct symbols even though both are technetium with A = 99. The more precise scientific definition should not be discarded merely because introductory exercises ignore excited states.

Important distinction: The phrase ‘carbon atom’ may describe a chemical element, while ‘carbon-14 nuclide’ specifies a particular nuclear version of that element.

1.A.1.15.4 The grammar of a nuclear symbol: A, Z, X and charge

The element symbol X occupies the centre. The upper left number is mass number A; the lower left number is atomic number Z. If a charge is written, it appears at the upper right of the symbol. A chemical atom count, as in O₂, is a lower right subscript and has a wholly different meaning.

AZXq     A = Z + N     N = A − Z
Schematic only: an illustrative hypothetical C²⁻ ion, not a claim that such a free ion is routinely stable. Read the positions; they encode distinct quantities.
Exam trap: Do not place electron number at lower left, and never treat the upper-left A as the relative atomic mass printed in a periodic table.

Read from left to right, but decide what each position means

The left superscript is the mass number A. Think ‘all nucleons’. The left subscript is the atomic number Z. Think ‘protons’. The centre is the element symbol X. Think ‘which element’. A right superscript written as 2+, 3+ or − normally represents ionic charge in an atomic-species expression. In multiatom chemical formulas, subscripts on the right count atoms of the preceding element or group, not neutrons.

Standard shorthand is still rigorous when context is clear

One may write carbon-14, ¹⁴C or ¹⁴₆C in different contexts. Because carbon always has Z = 6, the left subscript is often omitted when no ambiguity is possible. For a learning problem, restoring Z helps show the calculation. When particles or nuclear reactions are involved, complete A/Z notation often makes conservation easier to check.

The order of digits is not negotiable

If a question describes ¹⁵₇N, do not turn it into ⁷₁₅N. The first represents 7 protons and 8 neutrons; the reversed form would demand an element of Z = 15 with mass number 7, which is impossible. Reading the vertical placement of a superscript and subscript is as important as recognising the letters.

1.A.1.15.5 Protons, neutrons and electrons: the four-step counting method

Let P be the proton count, N the neutron count and E the electron count. Z = P, A = P + N and so N = A − Z. For an isolated atomic ion with signed charge q in units of elementary charge, q = P − E, hence E = Z − q. A positive q means electrons have been removed, while a negative q means electrons have been added.

P = Z   |   N = A − Z   |   E = Z − q

For 2713Al3+, protons = 13, neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14, electrons = 13 − 3 = 10. For 3517Cl−, protons = 17, neutrons = 18 and electrons = 17 − (−1) = 18. Neutral 3517Cl has 17 electrons. Nuclear identity does not change when an atom is ionized.

Four-step algorithm: ① Read X and confirm Z using the periodic table. ② Compute N = A − Z. ③ If charge is absent, interpret a neutral atom in elementary atomic examples, otherwise use E = Z − q. ④ Check physical sense: A and Z are non-negative integers with A ≥ Z; E cannot be negative.

Count the nucleus first, electrons second

A useful exam habit is to create three empty boxes: P, N and E. First fill P = Z from the symbol. Next fill N = A − Z. Only then ask about the total charge and fill E. This separates nuclear bookkeeping from electron bookkeeping and prevents the most frequent error in mixed questions.

Why the sign of the ion matters

A sodium atom with eleven protons and eleven electrons is neutral. If it loses one electron, it has eleven positive nuclear charges and ten electron negative charges. Its net charge is +1, not −1. If a chlorine atom gains an electron, its electrons outnumber its protons by one and its net charge is −1. The formula E = Z − q works for both cases when q is signed: +1, +2, −1 and so on.

What about a polyatomic ion?

In NH₄⁺ or SO₄²⁻ the total charge belongs to the whole molecular ion. It is generally incorrect to assign every atom that full charge and count its electrons using E = Z − q. Nuclear isotope labels on specific atoms still tell us each nucleus's P and N, but electron distribution in bonded molecules is a separate chemical model. The simple E = Z − q rule applies directly to a single-atom species.

For an atomic ion:   Z = P;   A = P + N;   q/e = P − E;   E = Z − q/e

Here q/e is the numerical charge in units of the elementary positive charge. In classroom notation we usually shorten it to q, so E = Z − q. If the symbol is Na⁺, q = +1; if Cl⁻, q = −1.

Important distinction: A neutral molecule does not necessarily have each atom in a neutral electronic state. Atomic oxidation numbers and formal charges are accounting conventions and should not be substituted directly for measured partial charges.

1.A.1.15.6 How to write a full nuclide symbol from a written question

Suppose a question says “an aluminium ion with thirteen protons, fourteen neutrons and charge +3”. The element is Al, since Z = 13. A = 13 + 14 = 27. Place 27 above-left, 13 below-left and 3+ above-right: 2713Al3+. Reverse-check E = 10. Similarly, a species with twenty protons, twenty neutrons and eighteen electrons is 4020Ca2+.

How to catch an inconsistent statement

If a question simultaneously claims an atom has 8 protons and is nitrogen, its premises disagree: Z = 8 identifies oxygen. If the statement says A = 10, Z = 12, the implied neutron count is −2 and the symbol is physically invalid. The correct response is to flag the contradiction rather than fabricate a nuclide.

Translate everyday English into nuclear symbols

Sentence: ‘A neutral oxygen atom with eight neutrons.’ Oxygen is O and has Z = 8. Its neutrons are N = 8. Add to find A = 16. Write ¹⁶₈O. Neutrality gives E = 8. To reverse the process, read back: oxygen, eight protons, eight neutrons and eight electrons.

Translate a more difficult ion problem

Sentence: ‘An ion has 16 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons.’ The element is sulfur because Z = 16. Mass number is A = 16 + 18 = 34. The numerical ionic charge is q = 16 − 18 = −2. The complete answer is ³⁴₁₆S²⁻. Check that charge has been placed on the right, not in the place reserved for the neutron count.

Write an answer that a marker can evaluate

For a CSIR-NET numerical, show the three intermediate steps before your final nuclide. Writing only ³⁴S²⁻ might be enough for a multiple-choice option, but showing Z = 16, A = 34 and q = −2 demonstrates reasoning and makes it easier to detect a sign error.

The three samples have the same Z and thus belong to the element carbon. Neutron variation changes nuclear mass, stability and some physical properties, but they share the electron number when neutral. Biologically their chemical behaviour is closely related, which helps explain why isotope labels can trace carbon pathways. In real isotope-fractionation studies their small mass-dependent differences still matter.

The name carbon-14 is a convenient hyphen notation equivalent to ¹⁴C; the lower-left 6 can be omitted when carbon is already unambiguously identified. Omitting Z from a symbol is not permission to forget the proton number.

Follow the same carbon identity through three nuclei

Start with ¹²₆C. Six protons identify carbon; the remaining 12 − 6 = 6 nucleons are neutrons. Next add one neutron without changing protons: ¹³₆C. Add another: ¹⁴₆C. This imaginary counting sequence helps visualize the isotope family, but it is a conceptual comparison—not a practical method for making isotopes. The element has not changed because Z stays six.

A small neutron change can matter enormously

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive. That difference comes from nuclear stability, not from a change in carbon's ordinary electron count. The carbon-14 nuclide can be incorporated into organic molecules but may later transform by beta decay. The label allows a biologist to distinguish a nucleus, while the choice of detection method depends on whether the isotope is stable or radioactive.

Do not overinterpret ‘chemically similar’

Isotopes of the same element share the same nuclear charge and, for corresponding neutral atoms, the same electron count. Their chemical reactions are often similar. However isotopic substitution changes mass-dependent motion and can produce kinetic or equilibrium isotope effects. Therefore ‘same chemical identity’ is not the same as ‘identical reaction rate under all conditions’.

1.A.1.15.8 Hydrogen's three named isotopes: protium, deuterium and tritium

Hydrogen provides an unusually clear contrast: 11H (protium) has zero neutrons; 21H (deuterium, D) has one; 31H (tritium, T) has two. All have Z = 1. D and T are conventional isotope symbols used in scientific practice; where confusion is possible write ²H and ³H. Deuterium is stable while tritium is radioactive.

Heavy water D₂O contains deuterium rather than ordinary protium at the hydrogen sites. It is a molecular formula, not a nuclide label; the 2 after D indicates two deuterium atoms per molecule. A learner who distinguishes left nuclear labels from right stoichiometric counts can now read both notations correctly.

Three familiar names, one proton number

Protium (¹H), deuterium (²H or D) and tritium (³H or T) all have one proton. Their neutron numbers are zero, one and two. The same element identity is maintained, but the neutron count changes. Hydrogen is uniquely convenient for teaching this concept because the isotope names are widely used in chemistry and biology.

Where the notation appears in actual molecules

H₂O, D₂O and HDO are different isotopic compositions of water. In D₂O, the right-hand subscript 2 counts deuterium atoms. It does not indicate that each deuterium nucleus has two neutrons: ²H has one neutron. In the symbol ²H, by contrast, the left superscript 2 counts one proton plus one neutron.

Why stable isotopes can be powerful in biology

Deuterium and many other stable isotope labels can be tracked by physical measurement without relying on radioactivity. For example, carefully designed experiments can use isotope enrichment to infer turnover or synthesis. Yet the mere fact that a sample is labelled does not prove a metabolic flux. Quantitative interpretation needs a model, a measured isotope pattern and experimental controls.

1.A.1.15.9 Ions, molecular subscripts and excited nuclear states

Compare 5626Fe2+ and 5626Fe3+. Both nuclei have 26 protons and 30 neutrons. Their electron counts differ: 24 and 23. Compare O₂ versus 168O: the former specifies two atoms in a molecule; the latter specifies one nuclide with 8 protons and 8 neutrons. A superscript before an element symbol encodes a mass number; a superscript after it commonly encodes net ionic charge.

Nuclear isomers demand another axis. ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc have the same A and Z but different nuclear energy states; “m” denotes a metastable state. They are not different elements and not different isotopes. When the excited state or its lifetime is important, writing only A and Z loses information.

Exam trap: The ionic charge written on a chemical species must not be confused with the charge of the nucleus, +Ze. A neutral atom still has a positively charged nucleus.

A thought experiment: remove electrons from ⁵⁶Fe

Start with one neutral ⁵⁶₂₆Fe atom: 26 protons, 30 neutrons and 26 electrons. Remove two electrons: ⁵⁶₂₆Fe²⁺. Its nucleus has not changed. Remove one more: ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺. Again, A and Z are exactly the same. The difference lies in the electron count and often the chemistry of coordination and redox state. This is the cleanest way to separate isotope identity from oxidation state.

Right superscript has several notational contexts

In a symbol for an isolated ion, a right superscript may be used for net charge. In nuclear spectroscopy a star or state label may mark excitation. In a compound name, parentheses with Roman numerals often indicate oxidation state. The placement and surrounding context tell the reader which convention is being used. It is unsafe to assume every raised right-hand mark answers the same question.

When the nucleus itself is excited

A metastable nuclear isomer such as technetium-99m has an excited nuclear state with a sufficiently long lifetime to observe. The letter m modifies the nuclide identity at the nuclear level, not the electron count. An isomeric transition typically emits gamma radiation or conversion electrons as the nucleus de-excites; A and Z can stay unchanged. This is not equivalent to losing an ordinary outer-shell electron in chemical ionization.

Think of A and Z as coordinates: movement at constant Z produces isotopes; movement at constant A produces isobars. Isotones share N = A − Z, so the relationship is diagonal if A is plotted against Z. This geometric interpretation makes classification easier in numerical questions.

For example, 146C has N = 8; 157N also has N = 8. They are isotones, not isotopes. Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14, meanwhile, are isobars despite having different electron structures and chemical identities.

An easy three-question identification routine

Put two labels beside one another and ask: ‘Same protons?’ If yes, they are isotopes when A differs. If not, ask: ‘Same nucleon count?’ If yes, they are isobars. If not, compute N = A − Z for both. If N is the same, they are isotones. Finally, if both A and Z are identical but an excitation-state label differs, nuclear isomers may be involved.

Avoid the false statement ‘every pair fits one category’

Some nuclide pairs share none of Z, A or N. For example, ¹²C (Z = 6, N = 6) and ²³Na (Z = 11, N = 12) are neither isotopes, isobars nor isotones. They are simply different nuclides. The categories describe specific mathematical relationships, not a forced classification of every possible pair.

Why classification comes up in molecular biology

When stable isotope labels are used in proteins or metabolites, a researcher first identifies the labelled nuclide (for example ¹³C or ¹⁵N). These labels belong to different elements, so they are not isotopes of each other despite both being stable tracers. A molecule can contain multiple isotope labels; the word ‘isotope’ always refers to one element's nuclides.

1.A.1.15.11 Mass number versus atomic mass and periodic-table averages

A periodic table identifies an element by Z and ordinarily shows a relative atomic mass, often a weighted average reflecting terrestrial isotopic composition. That displayed decimal, for instance carbon around 12.01, is not a mass number for a single carbon atom. An experimental sample may contain a different isotope mixture; the average is not a universal fixed integer.

When notation supplies X and A but omits Z, find Z from the element identity. For ²³Na, sodium has Z = 11 and thus N = 12. If supplied only ²³X with no defined X, the proton and neutron counts are underdetermined. Correct scientific reasoning includes recognizing when the given information is insufficient.

Exam trap: Do not round a periodic-table relative atomic mass to “discover” a unique nuclide. A natural chlorine sample contains mostly ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl; the reported average cannot tell you the isotope of an individual atom.

Mass number is not a scale reading

Mass number 12 for carbon-12 means that the nucleus contains twelve nucleons. The mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom is exactly 12 unified atomic mass units by definition of the unit. However, this convenient equality for that particular atom does not mean every nuclide has a mass in u numerically equal to A. Nuclear binding energy and constituent masses prevent such a general rule.

A decimal on the periodic table belongs to a different question

For a sample of elemental chlorine with approximately 75.8% ³⁵Cl and 24.2% ³⁷Cl atoms, a simple weighted average using integer masses would be about 35.484, not 35 or 37. Real standard atomic-weight calculations use precise isotopic masses and measured proportions, so the published value is not exactly obtained from rounding mass numbers. A decimal average characterizes the mixture, while a nuclide symbol specifies one nuclear species.

Do not infer abundance from the label

A notation such as ¹³C gives an isotope identity but not its concentration. To know that a sample is ‘99% ¹³C-enriched’ requires additional abundance information. To decide how strong a mass-spectrometry signal should be also requires knowing experimental ion formation, sample concentration and the instrument response.

1.A.1.15.12 Isotope abundance, average mass and molecular isotope patterns

From a single symbol to a population of atoms

So far every nuclide symbol has described a kind of nucleus. Real vials usually contain enormous numbers of atoms. The composition of a sample is therefore described by fractions, such as f₁₂, f₁₃ and f₁₄ for different carbon nuclides. Each fraction must be between zero and one and the fractions must sum to one. The notation ¹³C identifies a nuclide; it does not give f₁₃. To speak about enrichment we need both identity and abundance.

A worked weighted-average calculation

Take a simplified two-isotope sample with 80.0% atoms of a species whose isotopic mass is 10.0 u and 20.0% of a species whose isotopic mass is 11.0 u. The mean mass per atom in that model is 0.800 × 10.0 + 0.200 × 11.0 = 10.2 u. This is an abundance-weighted average. It is not a newly invented nuclide with mass number 10.2; no single nucleus can have a fractional nucleon count.

How isotope patterns appear in a molecular spectrum

Suppose a molecule contains two carbon atoms. In an intentionally simplified model where the fraction of ¹³C is p and the atoms are independently sampled, the probability of zero ¹³C substitutions is (1 − p)², of exactly one is 2p(1 − p), and of two is p². These are binomial terms. Real MS interpretation may need many isotopes, exact isotope masses, charge states and instrument effects. The model shows why a sample-level pattern cannot be read directly from one nucleus's A.

1.A.1.15.13 What a nuclear label can and cannot tell us

A properly specified nuclear notation provides a counting identity; it does not on its own reveal half-life, natural abundance, nuclear spin, chemical oxidation state in a compound, molecular bonding, measured atomic mass or biological toxicity. The element symbol and charge provide hints about electron population, but electron configuration still requires quantum-mechanical rules and may depend on state and environment.

A symbol also does not guarantee stability. Carbon-12 is stable; carbon-14 is radioactive. Stable versus unstable is an experimentally established nuclear property, not simply the answer to A − Z. Many practical nuclide charts include a separate half-life or stability classification.

Critical reading: Give the shortest conclusion warranted by the label. “¹⁴C has six protons and eight neutrons” follows directly; “this sample has a certain activity” does not, because amount, activity and decay history are missing.

Ask exactly which layer of reality the question addresses

A nuclide symbol records nuclear composition. A chemical formula records which atoms join a molecular species. A structural formula records connectivity. A mass spectrum records responses of ions at particular mass-to-charge ratios. An isotope tracing curve records time-dependent measured abundance. These are complementary scientific languages, and none can replace all the others.

A rigorous example of insufficient information

Suppose an exam says: ‘An unknown atomic species has mass number 24. Find the protons.’ Without Z, X or another independent clue, the answer cannot be unique: several nuclides may share a mass number. If it instead says ‘magnesium-24’, Z = 12 from the periodic table, so P = 12 and N = 12. The difference between the two questions is the independent element identity.

Important distinction: No label alone supplies an experiment's dose, radiation activity, half-life, isotope fraction, biological location, or safety classification.

1.A.1.15.14 Read a chart of nuclides using N and Z

Move from a line of text to a two-dimensional map

Imagine a graph with neutron number N along the horizontal axis and proton number Z along the vertical axis. Every permitted combination of N and Z corresponds to a possible nuclear species. Nuclides of one element lie along a horizontal row because Z is fixed. Isotones form a column of fixed N. Isobars satisfy N + Z = A and therefore lie along a descending diagonal.

Why stability is not a single simple line

Light stable nuclei often have similar numbers of protons and neutrons; many heavier stable nuclei require more neutrons than protons. The balance reflects competing nuclear forces and electrostatic repulsion, plus pairing and shell effects. No elementary rule such as ‘N must always equal Z’ can decide whether every nuclide is stable. The nuclide map is therefore empirical as well as mathematical.

Look at ¹²C, ¹³C and ¹⁴C on the map

All three have Z = 6. Their N coordinates are 6, 7 and 8, respectively. Moving across the row changes the isotope without changing element identity. If you move from ¹⁴C (N = 8, Z = 6) to ¹⁴N (N = 7, Z = 7) along a constant-A diagonal, you have increased Z by one and decreased N by one—precisely the nuclear accounting in β⁻ decay.

Schematic N–Z view. Carbon isotopes share Z = 6; ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N lie on the same A = 14 diagonal. This illustration is an educational extract, not a complete nuclide chart.

A nuclide chart lets students recognise a relationship in space. First identify the axes and what is held constant; then connect movement on the chart to A, Z or N. It is never enough to look at a coloured box and declare a nuclide radioactive without knowing the legend and evidence.

1.A.1.15.15 Nuclear stability and isomeric states without guesswork

The notation is a starting address, not a complete energy diagram

Two nuclei can have the same proton and neutron counts yet occupy different nuclear energy states. They may emit radiation when moving from a higher to a lower state. If an excited state lasts long enough to be identified separately, it can be marked with m, m1 or related state notation in nuclear data tables. Both states retain the same underlying element and nucleon count.

A stable label can be used without radioactivity

The practical difference between ¹³C and ¹⁴C is important. Carbon-13 is stable and commonly observed through NMR and isotope-sensitive MS. Carbon-14 undergoes radioactive decay and can also be measured by accelerator mass spectrometry in suitable research contexts. A scientific method is not determined solely by the left superscript: it depends on the experiment, sample and available instrumentation.

Three nuclear questions and three different sources of evidence

Question 1: ‘How many neutrons are there?’ Answer from A and Z. Question 2: ‘Is this nuclide stable?’ Answer from evaluated nuclear data and established experimental knowledge. Question 3: ‘What energy or radiation is involved?’ Answer from nuclear level and decay data. Confusing these sources produces explanations that sound confident but are not logically justified.

Reader challenge: Given ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc, determine what can be said for certain: both are technetium, both have A = 99, both have the same Z and N. Whether they occupy the same nuclear energy state? No; that is exactly what the m designation distinguishes.

1.A.1.15.16 A visual step-by-step decoding flow

Decision flow: decode counts first; use the counts to classify or compare nuclides second.

This is the same discipline used when reading a metabolic tracer label. Determine exactly which isotope or atom is being tracked before interpreting an experiment. An isotope label is a bookkeeping tool for atoms, not automatically a statement about radioactivity or health risk.

Make the diagram a habit, not a decoration

When a new nuclide symbol appears, pause and point to each position. Say aloud: ‘upper-left: A; lower-left: Z; centre: element; upper-right: ion charge’. Then write the three particles below it. Do this in the same order on every exercise. Routine reduces working-memory load so the difficult part of an exam can be the reasoning, not remembering which corner a number belongs in.

A simple visual boundary between nuclear and chemical data

Draw an imaginary vertical line through the central element symbol. The left-side labels identify nuclear composition. The right side often expresses a chemical condition such as an atom count or ion charge, depending on whether it is written high or low. This boundary is an aid to beginners, not a universal grammar for all complex spectroscopic notation.

1.A.1.15.17 Nuclear equations and conservation of A and Z

A nuclear reaction is written with nuclide labels so the total nucleon number A and nuclear charge number Z can be balanced across each side. Beta decay illustrates why elemental identity can change even when A remains fixed. In β⁻ decay, a neutron transforms into a proton; an electron and an electron antineutrino are emitted. Carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + anti-neutrino. A stays 14 and nuclear Z increases from 6 to 7.

In alpha decay an alpha particle (42He nucleus) leaves the parent: 23892U → 23490Th + 42He. Both A (238 = 234 + 4) and Z (92 = 90 + 2) balance. In β⁺ decay a proton transforms into a neutron while a positron and an electron neutrino are emitted, reducing nuclear Z by one with A unchanged.

Exam trap: Conserving A and Z is a necessary introductory check, not a complete proof that a reaction is energetically allowed. Charge, lepton number, momentum and energy must also be respected.

First learn the conservation test, then learn its limits

Nuclear equations are not balanced by counting valence electrons as in an ordinary chemical reaction. Instead, sum the mass numbers A of nuclear species on each side. Then sum the nuclear charge numbers Z, while including the charge numbers of emitted particles when required. A balanced pair of totals is a strong first check. It does not prove that a reaction is energetically allowed or occurs under ordinary conditions.

Read beta-minus decay as a story inside the nucleus

In β⁻ decay, a neutron converts into a proton through the weak interaction. The nucleus gains one proton without changing the total number of nucleons. The emitted beta electron and antineutrino carry away charge and lepton number appropriately. For ¹⁴₆C, the daughter is ¹⁴₇N. Carbon has changed to nitrogen because Z changed from six to seven. Notice that this is a nuclear transformation, not an ionization event.

Compare alpha, beta-plus and electron capture

Alpha emission removes a helium-4 nucleus from the parent and reduces parent A by four and Z by two. In β⁺ decay, Z decreases by one while A stays constant, with a positron and neutrino emitted. In electron capture, an inner-shell electron is captured and a proton converts to a neutron; Z falls by one and A stays the same, with a neutrino emitted. Different decay modes leave different signatures in nuclide notation.

1.A.1.15.18 Carbon-14 in life science and radiocarbon chronology

Carbon enters living systems through carbon-containing molecules. While alive, organisms exchange carbon with the environment; after death, exchange largely ceases and incorporated ¹⁴C gradually decays. Radiocarbon dating compares the sample's remaining ¹⁴C to a reference, with corrections and calibration needed for real-world age estimates. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years. This method is most useful for certain comparatively recent organic remains rather than for dating very ancient rocks.

N(t) = N₀ × (1/2)t / t½   ;   t½(¹⁴C) ≈ 5,730 years

After one ideal half-life, 50% of the original ¹⁴C remains; after two, 25%. This does not mean 50% of all carbon in a bone disappears in one half-life: ¹⁴C is only a tiny fraction of natural carbon. Real dating depends on background correction, contamination control and calibration curves.

In metabolism, ¹³C-labelled glucose can be traced using mass spectrometry or NMR, while ¹⁴C-labelled compounds can be followed through radioactivity detection in appropriately controlled laboratory systems. The isotope notation tells us which nucleus was labelled; positional labelling (such as [1-¹³C]glucose) additionally specifies the carbon position within the molecule.

Stage 1: carbon moves through a living system

Carbon dioxide from the environment enters photosynthetic organisms, and carbon atoms then move through food chains and organic molecules. Naturally occurring radiocarbon can join this biological exchange along with stable isotopes. The isotope label distinguishes one nuclear species, but the chemical pathways do not carry a special ‘radioactive arrow’ for the student to see; a measurement is needed.

Stage 2: exchange changes when an organism dies

For material that once exchanged carbon with its environment, that exchange greatly decreases after death. Radiocarbon remaining in suitable sample material decays while stable carbon remains stable. Scientists can compare the remaining radiocarbon signal with models and reference standards to estimate time since carbon exchange ended. This is not a simple universal stopwatch for every object.

Stage 3: why calibration and contamination matter

The atmospheric amount of ¹⁴C has not been constant through history. A realistic measurement must account for calibration data, sample contamination, chemical preparation, background and the sample's history. Some reservoirs such as old dissolved carbon can produce apparent-age differences. The introductory half-life formula teaches decay mathematics; it is not a complete dating protocol.

Calculate before interpreting

If an ideal isolated sample begins with N₀ radioactive nuclei, after t = 11,460 years (two periods of 5,730 years) N = N₀/4. The fraction is 25%. It does not mean that one quarter of the sample's entire carbon mass remains; almost all natural carbon in the sample may be stable ¹²C and ¹³C.

1.A.1.15.19 Hydrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and iodine in biology

Deuterium (²H) and ¹³C are commonly used as non-radioactive stable-isotope tracers in metabolic flux studies. Tritium (³H), phosphorus-32 (³²P), sulfur-35 (³⁵S) and iodine-131 (¹³¹I) are radionuclides with specialized scientific or clinical uses. Stable labels are typically detected through isotope-sensitive analytical methods; radioactive labels are detected through their decay emissions.

In nucleic acids, phosphorus belongs to the phosphate backbone, so phosphorus isotope labels can probe phosphate-containing material. Sulfur can be found in amino acids such as cysteine and methionine, aiding certain protein-oriented tracing experiments. Iodine isotope applications depend on iodine's biological uptake and the medical context; a label never provides a stand-alone indication for treatment.

Safety boundary: This is conceptual exam preparation only. Radioisotope work requires licensed, trained personnel, radiation protection and institutionally approved procedures; no practical handling or exposure protocol is provided here.

A protein-lab thought experiment

Imagine a biochemist is comparing labelled amino acid incorporation into newly synthesised protein. One experiment uses a stable isotope label in a carbon or nitrogen position; another uses a radioisotope label detected by radiation. Both require a defined question: which molecule carries the label, where is the label located, how much label was added, and which measured signal corresponds to incorporation? The nuclide notation resolves only the first part.

Phosphorus and sulfur show different biochemical pathways

A phosphorus label may be associated with nucleotides, phosphorylated metabolites or phospholipids, while sulfur labels can be associated with molecules containing sulfur such as cysteine and methionine. The isotope does not magically select DNA or protein on its own. Specificity depends on which labelled precursor is supplied, the biochemical pathway and the analytical method.

Iodine and medical context

Iodine nuclides are useful examples because iodine chemistry and biological uptake can be important in thyroid-related diagnostics or treatment. However a notation such as ¹³¹I or ¹²³I by itself is not a prescription. The same warning applies to technetium-99m: isotope identity is only one part of a carefully controlled medical procedure.

1.A.1.15.20 Stable isotope labels, isotopologues and mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry detects mass-to-charge ratios of ions, not “mass numbers” in the simple nuclear-notation sense. A molecule containing one ¹³C instead of ¹²C is about 1.00335 unified atomic mass units heavier (for the isotope substitution itself), and the exact isotopic pattern depends on the whole molecular composition. Thus a ¹³C-labelled metabolite may show a shifted isotopologue peak without being radioactive.

Carefully distinguish an isotopologue (a molecule differing in isotopic composition) from an isotopomer (same molecular isotope counts but different positions of labels). In a molecule with multiple carbon positions, the phrase “one ¹³C label” does not identify which carbon is labelled. By contrast, [1-¹³C] specifies one labelled carbon position according to a molecule's numbering convention.

At CSIR-NET level, interpret whether a question concerns an individual nucleus (A, Z, N), a whole molecular ion (mass-to-charge, net charge) or the positional distribution of isotope tags. Switching contexts unnoticed is a frequent source of wrong answers.

An isotopologue is a molecular species, not merely a nuclear symbol

Consider two glucose molecules with the same arrangement of atoms. If one carbon atom in one molecule is ¹³C instead of ¹²C, the two molecules differ in isotopic composition. They can be called isotopologues. A nuclide label specifies the substituted nuclear species; the molecular description is needed to identify the full isotopologue.

Position can change interpretation even when total label count is the same

In two molecules of glucose, one ¹³C atom may be at carbon 1 in the first and carbon 6 in the second. Both contain one heavy carbon and have essentially the same total isotopic mass, but the position can have different consequences in a tracer experiment when pathways rearrange carbon atoms. Positional details are not recoverable from a statement that merely says ‘one ¹³C atom is present’.

Mass-to-charge is an instrument observation, not the symbol's mass number

Mass spectrometry separates or measures ions according to their mass-to-charge ratios (m/z). The z in this instrument convention denotes the ion's charge number, not nuclear atomic number Z in nuclide notation. Mass-resolving capability and isotope pattern analysis can reveal isotope substitutions; they do not directly report a cell's metabolic flux without a biochemical model.

1.A.1.15.21 Six complete biological tracer case studies

Case 1 — Where did glucose carbon go?

A researcher feeds cells a carbon-labelled glucose substrate and later measures labelled lactate. Detecting labelled carbon in lactate supports the idea that substrate-derived carbon entered the observed product pool. The notation identifies the isotopic atoms; a quantitative conclusion about pathway flux needs a suitable model, time series and isotopologue analysis.

In an exam, separate tracer origin from reaction rate. Presence of a labelled atom alone does not determine absolute flux.

Case 2 — Which carbon of glucose was labelled?

Two samples each contain one ¹³C label per glucose molecule. In the first, label is at carbon 1; in the second, label is at carbon 6. The overall mass shift is similar, but pathways that split or rearrange carbons can place the label in different products.

A full answer requires positional information such as [1-¹³C]glucose, not merely the statement ‘¹³C glucose’.

Case 3 — Following nitrogen into amino acids

An experiment introduces a ¹⁵N-labelled nitrogen source. Later detection of ¹⁵N in amino acids indicates incorporation of that nitrogen into the measured amino-acid pool. Interpretation depends on precursor enrichment, exchange reactions, sampling time and pathway biology.

¹⁵N is an isotope of nitrogen, not of carbon. ¹⁵N and ¹⁴C are not isotopes of one another even though both are used as labels.

Case 4 — Water as a stable isotope probe

An experiment uses deuterium-labelled water to follow hydrogen incorporation into newly synthesized material. The isotope notation tells us which hydrogen nucleus is involved, while chemical structure and a measured isotopic enrichment pattern are necessary to determine what molecule was synthesized.

Do not read the molecular formula D₂O as if each D had two neutrons. D means ²H; its nucleus has one neutron.

Case 5 — A phosphate-associated radioactive label

A historical biochemical study uses a labelled phosphate precursor and detects radioactivity in phosphate-containing molecules. The nuclear symbol such as ³²P identifies phosphorus-32, but detection in any particular biomolecule depends on actual transfer pathways and sample isolation.

The label does not, by itself, mean the radioactive atom is part of DNA rather than another phosphorus-containing metabolite.

Case 6 — A dated organic specimen

A suitable ancient wood sample is analyzed for its residual ¹⁴C relative to appropriate references. If the result is interpreted in terms of radioactive decay, time since atmospheric exchange ceased may be estimated with calibration. The notation is necessary but not sufficient for a date.

The carbon-14 half-life is not the age of a specimen. It is a property of the nuclide, while age is inferred from a measured fraction plus assumptions and correction steps.

These stories connect the same nucleus label to very different scientific questions: identity, carbon fate, nitrogen incorporation, synthesis, radioactive tracing and chronology. The correct workflow is always read the symbol → identify the biological question → choose the relevant measurement → interpret only what the evidence supports.

1.A.1.15.22 Twelve worked calculations from foundational to advanced

Worked example 1. Carbon-14 neutral atom

Given ¹⁴₆C, P = 6; N = 14 − 6 = 8; E = 6. Its radioactive identity is a separate nuclear fact, not computed from electron count.

Worked example 2. Nitrogen-15 neutral atom

¹⁵₇N gives P = 7, N = 8, E = 7. Compare with ¹⁴₆C: both have N = 8 and are isotones.

Worked example 3. Sodium-23 cation

²³₁₁Na⁺ has P = 11, N = 12, E = 10. The plus sign means loss of one electron.

Worked example 4. Chlorine-37 anion

³⁷₁₇Cl⁻ has P = 17, N = 20, E = 18. Gaining an electron does not alter A.

Worked example 5. Iron-56 divalent cation

⁵⁶₂₆Fe²⁺ has P = 26, N = 30 and E = 24.

Worked example 6. Calcium species from particle counts

20 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons: Z = 20, A = 40 and q = +2; notation ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺.

Worked example 7. Phosphorus-32

³²₁₅P has N = 17, P = 15, E = 15 if neutral. Its applications cannot be derived from the symbol alone.

Worked example 8. Uranium-235 ion

²³⁵₉₂U⁴⁺ has N = 143, P = 92 and E = 88. Nuclear properties are not changed by ordinary loss of four electrons.

Worked example 9. Impossible proposed notation

A = 11 and Z = 12 gives N = −1. This is not a physical nucleus; identify inconsistent data rather than infer a new element.

Worked example 10. One isotope versus another

¹³₆C and ¹⁴₆C have the same Z but N = 7 and 8; they are isotopes. Their mass numbers differ by one.

Worked example 11. Same mass number, different species

¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N have A = 14 but different Z: isobars. The latter has one fewer neutron.

Worked example 12. Beta-minus carbon transformation

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + anti-neutrino: A is unchanged, nuclear Z rises by one; the outgoing beta particle is not an orbital electron peeled off the atom.

1.A.1.15.23 Eight integrated exam problems with model solutions

Use the following mixed-concept problems as short-answer examinations. Unlike one-step exercises, each question asks for a calculation and an explanation. Solve them in writing before opening the model solutions.

Integrated problem 1. Problem A — Two equal mass numbers

An unknown specimen contains ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N. Compare P, N and E for neutral atoms and explain why one name cannot be replaced by the other.

Model reasoning

Carbon-14: P = 6, N = 8, E = 6. Nitrogen-14: P = 7, N = 7, E = 7. A is 14 in both; Z changes, so they are isobars and have different element identities.

Integrated problem 2. Problem B — Ion and isotope together

Compare neutral ⁵⁶₂₆Fe with ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺. Identify what changed physically, and what did not.

Model reasoning

The nucleus remains 26 protons and 30 neutrons. Electron number falls from 26 to 23; hence ionic charge becomes +3. Neither a new isotope nor a different element was made.

Integrated problem 3. Problem C — Find a missing element

A neutral nuclide has A = 31 and N = 16. Write the full label and calculate E.

Model reasoning

Z = A − N = 31 − 16 = 15. Atomic number 15 is phosphorus (P), so the nuclide is ³¹₁₅P and the neutral atom has E = 15.

Integrated problem 4. Problem D — Name the relationship

A has Z = 8, A = 18. B has Z = 9, A = 19. Decide if they are isotopes, isobars, isotones or none.

Model reasoning

A has N = 18 − 8 = 10. B has N = 19 − 9 = 10. These are isotones. Different Z prevents an isotope relationship; different A prevents an isobar relationship.

Integrated problem 5. Problem E — One MS peak is not a pathway

The mass spectrum of a metabolite contains an M+1 isotope peak. Can a student conclude that the metabolite was made directly from one specifically labelled glucose carbon?

Model reasoning

No. An M+1 pattern alone is not a unique path or position assignment. Natural isotope abundance, other labelled precursors, ion chemistry, measurement conditions and possible isotopologues must be examined.

Integrated problem 6. Problem F — Nuclear versus chemical change

A sample changes from ²³Na to ²³Na⁺ in a chemical process. In another process ¹⁴C changes to ¹⁴N by beta-minus decay. Which step changes Z?

Model reasoning

Sodium ionization only changes electrons; Z = 11 remains. Carbon-14 beta-minus decay changes one neutron into a proton; daughter Z = 7 and the element changes to nitrogen.

Integrated problem 7. Problem G — Missing evidence

A vial is labelled ¹³C. A report says the vial is 99% isotopically enriched and highly radioactive. Which claims follow from notation alone?

Model reasoning

Neither numerical enrichment nor high radioactivity follows. The label identifies carbon-13, which is a stable nuclide; the stated enrichment requires measurement or certificate and ‘highly radioactive’ is not implied.

Integrated problem 8. Problem H — One half-life interpretation

A particular isolated radioactive tracer originally contains 8,000 ¹⁴C atoms. In a simple decay expectation model, approximately how many ¹⁴C atoms remain after one and two half-lives?

Model reasoning

Expected remaining counts are approximately 4,000 after one half-life and 2,000 after two. Actual radioactive decay is stochastic; the simple halves are expected fractions in this model, not guaranteed exact per-sample counts.

For high-scoring answers, state the definition, show the numerical relationship, use correct notation, and write one clear sentence distinguishing what is known from what is merely assumed.

1.A.1.15.24 Twenty-eight CSIR-NET and SET-style questions with explanations

Attempt each question on paper before revealing the answer. These are original practice items modelled on relevant concepts; they are not presented as past examination questions.

Question 1. For ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺, how many electrons are present?

Answer: 10

Reason: Z − q = 13 − 3 = 10. Neither A nor N counts electrons.

Question 2. Which pair represents isotopes: ¹²C/¹⁴C or ¹⁴C/¹⁴N?

Answer: ¹²C and ¹⁴C

Reason: Isotopes share Z = 6 but differ in A.

Question 3. What is N in ³¹₁₅P?

Answer: 16

Reason: N = 31 − 15 = 16.

Question 4. Which label gives a nucleon count?

Answer: Left superscript A

Reason: Mass number is the upper-left integer.

Question 5. What does right-hand superscript 2+ mean?

Answer: Two fewer electrons than protons

Reason: For a monatomic species E = Z − 2.

Question 6. Do ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N belong to the same element?

Answer: No

Reason: Z differs: carbon has 6 and nitrogen 7 protons.

Question 7. What remains unchanged during ordinary ion formation?

Answer: A and Z

Reason: The nucleus normally remains the same; electron number changes.

Question 8. Which pair is isotonic: ¹⁴C/¹⁵N or ¹²C/¹⁴C?

Answer: ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N

Reason: They each contain eight neutrons.

Question 9. Which entity is indicated by O₂?

Answer: A molecule with two oxygen atoms

Reason: The right subscript counts atoms; it is not a nuclear mass number.

Question 10. What does the notation ³H indicate?

Answer: Tritium, hydrogen with A = 3

Reason: Z = 1 and N = 2.

Question 11. What determines an element's chemical identity?

Answer: Proton number Z

Reason: Changing Z changes the element.

Question 12. Is the periodic-table decimal atomic mass identical to A?

Answer: No

Reason: A is an integer for one nuclide; listed atomic weight generally reflects an isotope mixture.

Question 13. For ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺, P/N/E are what?

Answer: 12 / 12 / 10

Reason: P = 12; N = 24 − 12; E = 12 − 2.

Question 14. For ¹⁸₈O²⁻, P/N/E are what?

Answer: 8 / 10 / 10

Reason: Negative charge means two additional electrons.

Question 15. After two half-lives, what fraction of an original radionuclide remains?

Answer: One quarter

Reason: (1/2)² = 1/4; applies to radioactive atoms, not all atoms of that element.

Question 16. Is ¹³C stable or radioactive?

Answer: Stable

Reason: This is a known nuclear property, not a deduction from A alone.

Question 17. What is the decay daughter of ¹⁴C in β⁻ decay?

Answer: ¹⁴N

Reason: Neutron-to-proton conversion changes Z from 6 to 7 while A remains 14.

Question 18. What additional information does [1-¹³C]glucose convey?

Answer: Position of the isotope label

Reason: It identifies the labelled carbon position, not merely the presence of one ¹³C atom.

Question 19. What does m mean in ⁹⁹ᵐTc?

Answer: Metastable nuclear state

Reason: It distinguishes a nuclear isomer, not another element.

Question 20. Can A = 9, Z = 10 describe a physical nucleus?

Answer: No

Reason: Negative neutron count is impossible.

Question 21. Which notation identifies an isotope of sodium having 12 neutrons?

Answer: ²³₁₁Na

Reason: A = 11 + 12 = 23.

Question 22. Which statement about nuclear charge is correct?

Answer: Nuclear charge is +Ze

Reason: A neutral atom's total charge may be zero despite a positive nucleus.

Question 23. During alpha emission from ²³⁸U, what are daughter A and Z?

Answer: 234 and 90

Reason: Subtract 4 and 2 respectively for the emitted alpha particle.

Question 24. Does a nuclide symbol alone determine specific activity?

Answer: No

Reason: Amount, half-life and sample conditions are needed.

Question 25. Are ¹³C-substituted compounds necessarily radioactive?

Answer: No

Reason: ¹³C is a stable isotope and can be detected with mass spectrometry/NMR.

Question 26. If Z = 17 and E = 18, what is the ionic charge?

Answer: −1

Reason: q = Z − E = −1; chloride anion.

Question 27. How many neutrons does neutral ⁴⁰Ca have?

Answer: 20

Reason: Calcium Z = 20; N = 40 − 20.

Question 28. Which notation feature denotes the nuclear isomer rather than ionization?

Answer: The m designation

Reason: It is nuclear-state information, not an electron count.

1.A.1.15.25 Misconception clinic: diagnose and correct errors

Misconception 1

Incorrect: “A is the measured mass of the atom.”

Correction: A is an integer count of nucleons, not the measured mass in daltons.

Misconception 2

Incorrect: “A positive ion has extra protons.”

Correction: Ordinary cation formation removes electrons; Z and A remain unchanged.

Misconception 3

Incorrect: “Two atoms with the same A must be the same element.”

Correction: They may be isobars with different Z.

Misconception 4

Incorrect: “Every isotope is radioactive.”

Correction: Many isotopes, including ¹²C and ¹³C, are stable.

Misconception 5

Incorrect: “Nuclide and isotope mean exactly the same thing.”

Correction: Nuclide identifies a nuclear species; isotope denotes a relationship among nuclides of one element.

Misconception 6

Incorrect: “Charge in the upper right changes neutrons.”

Correction: Charge tracks net electron deficit/excess in atomic ions; N comes from A − Z.

Misconception 7

Incorrect: “Mass number is the decimal in the periodic table.”

Correction: Periodic-table atomic weight often averages multiple nuclides.

Misconception 8

Incorrect: “¹³C label proves that the molecule is radioactive.”

Correction: ¹³C is stable; isotope composition and radioactivity are distinct.

Misconception 9

Incorrect: “A balanced nuclear equation is automatically possible.”

Correction: Conservation counts are necessary but energetics and other conservation laws matter.

Misconception 10

Incorrect: “The symbol always states the nuclear excitation state.”

Correction: A and Z alone may fail to distinguish a metastable nuclear isomer.

1.A.1.15.26 Master revision, glossary and self-check

Five equations/ideas to retain:
  1. Z = number of protons
  2. A = protons + neutrons
  3. N = A − Z
  4. For an atomic ion, q = Z − electrons
  5. Same Z → isotopes; same A → isobars; same N → isotones

Glossary: nuclide, atomic species specified by nuclear identity; mass number, nucleon count; atomic number, proton count; isotopic label, experimentally identified isotope in a molecule; radionuclide, unstable nuclear species; isomeric nuclear state, different nuclear excitation for a given A and Z; isotopologue, molecule differing in isotope composition.

Before moving to Node 16, verify that you can read ²³Na and ²³Na⁺ without confusing charge with A, classify ¹⁴C / ¹⁴N / ¹⁵N by the correct relationship, explain why [1-¹³C]glucose is a positional isotope label and distinguish the mass number from atomic weight.
