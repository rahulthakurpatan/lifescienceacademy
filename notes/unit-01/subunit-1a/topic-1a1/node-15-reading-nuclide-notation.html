1.A.1.15.1 A nucleus arrives with a label: why learn its grammar

A laboratory technician receives two vials: one labelled 146C and the other 147N. Both have fourteen nucleons, but they are not the same element. In a separate experiment, a vial marked 136C has a carbon atom with a different neutron count. The notation is therefore not decoration; it is a compact scientific statement about nuclear identity. Reading it correctly lets you connect chemistry, biochemistry, isotope tracing, radioactivity and examination questions without guessing.

This chapter builds on Node 13 (atomic number) and Node 14 (mass number). We will work from first principles, practise decoding symbols before attempting transformations, and mark the limits of what any label alone can establish. The aim is fluent interpretation rather than memorisation of isolated examples.

Learning contract: At the end you should identify the element, count protons and neutrons, calculate electrons from ionic charge, classify isotopes/isobars/isotones, balance simple nuclear equations and critically interpret common biological isotope labels.

1.A.1.15.2 The precise meaning of nuclide

In IUPAC terminology a nuclide is an atomic species characterized by atomic number, mass number and nuclear energy state, provided the state's lifetime is long enough for observation. In ordinary introductory notation we usually identify it through A and Z; when energy states matter we add a metastable-state designation such as technetium-99m. Thus equal A and Z alone do not always distinguish a nuclear ground state from a nuclear isomer.

An element is defined by its proton number Z. An isotope is a nuclide of that element with a different number of neutrons. A nuclide is not identical in meaning to a neutral atom: the nucleus may be represented while the electron population is separately described through ionic charge.

Nuclide, isotope, radionuclide, ion

A radionuclide has an unstable nucleus that undergoes radioactive transformation. An ion is an atom or group carrying net electric charge due to electron gain or loss. These terms refer to different aspects of matter and can overlap: radioactive iodine-131 may be present chemically as iodide, I⁻.

1.A.1.15.3 Anatomy of the standard nuclide symbol

The element symbol X occupies the centre. The upper left number is mass number A; the lower left number is atomic number Z. If a charge is written, it appears at the upper right of the symbol. A chemical atom count, as in O₂, is a lower right subscript and has a wholly different meaning.

Exam trap: Do not place electron number at lower left, and never treat the upper-left A as the relative atomic mass printed in a periodic table.

1.A.1.15.4 The three conservation counts and the one independent charge

Let P be the proton count, N the neutron count and E the electron count. Z = P, A = P + N and so N = A − Z. For an isolated atomic ion with signed charge q in units of elementary charge, q = P − E, hence E = Z − q. A positive q means electrons have been removed, while a negative q means electrons have been added.

P = Z   |   N = A − Z   |   E = Z − q

For 2713Al3+, protons = 13, neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14, electrons = 13 − 3 = 10. For 3517Cl−, protons = 17, neutrons = 18 and electrons = 17 − (−1) = 18. Neutral 3517Cl has 17 electrons. Nuclear identity does not change when an atom is ionized.

Four-step algorithm: ① Read X and confirm Z using the periodic table. ② Compute N = A − Z. ③ If charge is absent, interpret a neutral atom in elementary atomic examples, otherwise use E = Z − q. ④ Check physical sense: A and Z are non-negative integers with A ≥ Z; E cannot be negative.

The three samples have the same Z and thus belong to the element carbon. Neutron variation changes nuclear mass, stability and some physical properties, but they share the electron number when neutral. Biologically their chemical behaviour is closely related, which helps explain why isotope labels can trace carbon pathways. In real isotope-fractionation studies their small mass-dependent differences still matter.

The name carbon-14 is a convenient hyphen notation equivalent to ¹⁴C; the lower-left 6 can be omitted when carbon is already unambiguously identified. Omitting Z from a symbol is not permission to forget the proton number.

1.A.1.15.6 Hydrogen as a miniature isotope laboratory

Hydrogen provides an unusually clear contrast: 11H (protium) has zero neutrons; 21H (deuterium, D) has one; 31H (tritium, T) has two. All have Z = 1. D and T are conventional isotope symbols used in scientific practice; where confusion is possible write ²H and ³H. Deuterium is stable while tritium is radioactive.

Heavy water D₂O contains deuterium rather than ordinary protium at the hydrogen sites. It is a molecular formula, not a nuclide label; the 2 after D indicates two deuterium atoms per molecule. A learner who distinguishes left nuclear labels from right stoichiometric counts can now read both notations correctly.

1.A.1.15.7 The right side matters: charge, atom count and nuclear state

Compare 5626Fe2+ and 5626Fe3+. Both nuclei have 26 protons and 30 neutrons. Their electron counts differ: 24 and 23. Compare O₂ versus 168O: the former specifies two atoms in a molecule; the latter specifies one nuclide with 8 protons and 8 neutrons. A superscript before an element symbol encodes a mass number; a superscript after it commonly encodes net ionic charge.

Nuclear isomers demand another axis. ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc have the same A and Z but different nuclear energy states; “m” denotes a metastable state. They are not different elements and not different isotopes. When the excited state or its lifetime is important, writing only A and Z loses information.

Exam trap: The ionic charge written on a chemical species must not be confused with the charge of the nucleus, +Ze. A neutral atom still has a positively charged nucleus.

Think of A and Z as coordinates: movement at constant Z produces isotopes; movement at constant A produces isobars. Isotones share N = A − Z, so the relationship is diagonal if A is plotted against Z. This geometric interpretation makes classification easier in numerical questions.

For example, 146C has N = 8; 157N also has N = 8. They are isotones, not isotopes. Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14, meanwhile, are isobars despite having different electron structures and chemical identities.

1.A.1.15.9 Nuclide notation and the periodic table

A periodic table identifies an element by Z and ordinarily shows a relative atomic mass, often a weighted average reflecting terrestrial isotopic composition. That displayed decimal, for instance carbon around 12.01, is not a mass number for a single carbon atom. An experimental sample may contain a different isotope mixture; the average is not a universal fixed integer.

When notation supplies X and A but omits Z, find Z from the element identity. For ²³Na, sodium has Z = 11 and thus N = 12. If supplied only ²³X with no defined X, the proton and neutron counts are underdetermined. Correct scientific reasoning includes recognizing when the given information is insufficient.

Exam trap: Do not round a periodic-table relative atomic mass to “discover” a unique nuclide. A natural chlorine sample contains mostly ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl; the reported average cannot tell you the isotope of an individual atom.

1.A.1.15.10 What the symbol can and cannot tell you

A properly specified nuclear notation provides a counting identity; it does not on its own reveal half-life, natural abundance, nuclear spin, chemical oxidation state in a compound, molecular bonding, measured atomic mass or biological toxicity. The element symbol and charge provide hints about electron population, but electron configuration still requires quantum-mechanical rules and may depend on state and environment.

A symbol also does not guarantee stability. Carbon-12 is stable; carbon-14 is radioactive. Stable versus unstable is an experimentally established nuclear property, not simply the answer to A − Z. Many practical nuclide charts include a separate half-life or stability classification.

Critical reading: Give the shortest conclusion warranted by the label. “¹⁴C has six protons and eight neutrons” follows directly; “this sample has a certain activity” does not, because amount, activity and decay history are missing.

1.A.1.15.11 A reliable method to write notation from words

Suppose a question says “an aluminium ion with thirteen protons, fourteen neutrons and charge +3”. The element is Al, since Z = 13. A = 13 + 14 = 27. Place 27 above-left, 13 below-left and 3+ above-right: 2713Al3+. Reverse-check E = 10. Similarly, a species with twenty protons, twenty neutrons and eighteen electrons is 4020Ca2+.

How to catch an inconsistent statement

If a question simultaneously claims an atom has 8 protons and is nitrogen, its premises disagree: Z = 8 identifies oxygen. If the statement says A = 10, Z = 12, the implied neutron count is −2 and the symbol is physically invalid. The correct response is to flag the contradiction rather than fabricate a nuclide.

1.A.1.15.12 Illustrated reading map: one path, several branches

Decision flow: decode counts first; use the counts to classify or compare nuclides second.

This is the same discipline used when reading a metabolic tracer label. Determine exactly which isotope or atom is being tracked before interpreting an experiment. An isotope label is a bookkeeping tool for atoms, not automatically a statement about radioactivity or health risk.

1.A.1.15.13 Nuclear equations: A and Z as accounting checks

A nuclear reaction is written with nuclide labels so the total nucleon number A and nuclear charge number Z can be balanced across each side. Beta decay illustrates why elemental identity can change even when A remains fixed. In β⁻ decay, a neutron transforms into a proton; an electron and an electron antineutrino are emitted. Carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + anti-neutrino. A stays 14 and nuclear Z increases from 6 to 7.

In alpha decay an alpha particle (42He nucleus) leaves the parent: 23892U → 23490Th + 42He. Both A (238 = 234 + 4) and Z (92 = 90 + 2) balance. In β⁺ decay a proton transforms into a neutron while a positron and an electron neutrino are emitted, reducing nuclear Z by one with A unchanged.

Exam trap: Conserving A and Z is a necessary introductory check, not a complete proof that a reaction is energetically allowed. Charge, lepton number, momentum and energy must also be respected.

1.A.1.15.14 Why carbon-14 matters to life science

Carbon enters living systems through carbon-containing molecules. While alive, organisms exchange carbon with the environment; after death, exchange largely ceases and incorporated ¹⁴C gradually decays. Radiocarbon dating compares the sample's remaining ¹⁴C to a reference, with corrections and calibration needed for real-world age estimates. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years. This method is most useful for certain comparatively recent organic remains rather than for dating very ancient rocks.

N(t) = N₀ × (1/2)t / t½   ;   t½(¹⁴C) ≈ 5,730 years

After one ideal half-life, 50% of the original ¹⁴C remains; after two, 25%. This does not mean 50% of all carbon in a bone disappears in one half-life: ¹⁴C is only a tiny fraction of natural carbon. Real dating depends on background correction, contamination control and calibration curves.

In metabolism, ¹³C-labelled glucose can be traced using mass spectrometry or NMR, while ¹⁴C-labelled compounds can be followed through radioactivity detection in appropriately controlled laboratory systems. The isotope notation tells us which nucleus was labelled; positional labelling (such as [1-¹³C]glucose) additionally specifies the carbon position within the molecule.

1.A.1.15.15 Hydrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and iodine labels in biology

Deuterium (²H) and ¹³C are commonly used as non-radioactive stable-isotope tracers in metabolic flux studies. Tritium (³H), phosphorus-32 (³²P), sulfur-35 (³⁵S) and iodine-131 (¹³¹I) are radionuclides with specialized scientific or clinical uses. Stable labels are typically detected through isotope-sensitive analytical methods; radioactive labels are detected through their decay emissions.

In nucleic acids, phosphorus belongs to the phosphate backbone, so phosphorus isotope labels can probe phosphate-containing material. Sulfur can be found in amino acids such as cysteine and methionine, aiding certain protein-oriented tracing experiments. Iodine isotope applications depend on iodine's biological uptake and the medical context; a label never provides a stand-alone indication for treatment.

Safety boundary: This is conceptual exam preparation only. Radioisotope work requires licensed, trained personnel, radiation protection and institutionally approved procedures; no practical handling or exposure protocol is provided here.

1.A.1.15.16 Stable isotope labeling and analytical interpretation

Mass spectrometry detects mass-to-charge ratios of ions, not “mass numbers” in the simple nuclear-notation sense. A molecule containing one ¹³C instead of ¹²C is about 1.00335 unified atomic mass units heavier (for the isotope substitution itself), and the exact isotopic pattern depends on the whole molecular composition. Thus a ¹³C-labelled metabolite may show a shifted isotopologue peak without being radioactive.

Carefully distinguish an isotopologue (a molecule differing in isotopic composition) from an isotopomer (same molecular isotope counts but different positions of labels). In a molecule with multiple carbon positions, the phrase “one ¹³C label” does not identify which carbon is labelled. By contrast, [1-¹³C] specifies one labelled carbon position according to a molecule's numbering convention.

At CSIR-NET level, interpret whether a question concerns an individual nucleus (A, Z, N), a whole molecular ion (mass-to-charge, net charge) or the positional distribution of isotope tags. Switching contexts unnoticed is a frequent source of wrong answers.

1.A.1.15.17 Short conceptual history: from atomic identity to isotopic labels

The emergence of atomic-number and isotope concepts in early twentieth-century chemistry changed how scientists classified atoms. Atomic identity came to be linked to nuclear charge rather than apparent atomic mass. Once distinct nuclides of the same element were recognized, mass spectrometry, nuclear spectroscopy and radiotracers offered ways to study chemistry and biological processes at the level of individual isotopic species.

Modern notation encodes that history: X retains chemical identity, Z expresses the nuclear charge that defines it, A counts nucleons and supplementary charge or state indicators add context. The notation is a small example of a larger scientific principle: a standard symbol should preserve the relevant information while avoiding unsupported claims.

1.A.1.15.18 Twelve carefully solved examples

Worked example 1. Carbon-14 neutral atom

Given ¹⁴₆C, P = 6; N = 14 − 6 = 8; E = 6. Its radioactive identity is a separate nuclear fact, not computed from electron count.

Worked example 2. Nitrogen-15 neutral atom

¹⁵₇N gives P = 7, N = 8, E = 7. Compare with ¹⁴₆C: both have N = 8 and are isotones.

Worked example 3. Sodium-23 cation

²³₁₁Na⁺ has P = 11, N = 12, E = 10. The plus sign means loss of one electron.

Worked example 4. Chlorine-37 anion

³⁷₁₇Cl⁻ has P = 17, N = 20, E = 18. Gaining an electron does not alter A.

Worked example 5. Iron-56 divalent cation

⁵⁶₂₆Fe²⁺ has P = 26, N = 30 and E = 24.

Worked example 6. Calcium species from particle counts

20 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons: Z = 20, A = 40 and q = +2; notation ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺.

Worked example 7. Phosphorus-32

³²₁₅P has N = 17, P = 15, E = 15 if neutral. Its applications cannot be derived from the symbol alone.

Worked example 8. Uranium-235 ion

²³⁵₉₂U⁴⁺ has N = 143, P = 92 and E = 88. Nuclear properties are not changed by ordinary loss of four electrons.

Worked example 9. Impossible proposed notation

A = 11 and Z = 12 gives N = −1. This is not a physical nucleus; identify inconsistent data rather than infer a new element.

Worked example 10. One isotope versus another

¹³₆C and ¹⁴₆C have the same Z but N = 7 and 8; they are isotopes. Their mass numbers differ by one.

Worked example 11. Same mass number, different species

¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N have A = 14 but different Z: isobars. The latter has one fewer neutron.

Worked example 12. Beta-minus carbon transformation

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + anti-neutrino: A is unchanged, nuclear Z rises by one; the outgoing beta particle is not an orbital electron peeled off the atom.

1.A.1.15.19 CSIR-NET and State SET practice: 28 questions with explanations

Attempt each question on paper before revealing the answer. These are original practice items modelled on relevant concepts; they are not presented as past examination questions.

Question 1. For ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺, how many electrons are present?

Answer: 10

Reason: Z − q = 13 − 3 = 10. Neither A nor N counts electrons.

Question 2. Which pair represents isotopes: ¹²C/¹⁴C or ¹⁴C/¹⁴N?

Answer: ¹²C and ¹⁴C

Reason: Isotopes share Z = 6 but differ in A.

Question 3. What is N in ³¹₁₅P?

Answer: 16

Reason: N = 31 − 15 = 16.

Question 4. Which label gives a nucleon count?

Answer: Left superscript A

Reason: Mass number is the upper-left integer.

Question 5. What does right-hand superscript 2+ mean?

Answer: Two fewer electrons than protons

Reason: For a monatomic species E = Z − 2.

Question 6. Do ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N belong to the same element?

Answer: No

Reason: Z differs: carbon has 6 and nitrogen 7 protons.

Question 7. What remains unchanged during ordinary ion formation?

Answer: A and Z

Reason: The nucleus normally remains the same; electron number changes.

Question 8. Which pair is isotonic: ¹⁴C/¹⁵N or ¹²C/¹⁴C?

Answer: ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N

Reason: They each contain eight neutrons.

Question 9. Which entity is indicated by O₂?

Answer: A molecule with two oxygen atoms

Reason: The right subscript counts atoms; it is not a nuclear mass number.

Question 10. What does the notation ³H indicate?

Answer: Tritium, hydrogen with A = 3

Reason: Z = 1 and N = 2.

Question 11. What determines an element's chemical identity?

Answer: Proton number Z

Reason: Changing Z changes the element.

Question 12. Is the periodic-table decimal atomic mass identical to A?

Answer: No

Reason: A is an integer for one nuclide; listed atomic weight generally reflects an isotope mixture.

Question 13. For ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺, P/N/E are what?

Answer: 12 / 12 / 10

Reason: P = 12; N = 24 − 12; E = 12 − 2.

Question 14. For ¹⁸₈O²⁻, P/N/E are what?

Answer: 8 / 10 / 10

Reason: Negative charge means two additional electrons.

Question 15. After two half-lives, what fraction of an original radionuclide remains?

Answer: One quarter

Reason: (1/2)² = 1/4; applies to radioactive atoms, not all atoms of that element.

Question 16. Is ¹³C stable or radioactive?

Answer: Stable

Reason: This is a known nuclear property, not a deduction from A alone.

Question 17. What is the decay daughter of ¹⁴C in β⁻ decay?

Answer: ¹⁴N

Reason: Neutron-to-proton conversion changes Z from 6 to 7 while A remains 14.

Question 18. What additional information does [1-¹³C]glucose convey?

Answer: Position of the isotope label

Reason: It identifies the labelled carbon position, not merely the presence of one ¹³C atom.

Question 19. What does m mean in ⁹⁹ᵐTc?

Answer: Metastable nuclear state

Reason: It distinguishes a nuclear isomer, not another element.

Question 20. Can A = 9, Z = 10 describe a physical nucleus?

Answer: No

Reason: Negative neutron count is impossible.

Question 21. Which notation identifies an isotope of sodium having 12 neutrons?

Answer: ²³₁₁Na

Reason: A = 11 + 12 = 23.

Question 22. Which statement about nuclear charge is correct?

Answer: Nuclear charge is +Ze

Reason: A neutral atom's total charge may be zero despite a positive nucleus.

Question 23. During alpha emission from ²³⁸U, what are daughter A and Z?

Answer: 234 and 90

Reason: Subtract 4 and 2 respectively for the emitted alpha particle.

Question 24. Does a nuclide symbol alone determine specific activity?

Answer: No

Reason: Amount, half-life and sample conditions are needed.

Question 25. Are ¹³C-substituted compounds necessarily radioactive?

Answer: No

Reason: ¹³C is a stable isotope and can be detected with mass spectrometry/NMR.

Question 26. If Z = 17 and E = 18, what is the ionic charge?

Answer: −1

Reason: q = Z − E = −1; chloride anion.

Question 27. How many neutrons does neutral ⁴⁰Ca have?

Answer: 20

Reason: Calcium Z = 20; N = 40 − 20.

Question 28. Which notation feature denotes the nuclear isomer rather than ionization?

Answer: The m designation

Reason: It is nuclear-state information, not an electron count.

1.A.1.15.20 Misconception clinic: repair a wrong line of reasoning

Misconception 1

Incorrect: “A is the measured mass of the atom.”

Correction: A is an integer count of nucleons, not the measured mass in daltons.

Misconception 2

Incorrect: “A positive ion has extra protons.”

Correction: Ordinary cation formation removes electrons; Z and A remain unchanged.

Misconception 3

Incorrect: “Two atoms with the same A must be the same element.”

Correction: They may be isobars with different Z.

Misconception 4

Incorrect: “Every isotope is radioactive.”

Correction: Many isotopes, including ¹²C and ¹³C, are stable.

Misconception 5

Incorrect: “Nuclide and isotope mean exactly the same thing.”

Correction: Nuclide identifies a nuclear species; isotope denotes a relationship among nuclides of one element.

Misconception 6

Incorrect: “Charge in the upper right changes neutrons.”

Correction: Charge tracks net electron deficit/excess in atomic ions; N comes from A − Z.

Misconception 7

Incorrect: “Mass number is the decimal in the periodic table.”

Correction: Periodic-table atomic weight often averages multiple nuclides.

Misconception 8

Incorrect: “¹³C label proves that the molecule is radioactive.”

Correction: ¹³C is stable; isotope composition and radioactivity are distinct.

Misconception 9

Incorrect: “A balanced nuclear equation is automatically possible.”

Correction: Conservation counts are necessary but energetics and other conservation laws matter.

Misconception 10

Incorrect: “The symbol always states the nuclear excitation state.”

Correction: A and Z alone may fail to distinguish a metastable nuclear isomer.

1.A.1.15.21 High-yield summary, glossary and self-test checklist

Five equations/ideas to retain:
  1. Z = number of protons
  2. A = protons + neutrons
  3. N = A − Z
  4. For an atomic ion, q = Z − electrons
  5. Same Z → isotopes; same A → isobars; same N → isotones

Glossary: nuclide, atomic species specified by nuclear identity; mass number, nucleon count; atomic number, proton count; isotopic label, experimentally identified isotope in a molecule; radionuclide, unstable nuclear species; isomeric nuclear state, different nuclear excitation for a given A and Z; isotopologue, molecule differing in isotope composition.

Before moving to Node 16, verify that you can read ²³Na and ²³Na⁺ without confusing charge with A, classify ¹⁴C / ¹⁴N / ¹⁵N by the correct relationship, explain why [1-¹³C]glucose is a positional isotope label and distinguish the mass number from atomic weight.
