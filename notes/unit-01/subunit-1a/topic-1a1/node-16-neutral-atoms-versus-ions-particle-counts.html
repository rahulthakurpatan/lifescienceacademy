1.A.1.16.1 A question from a biology laboratory: what happened to the sodium atom?

Imagine a student enters a physiology laboratory for the first time. A teacher shows a bottle of sodium chloride, a picture of a nerve cell and two symbols: Na and Na⁺. The student already knows that sodium has eleven protons. But the teacher now asks a surprisingly powerful question: ‘If a sodium atom becomes Na⁺, has it become a different element?’ This question connects atomic structure with ions, chemistry, metabolism and the electricity of living cells.

Why the first answer must come from the nucleus

The chemical identity of an element is fixed by its proton number. Ordinary formation of Na⁺ removes an electron from a sodium atom, while its eleven protons remain unchanged. It is still sodium. Whether it is neutral or charged depends on how many electrons accompany those protons. The electron count may change without changing the mass number, neutron number or isotope identity.

Why the story matters beyond a numerical exam

Nerve cells use Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients, proteins bind Mg²⁺ and Zn²⁺, and muscle cells respond to Ca²⁺. To study these processes, we must understand what a charge symbol means. However, we should not jump straight from an atomic symbol to a whole physiological mechanism. The correct journey starts with electrons and nuclei, proceeds to chemical solutions, and ends with controlled ion transport across membranes.

Chapter objective: Read neutral atoms and monoatomic ions correctly; calculate P, N, E and signed charge; distinguish chemical and nuclear changes; interpret electronic structures, ionic compounds, molecular ions, oxidation states and selected biological ion processes.

A promise about scientific language

Every idea in this lesson will be explained in simple English but with complete scientific definitions. We will use familiar stories to introduce concepts, then formal equations and detailed examples to make them examinable. Whenever a simple classroom rule has exceptions or hidden assumptions, those limits will be stated rather than concealed.

1.A.1.16.2 The chronological scientific journey from electricity to ions

Early experiments: electrical effects came first

Scientists observed positive and negative electrical behavior and studied electrolysis before the modern internal structure of atoms was known. Conducting solutions suggested that matter could contain mobile charged species. The terms cation and anion became linked to migration toward oppositely charged electrodes during electrolysis. These observations created the need for a microscopic explanation of electrical charge.

Electron discovery explained negative charge

Cathode-ray experiments demonstrated that electrons are constituents of matter. Once an atom was understood to contain negatively charged particles, one new possibility became clear: an atom could lose or gain electrons and thereby acquire a net electrical charge. An ion need not involve a completely different kind of nucleus.

Nuclear discovery explained identity

The Rutherford nuclear model located most positive charge and nearly all mass in a small nucleus. Atomic number came to be understood as the number of protons. Later the neutron completed our simple count A = Z + N. These developments separated the question ‘which element?’ from ‘which isotope?’ and ‘which electronic charge state?’

Quantum mechanics explained which electronic changes are favorable

Electronic configurations helped explain why neutral sodium commonly forms Na⁺ and magnesium often forms Mg²⁺ in appropriate chemical settings. But the number of electrons that can be mathematically removed is not a prediction that a particular free ion will be stable in air, water or an organism. Energies and surroundings matter.

The next chapter in history was cellular electricity

Experiments on electrolytes, excitable membranes and selective channels eventually connected charged ions to nerve impulses and cellular signaling. When a Na⁺ ion passes through a membrane channel, it is primarily changing location, not becoming neutral sodium. This insight ties atomic bookkeeping to biology.

Conceptual chronological progression: electrical observations, electrons, nuclei, configurations, and biological membranes.

1.A.1.16.3 A neutral atom still contains charged particles

Start with three inventories

A nucleus contains protons and neutrons; an atom's electrons occupy electronic states around the nucleus. The atomic number Z is the count of nuclear protons P. The mass number A is P + N. To determine neutrality, we need a third count E for electrons. These numbers are related, but they describe separate physical properties.

Neutral means balanced charge, not empty of charge

Every proton carries positive elementary charge +e and every electron negative charge −e. Neutrons are electrically neutral. A neutral atom can contain many positive and negative charges, but their sum is zero. Therefore a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons: E = Z. Neutral oxygen has eight of each; neutral calcium has twenty of each.

One isotope can be neutral or charged

Neutral carbon-12 and carbon-12 cation have the same six protons and six neutrons. They differ in electron count. Carbon-13 has one additional neutron, but neutral carbon-13 has the same six electrons as neutral carbon-12. This comparison isolates two different processes: ionization changes electrons; isotope selection changes neutron identity.

P = Z   |   N = A − Z   |   Eneutral = Z
Concept check: A neutral atom does not have a neutral nucleus. Its nucleus has positive charge +Ze; the total charge is zero only after its electrons are considered.

1.A.1.16.4 Charge number q: derive the equation instead of memorizing it

Count positive charge first

If an atom or monatomic ion contains P protons, its total positive nuclear charge is +Pe. If the same species has E electrons, the total electron charge is −Ee. Adding these quantities gives Q = (P − E)e. Dividing by e gives the signed charge number q = P − E.

Why the sign matters

If P exceeds E, the positive total wins and q is positive. If E exceeds P, the negative total wins and q is negative. If the two counts agree, q is zero. This is more reliable than memorizing that ‘plus means minus’, because we can derive what plus and minus physically mean.

Turn the equation around when the question is reversed

Once the element fixes P = Z, the electron count is E = Z − q. If you know electron count and charge but not element identity, calculate Z = E + q. Then identify the element in the periodic table. The signed charge must keep its algebraic sign; Cl⁻ has q = −1, so E = 17 − (−1) = 18.

Q = (P − E)e   ;   q = Q/e = Z − E   ;   E = Z − q   ;   Z = E + q

The elementary charge is an SI constant

The elementary charge magnitude e is exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb in the modern SI. This lets us convert a charge number to an electric charge if needed. For a monatomic divalent cation q = +2, total charge is +2e. Such a conversion does not alter the count of protons.

Scientific check: A valid solution must satisfy P ≥ 0, E ≥ 0, N ≥ 0, A = P + N and q = P − E. Passing these arithmetic checks does not independently prove the resulting isolated ion is stable.

1.A.1.16.5 Cations: how a positive ion is formed

Watch Na become Na⁺ one electron at a time

Neutral sodium-23 has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. If it loses one electron, there remain 11 protons but only 10 electrons. The new net charge is +1: ²³Na⁺. Notice that the superscript 23 specifying the mass number is unchanged; removing an electron does not remove a nuclear nucleon.

What a +2 symbol really says

A neutral magnesium atom has 12 electrons. Mg²⁺ has 10, so it has lost two electrons compared with the neutral atom in a bookkeeping description. A neutral calcium atom has 20 electrons, but Ca²⁺ has 18. The 2+ tells us the deficit in negative electronic charge, not the number of calcium atoms or the number of neutrons.

Energy remains part of the scientific explanation

Removing electrons from an isolated gaseous atom requires ionization energy. Ion formation in a chemical environment depends on balancing the energies of reactants, products, bonding and solvation. Therefore the equation E = Z − q tells us the count once a charge state is specified; it does not claim that any atom can easily lose any number of electrons.

Neutral 23Na has eleven electrons, whereas 23Na+ retains the same nucleus with ten electrons.

1.A.1.16.6 Anions: why an extra electron makes charge negative

Chlorine offers a direct comparison

Neutral chlorine-35 contains 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 17 electrons. Gaining one electron gives chloride Cl⁻ with 18 electrons. Its proton and neutron counts are exactly what they were before. The net charge is 17 − 18 = −1. The minus sign is not a minus-proton or minus-neutron label.

Two extra electrons can give charge −2

Oxygen in many ionic compounds is represented formally by O²⁻. Relative to a neutral oxygen atom with eight electrons, the monatomic oxide ion has ten. Its charge is −2 because ten negative electronic charges outweigh eight positive proton charges. Sulfide S²⁻ follows the same counting principle with a different element.

There is a difference between notation and free-ion stability

A textbook charge state describes a species or a formal ionic constituent in a particular context. A monatomic O²⁻ ion in an ionic solid has a very different stabilization environment from a hypothetical isolated gas-phase O²⁻ ion. It is scientifically inaccurate to treat every written charged symbol as an equally stable free particle.

Concept check: Anions have extra electrons relative to the neutral atom. They do not have negatively charged protons or a different element identity.

1.A.1.16.7 The complete four-step particle-counting algorithm

Read the element first

The atomic number Z fixes the element and gives proton count P. If a problem supplies ‘sodium’ without Z, recall or look up Z = 11. If only an anonymous mass number is given, proton count may not be uniquely known. Do not guess the element by rounding the periodic-table average atomic mass.

Use A only for nuclear neutrons

Given a particular nuclide, calculate N = A − Z. If you know ²⁴Mg²⁺, then Z = 12 and N = 24 − 12 = 12. If the isotope is not specified—just Mg²⁺—you know 12 protons and 10 electrons but cannot assign a unique neutron count without another clue.

Use charge only for electrons

For Ca²⁺, E = 20 − 2 = 18. For Cl⁻, E = 17 − (−1) = 18. Put parentheses around a negative q in written algebra. This simple habit prevents a sign error even in complicated questions.

Finish by reconstructing the original statement

Verify P − E reproduces q and P + N reproduces A. Check whether you have counted one atom, one molecular ion or an entire chemical formula. A neat answer can still be wrong if the entity being counted was incorrectly identified.

1.A.1.16.8 Reading both nuclide symbols and ionic charges correctly

The symbol has four useful positions

A full monatomic nuclide-ion symbol can show mass number A at upper left, atomic number Z at lower left, element X in the center and charge at upper right. For ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺, P = 26, N = 30 and E = 23. The sign and position of 3+ are not interchangeable with the left-hand 56 or 26.

Isotope change and ion change are independent

The nuclides ⁵⁶Fe and ⁵⁷Fe differ in neutron count. A transition from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ changes the formal electron count but not which iron nuclide is involved. When a question contains both isotope notation and charge, first decode the nucleus and only then decode the electrons.

Do not confuse ionization with a nuclear reaction

In ordinary ionization the number of protons is unchanged. In beta decay, nuclear proton number may change and one element may become another. The fact that both processes may involve an electron should not make us treat them as the same mechanism.

⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺: P = 26; N = 56 − 26 = 30; E = 26 − 3 = 23

1.A.1.16.9 Electron configurations: what changes after ionization

Begin with sodium's neutral electronic structure

The neutral sodium atom can be written [Ne] 3s¹. Forming Na⁺ removes the 3s electron, leaving the [Ne] configuration with ten electrons. The result is not neon, however, because sodium still contains eleven nuclear protons. An electron configuration is not an element's nuclear identity.

Why magnesium often has charge +2

Neutral magnesium is [Ne] 3s² in its ground state. Its common divalent ion Mg²⁺ has ten electrons and the [Ne] configuration. Such comparisons illustrate valence-shell patterns in the periodic table. They help explain common ionic formulas when combined with bonding energetics.

Why the octet rule has limits

A noble-gas-like valence shell is a useful starting pattern for many main-group ions, but the octet rule is not a universal energy law. Some ions, transition-metal complexes and covalently bonded species cannot be correctly explained by merely declaring that every atom ‘wants eight electrons’.

Transition metals require extra care

For common first-row transition-metal cations, the 4s electrons are typically removed before 3d electrons in introductory configurations. Neutral Fe is written [Ar]3d⁶4s²; Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁵ for the corresponding isolated ionic configurations. The electron count and actual electronic state must be distinguished from a simplistic filling-order mnemonic.

1.A.1.16.10 Isoelectronic species: equal electrons, unequal nuclei

The neon family has ten electrons

Ne, O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ all have ten electrons in these simple monatomic ground-state comparisons. Their proton counts, however, are 10, 8, 9, 11, 12 and 13. Thus equal electronic occupation does not make them one element or one isotope.

Do not confuse isoelectronic with isotopic

Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron number. Isoelectronic species have the same electron count (or appropriate electronic configuration) even if their proton numbers differ. Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ are isoelectronic; they are not isotopes, since sodium has Z = 11 and magnesium Z = 12.

The ionic-size trend in an isoelectronic series

When comparable isoelectronic ions have the same number of electrons, a larger positive nuclear charge typically attracts the electrons more strongly, giving a smaller effective radius. The familiar approximate trend is O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺. Ionic radius depends on definitions and environment, so the trend should be interpreted as an introductory comparison rather than six uniquely measured universal sizes.

1.A.1.16.11 Periodic table trends and common ionic charges

Patterns emerge from valence electrons

Group 1 metals commonly form +1 cations and group 2 metals often +2. Fluorine and chlorine commonly give −1 anions in ionic compounds. Oxygen and sulfur often form formal −2 monatomic ions in suitable ionic materials. These patterns follow from electronic structure and energetic stabilization, not from changing proton numbers.

Keep the chemical context attached to a predicted charge

It is misleading to assume that an atom instantly forms its most familiar textbook ion in all surroundings. Sodium metal, sodium chloride crystal and Na⁺ in water are different chemical systems. Electron transfer, crystal formation, solvation and reaction energies determine the actual conditions.

Valence is helpful, but not a universal ion-charge formula

Al³⁺ is an important example of a +3 metal cation. But transition metals often exhibit multiple oxidation states and coordination chemistry can be complicated. A charge number given in a problem should take precedence over a rough periodic-table heuristic.

1.A.1.16.12 Ion size, electron repulsion and hydration

Why a cation is often smaller than its neutral parent atom

Removing an electron changes electronic repulsion and can eliminate an outer occupied shell. Common cations such as Na⁺ are therefore smaller than the corresponding neutral atom under standard atomic/ionic comparisons. The nuclear proton count has not changed; the electronic distribution has.

Why anions are often larger

Adding electrons increases electron-electron repulsion relative to the same nuclear charge, often making an anion larger than its neutral atom. The exact radius is method- and environment-dependent. Ionic radii in crystals depend on coordination assumptions, so we should not claim one unchanging physical sphere size for every context.

Water modifies real ion behaviour

Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in cells are surrounded by water molecules and may coordinate with proteins. Their charge and size influence hydration shells and transport. Two ions with equal charge magnitude do not necessarily have equal hydration or biological functions.

1.A.1.16.13 Oxidation state, formal charge and actual ionic charge

For one atomic ion the easiest count is direct

An isolated Fe²⁺ monatomic ion has 26 protons and 24 electrons. Its ionic charge number is +2. In elementary inorganic nomenclature the oxidation state of this monatomic ion is also +2. But equality in this case does not justify treating every oxidation number as a free localized atomic ionic charge inside a complex molecule.

Oxidation state is bonding-based bookkeeping

In water, H₂O is a neutral molecule while standard oxidation states are H = +1 and O = −2. These values arise from assigning bonding electrons under conventions. The oxygen atom is not literally a free O²⁻ ion sitting inside a neutral water molecule. Oxidation state tracks an electron-allocation model useful in redox reasoning.

Formal charge depends on the Lewis structure

In a conventional Lewis structure of H₂O, oxygen has formal charge zero and each hydrogen zero. Formal charge uses a different electron assignment rule from oxidation state. The molecular net charge is the sum of formal charges, but partial physical charges in a polar bond need not equal the formal-charge integers.

How to decide which question you have been asked

If it says ‘number of electrons in Fe³⁺,’ apply E = Z − 3. If it says ‘oxidation state of sulfur in SO₄²⁻,’ use oxidation-state rules and the whole ion charge. If it says ‘formal charge on oxygen in a drawn Lewis structure,’ use valence and bonding-electron assignments. Different tasks demand different methods.

1.A.1.16.14 Polyatomic ions: count an entire charged molecule correctly

Ammonium is not an isolated nitrogen cation

NH₄⁺ contains one nitrogen nucleus and four hydrogen nuclei. Their proton numbers sum to 7 + 4 = 11. The whole ion has charge +1, so it has 10 total electrons. It is incorrect to subtract one electron from every atom in the formula, since the charge belongs once to the entire species.

Sulfate is negatively charged as a whole

For SO₄²⁻, total protons are 16 + 4 × 8 = 48. Its net charge is −2, giving 50 electrons in the isolated molecular ion. This count does not assign 50 electrons to a particular atom or uniquely specify resonance. The internal bonding picture needs additional chemical rules.

Phosphate makes the same lesson stronger

PO₄³⁻ has total proton count 15 + 4 × 8 = 47. Because charge is −3, its total electron number is 50. Notice that sulfate and phosphate ions can be isoelectronic as whole molecular species under this count while being different molecules, different atoms and different chemical structures.

Molecular ion: total electrons = Σ(Z for every nucleus) − charge of the whole ion
Concept check: A net group charge is not automatically an identical charge on every atom of the group.

1.A.1.16.15 Ionic solids and charge-balanced formulas

NaCl is overall neutral even though it contains ions

A sodium chloride crystal has alternating ionic constituents Na⁺ and Cl⁻. A 1:1 ratio produces net charge zero. The simplest chemical formula NaCl expresses composition; it does not mean an isolated Na⁺ loses its charge when the crystal is electrically neutral.

The CaCl₂ example teaches stoichiometric logic

One Ca²⁺ has charge +2. Two Cl⁻ together contribute −2. The simplest whole-number ratio 1:2 gives CaCl₂. Similarly three Mg²⁺ ions balance two PO₄³⁻ ions, giving Mg₃(PO₄)₂. Formula subscripts reflect charge balance, not neutron counts or mass numbers.

Overall neutrality can coexist with many local ionic charges

A crystal with countless ions can be neutral as a bulk material. A cell can also maintain nearly balanced bulk charge while a very small charge separation near a membrane gives a measurable voltage. Counting charged components and computing net total must not be confused.

1.A.1.16.16 Dissolving salt versus ionizing a molecule

The salt-dissolution story

When NaCl dissolves in water, ions of the lattice become hydrated and separated in solution. We can describe the process as NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). This is dissolution and dissociation, not a nuclear transmutation and not the appearance of entirely new sodium protons.

A molecular acid illustrates ion formation through reaction

HCl is a molecular substance. In water it reacts strongly with solvent molecules, and an introductory equation is HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. Hydronium H₃O⁺ is a useful notation for solvated proton chemistry. This distinction explains why chemists often speak of ionic salts dissociating and molecular acids ionizing in water.

Dissolved ions experience an environment

The notation Na⁺(aq) stands for a hydrated ion in water. Its actual local environment contains interacting water molecules and may include other solutes. A gas-phase isolated ion and the ‘same’ formal ion in aqueous solution can have different energies, transport and coordination behaviour.

1.A.1.16.17 Hydration shells and selectivity in water

Why water interacts with an ion

Water is polar. Its partially negative oxygen region tends to orient toward positively charged ions, while its partially positive hydrogen regions can orient toward negative ions. This orientation produces dynamic hydration shells that stabilize ions and influence movement and binding. Aqueous ions are not bare symbols floating without interactions.

Why Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ are biologically different

Both magnesium and calcium have charge number +2, yet their nuclear charges, electronic structures and sizes differ. Mg²⁺ often has a strongly interacting hydration environment. Biological proteins can discriminate between these ions through pore geometry, coordination sites and hydration costs.

Charge balancing does not predict solubility

Even a formula with balanced charges may describe a poorly soluble ionic solid. Lattice stabilization, hydration enthalpy, entropy and chemical equilibria determine the extent of dissolution. The particle-counting model gives a necessary identity and charge description, not a complete prediction of solution behaviour.

1.A.1.16.18 The electrolyte environment of living cells

A cell contains ions in two connected solutions

The extracellular and intracellular fluids contain different amounts of Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺ and other charged species. Ions carry charge and can influence osmotic balance, protein function and electrical potential across cell membranes. Their numbers and distributions matter more to physiology than the mere presence of a plus or minus sign.

Charge does not tell you concentration

The symbol Ca²⁺ says two positive elementary charges per ion. It does not indicate the concentration of calcium in a particular compartment. A low concentration of Ca²⁺ and a much higher concentration contain ions with the same individual charge number, but their cellular consequences may differ.

Bulk electroneutrality and membrane voltage can coexist

An entire cell does not need to accumulate a vast net charge throughout its cytoplasm to create a membrane potential. Charge separation in a thin region near the membrane, maintained by ionic distributions and permeability differences, can create a significant potential while much of the bulk solution remains nearly electrically neutral.

1.A.1.16.19 The sodium-potassium pump and net charge movement

The pump maintains conditions needed for excitable cells

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to transport ions against gradients. In its commonly taught stoichiometric cycle, it moves three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in per ATP. Sodium and potassium remain themselves: there is no reaction that changes Na nuclei into K nuclei.

Calculate electrogenicity by signed ion flow

Three outward Na⁺ ions correspond to +3 elementary charges leaving the cell. Two inward K⁺ correspond to +2 entering. Net positive charge transported outward per cycle is +1 elementary-charge equivalent. This contributes to membrane electrical behavior, but does not by itself explain every aspect of the resting membrane potential.

Pumps and channels are distinct

A pump couples transport to an energy source, while a passive channel typically allows ions to move along their electrochemical driving force when the channel is open. Both are proteins; neither normally changes the nuclear proton number of the transported ion.

1.A.1.16.20 Ion channels, selectivity and nerve action potentials

A membrane channel allows selective ionic movement

Channels are membrane proteins with pores and selectivity properties. Potassium channels can distinguish K⁺ from Na⁺ despite both being monovalent cations. The distinction relies on interactions in the selectivity filter, hydration and protein geometry, not simply on whether the written charge is +1.

Changes in permeability produce electrical signals

A simplified nerve action potential involves increasing Na⁺ permeability during depolarization and changes in K⁺ permeability as the membrane repolarizes. The ions remain charged while moving. An action potential is not made by cells turning sodium ions into neutral atoms and back again.

Electrochemical gradients are essential

An open channel does not tell the direction of net ion flow by itself. Direction depends on concentration gradients and the voltage difference. A channel can allow ions to move down the combined electrochemical gradient, and the direction of movement may change as voltage approaches the ion's equilibrium potential.

1.A.1.16.21 The Nernst equation: charge number becomes a physical variable

Concentration and voltage jointly affect an ion

An ion is affected by chemical potential and electrical potential. The Nernst equation relates the equilibrium voltage for one ion to its concentrations or, more precisely, activities on two sides of a membrane. This goes beyond electron counting, but it shows why the signed ionic charge number matters in biology.

Write the convention before the formula

If we define voltage as inside minus outside, an introductory concentration-based form is E_ion = (RT/zF) ln([ion]_outside/[ion]_inside). R is the gas constant, T temperature in kelvin, F the Faraday constant and z the signed valence of the permeating ion. This formula is an equilibrium model under idealized assumptions; realistic membrane potentials reflect multiple ionic permeabilities.

Never mistake ionic valence z for atomic number Z

Potassium has atomic number Z = 19 because it has nineteen protons. Potassium ion K⁺ has signed charge valence z = +1. Calcium has Z = 20 but Ca²⁺ has z = +2. Similar lettering in different scientific fields must not cause the physical meanings to be interchanged.

Eion = (RT/zF) ln([ion]out / [ion]in)
Concept check: Changing voltage sign convention changes the form of the equation. ‘At equilibrium’ does not mean that all ion concentrations are equal on both sides.

1.A.1.16.22 Calcium signals, magnesium enzymes and zinc-binding proteins

Why Ca²⁺ is used as a signal

Calcium ions participate in cellular signaling, muscle contraction and secretion. A Ca²⁺ ion from the calcium-40 nuclide has 20 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons. Its role in cell signaling comes from biological control of free ion levels, membrane channels and binding proteins—not from transforming calcium into a different element.

Magnesium is central in nucleotide chemistry

Mg²⁺ can bind around phosphate-rich molecules and contribute to enzyme function. In simplified particle counting Mg²⁺ has Z = 12 and 10 electrons. Inside a protein, electronic charge distributions and ligand coordination are more nuanced than an image of a bare 2+ point charge.

Zinc and iron illustrate distinct metal-ion roles

Zn²⁺ commonly contributes to protein structure and catalysis, while Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ may participate in redox chemistry in proteins. Charge numbers and oxidation states organize our reasoning, but a biological metal site cannot be understood solely by a one-line electronic subtraction.

1.A.1.16.23 Hydrogen ions, pH and protonation

H⁺ is not a neutral hydrogen atom

A neutral protium atom contains one proton and one electron. The formal H⁺ ion lacks that electron. In water, hydrogen-ion chemistry involves hydration and proton-transfer networks, often written with H₃O⁺. It would be misleading to picture bare free H⁺ as a collection of isolated nuclei drifting independently through ordinary water.

What pH actually measures

In rigorous terms pH is related to hydrogen-ion activity: pH = −log₁₀(a_H+). In simplified dilute aqueous contexts one often uses concentration as an approximation with appropriate reference-state conventions. Charge and acidity are different pieces of information: H⁺ states a species and its net charge, while pH describes effective hydrogen-ion activity.

Protonation can change a molecule's net charge

Acidic and basic groups can gain or lose protons. Phosphate buffering involves H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻, among other forms. These changes alter molecular composition and net charge without altering the proton number inside phosphorus nuclei. Chemically transferring a hydrogen nucleus is not the same as nuclear decay of a phosphorus atom.

1.A.1.16.24 Zwitterions, amino acids and charged DNA

Net neutrality need not mean no charged groups

A zwitterion contains both positive and negative formal charges yet may have overall net charge zero. Amino acids often illustrate this behavior through ammonium-like and carboxylate groups. The phrase ‘neutral species’ describes net charge; it does not require every local part to have a zero formal charge.

Protein charge varies with chemistry and pH

Side-chain ionization states influence protein interactions and solubility. A protein's net charge is an aggregate property affected by many groups and conditions. We cannot determine it by treating every constituent atom as an isolated monatomic ion carrying the whole molecule's charge.

Nucleic acids contain charged phosphate groups

DNA and RNA backbones contain phosphate groups that are typically negative at physiological pH. Their charge contributes to interactions with ions and proteins. Nucleic acids do not gain a different atomic number when their phosphate groups protonate or deprotonate.

1.A.1.16.25 Mass spectrometry, m/z and the two meanings of z

A mass spectrometer commonly observes molecular ions

In many analytical methods, molecules are ionized and detected according to mass-to-charge ratio m/z. The lowercase z is an ionic charge number in that context. The uppercase Z of nuclide notation is the nuclear proton number. A doubly charged protein ion may contain thousands of atoms and many distinct nuclear atomic numbers.

A simple two-charge calculation

A molecular ion of approximately 1000 Da and net +1 charge has an m/z near 1000 for a simplified single-charge model. A comparable +2 ion may appear near m/z 500 after handling relevant mass additions and the exact instrument convention. This difference is due to charge state, not because the molecule's atoms have suddenly halved their nuclear mass numbers.

Spectrometry does not automatically identify a reaction mechanism

An instrument may show the mass-to-charge ratio of a molecular ion, but further measurements and analysis are required to identify structure or reaction path. A nuclide symbol tells us nuclear identity; a mass spectrum supplies experimental signals. Each answers different questions.

1.A.1.16.26 Why ionization energy and electron affinity matter

Electron count is easy, electron removal may be expensive

The equation E = Z − q is an exact count for a specified monatomic ion but not an energetic prediction. Removing a first electron from an isolated atom requires ionization energy; removing another usually has a different energy cost. This explains why the ability to write Al⁵⁺ as an electron count does not make it a common stable biochemical ion.

Electron addition also depends on the environment

Electron affinity describes certain energy changes associated with electron attachment, while solution and lattice effects can stabilize charged species in different ways. An anion in a salt need not behave like an isolated anion in the gas phase.

Why biological electrolytes differ from reactive elements

Metallic sodium and dissolved Na⁺ have different electron structures and chemical reactivities. Chlorine gas and chloride Cl⁻ likewise differ. This distinction is important when explaining why biological fluids can contain large amounts of sodium and chloride ions without behaving like the reactive neutral elements.

1.A.1.16.27 Five conceptual experiments to test understanding

Experiment 1 — remove an electron without changing a nucleus

Start with neutral sodium-23, remove one electron and compare before versus after. The element and isotope remain sodium-23, but net charge becomes +1. If you concluded the species became magnesium, you accidentally changed Z rather than E.

Experiment 2 — replace a neutron instead

Compare neutral chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Both have 17 protons and 17 electrons; they differ in neutron count. They are isotopes, not cations or anions of each other.

Experiment 3 — move Na⁺ across a membrane

A sodium channel transports a Na⁺ ion inward. The ion still has eleven protons and ten electrons. Its movement changes location and can contribute to electrical current, but no electron is necessarily added simply because it entered a cell.

Experiment 4 — compare NaCl and KCl

Both salts can have a neutral 1:1 charge balance from a +1 cation and −1 anion. Their ions have different proton counts, sizes and hydration properties. Electrical charge alone does not make their physiology identical.

Experiment 5 — inspect oxygen in H₂O

Oxidation state −2 for oxygen in water is a conventional electron-assignment result. It is not evidence that the water molecule literally contains an isolated O²⁻ ion with two separate H⁺ particles. Charge notation must be interpreted in chemical context.

This section will progress from neutral atoms through monatomic ions, reverse-identity problems, isoelectronic groups, molecular ions, isotope labels, ionic formulas and biological ion transport. Every answer includes explicit steps and an error check.

How to use this workshop

These examples begin with the simplest neutral-atom inventory and gradually combine atomic number, mass number, molecular charge, electronic configuration and physiology. Work each answer before reading it. Every solution is accompanied by a check and a named error to avoid.

Worked example 1: Neutral carbon-12: count all three particles

Problem: For a neutral ¹²₆C atom, identify the three subatomic particle counts.

Complete reasoning: Z = 6 fixes six protons. A = 12 is the total number of nucleons, so neutrons = 12 − 6 = 6. The word neutral tells us that electronic charge balances nuclear charge, so electrons = 6. No ionic charge is specified and none should be invented.

Independent check: Check: P + N = 12 and P − E = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: treating the mass number 12 as an electron count.

Worked example 2: Carbon-13 compared with carbon-12

Problem: What changes if a neutral ¹²C atom is compared with neutral ¹³C?

Complete reasoning: Carbon-13 still has Z = 6 protons and, when neutral, six electrons. Its neutrons are 13 − 6 = 7 rather than six. Thus these are isotopes, not charge states of one nuclide. Their chemical identity is the same, although isotope-dependent mass effects can sometimes be measured.

Independent check: Check: changes in N alter A while P and E are constant.

Exam trap: Mistake: calling ¹³C an anion because it contains an extra neutron.

Worked example 3: Sodium-23 cation

Problem: Count P, N and E in ²³₁₁Na⁺.

Complete reasoning: The lower-left 11 gives eleven protons. The neutron number is 23 − 11 = 12. Charge number q = +1, so E = 11 − 1 = 10. Sodium-23 remains the same isotope before and after ordinary electron loss.

Independent check: Check: P − E = 11 − 10 = +1.

Exam trap: Mistake: deducting one from 23 because a cation has lost an electron.

Worked example 4: Chlorine-35 anion

Problem: Interpret ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻ and explain the negative sign.

Complete reasoning: Chlorine has Z = 17 and 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons. The negative sign means an additional electron relative to its neutral atom, so E = 17 − (−1) = 18. The protons remain positive; the whole ion becomes negative because its electrons outnumber its protons.

Independent check: Check: 17 − 18 = −1 charge unit.

Exam trap: Mistake: calculating E = 17 − 1 = 16 despite the minus sign.

Worked example 5: Calcium-40 divalent ion

Problem: For ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺, calculate particles and identify what ionization does not change.

Complete reasoning: The calcium nucleus has P = 20 and N = 40 − 20 = 20. A 2+ charge corresponds to E = 20 − 2 = 18. Compared with neutral ⁴⁰Ca, two electrons are absent; proton and neutron counts are unchanged. Therefore this process is ionization, not formation of a new isotope.

Independent check: Check: 20 − 18 = +2 and 20 + 20 = 40.

Exam trap: Mistake: describing Ca²⁺ as containing two calcium nuclei.

Worked example 6: Aluminium-27 trivalent cation

Problem: Decode ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺.

Complete reasoning: Aluminium's Z is 13. Mass number A = 27 means 14 neutrons. Three positive elementary charges imply E = 13 − 3 = 10 electrons. A neutral aluminium-27 atom would have thirteen electrons, so the 3+ species has three fewer.

Independent check: Check: P − E = 3 and P + N = 27.

Exam trap: Mistake: treating 3+ as a third isotope number.

Worked example 7: Sulfur-34 anion

Problem: An isolated ³⁴₁₆S²⁻ species is specified. Determine its counts.

Complete reasoning: Protons = 16, neutrons = 34 − 16 = 18, and electrons = 16 − (−2) = 18. A charge of −2 requires two electrons more than the neutral atom. In chemistry this symbol is meaningful in suitable ionic contexts, but the numerical calculation alone does not establish free gas-phase stability.

Independent check: Check: 16 − 18 = −2.

Exam trap: Mistake: removing two electrons for a negative ion.

Worked example 8: Reverse problem from charge and electron count

Problem: A monatomic ion contains 18 electrons and has charge +2. Which element is it?

Complete reasoning: Use q = P − E. Therefore P = E + q = 18 + 2 = 20. Atomic number 20 identifies calcium. If no isotope or mass number is given, the neutron count is not uniquely known. The conclusion is Ca²⁺ with a specified electron count, not necessarily ⁴⁰Ca²⁺.

Independent check: Check: 20 − 18 = +2.

Exam trap: Mistake: guessing 20 neutrons without being told A.

Worked example 9: Reverse problem for a negative ion

Problem: A monatomic ion has 10 electrons and charge −1. Identify its element.

Complete reasoning: P = E + q = 10 + (−1) = 9. Atomic number nine is fluorine, giving F⁻. For a specified fluorine-19 isotope, neutrons would be ten, but without mass number the charge problem determines only the element and electrons.

Independent check: Check: 9 − 10 = −1.

Exam trap: Mistake: using E − q and arriving at 11 protons.

Worked example 10: Complete symbol from all particle counts

Problem: An ion has 16 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. Write its full nuclear and ionic notation.

Complete reasoning: Atomic number 16 corresponds to sulfur. Its mass number is A = 16 + 18 = 34. The net charge is q = 16 − 18 = −2. Write ³⁴₁₆S²⁻. Every number is placed in a distinct position because A, Z and q measure different properties.

Independent check: Check: read the proposed symbol back and recover the given counts.

Exam trap: Mistake: putting 18 at upper left because it was the electron count.

Worked example 11: Neutral particle counts with missing isotope

Problem: A neutral zinc atom has 30 protons. How many electrons and neutrons must it have?

Complete reasoning: Neutrality fixes E = P = 30. Zinc identity is established by Z = 30. Neutron count is not determined without a specific mass number, so a single number of neutrons cannot be given. A scientifically correct answer states exactly what is determined and what remains unknown.

Independent check: Check: q = 30 − 30 = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: rounding zinc's periodic-table atomic weight and assuming that is the only isotope.

Worked example 12: Isoelectronic with neon

Problem: Which ions among O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ share the electron number of Ne?

Complete reasoning: Neon has ten electrons. O²⁻: 8 + 2 = 10; F⁻: 9 + 1 = 10; Na⁺: 11 − 1 = 10; Mg²⁺: 12 − 2 = 10. All four species are isoelectronic with Ne in electron count. Their nuclei, sizes and chemical identities remain different.

Independent check: Check: each count gives ten independently.

Exam trap: Mistake: calling them isotopes of neon.

Worked example 13: Argon-like ions

Problem: Determine whether K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ are isoelectronic.

Complete reasoning: Potassium Z = 19 loses one electron to give eighteen. Calcium Z = 20 loses two to give eighteen. Chlorine Z = 17 gains one to give eighteen. These ions have the electron count of argon, but do not share atomic number and therefore are not one isotope family.

Independent check: Check: E = 18 for each using signed charge.

Exam trap: Mistake: equating electronic configuration with nuclear identity.

Worked example 14: Ionic size within a ten-electron series

Problem: Order O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ by an introductory isoelectronic radius trend.

Complete reasoning: All four have ten electrons, so the main comparison is their nuclear proton count: O 8, F 9, Na 11 and Mg 12. A stronger attractive nuclear charge generally contracts a comparable ten-electron cloud. Thus an approximate decreasing-size order is O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺. Actual tabulated ionic radii depend on the chemical environment.

Independent check: Check: electron totals must be equal before using this particular trend.

Exam trap: Mistake: ranking only by absolute ionic charge and ignoring the protons.

Worked example 15: Iron-56 with charge +2

Problem: Count particles and write the simplified configuration for ⁵⁶₂₆Fe²⁺.

Complete reasoning: P = 26, N = 56 − 26 = 30 and E = 26 − 2 = 24. Neutral Fe is written [Ar]3d⁶4s² in a common ground-state notation. For the corresponding common isolated cation model, 4s electrons are removed first, giving Fe²⁺ [Ar]3d⁶.

Independent check: Check: Fe²⁺ is 24-electron iron, not 24-proton chromium.

Exam trap: Mistake: removing 3d electrons before 4s in this standard textbook exercise.

Worked example 16: Iron-56 with charge +3

Problem: How does ⁵⁶Fe³⁺ differ from ⁵⁶Fe²⁺?

Complete reasoning: Both nuclei have 26 protons and 30 neutrons. Fe²⁺ has 24 electrons, while Fe³⁺ has 23. The higher positive charge is caused by one fewer electron. In a simple isolated-ion configuration Fe³⁺ may be represented as [Ar]3d⁵. Oxidation-state language in a complex may require more nuanced electronic analysis.

Independent check: Check: charge difference +1 matches electron loss of one.

Exam trap: Mistake: increasing the number of protons from 26 to 27.

Worked example 17: Copper ion comparison

Problem: Calculate the electrons in Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺.

Complete reasoning: Copper's atomic number is 29. Therefore Cu⁺ has 28 electrons and Cu²⁺ has 27. Both are copper irrespective of the charge state. Their coordination and redox behavior can differ significantly, showing that equal element identity does not imply identical chemistry.

Independent check: Check: 29 − 28 = +1 and 29 − 27 = +2.

Exam trap: Mistake: treating the ionic charge as the number of neutrons.

Worked example 18: Zinc ion and biological context

Problem: What electron count belongs to Zn²⁺, and what does this calculation fail to explain?

Complete reasoning: Zinc has Z = 30 and its formal monatomic Zn²⁺ count is 28 electrons. This is enough for a basic counting question. It does not explain the complete geometry, ligand binding or catalytic function of a zinc-binding protein; those require molecular structure and coordination chemistry.

Independent check: Check: net charge is 30 − 28 = +2.

Exam trap: Mistake: claiming all Zn²⁺-containing proteins have identical biochemical roles.

Worked example 19: Ammonium total electrons

Problem: Count electrons in the whole NH₄⁺ ion.

Complete reasoning: N contributes seven protons and four H nuclei contribute four, totaling eleven. The whole molecular ion has charge +1, so E_total = 11 − 1 = 10. The charge belongs to NH₄⁺ as one entity, not separately to every N or H atom.

Independent check: Check: total positive charges eleven minus electrons ten give +1.

Exam trap: Mistake: subtracting one electron from each constituent atom.

Worked example 20: Sulfate total electrons

Problem: Find the total number of electrons in SO₄²⁻.

Complete reasoning: One sulfur nucleus contributes Z = 16, and four oxygen nuclei contribute 4 × 8 = 32. Total nuclear positive charge is +48e. A net ionic charge of −2 means two electrons more than total protons, hence 50 electrons.

Independent check: Check: 48 − 50 = −2.

Exam trap: Mistake: assigning −2 separately to four oxygens and sulfur in the whole-ion calculation.

Worked example 21: Phosphate total electrons

Problem: Count total electrons in isolated PO₄³⁻ and compare with sulfate.

Complete reasoning: Phosphorus has Z = 15 and four oxygen atoms contribute 32 protons, giving 47 protons. Net charge −3 requires 50 electrons. Sulfate SO₄²⁻ also has fifty total electrons, illustrating a whole-molecule isoelectronic count without identical nuclei, bonds or species.

Independent check: Check: 47 − 50 = −3.

Exam trap: Mistake: calling sulfate and phosphate ‘isotopes’.

Worked example 22: Nitrate total electrons

Problem: Count total electrons of NO₃⁻.

Complete reasoning: Nitrogen contributes seven protons and three oxygens give 24, for 31 total. Net charge −1 requires 32 electrons. A Lewis structure may distribute electron density over the species, but the total electron count is fixed by nuclear charges and the net ionic charge.

Independent check: Check: 31 − 32 = −1.

Exam trap: Mistake: using nitrogen's Z alone as if nitrate were one atomic ion.

Worked example 23: Ionic formula for calcium chloride

Problem: Write the simplest neutral formula from Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.

Complete reasoning: One calcium ion contributes +2 charge. Each chloride contributes −1. Two chlorides balance one calcium, giving CaCl₂. The subscript 2 is a formula ratio and does not alter calcium's individual electron count of 18.

Independent check: Check: +2 + 2(−1) = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: using Ca₂Cl because the charge magnitude of calcium is 2.

Worked example 24: Ionic formula for aluminium oxide

Problem: Derive the simplest formula from Al³⁺ and O²⁻.

Complete reasoning: To balance +3 and −2, take the least common multiple of six charge units. Two Al³⁺ contribute +6 and three O²⁻ contribute −6, producing Al₂O₃. This gives stoichiometry, not a molecular formula for a discrete simple three-atom unit.

Independent check: Check: 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: writing AlO₂ by swapping signs without checking total charge.

Worked example 25: Magnesium phosphate ratio

Problem: Determine the neutral ionic formula combining Mg²⁺ and PO₄³⁻.

Complete reasoning: Three magnesium ions carry +6 and two phosphate ions −6. The smallest integer ratio gives Mg₃(PO₄)₂. Parentheses show two phosphate groups rather than only two oxygen atoms. A total formula-unit charge of zero does not make each constituent ion neutral.

Independent check: Check: 3(2) + 2(−3) = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: writing MgPO₄ because each formula component is listed once.

Worked example 26: Net charge across membrane pump cycle

Problem: A Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase cycle moves three Na⁺ outward and two K⁺ inward. What is the net positive charge transported outward?

Complete reasoning: Each Na⁺ carries +e, so outward movement carries 3e outward. Two inward K⁺ carry 2e in the opposite direction. Net outward charge transport is 3e − 2e = 1e per cycle in the common stoichiometric model. No Na⁺ changes into K⁺.

Independent check: Check: subtract opposing signed fluxes rather than adding magnitudes.

Exam trap: Mistake: concluding five electrons are transported because five ions moved.

Worked example 27: Calcium as a divalent carrier

Problem: Compare the charge transported by 100 Ca²⁺ ions with 100 Na⁺ ions moving in the same direction.

Complete reasoning: Each Ca²⁺ carries +2e, so 100 ions carry 200e. Each Na⁺ carries +e, so 100 sodium ions carry 100e. This compares electrical charge for equal ion counts, but not concentrations, currents or physiological impact without time and conditions.

Independent check: Check: number of ions × signed charge per ion.

Exam trap: Mistake: assuming the atomic number of calcium, 20, is its electrical valence.

Worked example 28: Convert ionic elementary charge to coulombs

Problem: What total positive charge do five Na⁺ ions carry, using e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C?

Complete reasoning: Each Na⁺ carries exactly +e. Five ions have Q = +5e = +8.01088317 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The SI conversion uses elementary charge magnitude, not proton mass or the mass number of sodium.

Independent check: Check: five positive ions must give positive Q.

Exam trap: Mistake: multiplying by sodium's atomic number eleven a second time.

Worked example 29: Net ion-charge balance in a mixture

Problem: A small model compartment contains 10 Na⁺ ions and 4 Ca²⁺ ions plus 18 Cl⁻ ions. What is its net charge?

Complete reasoning: Positive contributions are 10(+1) + 4(+2) = +18. Negative chloride contributes 18(−1) = −18. The overall count is zero. The compartment contains charged particles even though its total modeled electric charge cancels.

Independent check: Check: net charge +18 − 18 = 0.

Exam trap: Mistake: asserting a neutral solution has no ions.

Worked example 30: Difference between H and H+

Problem: Find P and E for neutral ¹H and formal ¹H⁺.

Complete reasoning: The protium nucleus has one proton and no neutrons. Neutral protium has one electron, while the formal H⁺ species has none. In aqueous solution proton chemistry is strongly solvated, so H⁺ shorthand does not imply bare protons float independently in bulk water.

Independent check: Check: 1 − 1 = 0 for H; 1 − 0 = +1 for H⁺.

Exam trap: Mistake: thinking H⁺ is the same electronic species as neutral hydrogen.

Worked example 31: Hydronium total electrons

Problem: How many total electrons does the H₃O⁺ ion contain?

Complete reasoning: Three hydrogen nuclei contribute three protons and oxygen contributes eight, total P = 11. With overall charge +1, total electron number is 11 − 1 = 10. This is a molecular total, not the orbital occupancy of an isolated oxygen cation.

Independent check: Check: total q = 11 − 10 = +1.

Exam trap: Mistake: applying E = 8 − 1 to oxygen alone and ignoring hydrogen nuclei.

Worked example 32: Mass spectrometry charge comparison

Problem: A simplified 1000 Da ion appears at z = +1. Where is a comparable ion with charge +2 expected, ignoring adduct and electron-mass corrections?

Complete reasoning: Use m/z ≈ 1000/|z| in the simplified convention. Charge +1 gives about 1000 while +2 gives about 500. Real electrospray ions often gain protons or other adducts, so exact peaks depend on ion mass and charge-state chemistry.

Independent check: Check: doubling the positive charge magnitude approximately halves m/z for the same ion mass.

Exam trap: Mistake: interpreting z = +2 as atomic number Z = 2.

Worked example 33: Isoelectric versus isoelectronic confusion

Problem: Does the word ‘isoelectronic’ mean two species have zero net charge?

Complete reasoning: No. Isoelectronic refers to the same electron count or appropriate configuration. Neutral Ne, Na⁺ and F⁻ can each have ten electrons but have charges 0, +1 and −1. ‘Isoelectric point’ is a separate concept applied to species such as proteins, describing pH of zero net charge under stated conditions.

Independent check: Check: compare both E and q as independent quantities.

Exam trap: Mistake: treating similar-sounding terms as identical.

Worked example 34: Oxidation state of sulfur in sulfate

Problem: If oxygen is assigned −2 in SO₄²⁻, calculate sulfur's oxidation state.

Complete reasoning: Let sulfur's oxidation state be x. The sum must equal the overall ionic charge: x + 4(−2) = −2. Therefore x − 8 = −2 and x = +6. This is formal oxidation-state bookkeeping, not the statement that a free atomic S⁶⁺ cation is sitting inside a sulfate ion.

Independent check: Check: +6 − 8 = −2.

Exam trap: Mistake: applying E = Z − 6 to calculate sulfur's actual electrons in the bonded ion.

Worked example 35: Neutral water with nonzero oxidation states

Problem: Explain how H₂O can be neutral when oxygen has oxidation state −2 and hydrogen +1.

Complete reasoning: Two hydrogens assigned +1 contribute +2 and oxygen assigned −2 contributes −2; their oxidation-state sum is zero. This model assigns bonding electrons by chemical convention. A standard Lewis structure of water gives formal charges zero, proving that oxidation state and formal charge need not be equal.

Independent check: Check: oxidation states sum to the molecule's net charge zero.

Exam trap: Mistake: saying the water molecule must contain free O²⁻ and two separate protons.

Worked example 36: Counting without mass number

Problem: For K⁺, state what P, E and N can be known from the symbol alone.

Complete reasoning: Potassium has Z = 19 protons. Charge +1 gives E = 19 − 1 = 18. Without a mass number or specified isotope, the neutron count cannot be uniquely determined. The most common natural isotope is not automatically the one intended in every question.

Independent check: Check: P − E = +1; N remains unspecified.

Exam trap: Mistake: silently assuming A = 39 if the prompt did not state it.

Worked example 37: Check an impossible nucleus specification

Problem: A question proposes an atom with A = 10 and Z = 12. Is this valid?

Complete reasoning: The implied neutron number is N = A − Z = 10 − 12 = −2. A physical nucleus cannot contain a negative number of neutrons. The data are inconsistent, regardless of what electronic charge might be attached to the symbol.

Independent check: Check: the necessary condition A ≥ Z fails.

Exam trap: Mistake: accepting a negative neutron count as a negative ion.

Worked example 38: An ion in a cell is transported, not transmuted

Problem: A potassium channel moves K⁺ from a cell compartment to another. What is preserved?

Complete reasoning: Potassium remains potassium, with Z = 19. If the same isotope and ionic state are transported, the neutron and electron counts remain unchanged during simple permeation. Movement changes location and contributes to electrical and concentration gradients; it does not change K⁺ into Na⁺.

Independent check: Check: the membrane process changes spatial distribution, not the nuclear element.

Exam trap: Mistake: treating ion transport as a chemical electron-transfer reaction by default.

Worked example 39: Two ionic states of the same metal in a protein

Problem: Compare the formal electron counts of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ while recognizing limits of the model.

Complete reasoning: Simple monatomic Fe²⁺ has 24 electrons and Fe³⁺ has 23; both have 26 protons. In a covalently coordinated protein site, oxidation-state assignments remain useful for electron-transfer bookkeeping but actual electron density can be delocalized across ligands. The elementary count should not be used as a literal localized metal-electron census in every complex.

Independent check: Check: only one formal electron differs between the stated charge states.

Exam trap: Mistake: thinking Fe³⁺ has 27 nuclear protons.

Worked example 40: A zwitterion has local charges but may be net neutral

Problem: An amino-acid zwitterion has one ammonium-like +1 group and one carboxylate −1 group. What is its net charge?

Complete reasoning: Adding local formal charges yields +1 + (−1) = 0. The molecule can therefore be electrically neutral overall while containing charged functional groups. Its actual protonation distribution depends on pH and environment.

Independent check: Check: overall balance is zero, not absence of internal charge.

Exam trap: Mistake: equating net zero with every atom's formal charge being zero.

Answer: Yes

Explanation: Positive and negative local formal groups can cancel overall.

Question 37. What is the net charge of NH₄⁺?

Answer: +1

Explanation: Charge belongs to the complete group, not separately to each atom.

Question 38. Which ion has the larger individual positive charge, Ca²⁺ or Na⁺?

Answer: Ca²⁺

Explanation: Two elementary positive charges versus one.

Question 39. Does the symbol Ca²⁺ state calcium concentration?

Answer: No

Explanation: It states charge per ion, not number of ions per volume.

Question 40. Which two ions are exchanged by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase?

Answer: Na⁺ and K⁺

Explanation: Common cycle transports three sodium out and two potassium in.

Question 41. How much net positive charge is transported outward per common pump cycle?

Answer: +1e

Explanation: Three Na⁺ outward minus two K⁺ inward gives net +1 outward.

Question 42. Does the Na⁺/K⁺ pump change sodium into potassium?

Answer: No

Explanation: Each ion retains its own nuclear identity while changing location.

Question 43. What generally drives passive ion movement through an open channel?

Answer: Electrochemical gradient

Explanation: Both concentration and voltage affect charged particle movement.

Question 44. Does a K⁺ channel necessarily pass Na⁺ equally well because both have +1 charge?

Answer: No

Explanation: Selectivity depends on pore chemistry, hydration and structural interactions.

Question 45. What ionic movement often contributes to neuronal depolarization?

Answer: Na⁺ influx

Explanation: Simplified action-potential models involve transient increased sodium permeability.

Question 46. What ionic movement contributes to repolarization in common neuronal models?

Answer: K⁺ efflux

Explanation: Potassium permeability influences the return toward resting membrane conditions.

Question 47. What does z mean in the Nernst equation?

Answer: Signed ionic valence

Explanation: It is charge number, not nuclear atomic number Z.

Question 48. For chloride, is Nernst z positive or negative?

Answer: −1

Explanation: Cl⁻ carries one negative elementary charge.

Question 49. Does Nernst equilibrium imply equal concentrations inside and outside?

Answer: No

Explanation: Electrical force can balance a chemical concentration gradient.

Question 50. Which form of calcium is important in signaling?

Answer: Ca²⁺

Explanation: Regulated ionic calcium participates in many cellular signals.

Question 51. What does H⁺ lack relative to neutral protium?

Answer: Its electron

Explanation: Neutral protium has one proton and electron; formal H⁺ has no electron.

Question 52. In aqueous solution, what species often represents proton hydration?

Answer: H₃O⁺

Explanation: Hydronium is a useful expression of proton solvation and acid-base reaction.

Question 53. Is pH rigorously defined from hydrogen-ion activity or just concentration?

Answer: Activity

Explanation: pH is tied to hydrogen-ion activity; concentration is an approximation in simple models.

Question 54. What charge is typically associated with DNA phosphate groups at physiological pH?

Answer: Negative

Explanation: Deprotonated phosphate groups contribute negative backbone charge.

Question 55. What does m/z measure in a mass spectrometer?

Answer: Mass-to-charge ratio

Explanation: The observed ion's mass and charge state determine the position, not atomic number Z.

Question 56. Can a 2+ molecular ion have roughly half the m/z of a comparable 1+ molecular ion?

Answer: Yes

Explanation: Under the simplified equal-ion-mass assumption, m/z is inversely proportional to charge magnitude.

Question 57. Why is an ion's electron count not enough to predict stability?

Answer: Energetics and environment matter

Explanation: Ionization energies, bonding, solvation and coordination affect feasibility.

Question 58. Can the total ion number in a neutral solution be nonzero?

Answer: Yes

Explanation: Equal positive and negative total charges can cancel while many ions remain present.

Question 59. If q = −2 and Z = 8, how many electrons?

Answer: 10

Explanation: E = 8 − (−2) = 10.

Question 60. If E = 18 and q = +1, what is Z?

Answer: 19

Explanation: Z = E + q = 18 + 1, so the element is potassium.

Eight integrative, explanation-based challenges

The next challenges combine multiple ideas. They are especially useful before moving from atomic structure to more demanding biochemistry and membrane physiology questions.

Integrated challenge 1. Clinical electrolyte label versus chemical state

Scenario: A report lists Na⁺ and K⁺ concentrations rather than masses of sodium and potassium metal. Explain why these symbols matter.

Reasoned answer: The report concerns solvated ions in biological fluid. Each Na⁺ and K⁺ carries +e, while their different nuclear identities and biological transport properties are important. Concentration is a separate quantity from charge. Metallic sodium or potassium would be entirely different chemical substances.

Integrated challenge 2. A protein with bound Zn²⁺

Scenario: An enzyme structure contains a zinc-binding site labelled Zn²⁺. What is secure from the notation, and what is not?

Reasoned answer: Formal Zn²⁺ denotes a zinc species with oxidation state/charge description +2 in the site, with zinc nuclear Z = 30. A simple free-ion count would be 28 electrons. The exact coordination geometry, electron-density distribution and function cannot be established from ‘Zn²⁺’ alone.

Integrated challenge 3. A hypothetical neuron and net ionic flux

Scenario: During one interval, 200 Na⁺ enter a cell and 50 K⁺ leave. Calculate net elementary charge moved into the cell, ignoring all other fluxes.

Reasoned answer: Positive charges entering: +200e. Positive charges leaving contribute −50e to inward charge accounting. Net inward movement is +150e. This is a bookkeeping exercise only, not a realistic full calculation of membrane voltage.

Integrated challenge 4. Same isotope, two chemical states

Scenario: Two samples contain ⁵⁶Fe²⁺ and ⁵⁶Fe³⁺. Are they different isotopes, and what count changes?

Reasoned answer: Both share iron-56 nuclei with 26 protons and 30 neutrons. The formal monatomic electron counts are 24 and 23, respectively. Their oxidation-state chemistry differs, but isotope identity does not.

Integrated challenge 5. Neutrality and zwitterion structure

Scenario: An amino acid has two formal functional-group charges, +1 and −1. The molecular charge is zero. Does this mean no part has electrical interactions?

Reasoned answer: No. Local charged groups can interact strongly with solvent and proteins despite net zero charge. Net neutrality refers to the total, not to the absence of polar or charged sites.

Integrated challenge 6. Salt ratios under a new charge combination

Scenario: Suppose a +3 metal cation reacts with a −2 monatomic anion to form a charge-balanced simple ionic compound. What is the smallest ratio?

Reasoned answer: Two cations contribute +6 and three anions contribute −6. The smallest integer ratio is 2:3, assuming the specified ionic charge model applies. Chemical existence and formula details require additional context.

Integrated challenge 7. A mass spectrometry peak at half m/z

Scenario: A peptide gives positive-ion signals at m/z approximately 1001 and 501. Does this prove two different peptide isotope masses?

Reasoned answer: No. A simple explanation may involve different positive charge states of roughly the same molecular ion, with proton-addition corrections. Additional charge-state analysis, isotope spacing and instrumentation are needed; one cannot assign a different nuclide from the m/z change alone.

Integrated challenge 8. A result that is not knowable

Scenario: A question gives ‘a neutral magnesium atom’ and asks for its exact neutron count. What is the scientifically complete answer?

Reasoned answer: Magnesium's atomic number is 12, so a neutral atom has 12 protons and 12 electrons. But magnesium has multiple nuclides and no mass number was supplied. Its exact neutron count is therefore not uniquely determined.

1.A.1.16.30 Common mistakes and their scientific corrections

A wrong answer often comes from applying the right equation to the wrong object. The following misconceptions will distinguish nuclear charges, ionic charges, formal charges, isotope identity, chemical stoichiometry and biological transport.

Twenty-four misconceptions repaired with evidence

A scientifically strong student can explain why a tempting answer is wrong. Each example identifies a mistaken claim, gives its accurate replacement and shows how to verify it.

Misconception 1

Incorrect claim: A positive ion has gained protons.

Scientific correction: Ordinary cation formation removes electrons; nuclear proton number remains fixed. Na⁺ still has eleven protons.

How to check it: Ask whether the proposed explanation describes a chemical electron transfer or a nuclear reaction.

Misconception 2

Incorrect claim: A negative ion has negative protons.

Scientific correction: Protons are always positively charged. Anions have more electrons than protons.

How to check it: Use q = P − E to show how a surplus of negative electrons produces charge −1 or −2.

Misconception 3

Incorrect claim: A neutral atom contains no charge.

Scientific correction: Positive protons and negative electrons can cancel in net charge.

How to check it: The word ‘neutral’ refers to the total signed charge, not absence of charged particles.

Misconception 4

Incorrect claim: The mass number equals the electron count.

Scientific correction: Mass number is protons plus neutrons and does not count electrons.

How to check it: Use A only for nuclear nucleons and E for the electron population.

Misconception 5

Incorrect claim: A 2+ ion has two protons.

Scientific correction: 2+ is its electrical charge number, not nuclear atomic number.

How to check it: Calcium-40 Ca²⁺ has twenty protons, not two.

Misconception 6

Incorrect claim: A neutral oxygen atom and oxide ion are isotopes.

Scientific correction: They may share the same nuclear isotope but have different electron counts.

How to check it: Isotope identity concerns neutron number at fixed Z; ionic charge concerns electrons.

Misconception 7

Incorrect claim: A chloride anion loses one electron.

Scientific correction: Cl⁻ has one additional electron compared with neutral chlorine.

How to check it: Negative ion formation involves net electron gain relative to the neutral atom.

Misconception 8

Incorrect claim: Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ are the same element because both have ten electrons.

Scientific correction: They are isoelectronic but have different proton numbers.

How to check it: Element identity follows Z, whereas isoelectronic means matching electron count.

Misconception 9

Incorrect claim: Every ion obeys an eight-electron rule.

Scientific correction: The octet rule is a limited pattern, not a universal law.

How to check it: Discuss electron configurations and bonding energetics; transition-metal ions are especially instructive counterexamples.

Misconception 10

Incorrect claim: The oxidaton state of O in H₂O means oxygen is free O²⁻.

Scientific correction: Oxidation state is conventional bonding electron assignment, not a directly observed isolated ionic species.

How to check it: Compare oxidation state −2 with formal charge zero in a standard water Lewis structure.

Misconception 11

Incorrect claim: Formal charge and oxidation state are always equal.

Scientific correction: Different electron assignment conventions can yield different values.

How to check it: Identify the specific bookkeeping model required by the question.

Misconception 12

Incorrect claim: Sulfate 2− means every sulfate atom is a 2− ion.

Scientific correction: The −2 charge belongs to the whole molecular ion.

How to check it: Sum all nuclear Z values once and adjust by net molecular charge.

Misconception 13

Incorrect claim: A crystal with zero total charge cannot contain ions.

Scientific correction: An ionic solid can have positive and negative constituent ions whose charges cancel.

How to check it: NaCl provides a straightforward example of electrically neutral bulk matter containing ions.

Misconception 14

Incorrect claim: All ionic compounds automatically dissolve completely in water.

Scientific correction: Solubility depends on energetic and equilibrium conditions.

How to check it: Lattice interactions, hydration and other thermodynamic factors matter.

Misconception 15

Incorrect claim: Na⁺ crossing a membrane becomes neutral Na inside the cell.

Scientific correction: A channel ordinarily transports a charged ion without changing its nuclear identity or ionic charge state.

How to check it: Treat movement across a membrane separately from electron-transfer reactions.

Misconception 16

Incorrect claim: Two ions with the same charge behave identically in biology.

Scientific correction: Hydration, ionic radius, binding sites and concentration gradients can differ.

How to check it: Compare Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ in enzymes and signaling.

Misconception 17

Incorrect claim: The sodium-potassium pump transports electrons rather than ions.

Scientific correction: The pump moves Na⁺ and K⁺ across the membrane.

How to check it: Net outward positive charge reflects opposite ionic transports, not electronic transmutation.

Misconception 18

Incorrect claim: Ionic charge tells the cell concentration of that ion.

Scientific correction: Charge states how much electric charge one ion carries.

How to check it: Concentration requires amount per solution volume, a separate measurement.

Misconception 19

Incorrect claim: Nernst z equals atomic number Z.

Scientific correction: Nernst z means signed ionic valence.

How to check it: Potassium has Z = 19 but K⁺ has valence +1.

Misconception 20

Incorrect claim: m/z uses z to mean the number of protons in an atom.

Scientific correction: In mass spectrometry z refers to ion charge state.

How to check it: Molecular ions can carry multiple charges independent of the constituent atomic numbers.

Misconception 21

Incorrect claim: All ions of charge +2 are equally stable in water.

Scientific correction: Energetics, solvation, coordination and chemical form differ.

How to check it: A bare hypothetical ion and a hydrated or bound ion cannot be treated as equivalent.

Misconception 22

Incorrect claim: A zwitterion cannot be neutral.

Scientific correction: A zwitterion can carry local opposite formal charges summing to zero.

How to check it: Separate local charge assignment from total net molecular charge.

Misconception 23

Incorrect claim: H⁺ always appears as a free bare proton in ordinary aqueous solution.

Scientific correction: Aqueous proton chemistry strongly involves hydration and proton-transfer environments.

How to check it: Use H₃O⁺ where suitable and recognize it as a simplification of broader solvation.

Misconception 24

Incorrect claim: If a question does not give an isotope, neutron count can always be inferred from an average atomic mass.

Scientific correction: An elemental name alone fixes Z but may not specify A and N.

How to check it: State that neutron count is underdetermined unless a nuclide or mass number is specified.

1.A.1.16.31 Master revision, glossary and node continuity

Six essential results:
  1. Atomic number Z = number of protons.
  2. Mass number A = protons + neutrons.
  3. Neutrons N = A − Z.
  4. Neutral monatomic atom has E = Z.
  5. Monatomic ion q = Z − E, so E = Z − q.
  6. Whole molecular-ion electrons = sum of atomic numbers − net ionic charge.

How to revise scientifically

First write P, N and E separately for five ions without looking at the answer. Next explain the difference between neutral atoms, ions, isotopes and molecular ions. Then solve a formula-charge problem, a protein/physiology interpretation, and an electronic-configuration problem. Return to the relevant concept if any error occurs.

Concise glossary

Neutral atom: equal protons and electrons. Cation: positively charged ion. Anion: negatively charged ion. Isoelectronic: species with the same electron count or electronic configuration. Oxidation state: bonding-based electron-allocation convention. Formal charge: Lewis-structure electron assignment. Electrolyte: a substance giving rise to mobile ionic charge carriers in appropriate media. Hydration: interactions with surrounding water. Electrochemical gradient: combined chemical and electrical driving force.

Chronological chapter map: follow the connected scientific idea

We began with observations of electrical charge and the electron's discovery. Nuclear atomic number then separated element identity from electronic charge. Once electron configurations were understood, we could explain typical cations and anions. Chemical contexts such as salts and hydration then led to the living cell, where selective transporters and channels exploit the fact that ions already carry charge. Finally, exam calculations and biological case studies tested whether the same logic works at every scale.

A high-yield oral examination

Explain in your own words why ²³Na and ²³Na⁺ share the same atomic and mass numbers, why Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ can both have ten electrons yet be different elements, and why sulfate's net charge is not the oxidation state of every atom. If these three questions are answered well, the central conceptual distinctions are secure.

An exam-ready worked derivation

If a monatomic ion has E = 18 and charge number q = +3, then Z = E + q = 21, identifying scandium. With mass number A = 45, N = A − Z = 24. Thus a complete notation could be ⁴⁵₂₁Sc³⁺. Reverse-check 21 − 18 = +3 and 21 + 24 = 45. This solution shows how to move from electronic data to nuclear identity without confusing charge with isotope.
