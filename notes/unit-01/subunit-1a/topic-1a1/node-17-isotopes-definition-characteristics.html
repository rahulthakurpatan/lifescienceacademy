1.A.1.17.1 Two carbon samples, one element: the puzzle behind isotopes

Imagine a graduate student working in a plant biology laboratory. One jar contains glucose made mostly with ordinary carbon-12. Another contains glucose enriched with carbon-13. Both jars contain molecules built from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen; their carbon atoms belong to the same chemical element. Yet a sensitive instrument can separate signals from the two samples. Why can a chemical element have different atomic masses without becoming a different element? The answer is the idea of isotopes.

The identity card of an atom

Carbon is defined by six protons in its nucleus. Every carbon isotope has Z = 6, but different isotopes contain different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12 has six; carbon-13 has seven; carbon-14 has eight. The extra neutrons change mass number and some nuclear and mass-dependent behavior, but not the proton number that makes carbon carbon.

Why a simple definition is not enough

In life science, isotope knowledge reaches beyond counting neutrons. We must distinguish a nuclide from an element, an atom from an ion, a stable isotope from a radionuclide, and a tracer label from a metabolic flux. We must also understand why two isotopes can have nearly the same chemistry yet show measurable kinetic and equilibrium isotope effects. The following journey will connect these ideas carefully.

A logical route for this complete chapter

First we follow the history: early atomic ideas, unexpected radio-elements, atomic number, mass spectrographs and discovery of the neutron. Then we derive the isotope definition and notation. Next come physical properties, natural abundances, mass measurement, nuclear stability, fractionation and analytical methods. We finish with biological applications, solved calculations, case studies, misconception diagnosis and original exam practice.

Core question: What remains the same when isotopes differ? Their atomic number Z and elemental identity. What changes? Their neutron count N and therefore mass number A; nuclear and mass-sensitive properties may also differ.

1.A.1.17.2 Before the word isotope: the chronological scientific problem

Dalton's useful starting point

Early chemical atomic theory treated atoms as the building blocks of elements and compounds. The assumption that all atoms of one element have the same mass worked as an approximation in much early chemical reasoning, but it was not the final story. Later observations would show that a single element can contain nuclei with different neutron counts and masses.

Radioactivity introduced atoms with surprising relationships

At the beginning of the twentieth century, chemists studying radioactive decay saw substances with different radioactive behaviors that nevertheless occupied the same chemical position in the periodic classification. This challenged the simple idea that each chemically distinct element should correspond to only one atomic mass. Nuclear transformations could change an element, but some related radioactive products behaved chemically almost indistinguishably.

Soddy's insight and the meaning of the name

Frederick Soddy and others clarified that species with distinct atomic masses could occupy the same position in the periodic table. The word isotope expresses this same-place idea. The discovery was not merely the invention of terminology: it revised what ‘one chemical element’ means when atomic weights and nuclear properties vary.

Atomic number provided the decisive identity rule

Henry Moseley's X-ray studies connected periodic position with nuclear charge. A modern element is defined by the number of protons Z, not by one fixed mass number. Once this rule is adopted, two nuclides of equal Z but different A are recognized as isotopes. The chemical element remains the same because the nuclear charge number remains the same.

Mass spectrographs made multiple stable masses visible

Thomson's observations of neon-related mass signals and Francis Aston's later mass-spectrograph work showed that stable elements can appear in distinct mass species. Chlorine, neon and other elements became powerful evidence that natural samples can contain several isotopes. A reported average atomic weight can therefore arise from a mixture, rather than every atom having an intermediate fractional nucleon count.

The neutron completed the clean explanation

After the neutron was established in 1932, the counting picture became straightforward: an isotope of an element differs mainly by how many neutrons reside in its nucleus. With A = Z + N, keeping Z fixed and changing N automatically changes A. This equation gives the mechanism behind the definition but is not, by itself, a prediction of stability.

1.A.1.17.3 Exact isotope definition: what must be equal and what must differ?

IUPAC gives a concise rule

The IUPAC Gold Book defines isotopes as nuclides having the same atomic number but different mass numbers. In our particle language: Z must be equal, while A must be different. Because A = Z + N, it follows that isotope partners differ in neutron number N. The definition is a relationship between nuclear species, not a claim about their chemical charge or molecular surroundings.

The chemical element is one, the nuclides are different

The expression ‘carbon has isotopes’ means that multiple carbon nuclides exist, all with six protons. A sample may contain atoms of more than one nuclide. The phrase ‘carbon isotope’ names a nuclear version of carbon; it does not name a new periodic-table element. This is important for both chemistry and biological isotope tracing.

A scientific definition is necessary and sufficient for the classification

If two nuclides have the same Z and different A, the pair is isotopic. If their Z differs, they are not isotopes of each other even if they have the same mass number. If their A and Z are equal, they are the same nuclide identity at the introductory count level; a differing nuclear energy state can still require a nuclear-isomer label.

Isotopes: Z₁ = Z₂,   A₁ ≠ A₂   ⇔   N₁ ≠ N₂ when Z is fixed
Concept checkpoint: The word isotope does not mean ‘same neutrons’. Same neutron number with different Z defines isotones, not isotopes.

1.A.1.17.4 Nuclide notation and the isotope family's shared address

Decode the left labels carefully

In ¹³₆C, the upper-left 13 is mass number A and lower-left 6 is atomic number Z. The chemical symbol C gives the same elemental identity. So carbon-13 has six protons and seven neutrons. Neutral carbon-13 has six electrons. If it is an ion, electron count changes according to ionic charge without changing whether its nucleus is carbon-13.

Recognize the short forms that scientists use

Carbon-12, ¹²C and ¹²₆C express the same isotope identity when context is clear. The periodic-table element symbol alone, C, does not say which isotope was selected. A label such as [1-¹³C]glucose also includes the position of a carbon isotope within a molecule; the numeral 1 is a molecular location, not the isotope's atomic number.

Write the isotope pair from a verbal description

Suppose an element contains eight protons in both nuclei but eight neutrons in one and ten in the other. Z = 8 identifies oxygen. The first has A = 8 + 8 = 16, the second A = 8 + 10 = 18. They are ¹⁶₈O and ¹⁸₈O, isotopes of oxygen. You do not need their electron counts to establish the isotope relationship.

1.A.1.17.5 Carbon isotopes: one nucleus family, three clear examples

Carbon-12 anchors the picture

Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons, giving A = 12. It is stable. It is also central to the unified atomic mass unit definition. A neutral carbon-12 atom has exactly twelve unified atomic mass units by definition of that unit, but this convenient fact does not mean all isotope masses equal their integer mass numbers.

Carbon-13 provides the stable heavier partner

Carbon-13 has six protons and seven neutrons, so A = 13. It is stable and is widely used in isotope measurements, molecular NMR and metabolic tracing. Its neutral atomic mass is approximately 13.00335 u, not exactly 13 u; binding energies and particle masses explain why measured isotopic masses are not generally whole numbers.

Carbon-14 shows why nuclear stability is a separate property

Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons. It is radioactive, undergoing beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14. The half-life is about 5,730 years. The symbol tells us six protons and eight neutrons directly, but the fact that this nuclide is unstable comes from nuclear evidence, not a universal rule that two extra neutrons automatically cause radioactivity.

1.A.1.17.6 Hydrogen isotopes: a remarkable natural teaching example

Protium has zero neutrons

The common hydrogen-1 nuclide, often called protium, has one proton and no neutron. Its nucleus is simply a proton. Neutral protium has one electron. This does not mean that all hydrogen consists of the same isotope; hydrogen has heavier isotopic forms.

Deuterium doubles the nucleon count

Deuterium, ²H or D, has one proton and one neutron. It is a stable isotope of hydrogen. Its mass difference from ¹H is proportionally large, so deuterium substitution can create noticeable chemical isotope effects. Heavy water D₂O contains deuterium at hydrogen positions, with the subscript 2 indicating two D atoms in a molecule.

Tritium adds another neutron

Tritium, ³H or T, has one proton and two neutrons and is radioactive. The hydrogen isotope family is therefore useful for comparing stable and radioactive nuclear identities with one constant Z. However the full applications and radiological behavior of tritium are distinct from merely counting its neutrons.

Why hydrogen is especially important for isotope effects

Because ²H is roughly twice the atomic mass of ¹H, replacing H with D can alter vibrational frequencies and reaction rates more noticeably than many heavier-element substitutions. This underlies important primary kinetic isotope effects when a labelled bond is broken in a rate-influencing step. The effect is not ‘different chemistry because of different electrons’; it is a mass-sensitive consequence of nuclear substitution.

1.A.1.17.7 Oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and chlorine isotope families

Oxygen gives three commonly discussed stable nuclides

Oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 all have eight protons, with eight, nine and ten neutrons respectively. Stable oxygen isotope variations are useful in environmental studies and in some biological labeling contexts. Their abundances differ; the element name alone does not specify which oxygen isotope a sample contains.

Nitrogen-14 and nitrogen-15 support biological tracing

Both have seven protons. Nitrogen-14 has seven neutrons and nitrogen-15 has eight. Stable ¹⁵N tracing can help investigate nitrogen incorporation into metabolites, amino acids or other biomolecules when paired with suitable biochemical experiments and analysis. The label shows nuclear identity, not a unique metabolic pathway by itself.

Sulfur illustrates both isotope diversity and chemical commonality

Sulfur has several stable isotopes, including ³²S, ³³S, ³⁴S and ³⁶S. Their proton number remains sixteen. Sulfur isotope ratios can inform environmental and chemical process studies, while sulfur-bearing amino acids connect this elemental chemistry to living systems.

Chlorine is a simple bridge to periodic-table average mass

Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 have seventeen protons and eighteen or twenty neutrons. Their natural mixture helps explain why chlorine has a conventional atomic-weight value near 35.45 instead of an integer. This average describes relative isotope abundance, not an atom with 18.45 neutrons.

1.A.1.17.8 Isotopes versus ions: restore the distinction from Node 16

A change in charge is not a change in isotope

Neutral ²³Na and ²³Na⁺ have the same nucleus: eleven protons and twelve neutrons. Their electron counts are 11 and 10. They are different charge states of the same nuclide, not two isotopes. Isotopic comparison needs two different A values at equal Z.

An isotope may itself be an ion

A neutral carbon-13 atom and a carbon-13 ion retain the same nuclear isotope identity. The electron count changes with charge, but the mass number and proton count stay fixed. Some mass spectrometry methods measure ions of isotope-labelled molecules; the ion charge controls m/z while the isotopic substitutions contribute mass differences.

A useful two-step test

First compare Z and A to decide isotope identity. Next, if the question asks about charge, use q = Z − E for a monatomic atomic species. This avoids the common mistake of concluding that two species with different electron numbers are necessarily different isotopes.

Concept checkpoint: A label such as ³⁷Cl⁻ contains both a nuclear label and an ionic charge. The left 37 specifies a chlorine nuclide; the right minus specifies its electronic charge state.

1.A.1.17.9 Isotopes, isobars, isotones and nuclear isomers

Isotopes keep the proton number fixed

For ¹²C and ¹⁴C, Z = 6 in both while A differs. Their neutron counts differ, so they are isotopes. If an exam says ‘same element and different mass number’, isotope is the appropriate nuclear relationship.

Isobars keep the total nucleon count fixed

For ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N, A = 14 in both, but Z differs: six versus seven. Their element identities differ and neutron counts are eight and seven. The pair is isobaric, not isotopic.

Isotones keep the neutron number fixed

Carbon-14 has N = 8, and nitrogen-15 has N = 8. They are isotones because they have the same neutron number but different atomic numbers. These comparisons require computing N, not merely looking for equal left superscripts.

Nuclear isomers keep A and Z but differ in nuclear energy state

Some nuclear species can occupy metastable excited states. The familiar ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc notation differs by state labeling, not by proton or neutron count. This is not an isotope pair with different A. A complete nuclear identity may require the energy-state designation.

1.A.1.17.10 The N–Z chart: see isotope families as lines

Turn two symbols into coordinates

A chart of nuclides can place neutron number N on the horizontal axis and proton number Z vertically. Holding Z fixed while changing N moves horizontally across one element's isotope row. Holding N fixed creates an isotone column. Holding A = N + Z constant creates a diagonal of isobars.

Find the carbon family on the map

For carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14, Z = 6 and N takes values 6, 7 and 8. On an N-horizontal, Z-vertical map they align along the same Z row. If carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14, Z rises by one while N falls by one; the total A stays fourteen.

The stability boundary is not one simple classroom formula

Many lighter stable nuclei have N near Z, while heavier stable nuclei often require more neutrons than protons. Binding energy, nuclear pairing and shell effects also matter. A chart of nuclides contains empirical nuclear stability information; the mathematical isotope definition alone cannot tell you which isotopes are stable.

1.A.1.17.11 Chemical similarity: what ‘same element’ truly guarantees

Nuclear charge organizes the electron structure

When corresponding neutral atoms have the same number of protons and electrons, they have the same basic electronic configuration pattern. That is why isotopes of the same element generally share chemical identity, common valence and placement in the periodic table. They can participate in the same kinds of chemical compounds.

The word ‘identical’ is too strong for all physical processes

Changing neutron number changes isotope mass and can affect molecular vibration, diffusion, rotational motion and reaction kinetics. Hydrogen and deuterium substitutions can cause especially large relative effects. Thus isotope chemistry is often similar, but isotope-resolved behavior is not always numerically identical.

Nuclear properties can differ greatly without a new element

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon, yet one is stable and the other radioactive. Nuclear spin also differs among isotopes and affects which nuclei are convenient for magnetic resonance. The distinction between nuclear and electron-governed properties is essential for understanding isotope applications.

Strong exam sentence: Isotopes share atomic number and broadly similar chemical identity, but they may differ in nuclear stability, atomic mass, nuclear spin and mass-dependent chemical/physical behavior.

1.A.1.17.12 Mass number A is not the measured isotope mass

Count versus measure

Mass number A is an integer number of protons plus neutrons. Measured atomic mass is a quantity with units such as unified atomic mass units. Nuclear binding energy and the masses of constituent particles mean an isotope's measured neutral-atom mass is not generally exactly equal to A.

Why carbon-12 is a special reference

The unified atomic mass unit is defined using one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. Consequently the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom is exactly 12 u by definition. Carbon-13 has a measured mass near 13.003355 u, not precisely 13 u. This makes it crucial not to round real isotope masses prematurely in accurate mass spectrometry calculations.

Mass defect is an energy accounting concept

The mass of a bound nucleus is generally smaller than the sum of the masses of its separate protons and neutrons because binding energy contributes to the mass–energy balance. This is called mass defect. The concept is closely related to the relation E = mc², but an isotope notation problem does not usually provide enough data to calculate binding energy unless particle and nuclear masses are given.

A = Z + N (exact integer count)   ≠   measured mass in u (generally non-integer)

1.A.1.17.13 Isotopic abundance: a property of a sample, not a nucleus

One nucleus cannot be 20 percent an isotope

Every nucleus has a particular A and Z. A sample containing many atoms, however, can have a mixture of isotopes. Isotopic abundance is the fraction of that element's atoms belonging to a specified isotope. If 80 out of 100 atoms are isotope X-10 and 20 are X-11, the atom fractions are 0.80 and 0.20.

Natural abundance and tracer enrichment are different contexts

A naturally occurring material may have isotope proportions characteristic of terrestrial conditions. A prepared stable-isotope tracer can be deliberately enriched in a rarer isotope. Writing ¹³C specifies a nucleus, while writing 99 atom% ¹³C specifies an approximate composition of a material. The label is not an abundance measurement.

Values vary in nature and across reference data systems

The CIAAW reports composition ranges for some elements because natural processes fractionate isotopes. For carbon, a common terrestrial sample is dominated by ¹²C, with ¹³C generally around one percent. Exact natural abundances vary, so a specific sample's isotope ratio should be measured rather than assumed from a single universal number.

1.A.1.17.14 Weighted average atomic mass: derive the correct formula

A mixture requires an average over isotope masses

If an element has isotopes of measured mass m₁, m₂, … and atom fractions x₁, x₂, … with sum one, the mean neutral atomic mass for that sample is m̄ = Σ xᵢmᵢ. The dimensionless relative atomic mass compares the mean to the unified mass unit. In an introductory classroom problem, isotope mass numbers may be used as approximations when the question explicitly supplies them.

Work with an artificial two-isotope example

An imaginary element has masses 10.00 u and 11.00 u with fractions 0.20 and 0.80. Its mean atomic mass is 0.20(10.00) + 0.80(11.00) = 10.80 u. This does not create a nucleus with mass number 10.8. It is the mean over many nuclei of distinct isotope identities.

Use a realistic chlorine illustration with appropriate caution

Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 commonly occur in a ratio near three to one. An elementary approximate calculation using 35 and 37 gives 0.75(35) + 0.25(37) = 35.5. A precise atomic-weight value uses measured isotopic masses and sample compositions rather than simply multiplying integer mass numbers.

How to solve inverse abundance questions

If two given isotope masses are 20 u and 22 u and the sample average is 20.4 u, write 20(1−x)+22x = 20.4. This gives x = 0.20 for the heavier isotope. Always state that the calculation assumes the two specified species account for the whole sample and that the masses are those supplied in the problem.

Mean mass m̄ = Σ xᵢ mᵢ,   Σ xᵢ = 1,   xᵢ ≥ 0

1.A.1.17.15 Isotope ratios, delta notation and fractionation

Ratios compare two isotope populations directly

An isotope ratio such as R = n(¹³C)/n(¹²C) is the number of atoms of one isotope divided by the number of atoms of another isotope of the same element in a sample. It is different from the atom fraction x(¹³C), which divides by the total number of carbon atoms of all relevant isotopes.

The delta notation compares an isotope ratio to a reference

In environmental isotope science, a delta value commonly expresses the fractional difference between a sample isotope ratio and a reference ratio, multiplied by 1000 and stated in per mille (‰). For example, δ¹³C compares a carbon isotope ratio with a specified reference scale. A negative value generally indicates a lower heavy-to-light ratio than the chosen reference, not a negative number of atoms.

Fractionation is physically meaningful

Physical and chemical processes can distinguish isotopes slightly because of their different masses. Evaporation, diffusion, biological metabolism and equilibrium exchange can yield different isotope ratios in products and reactants. Such fractionation can provide evidence for environmental processes, but interpreting the direction and magnitude requires the relevant model and reference scale.

Be cautious with the word ‘heavier’

The heavier isotope is heavier at the nuclear/atomic mass level; its chemical atomic number stays the same. Environmental interpretations based on heavy versus light isotope ratios do not imply that one sample contains a new element.

δ = (Rsample / Rreference − 1) × 1000 ‰

1.A.1.17.16 Kinetic isotope effects and equilibrium isotope effects

Follow a bond in a chemical reaction

Imagine a reaction in which a bond involving hydrogen participates in a rate-influencing step. Replace protium with deuterium, leaving the rest of the molecular chemistry appropriately comparable. The bond vibration and zero-point energy change with nuclear mass, which can change the reaction rate. This is a kinetic isotope effect.

Primary and secondary effects must be distinguished

A primary kinetic isotope effect involves isotope substitution at a bond directly broken or formed in the rate-controlling chemistry. A secondary isotope effect occurs when substitution is elsewhere and influences the reaction indirectly. The size and direction of the observed effect depend on mechanism, conditions and isotopic substitution.

Equilibrium isotope effects concern relative distribution

Isotopic substitution can also alter an equilibrium constant or the relative partition of isotopes between chemical species. This is a thermodynamic or equilibrium isotope effect, not merely a measured change in reaction speed. Distinguishing rate from equilibrium is fundamental in chemical and biological interpretation.

Why isotope effects are experimentally useful but not proof alone

An observed isotope effect can support hypotheses about chemical steps and bond involvement, but multiple mechanistic factors can affect the measured result. A rigorous conclusion needs suitable controls and a model. A simple isotope label by itself cannot prove which bond breaks in an enzyme transition state.

A common kinetic comparison: KIE = klight / kheavy for appropriately matched reactions

1.A.1.17.17 Diffusion and physical properties: similar chemistry, measurable differences

Why physical transport can differ

Isotopes differ in atomic mass. Molecular samples whose isotopes differ can show small differences in diffusion, evaporation, rotational properties and vibrational spectra. Such effects are often strongest in relative terms for hydrogen isotopes because replacing ¹H with ²H is a substantial proportional mass increase.

Do not overstate isotope separation from a simple formula

A mass-related tendency does not mean isotopes can always be separated with a casual physical process. Separation methods depend on small effects, precise instrumentation and specialized contexts. For classroom purposes, the important principle is that measured properties may differ even when elemental identity does not.

Biological relevance follows from isotope-sensitive measurements

A plant's carbon isotope composition can reflect aspects of photosynthetic pathways and environmental conditions. Water isotope ratios can inform hydrological processes. Neither observation is a one-to-one fingerprint without other information, because multiple processes can influence measured isotopic composition.

1.A.1.17.18 Why nuclear stability differs among isotopes

Neutrons influence the competition of nuclear forces

Protons repel each other electrically, while nuclear interactions bind nucleons at short range. Neutrons contribute to nuclear binding without adding electric charge. Different neutron counts can change nuclear energy and stability. This explains why isotope families often include both stable and radioactive members.

The valley of stability is an empirical nuclear pattern

For lighter elements, stable nuclides often have neutron counts near proton counts. For heavier nuclei, stable species commonly contain more neutrons than protons. Pairing, shell structure and other nuclear effects complicate any simple ratio rule. The isotope definition identifies a pair but does not allow a universal stability prediction.

Stability and natural abundance are not synonymous

A stable isotope can be rare; an unstable isotope can be produced continuously in nature. Some radioisotopes persist due to very long half-lives. The abundance of a nuclide depends on how it is formed, destroyed and distributed as well as on its stability.

Concept checkpoint: Never classify a nuclide as radioactive solely because N ≠ Z. Many stable nuclides have unequal neutron and proton counts.

1.A.1.17.19 Stable isotopes and radioisotopes: separate identity from decay

A stable isotope does not undergo observed radioactive decay

Stable isotopes, within current experimental evidence, do not show spontaneous radioactive decay. Common examples include ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴N, ¹⁵N and ¹⁸O. Their chemical use does not require ionizing radiation detection; they can be measured through isotope-sensitive analytical methods.

A radionuclide has an unstable nucleus

Radioisotopes undergo spontaneous nuclear transformations and may emit particles or radiation. Carbon-14 is a classic example, but isotopes of many elements can be radioactive. The kind of decay and half-life are properties of the specific nuclear species, not all isotopes of its parent element.

Half-life describes an exponential statistical process

A radioactive population decays stochastically. Half-life is the time at which its expected remaining parent nuclei are half the original number under ideal isolated conditions. After two half-lives the expected fraction is one quarter, after three one eighth. This applies to the radioactive nuclide, not to the entire mass of a sample containing stable isotopes.

N(t) = N₀ × (1/2)t / t₁⁄₂   ;   λ = ln 2 / t₁⁄₂

Safety belongs in the interpretation

Radioactivity is a distinct physical hazard category and experimental radioisotope work requires appropriate licensed facilities, training and protection. A study note can explain nuclides and decay mathematics without providing handling or exposure procedures.

1.A.1.17.20 Alpha, beta, gamma and electron capture: what happens to isotope identity?

Alpha emission changes both A and Z

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus with two protons and two neutrons. If a parent nucleus emits an alpha particle, daughter A decreases by four and Z decreases by two. The chemical element therefore changes, and the daughter is not an isotope of the parent element.

Beta-minus can keep A while changing the element

In β⁻ decay a neutron converts to a proton and an electron plus electron antineutrino are emitted. Nuclear A is conserved within the parent-to-daughter nuclide, but Z increases by one. Carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14; those nuclides are isobars, not isotopes, because their proton numbers differ.

Beta-plus and electron capture reduce Z

In β⁺ decay a proton transforms into a neutron accompanied by positron and neutrino emission; A stays unchanged while Z decreases by one. Electron capture likewise changes a proton into a neutron with emission of a neutrino. These are nuclear processes, not simple ionization of an electron cloud.

Gamma transitions change nuclear energy rather than nucleon counts

A gamma transition commonly lowers nuclear excitation while retaining A and Z. A metastable nuclear isomer may transition toward a lower nuclear energy state without producing a new isotope by changing neutron number. The distinction between energy state and isotope identity is important for advanced examinations.

1.A.1.17.21 Radiocarbon as a bridge from isotope identity to time

Carbon enters organisms long before an age is estimated

Carbon dioxide is incorporated into photosynthetic products, which enter food webs. A small fraction of environmental carbon is ¹⁴C. While suitable biological material exchanges carbon with its environment, the isotopic composition reflects ongoing exchange and associated chemical processes. After relevant exchange ceases, the remaining ¹⁴C population continues to decay.

Use the half-life equation as an ideal model

With a half-life near 5,730 years, an ideal original ¹⁴C population is expected to have half remaining after one half-life and one quarter after two. This teaches decay arithmetic. The total carbon mass does not decrease by half, because most naturally occurring carbon is stable carbon-12 or carbon-13.

Real radiocarbon dating requires more than the textbook equation

Actual dating relies on measurement, sample suitability, contamination assessment, reference materials and calibration for variations in past environmental ¹⁴C. Reservoir effects and other sample histories may change apparent ages. The symbol ¹⁴C alone cannot tell an object's age.

Why age and isotope half-life are separate quantities

Half-life is an intrinsic decay statistic for a nuclide. Age is an inference about a sample from measured isotope composition plus a model and assumptions. Confusing the two leads to errors such as declaring a specimen 5,730 years old merely because it contains carbon-14.

1.A.1.17.22 Nuclear spin, NMR and why some isotopes are easier to observe

Isotopes can differ in nuclear spin

Nuclear spin depends on the arrangement of protons and neutrons, not just the chemical element. Carbon-12 has nuclear spin zero and is not directly observed by conventional ¹³C NMR detection. Carbon-13 has spin one-half and is NMR-active, enabling spectroscopy of carbon environments in molecules.

Hydrogen-1 is central in many NMR experiments

Protium nuclei have spin one-half and are abundant in organic molecules. Deuterium nuclei have spin one and different magnetic characteristics; deuterated solvents are often used in NMR to reduce interfering proton signals and provide field-lock signals. These differences arise from the nuclei, even though the chemical element is hydrogen in both cases.

NMR interpretation needs chemical environment as well

A ¹³C NMR peak depends on nuclear magnetic properties and the local electronic shielding environment. The isotope label enables detection, but a complete assignment needs information about chemical shifts, coupling, sample preparation and spectra. The isotope itself does not directly encode molecular structure.

1.A.1.17.23 Mass spectrometry: isotope peaks and isotopologues

A molecular ion contains multiple isotope-bearing atoms

Consider a molecule with two carbon atoms. It may contain two ¹²C nuclei, one ¹²C plus one ¹³C, or two ¹³C, producing related isotopologues. A mass spectrometer can detect multiple related m/z signals where sufficiently resolved. The relative pattern depends on isotope abundance, molecular composition and charge state.

Mass number and m/z are not the same symbol

The upper-left A in ¹³C counts nucleons in that one nucleus. The m/z of a molecular ion describes the ratio of its measured mass to its charge state. Multiple isotopes in a molecule and multiply charged ions can shift peaks. The lowercase z of mass spectrometry must not be confused with nuclear atomic number Z.

One ¹³C substitution produces an approximately 1.00335 u mass difference

The atomic mass difference between neutral ¹³C and ¹²C is about 1.003355 u. In a singly charged molecule it often produces an approximately 1.003355 m/z shift, assuming appropriate comparable ion chemistry. For a doubly charged ion the m/z spacing is approximately half as large.

Why an isotope pattern is not the same as positional tracing

A mass-spectrometric M+1 signal can indicate a heavier isotope substitution but does not necessarily state which carbon position is labelled. Positional information may require fragmentation, NMR or tracer modeling. A molecular isotopologue count and a positional isotopomer are not identical concepts.

1.A.1.17.24 A binomial model for isotope patterns in small molecules

Start with a two-carbon teaching molecule

Suppose each carbon site independently has probability p of containing ¹³C in a simple model. With two carbon atoms, the probability of zero substitutions is (1−p)². There are two ways to have exactly one substitution, so that probability is 2p(1−p). Both sites labelled gives p². The three probabilities sum to one.

Test the model with p = 0.10

The no-label probability is 0.9² = 0.81. Exactly one label has probability 2×0.1×0.9 = 0.18, and two labels have 0.1² = 0.01. These are fractions 81%, 18% and 1%. The example is deliberately simplified; real spectra include isotope masses of other elements, adducts, natural abundance corrections and instrument behavior.

Why the combinatorial factor matters

For a molecule with n equivalent, independently sampled carbon sites, the probability of exactly k heavy isotope sites is C(n,k) pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. The binomial coefficient counts how many different site selections produce the same total heavy-label count. This is an important bridge from basic probability to isotopologue distributions.

P(k of n ¹³C sites) = C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k
Concept checkpoint: A binomial mass-pattern model assumes independent equivalent site probabilities; in a deliberate positional tracer experiment, that assumption may be wrong.

1.A.1.17.25 Stable isotope tracing in metabolism: follow atoms, not assumptions

A labelled precursor can report the fate of its atoms

Suppose cells receive a defined ¹³C-labelled glucose precursor. After suitable analysis, ¹³C can be detected in downstream metabolites. This supports transfer of substrate-derived carbon into observed product pools. It does not immediately prove a single unique pathway or an absolute flux, because metabolites can exchange through multiple routes.

Mass isotopologue distributions provide more information

A molecule with several carbon atoms can exhibit M+0, M+1 and higher labels corresponding to the number of heavier-carbon substitutions. With tracer and network knowledge, isotope distribution data can support reconstruction of carbon transitions through pathways. Real ¹³C metabolic flux analysis uses a quantitative network model and carefully characterized labelling measurements.

Why input labelling matters

[U-¹³C]glucose denotes glucose uniformly labelled at carbon positions under the appropriate tracer specification, while [1-¹³C]glucose denotes a label at carbon position one. Different tracer positions can distinguish biochemical routes that would appear similar if only the overall molecule formula were known.

An isotope label is a marker, not a metabolic speedometer

The presence of a label establishes certain atom-source relationships under a properly designed experiment. Flux requires a time- or steady-state analysis with a network model, dilution and mixing considerations, measured patterns and controlled assumptions. A high-quality answer must state this inference boundary.

Evidence rule: isotope identity → where the labelled atom may be observed → possible biochemical route; a quantitative flux conclusion demands additional model and measurements.

1.A.1.17.26 Nitrogen-15 and phosphorus isotope applications in living systems

Nitrogen-15 reveals nitrogen incorporation pathways

¹⁵N has seven protons and eight neutrons. It is stable and can label nitrogen-containing compounds in a tracer study. Detecting ¹⁵N in amino acids can indicate that labelled nitrogen entered those molecules. Which reaction moved the nitrogen requires biochemical pathway evidence and experimental control.

Phosphorus-32 is a radionuclide, not a stable isotope tracer

Phosphorus-32 has 15 protons and 17 neutrons and is radioactive. It has been used historically in controlled studies of phosphate-associated compounds, including nucleic acids. Such work involves radiation detection and special safety infrastructure; its educational point is that nuclear labels identify phosphorus nuclides, not that the atom selectively chooses one biomolecule.

Why ‘labelled’ does not equal ‘radioactive’

A stable-isotope label such as ¹⁵N or ¹³C is not intrinsically radioactive. A radioisotope label such as ³²P or ³H undergoes nuclear decay. These classes can both support tracer questions, but their detection methods, safety requirements and interpretations are not interchangeable.

Take the biological context seriously

If a question asks about nitrogen assimilation, a proton/neutron calculation is only the foundation. Biochemical uptake, chemical pools, turnover and instrumentation are additional explanatory layers. Building a strong Life Sciences answer means connecting the nucleus to the molecule and then to the organism.

1.A.1.17.27 Water isotopes and the biology of environmental records

Hydrogen and oxygen isotopes can describe water composition

A water sample may contain different proportions of ¹H, ²H, ¹⁶O and ¹⁸O. Because physical processes sometimes fractionate heavier and lighter isotopes differently, isotope ratios can help interpret aspects of evaporation, precipitation and water movement. These ratios describe sample composition, not different chemical elements.

Fractionation creates signals, but interpretation is not unique

A plant water sample or environmental water record can reflect source water, evaporation, exchange and other processes. An isotope ratio may be consistent with several possible histories. Interpreting it requires knowledge of reference scales, sampling conditions and hydrological or biological models.

The importance of reference standards

Environmental isotope measurements are often compared against internationally used reference scales. A reported δ value without naming an appropriate reference can be ambiguous. Scientists use calibrated measurements and quality-control materials so results from different laboratories can be compared responsibly.

1.A.1.17.28 Protein and enzyme isotope effects: a mechanism requires evidence

Why swapping H for D can slow a reaction

If a bond to hydrogen is broken during a rate-influencing enzyme step, replacing H with D changes vibrational energies and can alter the observed rate. Under appropriate comparisons, a kinetic isotope effect may give evidence about bond involvement. But the apparent effect may combine chemistry with binding, conformational changes or other steps.

Primary versus solvent isotope effects

An atom-specific H/D substitution in a substrate differs from replacing part of the solvent's hydrogen content with deuterium. Solvent isotope effects can reflect a broader set of exchangeable protons and solvent properties. One must not treat both experiments as equivalent without examining the design.

A measured ratio needs a definition of rate constants

A commonly discussed kinetic isotope effect is k_H/k_D, but the rates must refer to appropriately matched reaction conditions and definitions. A value above one is often called a normal kinetic isotope effect, while below one is inverse. Neither outcome alone proves a unique transition-state geometry.

1.A.1.17.29 Medical and ecological applications: useful, but context-dependent

Clinical diagnostic tracers differ in isotope and purpose

Stable isotope tracers can support research and selected clinical measurements, while radioisotopes may be used in appropriately regulated diagnostic or therapeutic contexts. The atomic label determines nuclear identity, but clinical suitability depends on dose, chemical form, biodistribution and medical judgement. The same isotope is not an automatic treatment.

Ecological food webs can carry isotope patterns

Carbon and nitrogen isotope ratios in tissues may reflect features of diet and ecological pathways, but many factors can alter isotope signatures. Changes in baseline environment, metabolism and turnover must be considered. Isotopic composition is evidence that needs contextual interpretation, not a universal one-to-one label for a diet.

Environmental contamination and provenance studies need multiple clues

Isotope ratios can help constrain potential origins or processes, sometimes alongside elemental concentrations and other measurements. A single ratio rarely proves one exclusive source. Scientific caution is a strength of isotope-based reasoning, not a weakness.

Concept checkpoint: The presence of an isotope label alone does not establish a diagnosis, a unique metabolic route, a specific environmental source or safe handling conditions.

1.A.1.17.31 Forty fully solved examples: isotope numericals and scientific inference

This extended workshop begins with isotope counting and pair classification, then progresses through average atomic mass, isotopic ratios, tracer probabilities, radiocarbon decay and biological interpretation. Every problem will give intermediate steps, a verification and a common error to avoid.

Part I — Nuclear identity and isotope mixture calculations

Worked example 1. Neutral carbon-12

Question: Find proton, neutron and electron counts in ¹²₆C.

Step-by-step explanation: Z=6 gives six protons; A=12 gives 12−6=6 neutrons. The word neutral means electron count also equals six. This calculation identifies a single carbon nuclide without saying how abundant it is in a sample.

Independent verification: Check: 6+6=12 and 6−6=0.

Exam trap: Do not mistake A=12 for electron count.

Worked example 2. Carbon-13 versus carbon-12

Question: What changes between neutral ¹²C and ¹³C?

Step-by-step explanation: Both nuclei have six protons, but their neutron counts are six and seven. Each neutral atom has six electrons. Equal Z with different A defines the isotope relation; their different nuclear masses can produce small physical isotope effects.

Independent verification: Check: ΔA=1 and ΔN=1 at fixed Z.

Exam trap: An extra neutron is not an extra electron.

Worked example 3. Carbon-14

Question: Explain the particle counts and the separate stability issue.

Step-by-step explanation: Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons; a neutral atom has six electrons. It is an isotope of both ¹²C and ¹³C. Carbon-14 is radioactive, but stability requires experimental nuclear information and is not universally determined by the simple A−Z calculation.

Independent verification: Check: Z=6 in all three carbon isotopes.

Exam trap: Not every nuclide with N>Z is radioactive.

Worked example 4. The hydrogen family

Question: Compute N for ¹H, ²H and ³H.

Step-by-step explanation: Each nucleus has one proton. Protium ¹H has N=0, deuterium ²H has N=1, and tritium ³H has N=2. All belong to the same element. Deuterium is stable and tritium is radioactive, illustrating that isotope identity and stability are different properties.

Independent verification: Check A=1+N for each.

Exam trap: D₂O's subscript 2 is a molecular atom count.

Worked example 5. Oxygen-16 and oxygen-18

Question: Write particle counts for the two neutral atoms.

Step-by-step explanation: Oxygen has Z=8. ¹⁶O contains eight neutrons; ¹⁸O contains ten. Each neutral atom has eight electrons. The nuclei differ in N by two while preserving proton number and elemental identity.

Independent verification: Check A increases by two at fixed Z.

Exam trap: The heavier isotope is not a new element.

Worked example 6. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37

Question: How many extra neutrons are in the heavier isotope?

Step-by-step explanation: Chlorine has Z=17. The first nuclide has N=35−17=18; the second N=37−17=20. Thus ³⁷Cl has two additional neutrons. Their chemistry is broadly related but mass-dependent physical differences are measurable.

Independent verification: Check 37−35=20−18=2.

Exam trap: Proton count is seventeen for both.

Worked example 7. A sodium isotope from words

Question: Sodium has eleven protons and thirteen neutrons. Write its nuclide.

Step-by-step explanation: Elemental identity fixes Z=11. Adding nucleons gives A=11+13=24, so the nuclide is ²⁴₁₁Na. It is a sodium isotope distinct from sodium-23, although both have eleven protons.

Independent verification: Check 24−11=13.

Exam trap: Do not assume all sodium has A=23.

Worked example 8. Two phosphorus nuclides

Question: Z=15, with neutron counts 16 and 17. Identify both.

Step-by-step explanation: For a given element, A=Z+N. The two mass numbers are 31 and 32. Atomic number 15 identifies phosphorus, giving ³¹P and ³²P. They are isotopes because the proton number is held constant.

Independent verification: Check A differs exactly as N differs.

Exam trap: Different mass number is not different element.

Worked example 9. Sodium ionization is not an isotope

Question: Are ²³Na and ²³Na⁺ isotope partners?

Step-by-step explanation: They have identical A=23 and Z=11, hence twelve neutrons in either. Neutral Na has eleven electrons and Na⁺ ten. The difference is electronic charge, not nuclear composition. A true isotope pair would require different A values.

Independent verification: Check q=11−10=+1 for Na⁺.

Exam trap: Do not equate ionization with nuclear transmutation.

Worked example 10. A charged calcium isotope

Question: For ⁴⁴₂₀Ca²⁺ calculate P, N and E.

Step-by-step explanation: Calcium fixes P=20. A=44 gives N=24. Charge +2 gives E=20−2=18 for a monatomic ion. This example deliberately combines isotope identity with electronic charge; process each piece separately.

Independent verification: Check 20+24=44 and 20−18=+2.

Exam trap: Charge does not subtract neutrons.

Worked example 11. An isobar pair

Question: Classify ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N.

Step-by-step explanation: Both have A=14. Carbon has Z=6 and nitrogen Z=7, so elemental identity differs. Their neutron counts are eight and seven. They are isobars rather than isotopes; equal mass number alone is not enough for isotope classification.

Independent verification: Check Z differs despite equal A.

Exam trap: Do not call all equal-A species isotopes.

Worked example 12. An isotone pair

Question: Classify ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N.

Step-by-step explanation: Carbon-14 has N=14−6=8. Nitrogen-15 has N=15−7=8. Thus they are isotones, sharing neutron number but not proton number. Their A values differ, so they are not isobars either.

Independent verification: Check N is eight for both.

Exam trap: The word isotope means equal Z, not equal N.

Worked example 13. Technetium isomer

Question: Compare ⁹⁹ᵐTc with ⁹⁹Tc.

Step-by-step explanation: Both nuclides have Z=43 and A=99, leaving N=56. The m designation indicates a metastable nuclear energy state, not a changed neutron number. These are nuclear isomers; they are not a pair of nuclides with different mass numbers.

Independent verification: Check P and N are the same.

Exam trap: The m does not mean another isotope mass.

Worked example 14. Classify three different pairs

Question: Compare ¹²C/¹³C, ¹⁴C/¹⁴N and ¹⁴C/¹⁵N.

Step-by-step explanation: First pair: equal Z but different A, isotopes. Second: equal A but different Z, isobars. Third: both have N=8 but differing Z, isotones. A disciplined solution checks Z, then A, then N.

Independent verification: Check each pair against its definition.

Exam trap: Do not classify only by a coincident matching digit.

Worked example 15. Measured mass of carbon-13

Question: Is its mass exactly 13 u because A=13?

Step-by-step explanation: No. A counts thirteen nucleons; the measured neutral-atom mass is approximately 13.003355 u. Neutral carbon-12 is exactly 12 u by the unit's definition, a special reference rather than a rule for all isotopes. Binding-energy contributions matter.

Independent verification: Check that mass units and nucleon counts are distinct.

Exam trap: Do not round isotope masses in high-resolution MS.

Worked example 16. A toy isotope average

Question: Masses 10 u and 11 u occur at fractions 0.20 and 0.80. Find mean.

Step-by-step explanation: Use a weighted sum of the actual masses provided: m̄=0.20×10+0.80×11=2+8.8=10.8 u. This is the average mass per atom for the assumed mixture. An individual nucleus still has an integer A.

Independent verification: Check m̄ lies between 10 and 11.

Exam trap: Do not invent a mass number of 10.8.

Worked example 17. Approximate chlorine abundance calculation

Question: Fractions 0.75 and 0.25 have mass numbers 35 and 37. Calculate approximate average.

Step-by-step explanation: For the classroom approximation m̄≈0.75×35+0.25×37=35.5 u. This uses integer mass numbers rather than precise isotope masses. Actual standard atomic-weight reporting uses measured masses and isotope compositions.

Independent verification: Check fractions add to one.

Exam trap: Do not claim 35.5 u is an exact universal chlorine atomic mass.

Worked example 18. Inverse isotope mixture

Question: Masses 20 u and 22 u give average 20.4 u. Find heavy fraction.

Step-by-step explanation: Let x represent the fraction of mass-22 atoms. Then 20(1−x)+22x=20.4, giving 20+2x=20.4 and x=0.20. Hence 20% heavier isotope and 80% lighter one under the two-isotope assumption.

Independent verification: Check 0.8×20+0.2×22=20.4.

Exam trap: Do not divide 20.4 by the heavy mass.

Worked example 19. Precise-ish carbon example

Question: Use ¹²C mass 12 and ¹³C mass 13.003355 at fractions 0.99 and 0.01.

Step-by-step explanation: The sample average is 0.99×12+0.01×13.003355=12.01003355 u. This is an illustrative ratio, not a universal constant for natural carbon. The near-12 value reflects the dominance of ¹²C.

Independent verification: Check average is between the two isotope masses.

Exam trap: Convert 1% to 0.01 rather than 1.

Worked example 20. Isotope ratio versus fraction

Question: A sample has 99 atoms ¹²C and one atom ¹³C. Compute both.

Step-by-step explanation: Heavy isotope atom fraction x(¹³C)=1/(99+1)=0.01. Heavy/light ratio R=1/99≈0.010101. The values are close at low abundance but have different denominators, and environmental delta calculations ordinarily use a specified ratio.

Independent verification: Check x=R/(1+R) in a two-isotope mixture.

Exam trap: Do not use atom fraction as the heavy/light ratio without conversion.

Part II — Isotope ratios, radioactive decay, mass spectra and biology

Worked example 21. The meaning of enriched ¹³C

Question: A bottle is labelled 99 atom% ¹³C. What follows?

Step-by-step explanation: The stated material has approximately 99% of the relevant carbon atoms as carbon-13. It is an isotope abundance claim, not a claim that the molecule is radioactive or that a biological culture will show 99% label incorporation. Tracer efficiency depends on more information.

Verification: Identity and sample fraction are separate quantities.

Common error: Never convert input enrichment directly into metabolic flux.

Worked example 22. Negative isotope delta

Question: R_sample = 0.99 R_reference. Find δ.

Step-by-step explanation: Use δ = (R_sample/R_reference − 1)×1000‰. Here δ=(0.99−1)×1000=−10‰. This means the sample has a lower specified heavy/light ratio than the reference, not a physically negative isotope count.

Verification: −0.01×1000=−10.

Common error: Negative δ is not negative isotope abundance.

Worked example 23. Positive isotope delta

Question: R_sample = 1.002 R_reference. Find δ.

Step-by-step explanation: Compute (1.002−1)×1000=+2‰. The per-mille result represents two parts in a thousand relative to the reference ratio. Meaningful reporting also names the isotope pair and reference scale.

Verification: 0.002×1000=2.

Common error: Per mille is not percentage.

Worked example 24. A mixed sample

Question: 80 atoms are ¹²C, 19 are ¹³C, one is ¹⁴C. Determine atom fractions.

Step-by-step explanation: There are 100 carbon atoms. Their fractions are 80/100 =0.80, 19/100=0.19 and 1/100=0.01. All are carbon because Z=6. These deliberately illustrative counts do not describe natural carbon composition.

Verification: Fractions add to 1.00.

Common error: An isotope mixture does not represent three different elements.

Worked example 25. Radiocarbon after one half-life

Question: Start with 8000 radioactive ¹⁴C nuclei. Expected number after one t½?

Step-by-step explanation: A half-life reduces the expected surviving parent population by one half. Thus 8000×(1/2)=4000. For ¹⁴C the half-life is about 5730 years. The exact decay outcome is stochastic, but large-population expectation follows the exponential law.

Verification: 4000/8000=0.5.

Common error: This does not halve the total stable carbon in the material.

Worked example 26. Radiocarbon after two half-lives

Question: What fraction survives after two t½?

Step-by-step explanation: First half-life leaves 1/2. The next leaves half of that remainder: (1/2)²=1/4=25%. Exponential decay is multiplicative, not subtracting 50% of the initial sample repeatedly.

Verification: 100% → 50% → 25%.

Common error: 100−50−50 is not correct decay arithmetic.

Worked example 27. Radiocarbon after three half-lives

Question: For ideal ¹⁴C isolation, how much remains after 17190 years?

Step-by-step explanation: 17190/5730=3 half-lives. Expected remaining fraction=(1/2)³=1/8=12.5%. Real radiocarbon dating of objects needs measurement, reference correction and calibration for historic environmental changes.

Verification: 3×5730=17190.

Common error: An ideal fraction does not automatically yield calibrated calendar age.

Worked example 28. Carbon-14 beta-minus daughter

Question: Find daughter of ¹⁴₆C by β⁻ decay.

Step-by-step explanation: A neutron becomes a proton, so A stays 14 while Z rises from 6 to 7. Atomic number seven is nitrogen: daughter ¹⁴₇N. An electron and antineutrino are also emitted, satisfying appropriate conservation laws.

Verification: Parent and daughter have equal A but different Z: isobars.

Common error: This is not removal of an ordinary orbital electron.

Worked example 29. Alpha emission accounting

Question: ²³⁸₉₂U emits ⁴₂He. Determine daughter.

Step-by-step explanation: Subtract the emitted alpha particle's A=4 and Z=2. Daughter A=234, Z=90, which is thorium-234. This is a nuclear change of element identity rather than another uranium isotope.

Verification: 234+4=238 and 90+2=92.

Common error: Alpha has two protons, not four.

Worked example 30. Two-carbon isotopologue: no heavy sites

Question: With independent ¹³C fraction p=0.10 at two carbon sites, find P(M+0).

Step-by-step explanation: Both sites must be light: (1−p)²=0.9²=0.81. This 81% is the probability of a molecule with zero heavy carbon substitutions in a simplified random model. A deliberate positional tracer need not obey this independence assumption.

Verification: Probability is between zero and one.

Common error: Use two factors because there are two sites.

Worked example 31. Exactly one heavy carbon

Question: Same two-site model, calculate P(M+1).

Step-by-step explanation: Either the first or second site can be heavy. The two exclusive arrangements give 2p(1−p)=2×0.1×0.9=0.18 (18%). This counts the total number of substitutions but not which position was labelled.

Verification: With M+0=0.81 and M+2=0.01, probabilities sum to 1.

Common error: Do not forget factor two.

Worked example 32. Both carbon sites heavy

Question: Same model, calculate P(M+2).

Step-by-step explanation: Both sites must be ¹³C, giving p²=(0.10)²=0.01 (1%). The three categories M+0, M+1, M+2 sum to one in this simplified two-carbon isotope model.

Verification: 0.81+0.18+0.01=1.

Common error: This 1% molecular category is not the 10% heavy-atom fraction.

Worked example 33. A mass-spectrometry isotope spacing

Question: One ¹³C replaces ¹²C in a comparable singly charged ion. Expected m/z shift?

Step-by-step explanation: Carbon-13 neutral atomic mass exceeds carbon-12 by approximately 1.003355 u. For otherwise comparable singly charged ions, the observed m/z separation is near 1.003355. Exact comparisons require the same adduct and charge-state assumptions.

Verification: Shift is not exactly 1.000000.

Common error: Do not equate A with m/z.

Worked example 34. A doubly charged ion

Question: What isotope spacing follows for z=+2?

Step-by-step explanation: The approximately 1.003355 u isotope mass difference is divided by charge magnitude two, giving an m/z separation near 0.501678. A doubly charged molecule does not have different isotope nuclear masses; the ratio's denominator changed.

Verification: 2×0.501678≈1.003356.

Common error: Do not multiply mass shift by charge.

Worked example 35. Label location versus isotope count

Question: Compare [1-¹³C]glucose and [2-¹³C]glucose.

Step-by-step explanation: Each contains one specifically placed carbon-13 substitution, but positions differ. They are positional isotopomers: their total isotope mass shift can be the same while their behavior as carbon tracers differs after pathways rearrange atoms.

Verification: One label in each, different carbon locant.

Common error: A lone M+1 peak may not reveal position.

Worked example 36. Stable versus radioactive tracer

Question: Is ¹³C-labelled glucose a radioactive tracer?

Step-by-step explanation: No. ¹³C is stable and can be measured by isotope-sensitive MS or NMR. ¹⁴C is a radioactive carbon isotope. Both share Z=6 but different N and nuclear properties. Selection of an analytical method depends on the label and experimental question.

Verification: The identity of carbon is unchanged.

Common error: ‘Isotope’ does not mean ‘radioisotope’.

Worked example 37. Nitrogen-15 in a metabolite

Question: What can detection of ¹⁵N in amino acid tell us?

Step-by-step explanation: ¹⁵N has seven protons and eight neutrons. Its presence supports incorporation or exchange of isotope-labelled nitrogen in that measured pool under the study design. It does not prove a unique route, reaction speed or flux without models and additional evidence.

Verification: The isotope is of nitrogen, not carbon.

Common error: Presence of label alone is not a flux estimate.

Worked example 38. A normal kinetic isotope effect

Question: k_H = 4.0 s⁻¹, k_D = 1.0 s⁻¹. Find KIE.

Step-by-step explanation: Under the specified definition k_H/k_D=4.0/1.0=4.0. The rate difference is consistent with isotopic substitution affecting a reaction-related process. Its detailed mechanistic cause needs independent study because observed rates may include several steps.

Verification: Rate units cancel.

Common error: One KIE value is not a complete transition-state proof.

Worked example 39. An inverse kinetic isotope effect

Question: k_H=0.8, k_D=1.0 in matching units. Find KIE.

Step-by-step explanation: The ratio k_H/k_D=0.8, below unity. By this convention it is an inverse isotope effect. The interpretation depends on how the observed rate constants relate to the chemical steps and the comparison conditions.

Verification: k_H is smaller, so ratio <1.

Common error: Not all isotope effects must exceed one.

Worked example 40. An undetermined archaeological age

Question: A wood sample is described only as containing ¹⁴C. What age follows?

Step-by-step explanation: No numerical age follows. Need measured isotope fraction/activity, suitable reference and calibration, plus contamination and sample context. The radionuclide half-life is an intrinsic property, not the age of any object containing the nuclide.

Verification: No measured ratio or starting amount supplied.

Common error: Do not answer 5730 years based only on isotope name.

1.A.1.17.32 Sixty explained CSIR-NET and SET practice questions

These are newly written practice questions inspired by the relevant scientific concepts, not claimed verbatim previous-year exam items. Answer each before opening the explanation. The purpose is to develop understanding and scientific judgment, not rote recognition of a letter.

Sixty original examination questions with fully reasoned answers

These questions are educational CSIR-NET and SET-style exercises, not verbatim official past-paper questions. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

Question 1. What defines a pair of isotopes?

Answer: Same Z, different A

Why: Isotopes are nuclides of one element with a fixed proton number but differing mass numbers and hence neutron counts.

Question 2. What makes an element carbon?

Answer: Z = 6

Why: Six nuclear protons fix elemental carbon identity, independent of charge state or neutron number.

Question 3. How many neutrons does ¹³C contain?

Answer: 7

Why: Carbon has six protons, so N = 13 − 6 = 7.

Question 4. How many neutrons does ¹⁴C contain?

Answer: 8

Why: N = A − Z = 14 − 6 = 8.

Question 5. What is unchanged in ¹²C and ¹⁴C?

Answer: Number of protons

Why: Both have Z = 6; neutral atoms also have six electrons, but their neutron counts differ.

Question 6. Are ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl isotopes?

Answer: Yes

Why: Both contain 17 protons, but their mass numbers and neutron counts differ.

Question 7. Are ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N isotopes?

Answer: No, isobars

Why: They share mass number 14 but have different atomic numbers, six versus seven.

Question 8. Are ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N isotones?

Answer: Yes

Why: Both have eight neutrons although their proton numbers differ.

Question 9. Does gaining an electron create a new isotope?

Answer: No

Why: Electron changes affect ionic charge, not nuclear neutron number.

Question 10. Does losing a neutron change the isotope?

Answer: Yes, if a different nucleus results

Why: At fixed Z, a different neutron number changes A and hence nuclear isotope identity; physical feasibility is separate.

Question 11. Is ¹³C stable?

Answer: Yes

Why: Carbon-13 is a stable nuclide used in isotope measurements and NMR.

Question 12. Is ¹⁴C stable?

Answer: No

Why: Carbon-14 is a radionuclide that undergoes beta-minus decay.

Question 13. Does isotope always imply radioactive?

Answer: No

Why: Isotopes include stable and unstable nuclear species.

Question 14. How many neutrons does deuterium have?

Answer: 1

Why: ²H has Z = 1 and A = 2, so N = 1.

Question 15. How many neutrons does tritium have?

Answer: 2

Why: ³H has one proton and two neutrons; it is radioactive.

Question 16. What does ²H mean?

Answer: Deuterium isotope of hydrogen

Why: The upper-left 2 is mass number, not the number of neutrons.

Question 17. What does D₂O's 2 specify?

Answer: Two D atoms per molecule

Why: The right lower subscript is molecular stoichiometry; deuterium itself has one neutron.

Question 18. What is mass number?

Answer: Total nucleons

Why: A = proton count + neutron count, an integer for one nucleus.

Question 19. Is measured ¹³C atomic mass exactly 13 u?

Answer: No

Why: The neutral atomic mass is approximately 13.003355 u; mass number is a count.

Question 20. Which isotope defines the unified mass unit?

Answer: Carbon-12

Why: A neutral free ground-state ¹²C atom has mass exactly 12 u under the definition.

Question 21. What is isotope atom fraction?

Answer: Isotope atoms divided by all atoms of that element

Why: Atom fraction is a property of a sample's isotope composition.

Question 22. Can an isotope itself be 20% present?

Answer: A population can, not a single atom

Why: An individual nucleus has a definite isotope; percentage refers to a sample.

Question 23. What is R(¹³C/¹²C)?

Answer: Heavy-to-light atom count ratio

Why: R = number of ¹³C atoms divided by number of ¹²C atoms in the stated sample.

Question 24. Does x(¹³C) always equal ¹³C/¹²C?

Answer: No

Why: The fraction divides by all carbon atoms; the ratio divides by ¹²C atoms.

Question 25. What does 99 atom% ¹³C signify?

Answer: Strong enrichment of carbon-13

Why: It states sample composition, not radioactivity or biological flux.

Question 26. Why is chlorine's periodic atomic weight non-integer?

Answer: It represents a mixture-weighted value

Why: Different isotopic masses and abundances produce an average.

Question 27. Can average atomic mass equal a fractional mass number?

Answer: No

Why: A is an integer per nucleus even when average mass is fractional.

Question 28. What is required for an average isotope mass calculation?

Answer: Isotope masses and atom fractions

Why: Use weighted sum Σxᵢmᵢ with fractions adding to one.

Question 29. For 20% mass-10 and 80% mass-11 atoms, mean?

Answer: 10.8 u

Why: 0.2×10 + 0.8×11 = 10.8.

Question 30. Which way does average shift as heavier-isotope fraction rises?

Answer: Generally toward heavier mass

Why: A weighted mean increases when heavier isotope mass has a larger fraction, with other values fixed.

Question 31. What does δ isotope notation compare?

Answer: Sample isotope ratio with reference ratio

Why: δ = (R_sample/R_ref − 1)×1000‰.

Question 32. What does negative δ mean?

Answer: Lower sample ratio than reference

Why: Negative delta does not mean a negative amount of an isotope.

Question 33. If R_sample/R_ref=1.001, δ?

Answer: +1‰

Why: (1.001−1)×1000 = +1 per mille.

Question 34. What causes isotope fractionation?

Answer: Isotope-sensitive physical or chemical processes

Why: Mass-dependent effects can change isotope ratios between products and reactants.

Question 35. Are all isotope chemical rates exactly identical?

Answer: No

Why: Kinetic isotope effects can alter rates even at identical element Z.

Question 36. What is a kinetic isotope effect?

Answer: Effect of substitution on reaction rate

Why: An H/D substitution may alter rate constants in otherwise comparable reactions.

Question 37. What is an equilibrium isotope effect?

Answer: Effect of substitution on equilibrium

Why: Isotope substitution can influence equilibrium constants or isotope partitioning.

Question 38. Is k_H/k_D always > 1?

Answer: No

Why: Inverse isotope effects have ratios below one under that convention.

Question 39. Does a single KIE prove one reaction mechanism?

Answer: No

Why: Coupled processes and conditions may influence the observation.

Question 40. What is a radionuclide?

Answer: A nuclear species that undergoes radioactive decay

Why: Instability and decay are nuclear characteristics, not the definition of isotope itself.

Question 41. Does N > Z guarantee instability?

Answer: No

Why: Many stable nuclei contain more neutrons than protons.

Question 42. What is radioactive half-life?

Answer: Time for expected parent population to halve

Why: Under exponential decay it corresponds to N/N0 = 1/2.

Question 43. After two half-lives, fraction remaining?

Answer: 1/4

Why: Each half-life halves the remaining population.

Question 44. After three half-lives, fraction remaining?

Answer: 1/8

Why: (1/2)³ = 0.125.

Question 45. About how long is ¹⁴C half-life?

Answer: 5,730 years

Why: The widely used physical half-life is approximately 5.73×10³ years.

Question 46. Does ¹⁴C half-life itself date a specimen?

Answer: No

Why: Dating needs measured remaining isotope, calibration and sample history.

Question 47. What is daughter nuclide of ¹⁴C β⁻ decay?

Answer: ¹⁴N

Why: Neutron-to-proton conversion raises Z from six to seven while A remains fourteen.

Question 48. What changes in alpha emission?

Answer: A decreases 4, Z decreases 2

Why: A helium-4 nucleus containing two protons and two neutrons is emitted.

Question 49. What changes in a gamma transition?

Answer: Nuclear energy state

Why: Ordinary gamma de-excitation leaves A and Z unchanged.

Question 50. Which carbon isotope is prominent in ¹³C NMR?

Answer: Carbon-13

Why: ¹³C has nuclear spin one-half; ¹²C has spin zero.

Question 51. What does M+1 in a mass spectrum often reflect?

Answer: A heavier-isotope substitution

Why: It requires appropriate formula and charge analysis and does not automatically localize a label.

Question 52. Does m/z equal nuclear mass number?

Answer: No

Why: Mass spectrometry measures molecular/atomic ion mass-to-charge behavior.

Question 53. What approximate mass does one ¹³C-for-¹²C substitution add?

Answer: About 1.003355 u

Why: This is a measured isotopic mass difference, not exactly one u.

Question 54. At z=+2, approximate ¹³C m/z isotope spacing?

Answer: 0.501678

Why: The ≈1.003355 u mass difference is divided by two.

Question 55. What is a molecular isotopologue?

Answer: Molecule with a specified isotope composition

Why: Isotopologues can differ in counts or identities of isotopic substitutions.

Question 56. What is a positional isotopomer?

Answer: A label at a different molecular position

Why: [1-¹³C]glucose and [2-¹³C]glucose differ in labelled position.

Question 57. Does labelled metabolite presence equal flux?

Answer: No

Why: Quantifying flux requires pathway network, tracer input, measurements and model assumptions.

Question 58. What does stable ¹⁵N label follow?

Answer: Labelled nitrogen

Why: Its use can help study nitrogen incorporation without nuclear decay detection.

Question 59. Can isotope data assist ecological inference?

Answer: Yes, with context

Why: Food-web interpretation also depends on baselines, fractionation and turnover.

Question 60. What must a defensible isotope answer separate?

Answer: Nuclear identity, measurement, and model-based inference

Why: A nuclide symbol cannot by itself prove a unique reaction, source or flux.

Misconception 1

Incorrect: Isotopes are different elements.

Correction: Isotopes have equal proton number and thus the same element.

Scientific check: ¹²C and ¹³C both have six protons.

Misconception 2

Incorrect: Isotopes have equal neutrons.

Correction: At fixed Z they differ in N and A.

Scientific check: ¹²C has six neutrons; ¹⁴C has eight.

Misconception 3

Incorrect: Every isotope is radioactive.

Correction: Many isotopes are stable.

Scientific check: ¹²C and ¹³C are stable carbon isotopes.

Misconception 4

Incorrect: A positive ion is a different isotope.

Correction: Ionization usually changes electrons, not the nucleus.

Scientific check: Na and Na⁺ can both be sodium-23.

Misconception 5

Incorrect: Neutral isotopes of an element must have different electron counts.

Correction: Both have the same electron number equal to Z.

Scientific check: Neutral ¹²C and ¹³C each have six electrons.

Misconception 6

Incorrect: The periodic-table decimal is a mass number.

Correction: It is commonly an isotope-composition-weighted atomic weight.

Scientific check: One chlorine atom cannot have A = 35.45.

Misconception 7

Incorrect: Every nuclide has mass exactly A u.

Correction: A is a count; measured neutral isotope mass is not generally an integer.

Scientific check: ¹³C mass is about 13.003355 u.

Misconception 8

Incorrect: Equal mass number proves isotopes.

Correction: Equal A with differing Z defines isobars.

Scientific check: ¹⁴C versus ¹⁴N.

Misconception 9

Incorrect: Equal neutron counts prove isotopes.

Correction: Same N and differing Z defines isotones.

Scientific check: ¹⁴C versus ¹⁵N.

Misconception 10

Misconception 11

Correction: MS analyzes ion mass-to-charge and isotope patterns.

1.A.1.17.34 Integrated scientific reasoning: difficult but fair applied problems

These integrative challenges combine two or more ideas. Use definitions, explicit calculations and evidence limits in the answer. A complete solution may explain why the available information is insufficient rather than supply a guessed number.

Ten integrated problems: combine calculation with scientifically limited inference

Integrated problem 1. A labelled product is not a complete flux model

Problem: A culture fed uniformly labelled ¹³C-glucose produces metabolites with M+2 and M+3 labels. Can those two observations determine every carbon pathway rate?

Model solution: No. The isotopologue distributions constrain pathways but usually do not uniquely determine their rates. Quantitative interpretation requires the tracer input, atom transitions, network stoichiometry, mixing and measured labeling patterns under defined dynamic or steady-state assumptions.

Integrated problem 2. Inverse isotope composition

Problem: Two isotope masses are 48.0 u and 50.0 u, mean 48.6 u. Find heavier isotope fraction.

Model solution: Let x be heavier fraction: 48(1−x)+50x =48.6. Rearranging, 48+2x=48.6 and x=0.30. The sample has 30% heavy isotope and 70% light, under the stated two-isotope model.

Integrated problem 3. A nuclide remains the same during ion formation

Problem: A carbon-13 atom loses one electron. Which isotope quantity changes?

Model solution: Neither Z nor A changes. The species becomes a positively charged carbon-13 ion, while nuclear P=6 and N=7 remain fixed. Its electron count falls from six to five if it started neutral.

Integrated problem 4. An artificial carbon isotope mixture

Problem: A sample has 80 ¹²C, 19 ¹³C and one ¹⁴C atom. Determine all atom fractions.

Model solution: Total carbon atoms = 100, giving x₁₂=0.80, x₁₃=0.19 and x₁₄=0.01. All three isotope species remain the same element with Z=6. The sample composition is illustrative and not meant as natural abundance.

Integrated problem 5. Carbon-14 radioactive daughter classification

Problem: After β⁻ decay, ¹⁴C gives ¹⁴N. Are they isotopes?

Model solution: No. Their A values are both 14, but Z changes from 6 to 7, so the parent and daughter are isobars. The decay changes element identity rather than yielding a heavier carbon isotope.

Integrated problem 6. Two delta values are not a complete environmental history

Problem: Two plant samples differ in δ¹³C. Can one assign a unique cause?

Model solution: No. The delta values directly indicate an isotope-ratio relationship to a defined reference. Photosynthetic physiology, environmental conditions, sample selection and fractionation can all influence measurements. A causal explanation needs independent evidence.

Integrated problem 7. Same neutrons without same element

Problem: Compare ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N, and ask whether stability follows mathematically.

Model solution: N=8 in both, so they are isotones. The arithmetic does not by itself determine stability; that requires nuclear data. They also cannot be isotopes of one element because their proton numbers are six and seven.

Integrated problem 8. M+1 cannot localize a label by itself

Problem: A molecule has an M+1 mass-spectrum peak. Which atom position is labelled?

Model solution: Not determined. M+1 may involve one heavier isotope substitution, but the atom identity and its position require additional molecular formula, isotope-pattern, fragmentation or NMR evidence.

Integrated problem 9. Radioactive population arithmetic

Problem: An ideal ¹⁴C population starts at 6400 nuclei and undergoes three half-lives. Expected remainder?

Model solution: Remaining fraction is 1/8, so 6400/8=800 expected parent nuclei. This models nuclear decay, not total sample carbon mass, and exact individual decays remain stochastic.

Integrated problem 10. A heavier oxygen isotope

Problem: What establishes that oxygen-18 is an isotope of oxygen-16?

Model solution: Both nuclei have Z=8; A values are 16 and 18, so neutron numbers are 8 and 10. They belong to the same chemical element. Their stable nuclear identities do not by themselves specify water's isotope ratio or environmental origin.

1.A.1.17.35 Master revision: concept map, glossary and study sequence

Essential facts:
  1. Isotopes have the same atomic number Z and different mass numbers A.
  2. At fixed Z, neutron numbers N = A − Z differ.
  3. Changing electronic charge does not create a new isotope.
  4. Atomic weight is an isotope-abundance-weighted quantity, not an individual mass number.
  5. Stable and radioactive isotopes are both isotopes; isotope status alone does not predict decay.
  6. A biological label tracks atom identity, while pathways and rates require independent evidence.

Glossary in plain scientific English

Nuclide: a defined atomic nuclear species. Isotope: a nuclide of an element with the same Z but different A. Isobar: a nuclide sharing A with another different-Z nuclide. Isotone: a nuclide sharing N with another different-Z nuclide. Atom fraction: proportion of atoms of an element belonging to a specified isotope. Isotopologue: molecular species differing in isotopic composition. Isotopomer: arrangement differing in labelled positions. Radionuclide: unstable nuclide. Half-life: time for the expected parent population to fall by half. Kinetic isotope effect: a reaction-rate effect due to isotope substitution. Fractionation: isotope-dependent differences in partitioning or process outcomes.
