1.A.1.18.1 Three bottles, one element: the mystery begins

Imagine a young life-science researcher entering a laboratory and noticing three carefully labelled reference images: ordinary water, deuterated water, and tritiated water. At first they appear to involve the same simplest element—hydrogen. But their isotope labels tell different stories. One nucleus contains no neutron, another contains one neutron, and the third contains two. That tiny change connects early atomic physics, molecular vibration, isotope-tracer biology and nuclear decay.

The question that joins the entire chapter

If all three belong to hydrogen, why can some measurements tell them apart? Hydrogen's atomic number is always Z = 1; it is not proton number that changes. Each isotope has a different neutron count, so mass number and certain nuclear properties differ. Because the masses differ so much relative to one another, hydrogen isotopes are particularly powerful for studying mass-dependent effects in chemistry.

The scientific journey ahead

We will build the idea chronologically: what chemists knew about hydrogen, how heavy hydrogen was discovered, and how tritium entered nuclear science. Then we will decode nuclear notation, molecular formulas and electronic charge. After that we will explain vibrational motion, kinetic isotope effects, heavy water, isotope ratios, NMR, metabolism, environmental science and tritium decay. Practice will move from fundamental counts to research-style interpretation.

Learning outcomes: Distinguish ¹H, ²H and ³H by nucleons and stability; read D and T isotope symbols in molecules; calculate isotope masses, mixture fractions, D/H ratios, reduced masses and tritium decay; explain isotope effects, hydrogen exchange and safe conceptual use of labels in life-science research.

1.A.1.18.2 A chronological discovery story: from hydrogen to heavy hydrogen

Stage 1 — Hydrogen became a fundamental chemical element

Hydrogen was studied as a distinct gas in early modern chemistry and became central to understanding water and acids. At that stage scientists did not know its nucleus contained a proton, nor that atoms of the same element could have different masses. The ordinary hydrogen found in most samples seemed to be a single chemical substance.

Stage 2 — Atomic structure created the possibility of isotopes

Electron discoveries, Rutherford's nuclear model and the atomic-number concept established that a chemical element is defined by nuclear positive charge, not solely by a measured atomic mass. Once scientists recognized isotopes among other elements, it became possible to ask whether hydrogen also had heavier nuclear versions.

Stage 3 — Deuterium was discovered through isotope-sensitive measurements

In 1931, Harold Urey and collaborators identified heavy hydrogen through spectroscopic work informed by atomic physics. The discovery showed that hydrogen nuclei could share Z = 1 yet differ in mass. Deuterium's nucleus contains a proton and a neutron. The isotope was subsequently important in chemistry, physics and isotope-tracer research.

Stage 4 — Tritium emerged from nuclear experiments

In 1934, Rutherford, Oliphant and Harteck produced tritium in nuclear experiments involving deuterium. Later work established tritium's radioactive behavior. Its mass number is three and its nucleus has one proton and two neutrons, making it an isotope of hydrogen, not a form of helium.

Stage 5 — Isotopes became scientific tools rather than curiosities

Mass spectrometry, NMR and isotope-ratio methods allowed scientists to measure isotope signatures; radioactivity measurements opened a separate window onto tritium. Modern biology uses stable deuterium labels to study turnover and synthesis, while environmental scientists measure hydrogen isotope ratios in water. The same nuclear identity therefore underpins several distinct measuring methods.

1.A.1.18.3 The rigorous nuclear definition: three isotopes, one proton

Start with the common nuclear identity

All nuclides called hydrogen possess one nuclear proton: Z = 1. To change the isotope while retaining hydrogen identity, change neutron count N. Because A = Z + N, the three familiar mass numbers one, two and three correspond to N = 0, 1 and 2. This is the entire counting foundation.

Why proton count must not change

If a nucleus with one proton were to gain an additional proton, it would no longer be hydrogen. Atomic number two would identify helium. Isotope changes within an element do not occur by changing Z. This is especially important when tritium beta-decays: its daughter does become helium because the nuclear proton number changes.

Each isotope has a specific name

Hydrogen-1 is known as protium, hydrogen-2 as deuterium and hydrogen-3 as tritium. The conventional symbols ¹H, ²H and ³H express the most precise basic nuclear notation; D is widely used for ²H and T for ³H in chemical contexts. A formula such as D₂O is molecular notation, not an isotope name by itself.

1.A.1.18.4 Visualizing the three nuclei without inventing planetary atoms

A simple count diagram has one limited purpose

Each nucleus contains one positive proton. Deuterium adds one electrically neutral neutron, and tritium contains two neutrons. A classroom picture of coloured circles can help count nucleons, but it does not show actual nuclear wavefunctions, spacing or orbital sizes. The true quantum-mechanical structures are more subtle.

Electron number is another layer

Neutral atoms of ¹H, ²H and ³H each have one electron because each has Z = 1. If these atoms form H⁺, D⁺ or T⁺, they have zero electrons as monatomic ions. Their isotope identity is unchanged by ordinary electron loss. This gives a clean bridge back to Node 16.

1.A.1.18.5 Protium (¹H): the simplest common hydrogen atom

The nucleus contains only one proton

A ¹H nucleus has no neutron. Neutral protium has one electron in its lowest-energy atomic state. Because it is so simple, the hydrogen atom is a foundational system in quantum mechanics and spectroscopy; its energy levels have been studied with extraordinary precision.

Most naturally occurring hydrogen atoms are protium

In typical terrestrial materials, ¹H overwhelmingly dominates hydrogen composition. The deuterium fraction is much smaller and varies among water sources and compounds, while natural tritium normally occurs only at trace levels. Exact isotope composition must be specified by a sample or standard rather than assuming one global unchanging fraction.

Proton versus protium: be precise about context

The nucleus of ¹H is a single proton; the neutral ¹H atom includes an electron. In acid-base chemistry, H⁺ is a shorthand for a protonic species, but aqueous proton chemistry involves hydration and proton transfer rather than a free bare proton drifting alone through ordinary bulk water.

Hydrogen-1 connects to NMR

The ¹H nucleus has spin one-half and a magnetic moment, enabling proton NMR of many organic compounds. The observed chemical shift reports how local electrons shield the nucleus, not how many neutrons it has. This is why an isotope can be useful as a spectroscopic probe without being radioactive.

1.A.1.18.6 Deuterium (²H, D): one extra neutron, a distinctive mass

What makes deuterium different

The deuterium nucleus, called a deuteron, contains one proton and one neutron. Its mass is roughly twice that of a protium atom when discussing approximate isotope mass ratios. Yet both nuclei have Z = 1; chemically they are isotopes of the same element.

A stable isotope with a strong physical signature

Deuterium is stable. Its extra neutron substantially changes nuclear mass relative to protium. In molecules, this can change vibrational frequencies, diffusion and chemical rates in suitably defined comparisons. The isotope is not a new element or a radioactive variant.

D is useful but context still matters

The symbol D is a conventional way to specify deuterium at a molecular position. For instance C–D means a bond between carbon and deuterium, while O–D means a deuterium-containing hydroxyl site. Molecular drawings specify where deuterium is attached; an isolated label ²H merely states which hydrogen nuclide is present.

Natural D/H must be measured

Terrestrial hydrogen isotope composition varies. VSMOW and related standards anchor isotope-ratio comparisons, but no single exact D/H fraction applies to every lake, leaf, mineral or biological material. The common teaching statement that deuterium is rare is correct; a precise percentage without a source and context may be misleading.

1.A.1.18.7 Tritium (³H, T): the radioactive member

Three nucleons but still hydrogen

Tritium contains one proton and two neutrons. It has Z = 1 and A = 3, and a neutral atom has one electron. The extra neutrons make it different from stable protium and deuterium at the nuclear level; its basic elemental identity remains hydrogen until a nuclear transformation occurs.

Tritium undergoes beta-minus decay

A tritium nucleus transforms a neutron into a proton, emitting a beta electron and an electron antineutrino. Its daughter is helium-3: ³₁H → ³₂He + e⁻ + anti-neutrino. The mass number remains three, while Z changes from one to two. Tritium and helium-3 are therefore isobars, not isotopes of one another.

Time scale and radiation energy are distinct facts

The half-life is approximately 12.32 years. The emitted beta electrons have a continuous energy distribution with a maximum around 18.6 keV; their mean energy is much lower. These numbers describe different properties: half-life concerns decay probability over time, while electron energy concerns the radiation spectrum.

Tritium can arise naturally and artificially

Interactions of cosmic rays with atmospheric nuclei generate trace tritium, and human nuclear activities can also produce it. Water molecules containing tritium can participate in environmental water movement, making tritium relevant to hydrological studies when measured professionally. Trace presence and human safety are separate subjects requiring appropriate specialized evaluation.

Keep safety discussions conceptual

Tritium is a radionuclide, so practical research requires institutional procedures, professional radiation protection and appropriate facilities. A general study node should focus on notation, decay laws and interpretation, not on obtaining, preparing, concentrating or handling radioactive material.

³₁H → ³₂He + β⁻ + anti-neutrino   ;   t½(³H) ≈ 12.32 y
Critical distinction: The beta particle is created in nuclear decay; it is not simply the atom's original orbital electron being removed.

1.A.1.18.8 A complete comparison: nuclear and physical identity

Keep invariant properties separate from changing properties

Across the three isotopes, atomic number is fixed at one and neutral electron count is one. The number of neutrons, mass number, measured isotope mass and nuclear spin/stability can differ. Thus broadly similar chemical behavior does not imply identical measured properties.

Measured isotope masses are not integers

Representative neutral-atom masses are approximately 1.007825 u for ¹H, 2.014102 u for ²H and 3.016049 u for ³H. These are not exactly the integers one, two and three because mass number is a nucleon count, whereas measured mass includes real particle masses and binding-energy effects.

Half-life is not an abundance fraction

The approximately 12.32-year half-life of tritium describes survival of a radioactive population. Natural tritium abundance, on the other hand, depends on production, decay, transport and human contributions. A fraction of deuterium in a sample is a population composition, not a radioactive decay quantity.

1.A.1.18.9 What the symbols H, D and T mean in molecules

Atomic isotope labels and molecular atom counts use different positions

¹H, ²H and ³H specify nuclear species. H₂, D₂ and T₂ describe diatomic molecules whose atoms carry those isotope identities. The right-hand lower subscript counts atoms per molecule. The left-hand upper isotope number counts nucleons in one nucleus. Confusing the positions leads to a wrong answer even when the arithmetic is memorized.

Mixed molecular hydrogen species

HD is a diatomic molecule with one protium atom and one deuterium atom. HT has protium and tritium, and DT has deuterium and tritium. Molecules of the same element's isotopes are sometimes called isotopologues because their isotopic compositions differ.

Isotope identity must be followed at each atomic site

Water molecules may be H₂O, HDO, D₂O, HTO or another isotope-labelled form. The chemical oxygen isotope may be specified separately, as in H₂¹⁸O. A label such as D₂O says that its two hydrogen sites are deuterium; it says nothing by itself about oxygen isotope composition.

Avoid overclaiming ordinary abundance

A formula D₂O is an idealized molecular species. Natural water has a mixture of isotope compositions, dominated by protium-containing molecules, with deuterium variations. Distinguishing a pure named isotopologue from the mixture in a real bottle is essential for scientific language.

1.A.1.18.10 Atomic mass versus molecular mass: learn both calculations

Mass number is a count, isotope mass is a measured quantity

A protium atom has mass number one, but an approximate mass 1.007825 u. Deuterium has A = 2 but mass about 2.014102 u. Such differences matter in accurate mass spectrometry. Introductory calculations may use integer masses only when clearly stated as approximate.

Compare H₂O and D₂O using simplified mass numbers

Using oxygen mass number sixteen, protium one and deuterium two, the approximate molecular mass of H₂O is 16 + 2(1) = 18 u, whereas D₂O is 16 + 2(2) = 20 u. The shift of about two mass units is substantial relative to water's small molecular mass.

Accurate masses require a specific oxygen nuclide

If oxygen-16 and exact isotope masses are used, the molecular mass difference between H₂¹⁶O and D₂¹⁶O is roughly twice the ²H−¹H neutral atomic mass difference, about 2.01255 u. The exact molecular mass further involves molecular binding energy and the exact definition of the species.

A mixture does not have one nuclide label

A bottle described as ‘partly deuterated water’ may contain H₂O, HDO and D₂O in some proportions. The average composition of that bottle is a sample property and cannot be substituted for the nucleon count of any individual nucleus.

Approximate H₂O mass ≈ 18 u; D₂O ≈ 20 u; HDO ≈ 19 u (using integer atomic masses)

1.A.1.18.11 Hydrogen bonding: related electronic behavior with different dynamics

The isotope does not turn oxygen or hydrogen into a new element

O–H and O–D bonds share the same nuclear charges at their atoms and have broadly comparable electronic potential energy surfaces. But the different nuclear mass changes vibrational energy levels and molecular motion. This makes it possible for deuteration to alter properties even without changing the basic chemical bonding category.

Why heavy water has distinguishable bulk properties

D₂O and H₂O differ in physical properties such as density and phase-transition temperatures. Such differences reflect isotope-sensitive vibrational and intermolecular behavior. We should not claim they are chemically indistinguishable in every quantitative experiment.

Biological effects are concentration- and context-dependent

Large-scale replacement of ordinary hydrogen with deuterium can alter biological processes. Deuterium labelling experiments use carefully defined conditions and are not equivalent to advising anyone to drink heavy water. A pedagogical explanation must not turn the fact that ²H is stable into a blanket assertion that every exposure or concentration is harmless.

Critical distinction: ‘Stable isotope’ means the nucleus is not radioactive under known observations; it does not mean all chemical or biological exposures have zero effect.

1.A.1.18.12 Reduced mass: why the same bond can vibrate differently

A simple two-body vibration model

For a chemical bond approximated as two masses joined by a spring, vibrational frequency is proportional to the square root of the force constant divided by reduced mass. Reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). If the bonding force constant is similar when H is replaced by D, the larger μ lowers the characteristic vibrational frequency.

Do not replace reduced mass with isotope mass without thinking

For a heavy atom X bound to hydrogen, the mass of X also contributes to μ. If the partner is extremely heavy relative to H, the approximate frequency ratio tends toward √(m_H/m_D). For a carbon–hydrogen bond the actual elementary reduced-mass approximation differs slightly from √(1/2).

A carbon bond worked in symbols

Take approximate masses carbon = 12, hydrogen = 1, deuterium = 2. Reduced mass of C–H is μ_CH = 12×1/13 ≈ 0.923 u. For C–D, μ_CD = 12×2/14 ≈ 1.714 u. Assuming similar force constant, ν_CD/ν_CH ≈ √(0.923/1.714) ≈ 0.734. This is an instructive model, not an exact measured spectrum.

μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂);   ν ≈ (1 / 2π)√(k/μ);   νCD/νCH ≈ √(μCH/μCD)
Simplified bond-oscillator comparison: the larger deuterium mass raises reduced mass and lowers modeled frequency.

1.A.1.18.13 Zero-point energy: the subtle cause of many isotope effects

Why a bond has energy even at its lowest vibrational level

A quantum harmonic oscillator has a lowest vibrational energy of (1/2)hν, not zero. If a deuterated bond has lower ν under a comparable potential, its zero-point energy is also lower. This influences relative energy levels and can contribute to isotope-dependent rates and equilibria.

Rate effects emerge from energy differences, not changed chemistry names

A reaction's activation barrier involves the energy difference between reactant and transition-state ensembles. Isotopic substitution can change vibrational contributions to these energies, and quantum tunnelling may also matter for light hydrogen. Thus isotope substitution can influence a rate even when the chemical element and electrons are broadly similar.

An exact mechanism is not read from a single isotope label

The existence of an H/D kinetic isotope effect may support a mechanistic hypothesis, but it does not automatically prove which bond was broken or which enzyme intermediate is rate limiting. Binding, commitment factors, solvent contributions and multiple steps can affect observed kinetics.

Harmonic zero-point energy E₀ = ½hν; heavier isotope often lowers ν at comparable force constant

1.A.1.18.14 Kinetic isotope effects: primary, secondary, normal and inverse

What IUPAC calls a kinetic isotope effect

A kinetic isotope effect is a change in reaction rate associated with substitution of one isotope for another in otherwise comparable reaction components. The ratio of rate constants is often written k_H/k_D for an appropriately matched hydrogen/deuterium comparison. Experimental definitions and conditions should accompany the number.

Primary versus secondary effects

A primary isotope effect involves isotope substitution at a bond made or broken in the rate-controlling chemistry or its relevant pre-equilibrium. A secondary isotope effect concerns substitution at a site where bonds to the isotope are not made or broken in those steps. These are mechanistic distinctions, not merely ‘large effect versus small effect’.

Normal versus inverse

If k_H/k_D exceeds one under that ratio convention, the effect is described as normal; if less than one, inverse. An isotope effect's sign and magnitude depend on the chemical mechanism and measurement. There is no universal rule that every H/D substitution must make every reaction slower.

Biochemical interpretation

Hydrogen-transfer enzymes are frequent contexts for H/D isotope-effect measurements. An observed ratio may reveal that isotopic mass matters to a kinetic step, but a full interpretation can require measurements of elementary rate constants, enzyme commitments, pH/pD conditions and control reactions.

1.A.1.18.15 Solvent isotope effects: water itself can be the variable

D₂O affects more than one exchangeable position

Replacing ordinary water with deuterium-enriched water can alter hydrogen-bond networks, proton-transfer equilibria, viscosity and exchangeable sites of biomolecules. A measured solvent isotope effect can therefore reflect multiple contributions. It is not always equivalent to changing one fixed C–H bond to C–D.

Exchangeable hydrogens need special attention

Hydrogens attached to oxygen, nitrogen or sulfur may exchange with solvent under suitable conditions, while many carbon-bound hydrogens exchange far less readily without specific chemistry. Isotope incorporation depends on molecular environment and reaction mechanism. A labelled starting molecule does not guarantee every labelled site remains fixed indefinitely.

pH and pD measurements need appropriate conventions

Acidity scales and electrode response in heavy water have subtleties. A simple reported glass-electrode pH reading in D₂O is not necessarily the same as a rigorously defined pD. When discussing enzyme isotope experiments, conditions and measurement conventions must be matched before comparing rates.

Critical distinction: Do not attribute every D₂O experiment to the primary isotope effect of one substrate bond. Solvent and exchange effects can be substantially broader.

1.A.1.18.16 Equilibrium isotope effects and isotope exchange

Rates and equilibria answer different questions

A kinetic isotope effect changes a reaction rate ratio. An equilibrium isotope effect concerns how isotope substitution changes an equilibrium constant or the distribution of isotopes between chemical species. Two reactions can have similar chemistry but different isotope-sensitive equilibrium populations.

The role of vibrational energy in equilibrium

Because isotopes differ in vibrational partition functions, isotopic substitution can shift equilibrium energetics. Fractionation between water and another hydrogen-bearing compound reflects a balance of these contributions and conditions. The direction and size of the effect depend on temperature and chemistry.

Hydrogen isotope exchange does not imply nuclear transformation

An exchange event can swap which hydrogen isotope occupies a molecular position while each nucleus stays the same isotope. For example, under suitable chemistry an O–H site may exchange with deuterium-rich solvent to produce O–D. The isotope labels move; a neutron was not added to the original hydrogen atom during ordinary exchange.

Follow the actual atom, not just the letter on a structural formula

In tracer studies, isotope exchange can cause a label to relocate. An experimentally detected deuterium atom in one molecule may have come via exchange rather than a specific metabolic reaction. Correct interpretation may need atom-mapping, timing and controls.

Equilibrium isotope effect = Klight/Kheavy or a clearly specified reciprocal convention

1.A.1.18.17 Heavy water and isotopologues: H₂O, HDO, D₂O and HTO

How to read water's molecular formula

An ordinary H₂O isotopologue with two ¹H atoms has two hydrogen sites and one oxygen. HDO has one protium and one deuterium site. D₂O has two deuterium sites. HTO includes tritium at one hydrogen position, but is radioactive because it contains a tritium nucleus.

Two deuteriums change approximate molecular mass

Using integer mass numbers, H₂¹⁶O ≈ 18 u, HD¹⁶O ≈ 19 u, and D₂¹⁶O ≈ 20 u. These are approximations. An exact measured molecular mass requires specified isotopic composition including oxygen and corresponding precision.

A bottle is a mixture, not a single nuclide

Ordinary water samples have mixtures of isotopologues, even when one dominates overwhelmingly. An expression like ‘deuterium enrichment 10%’ is a sample-level label and must specify whether it refers to hydrogen atom fraction, molecule fraction or another metric. Without such a definition, it is easy to compute the wrong result.

Tritiated water is not simply ‘heavy water’ in the stable-isotope sense

HTO contains radioactive tritium, whereas D₂O is based on stable deuterium. A chemistry lesson may compare their formulas for isotope counting, but their radioactivity and safe-use contexts are very different. No classroom explanation should imply they are interchangeable liquids.

1.A.1.18.18 Combinatorial isotopologues: a two-hydrogen water model

A sample-level isotope fraction predicts molecular categories only under assumptions

Suppose the probability that a hydrogen site contains deuterium is p and the two water hydrogen sites are independent and equivalent under an ideal model. Then P(H₂O)=(1−p)², P(HDO)=2p(1−p), and P(D₂O)=p². These categories sum to one.

Take a deliberately enriched teaching sample with p = 0.10

The ideal model predicts H₂O category 0.9²=0.81, HDO 2×0.1×0.9=0.18 and D₂O 0.1²=0.01. So 81%, 18%, 1% of water molecules fall into these categories under the model. This is not the natural composition of seawater or an experimental recipe.

Why twice appears in the middle term

With exactly one deuterium in a two-site molecule, it can occupy either of two equivalent labelled positions. The binomial coefficient 2 counts these alternatives. In experimental water, isotope exchange and distribution conditions can affect details; the simplified calculation is useful for probability logic.

P(k D in n independent H sites) = C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k

1.A.1.18.19 Natural deuterium abundance, D/H ratio and VSMOW

The natural abundance of deuterium is small but variable

The CIAAW documents that terrestrial hydrogen isotopic composition varies substantially among normal materials. The dominant ¹H atom fraction is close to one and ²H is minor. Rather than memorizing one absolute global ratio, recognize why environmental standards and calibrated measurements are necessary.

D/H atom ratio is not the deuterium atom fraction

The ratio R = number of ²H atoms divided by number of ¹H atoms. The atom fraction x_D = number of ²H atoms divided by all hydrogen atoms, including any relevant minor isotopes. If only ¹H and ²H matter, x_D = R/(1+R). The difference is small at low D abundance but conceptually exact.

Reference water makes comparisons meaningful

VSMOW (Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water) is a key reference for hydrogen and oxygen isotope measurements. A reported δ²H compares a sample D/H ratio with an established reference ratio. Without a named reference, a delta number is not a complete scientific report.

A negative δ value is not a negative isotope count

If R_sample is 0.95 times the reference ratio, δ²H = (0.95−1)×1000 = −50‰. This means the sample has a 5% lower D/H ratio than the reference ratio, not that deuterium is absent or negative.

R = D/H;   xD = R/(1+R) (two-isotope approximation);   δ²H = (Rsample/Rref−1)×1000‰

1.A.1.18.20 Fractionation in the water cycle: follow the isotope through weather

Evaporation and condensation can sort isotope ratios

Different isotopic masses affect vapor–liquid equilibrium and kinetic processes. In many common environmental settings, water vapor is relatively depleted in heavier isotopes compared with associated liquid water, while details depend on temperature, humidity, transport and non-equilibrium effects. Such patterns form part of environmental isotope hydrology.

A raindrop is not a simple unique thermometer

Measured δ²H and δ¹⁸O can reflect precipitation source, air-mass history, condensation conditions, evaporation and mixing. One ratio by itself cannot uniquely reveal the temperature, rainfall source or age of a water sample. This is a recurring theme in scientific inference: isotope observations constrain possibilities, but context determines interpretation.

Why hydrogen and oxygen labels are studied together

Hydrogen and oxygen isotope signatures in water provide complementary evidence about physical processes. Their combined patterns can help investigate evaporation and water sources, although reference scales, local climate, hydrological models and sample preparation must be considered.

Hydrogen and oxygen isotope fractionation are not radioactive changes

Neither ordinary evaporation nor condensation converts protium into deuterium or oxygen-16 into oxygen-18. Instead the proportions of pre-existing isotopes become different between phases and samples. This distinction preserves the nuclear identity of every atom.

Conceptual fractionation during evaporation and condensation; specific ratios depend on conditions.

1.A.1.18.21 Tritium in environmental hydrology and tritium units

Natural atmospheric production introduces a time-sensitive label

Cosmic-ray interactions can create tritium in the atmosphere, and it may become incorporated into water. Because tritium decays with a half-life around twelve years, its concentration can offer information about relatively recent water histories when combined with appropriate environmental models and reference records.

A tritium unit is a ratio convention

One tritium unit (TU) is defined as one ³H atom per 10¹⁸ hydrogen atoms in a water sample. This is a ratio, not an activity concentration by itself. Translating TU to activity depends on radioactive decay constants and the amount of sample; those steps should be stated explicitly when needed.

Why tritium alone may not give a unique water age

Environmental concentrations can depend on atmospheric input history, mixing, recharge, tritium decay and human contributions. A measured tritium concentration is therefore not necessarily a direct clock reading for one unique water parcel. Hydrological models and other tracers may be needed.

Keep the conceptual and operational scopes distinct

Tritium measurements involve radioactive materials and professional procedures. This educational discussion defines nuclear and environmental quantities but deliberately does not describe radioactive sample preparation or handling.

1.A.1.18.22 Tritium half-life, decay constant and population statistics

Write the physical law carefully

For an isolated population of parent nuclei decaying with constant probability per unit time, expected remaining count follows N(t)=N₀e^(−λt). The half-life t½ satisfies e^(−λt½)=1/2, giving λ=ln 2/t½. This is a statistical law for radioactive nuclei.

After one and two half-lives

Using a rounded tritium half-life of 12.32 years, an ideal initial population has expected 50% remaining after 12.32 years, 25% after 24.64 years, and 12.5% after 36.96 years. These are expectations, not guaranteed exact outcomes for tiny populations.

Activity is not the same as number of atoms

The activity is A_activity = λN in decays per unit time. A larger number of nuclei at a fixed λ produces greater expected activity. Be careful: the symbol A is also used for nuclear mass number, so activity should be identified by context or an explicit label to avoid notational confusion.

Daughter production and beta energy

In a simplified closed-system parent-to-daughter picture, every tritium nucleus that decays creates a helium-3 nucleus. The beta electron carries a variable share of the decay energy, with an endpoint near 18.6 keV. The electron antineutrino and recoiling nucleus share the remaining energy and momentum.

N(t)=N₀(1/2)t/t½;   λ=ln(2)/t½;   activity = λN

1.A.1.18.23 Beta-minus decay bookkeeping: hydrogen becomes helium

The nuclear reaction changes Z but not total nucleon count

Tritium starts with one proton and two neutrons. In beta-minus decay, one neutron becomes a proton, leaving two protons and one neutron: helium-3. Thus A stays three but Z increases from one to two. Element identity changes, so tritium and helium-3 are isobars.

Include emitted particles in conservation checks

The daughter nuclear charge is +2 instead of +1; the emitted electron has −1 electric charge, maintaining total electric charge. The electron antineutrino is part of the complete weak-interaction process and is essential to lepton-number and energy–momentum accounting.

Why the beta spectrum is continuous

The available decay energy is shared variably among the electron, antineutrino and recoil. Therefore beta electrons do not all have exactly 18.6 keV. That figure is an approximate endpoint. A statement that every tritium beta electron has that energy would be physically incorrect.

Do not confuse isotope transformation with ionization

A neutral tritium atom can lose an ordinary orbital electron and become T⁺ without changing its nucleus. By contrast beta-minus decay actually changes nuclear proton number and element identity to helium-3. Both involve electrons in different ways but are not the same process.

Exam-safe reaction: ³₁H → ³₂He + e⁻ + electron antineutrino. Check nuclear A: 3 = 3; electric charge: +1 = +2 − 1.

1.A.1.18.24 Nuclear spin, magnetic resonance and isotope-specific signals

Protium, deuterium and tritium have different nuclear spins

¹H has spin 1/2, ²H has spin 1 and ³H has spin 1/2. Nuclear spin affects magnetic resonance behavior, energy-level structure and selection of analytical methods. It cannot be inferred from atomic number alone; the isotopes have different nuclear composition.

The practical role of deuterated NMR solvents

Deuterated solvents are commonly used to reduce interfering ¹H solvent signals and assist instrument magnetic-field locking. A deuterated molecule can still contain residual ¹H and may show exchange behavior, so ‘deuterated’ should not automatically be read as absolute 100% substitution everywhere.

²H NMR is not the same as ordinary ¹H NMR

The deuterium nucleus has distinct magnetic properties and spin one. Spectroscopy can distinguish ¹H from ²H signals in suitably configured experiments. The isotope label provides nuclear identity, while chemical environment determines the actual resonance behavior.

Tritium spectroscopy is a separate research context

Tritium's radioactive nature and nuclear magnetic properties make it distinct from using stable deuterium as a routine NMR lock isotope. It should not be introduced as a casual substitute for ordinary solvents or teaching materials.

1.A.1.18.25 Hydrogen isotope labeling in biological molecules

An isotope label allows selected hydrogens to be followed

A biochemical substrate containing one or more deuterium atoms can sometimes reveal which atoms remain at a position and which are transferred or exchanged during a reaction. The label acts as a recognizable nuclear species. However hydrogen atoms often exchange more readily than many heavy-atom labels, so interpretation requires chemistry-aware controls.

A deuterium label can be positional or non-positional

A molecule may be labelled at one particular carbon-bound hydrogen, or described as uniformly deuterated. These statements convey different experimental constraints. One deuterium signal does not automatically report which bond originally carried it if isotope exchange or a scrambling mechanism has occurred.

The method of detection depends on the experiment

Mass spectrometry can identify isotope-induced mass shifts, while NMR may provide position-sensitive information where feasible. Other analytical approaches can be used in particular contexts. Measurement choice and sample chemistry must accompany any inference.

A label tracks atom fate, not automatically reaction speed

Detecting deuterium in a product supports the presence of a labelled hydrogen nucleus but does not uniquely prove a specific pathway rate. A quantitative conclusion may need substrate enrichment, time-course data, isotope scrambling analysis and kinetic modeling.

1.A.1.18.26 Deuterated water in turnover and synthesis studies

Why body-water isotope enrichment can be informative

In carefully designed research, deuterium from water may be incorporated into newly synthesized molecules through metabolic reactions. Measuring isotope enrichment in a defined product pool relative to an appropriate precursor enrichment can help estimate synthesis or turnover under a validated model.

Not every hydrogen in a biomolecule is equally informative

Some hydrogen positions exchange readily after synthesis, while other carbon-bound positions can retain label differently. An assay must distinguish newly incorporated non-exchangeable labels from rapidly exchanging positions. Otherwise a measurement might overstate synthesis.

Fractional synthesis is a model-based quantity

A labelled product fraction depends on precursor enrichment, the number of labeling opportunities, sampling time, pool turnover and other biological assumptions. A simple statement such as ‘we detected ²H in protein’ cannot yield an absolute protein synthesis rate without those data.

Stable isotope does not automatically mean unrestricted human use

The fact that deuterium is not radioactive is separate from the practical safety, ethics and institutional approval of isotope studies. This chapter explains the reasoning behind tracer measurements and does not provide clinical dosing or experimental administration instructions.

1.A.1.18.27 Enzymes, hydrogen transfer and mechanistic insight

Follow one hydrogen through a reaction conceptually

An enzyme may transfer a hydride, proton or hydrogen atom depending on its chemistry. Using a suitable isotope-labelled substrate can help test which hydrogen participates in a measured step. These transfer types are not interchangeable: a proton H⁺, hydrogen atom H• and hydride H⁻ differ in electronic composition.

A label can alter the process it is tracking

Replacing ¹H with ²H can change reaction rates due to isotope effects. Thus a tracer is not always perfectly non-perturbing, especially in hydrogen-transfer chemistry. A careful design compares labelled and unlabelled kinetics and interprets possible perturbation.

Primary versus solvent isotope experiments

A specifically deuterated substrate targets a selected bond, while D₂O solvent changes many exchangeable sites and solvent properties. An observed rate effect from the two situations can arise through different mechanisms. Keeping these conditions separate is essential for a defensible mechanistic explanation.

Science favors conditional conclusions

Even a large k_H/k_D ratio does not by itself establish an exact transition-state structure. It is mechanistic evidence that must be combined with kinetic models, chemical perturbations and other measurements.

1.A.1.18.28 Hydrogen isotope tracing versus other stable isotope tracers

Why hydrogen labels can exchange

Many hydrogen positions are susceptible to proton-transfer chemistry; oxygen-, nitrogen- or sulfur-bound hydrogen may exchange with solvent. Carbon-bound hydrogen can be more persistent in some contexts but not immune. Label-retention assumptions must therefore be checked before inferring atom transfer.

Contrast with carbon-13 and nitrogen-15

¹³C and ¹⁵N labels alter heavier-element nuclei and can be useful for tracing carbon or nitrogen skeletons through metabolism. Hydrogen isotopes may be especially sensitive to solvent exchange and kinetic isotope effects. No tracer type is universally best; the question and chemical pathway determine usefulness.

A nuclide label is not a complete experimental plan

To infer metabolic routes, one needs the labelled position, precursor composition, measured product patterns, biological pool mixing and a suitable model. A label alone reveals nuclear identity and perhaps placement; it does not prove unique atom movement under every circumstance.

1.A.1.18.29 Hydrogen isotope mass spectrometry: isotopologues and charges

A one-deuterium substitution shifts molecular mass by about one unit

Replacing ¹H with ²H changes neutral atomic mass by about 1.006277 u, not exactly one u. In a comparable singly charged molecular ion, the m/z shift is approximately that mass difference, subject to other isotope and adduct contributions. High-resolution instruments can distinguish some related isotope substitutions.

Charge can divide a mass shift

For a comparable ion at charge magnitude 2, one deuterium substitution typically produces roughly half the m/z separation of the singly charged case: about 0.503139. The nuclear isotope itself has not changed its mass because the ion carries two charges; only the measurement's mass-to-charge ratio changes.

Why positional information may not be available from whole-molecule mass

Two molecules labelled with one D at different positions can have nearly the same overall mass. Such positional isotopomers may require fragmentation, NMR or reaction chemistry analysis to distinguish. A deuterium-containing M+1-like signal is not a map of exactly where the hydrogen label is situated.

Hydrogen exchange can create false pathway assignments

If a labelled hydrogen exchanges with solvent during sampling or sample handling, an observed isotope shift may reflect exchange rather than metabolic transformation. High-quality inference requires addressing chemical stability and sample history.

Neutral atomic mass difference ²H−¹H ≈ 1.006277 u;   Δ(m/z) ≈ Δm/|z|

1.A.1.18.30 Natural D/H and biological water: do not confuse ratio, amount and rate

D/H is a ratio of isotopic atoms

A D/H value tells us how many deuterium atoms occur relative to protium atoms in the defined material. It is not a measurement of total water volume or a metabolic turnover rate. The same water quantity can have different isotope ratios due to its origin or past fractionation.

A sample can be enriched without changing hydrogen's elemental identity

A sample with deuterium fraction 10% still contains hydrogen nuclei, all with Z = 1. The isotope proportions have changed, not the element identity. Its water molecules may include H₂O, HDO and D₂O depending on distribution and exchange.

Interpret biological water isotopes within a system

Water sources, evaporation, metabolic water formation and physiological exchange may influence measured hydrogen isotope ratios. Any mechanistic statement requires a model. A change in δ²H alone is not an automatic diagnosis of one biological process.

1.A.1.18.31 Thirty-five scientific checkpoints and extended exam practice preparation

The remaining sections contain worked numericals, misconception repairs and integrated problems to connect the nuclear definition with molecular isotopologues, chemical isotope effects, radioactive decay and biological tracing. The academic rule is simple: solve the question, show the reasoning, verify the answer and state any assumptions.

Build a decision tree before answering

First ask if the problem concerns one nuclear isotope, a neutral atom, an ion, a molecule or a sample mixture. Next identify what A, Z, N, electron count, mass, isotope ratio or decay quantity is being requested. Finally decide whether the answer follows from symbol alone or requires external nuclear, chemical or biological measurements.

Practice with appropriate scientific humility

Many examination mistakes come from inventing information, such as assuming every label is radioactive or that a sample's isotope ratio automatically reveals its age. A strong answer may correctly say that the data are insufficient. Quantitative calculations require declared masses, concentrations, decay times or rate constants.

1.A.1.18.33 Sixty original CSIR-NET and SET-style questions with explanations

These are original examination-style practice items about hydrogen isotope fundamentals and applications, not copied past-paper questions. A learner should first attempt each question independently, then read the answer and scientific reason. Mixed questions test the difference between nuclear identity, molecular labeling and measured data.

Part I — Twenty detailed worked examples from nuclear counting to isotope effects

Worked example 1. Protium: count the simplest nucleus

Problem: Neutral ¹₁H is specified. Determine P, N, E and the meaning of its isotope name.

Complete scientific reasoning: Hydrogen atomic number Z = 1 fixes one proton. Mass number A = 1 means N = A − Z = 0 neutrons. Neutrality gives E = Z = 1 electron. The nuclide is protium, the overwhelmingly dominant stable hydrogen isotope in ordinary terrestrial matter.

Independent check: P + N = 1 and P − E = 0.

Common mistake: Do not mistake the proton itself for a neutral atom; neutral ¹H includes an electron.

Worked example 2. Deuterium particles from notation

Problem: Find P, N and E for a neutral ²₁H atom, also written D.

Complete scientific reasoning: The symbol ²H gives A = 2 and hydrogen identity gives Z = 1. Thus P = 1, N = 2 − 1 = 1 and, for a neutral atom, E = 1. It is stable deuterium. The D abbreviation is a conventional isotope symbol, not a distinct periodic-table element.

Independent check: 1 proton + 1 neutron = 2 nucleons.

Common mistake: Do not call D a two-proton nucleus; that would imply helium.

Worked example 3. Tritium particle counting

Problem: Count particles for neutral ³₁H.

Complete scientific reasoning: Tritium has Z = 1, A = 3, so P = 1 and N = 3 − 1 = 2. A neutral tritium atom has one electron. Its radioactive instability is an experimentally established nuclear property and is separate from the simple particle calculation.

Independent check: P + N = 1 + 2 = 3 and neutral charge 1 − 1 = 0.

Common mistake: The tritium symbol ³H does not mean three neutrons.

Worked example 4. Neutral hydrogen versus hydrogen cation

Problem: Compare neutral ²H and ²H⁺ for nuclear and electronic identity.

Complete scientific reasoning: Both species contain the same deuterium nucleus: one proton and one neutron. Neutral ²H has one electron, while ²H⁺ has zero electrons as a bare monatomic cation. Losing an electron changes charge by +1 but cannot convert ²H to ¹H or ³H.

Independent check: Charge 1 − 0 = +1 for the cation.

Common mistake: Ionization is electronic; isotope conversion is nuclear.

Worked example 5. From nuclear counts to isotope name

Problem: A hydrogen nuclide has one proton and two neutrons. Identify its full nuclear symbol.

Complete scientific reasoning: Add nucleons to find A = 1 + 2 = 3. The proton count Z = 1 fixes hydrogen. Therefore the isotope is ³₁H, called tritium or T. The existence of a neutron pair does not change elemental identity to helium because Z remains one.

Independent check: A = 3, Z = 1, N = 2.

Common mistake: Do not identify an element from mass number alone.

Worked example 6. A helium daughter from beta decay

Problem: A ³₁H nucleus undergoes β⁻ decay. What daughter is predicted?

Complete scientific reasoning: Beta-minus converts one neutron into one proton. Thus the daughter still has A = 3 but Z rises from 1 to 2, identifying helium-3: ³₂He. An electron and electron antineutrino are emitted; they are required for full charge and lepton-number bookkeeping.

Independent check: A: 3 remains 3; electric charge +1 = +2 − 1.

Common mistake: Tritium decay changes element, not just electronic ionization.

Worked example 7. Stable hydrogen isotope or radionuclide

Problem: Which of ¹H, ²H, ³H are stable?

Complete scientific reasoning: ¹H and ²H are stable nuclides; ³H is radioactive. All are hydrogen because they share Z = 1, but nuclear stability varies with neutron content and nuclear structure. The definition of isotope by itself does not guarantee stability or instability.

Independent check: All three share proton count one.

Common mistake: The word isotope does not automatically mean radioactive.

Worked example 8. Molecules H₂, HD and D₂

Problem: Count how many isotope-labelled hydrogen atoms are in each molecule.

Complete scientific reasoning: H₂ contains two protium atoms in the standard isotopic reading, HD contains one protium and one deuterium, and D₂ contains two deuterium atoms. The right subscript 2 counts atoms per molecule. It does not represent the neutron number of a single hydrogen nuclide.

Independent check: Molecule counts are two atoms in every case.

Common mistake: D₂ is molecular notation; ²H is nuclear notation.

Worked example 9. Water isotopologue formulas

Problem: Identify the hydrogen isotopes in H₂O, HDO and D₂O.

Complete scientific reasoning: H₂O has two ¹H sites in the explicitly protium model; HDO has one ¹H and one ²H; D₂O has two ²H nuclei. Oxygen's isotope is not specified unless separately marked. These are related molecular isotopologues, not different chemical elements.

Independent check: Two hydrogen isotope sites are present in every formula.

Common mistake: The D in D₂O has one neutron, not two.

Worked example 10. Approximate water masses

Problem: Using integer masses H=1, D=2, O=16, calculate H₂O, HDO and D₂O.

Complete scientific reasoning: Add isotope-specific approximate atomic masses: H₂O = 1+1+16 = 18 u; HDO = 1+2+16 = 19 u; D₂O = 2+2+16 = 20 u. This is a valid introductory approximation, not an exact high-resolution mass-spectrometry calculation.

Independent check: Each deuterium substitution adds roughly one mass unit.

Common mistake: A formula mass must sum all atoms, not just hydrogen.

Worked example 11. Water with one tritium

Problem: Approximate the molecular mass of HTO using isotope mass numbers and ¹⁶O.

Complete scientific reasoning: HTO has one protium hydrogen of approximate mass 1, one tritium of mass 3 and one oxygen-16 of mass 16. Approximate total is 1+3+16 = 20 u. Although its approximate mass resembles D₂O, HTO is radioactive while D₂O is based on stable deuterium.

Independent check: The hydrogen sites are H and T, total mass-number sum 4.

Common mistake: Equal approximate mass does not imply equal isotope composition or stability.

Worked example 12. Accurate atomic isotope masses

Problem: Given masses ¹H=1.007825 u and ²H=2.014102 u, compute their difference.

Complete scientific reasoning: Subtract the neutral-atom masses: 2.014102 − 1.007825 = 1.006277 u. This is the approximate mass shift for a single H-to-D substitution at a comparable molecular site. It is not exactly one unit because nuclear and electron binding affect measured isotope mass.

Independent check: Re-add 1.007825+1.006277=2.014102.

Common mistake: Do not replace accurate isotope masses with A values in precision problems.

Worked example 13. Compare ³H and ²H measured masses

Problem: Using approximate masses ³H = 3.016049 u, ²H = 2.014102 u, compute difference.

Complete scientific reasoning: The atomic mass difference is 3.016049−2.014102 = 1.001947 u. The two isotopes have the same Z = 1 but differ by one neutron in their nuclear composition. The exact mass shift is not identical to the ²H−¹H shift.

Independent check: Sum 2.014102+1.001947=3.016049.

Common mistake: Do not assume every added neutron changes neutral atomic mass by exactly one u.

Worked example 14. Protium versus deuterium mass ratio

Problem: Calculate the approximate ²H/¹H neutral atomic mass ratio with 2.014102 and 1.007825 u.

Complete scientific reasoning: Divide 2.014102 by 1.007825, giving approximately 1.9985. Deuterium is almost twice as massive as protium as an atom, which helps explain why isotope mass effects are particularly evident for hydrogen compared with many heavier elements.

Independent check: 2×1.007825=2.01565, slightly above 2.014102.

Common mistake: ‘Almost twice’ is an approximation, not an exact equality.

Worked example 15. Reduced mass of C–H

Problem: In a two-body harmonic approximation take carbon mass 12 and H mass 1. Calculate μ.

Complete scientific reasoning: Reduced mass μ = m_C m_H/(m_C+m_H) = 12×1/(12+1)=12/13≈0.9231 mass units. This is not the mass of the entire molecule; it is the effective mass for one idealized relative-motion coordinate of the pair.

Independent check: μ must be less than the smaller mass 1.

Common mistake: Do not calculate μ as m_C+m_H.

Worked example 16. Reduced mass of C–D

Problem: Using C=12 and D=2 in the same model, compute μ_CD.

Complete scientific reasoning: The reduced mass is 12×2/(12+2)=24/14≈1.7143 mass units. This is greater than μ_CH≈0.9231 because the hydrogen isotope is heavier. An identical force constant would therefore lead to a lower modeled C–D stretching frequency.

Independent check: μ_CD≈1.7143 is less than smaller partner mass 2.

Common mistake: The force constant was assumed comparable; it was not derived from neutron count.

Worked example 17. C–D to C–H frequency ratio

Problem: Using μ_CH=12/13 and μ_CD=12/7, approximate ν_CD/ν_CH.

Complete scientific reasoning: For a harmonic oscillator with similar k, frequency is proportional to 1/√μ. The ratio ν_CD/ν_CH=√[(12/13)/(12/7)]=√(7/13)≈0.734. This predicts a lower deuterated stretching frequency in the simple diatomic-model approximation.

Independent check: Squared ratio ≈7/13≈0.53846.

Common mistake: Do not use √2 in the wrong direction; heavier isotope generally lowers frequency.

Worked example 18. Zero-point energy comparison

Problem: Assuming a harmonic mode ν_H = 3000 cm⁻¹ and ν_D = 2200 cm⁻¹, compare their zero-point energies.

Complete scientific reasoning: For a harmonic vibration E₀ = ½hν. Under comparable conversion from wavenumber to frequency, ratio E₀,D/E₀,H equals 2200/3000≈0.733. Therefore the heavier-isotope mode has lower zero-point energy. Actual molecular vibrations may be coupled and are not exactly harmonic.

Independent check: Ratio is less than one because ν_D < ν_H.

Common mistake: Zero-point energy is not zero at the vibrational ground state.

Worked example 19. Kinetic isotope effect, normal

Problem: Matched H- and D-reactions have k_H=6 s⁻¹ and k_D=2 s⁻¹. Find KIE.

Complete scientific reasoning: With the specified convention KIE=k_H/k_D=6/2=3. The H reaction is three times as fast for these measured rate constants. The value is consistent with an isotope-sensitive step but is insufficient alone to identify exact transition-state geometry or which coupled step controls kinetics.

Independent check: Rates have matching units, so their ratio is dimensionless.

Common mistake: A normal isotope effect is not automatically proof of C–H bond cleavage.

Worked example 20. Kinetic isotope effect, inverse

Problem: Matched rates are k_H=0.8 min⁻¹ and k_D=1.0 min⁻¹. Interpret ratio.

Complete scientific reasoning: KIE=k_H/k_D=0.8/1.0=0.8, which is below one. Under this convention it is an inverse kinetic isotope effect: the D-labelled reaction shows higher measured rate than the H-labelled reaction. Mechanistic interpretation needs independent chemistry and comparable conditions.

Independent check: k_H

Common mistake: Not every hydrogen isotope substitution slows a reaction.

Part II — Twenty advanced worked examples on D/H, water isotopologues, tritium decay and biological inference

Worked example 21. A two-isotope hydrogen mean

Problem: Assume a sample has 90% ¹H and 10% ²H with neutral atomic masses 1.007825 and 2.014102 u. Calculate its illustrative mean hydrogen atomic mass.

Detailed solution: Use atom fractions and the actual masses supplied. Mean = 0.90×1.007825 + 0.10×2.014102 = 0.9070425 + 0.2014102 = 1.1084527 u. This strongly enriched sample is not an example of natural hydrogen abundance.

Cross-check: The mean must lie between 1.007825 and 2.014102 u.

Exam pitfall: A weighted mean is not the mass of one hydrogen nucleus.

Worked example 22. D/H ratio from atom counts

Problem: There are 9990 ¹H atoms and 10 ²H atoms. Find deuterium atom fraction and D/H.

Detailed solution: Total hydrogen atom count is 10000, so x_D=10/10000=0.001 or 0.1%. The D/H ratio divides by protium only: R=10/9990≈0.001001001. The values differ because their denominators are not identical.

Cross-check: R/(1+R) reproduces x_D=0.001 for a two-isotope mixture.

Exam pitfall: Do not use deuterium fraction as the D/H ratio without conversion.

Worked example 23. Convert a D/H ratio to atom fraction

Problem: A model water sample has D/H ratio R=1/999. What is x_D?

Detailed solution: In the two-stable-isotope approximation x_D=R/(1+R). Substitute (1/999)/(1+1/999)=1/1000=0.001. That is a 0.1% deuterium fraction for the specified artificial example.

Cross-check: D fraction plus protium fraction equals one.

Exam pitfall: The ratio and fraction are only approximately equal when R is very small.

Worked example 24. Reference-relative δ²H

Problem: A sample's D/H ratio is 0.95 times the reference ratio. Calculate δ²H.

Detailed solution: δ²H = (R_sample/R_ref − 1)×1000‰. Substitute 0.95 to get (0.95−1)×1000=−50‰. The sample is depleted in D relative to that particular reference by 5% in ratio terms.

Cross-check: −50 per mille corresponds to −0.05 of the reference ratio.

Exam pitfall: A negative isotope delta does not imply a negative number of deuterium nuclei.

Worked example 25. Slight enrichment δ²H

Problem: The sample ratio is 1.002 times the reference. Find δ²H.

Detailed solution: δ²H=(1.002−1)×1000=+2‰. It means the D/H ratio is 0.2% higher than the reference, because two per mille equals 0.2 percent. Real reporting needs the named isotope reference scale.

Cross-check: 0.002×1000=2 and 0.002×100=0.2%.

Exam pitfall: Do not confuse per mille and percent.

Worked example 26. Ideal water isotopologues at 10% D

Problem: In a simplified two-hydrogen-site water model with D probability p=0.10, calculate H₂O, HDO and D₂O percentages.

Detailed solution: The no-D fraction is (1−p)²=0.81; exactly one D is 2p(1−p)=0.18; two D is p²=0.01. Therefore H₂O 81%, HDO 18%, D₂O 1% under independent-site assumptions. This is deliberately enriched teaching water, not a natural abundance claim.

Cross-check: 0.81+0.18+0.01=1.

Exam pitfall: Do not forget the two arrangements for HDO.

Worked example 27. One D at p=0.02

Problem: Under the same model p=0.02, how common is HDO?

Detailed solution: P(HDO)=2p(1−p)=2×0.02×0.98=0.0392, or 3.92% of molecules. This molecular percentage differs from the hydrogen-site atom fraction p=2%. The model assumes independent equivalently sampled sites.

Cross-check: The probability is below 2p=0.04 because the other site must be H.

Exam pitfall: Molecular isotopologue percentage is not identical to atom-level enrichment.

Worked example 28. Infer D fraction from D₂O fraction

Problem: An ideal two-site random mixture has P(D₂O)=0.04. What is p?

Detailed solution: The double-heavy probability is p². Solve p²=0.04; since 0≤p≤1, p=√0.04=0.20. Then HDO fraction would be 2×0.2×0.8=0.32 if the same independent model holds.

Cross-check: P(H₂O)=0.8²=0.64; all categories 0.64+0.32+0.04=1.

Exam pitfall: The positive square root is the physically meaningful probability.

Worked example 29. Tritium after one half-life

Problem: An ideal isolated population starts with 10000 tritium nuclei. Expected remainder after 12.32 years?

Detailed solution: Use N=N₀(1/2)^(t/t½). At t=t½, remaining expected N=10000×0.5=5000 tritium nuclei. Approximately five thousand daughter helium-3 nuclei would be produced by decays in this ideal no-loss model, assuming none were initially present.

Cross-check: 5000/10000=0.5.

Exam pitfall: Half-life does not mean one atom itself becomes half an atom.

Worked example 30. Tritium after two half-lives

Problem: What expected number remains from the same 10000 nuclei after 24.64 years?

Detailed solution: 24.64/12.32=2 half-lives. Expected fraction (1/2)²=0.25, so N=2500. The expected number decayed is 7500. Tritium beta decay changes the parent to helium-3.

Cross-check: 2500+7500=10000 in the ideal parent/daughter bookkeeping.

Exam pitfall: Do not subtract a fixed 5000 nuclei at each half-life.

Worked example 31. Tritium after three half-lives

Problem: What fraction and count remain after 36.96 years from 10000 nuclei?

Detailed solution: 36.96/12.32=3. Thus remaining fraction is 1/8=12.5%, corresponding to expected 1250 parent tritium nuclei. About 8750 would have transformed in the ideal population model.

Cross-check: One-eighth of 10000 is 1250.

Exam pitfall: The fraction is statistical expectation rather than exact for every small population.

Worked example 32. Half a tritium half-life

Problem: At 6.16 years with t½=12.32 years, what ideal fraction remains?

Detailed solution: Time is 0.5 half-life, so N/N₀=(1/2)^0.5=1/√2≈0.7071, or 70.71%. Exponential decay is continuous; half of a half-life is not synonymous with a quarter of initial nuclei decayed.

Cross-check: After 12.32 years the fraction would fall further to 0.5.

Exam pitfall: Do not linearly interpolate from 100% to 50% and assume 75%.

Worked example 33. Calculate tritium decay constant

Problem: For half-life 12.32 years, compute λ in per year.

Detailed solution: λ=ln2/t½≈0.693147/12.32≈0.05626 year⁻¹. The decay constant represents an exponential-rate parameter, not a probability greater than one or an amount of tritium. It is valid for the stated half-life convention.

Cross-check: λ×t½≈0.6931.

Exam pitfall: Do not divide half-life by ln2; the correct direction is ln2/t½.

Worked example 34. A larger parent-population example

Problem: Initial tritium count is 20000 nuclei. Expected survivors after 24.64 years?

Detailed solution: Two half-lives have passed, so fraction remaining is 1/4. N=20000/4=5000. The result depends on the half-life count, not the numeric size of one tritium isotope mass number.

Cross-check: 5000/20000=0.25.

Exam pitfall: The initial population is not reduced by the same absolute number each half-life.

Worked example 35. Find time from a 10% remaining fraction

Problem: For tritium half-life 12.32 years, when is N/N₀=0.10 in the ideal model?

Detailed solution: Set (1/2)^(t/12.32)=0.10. Taking logarithms gives t=12.32×ln(0.10)/ln(0.5)=12.32×log₂(10)≈12.32×3.321928≈40.93 years. This is an ideal expected population calculation, not an environmental sample age without source history.

Cross-check: 3.322 half-lives gives roughly one tenth remaining.

Exam pitfall: Do not assume 10% remaining means 90% of one half-life.

Worked example 36. Tritium units from a ratio

Problem: A water record indicates three ³H atoms per 10¹⁸ hydrogen atoms. Express in TU.

Detailed solution: By definition one TU is one tritium atom per 10¹⁸ hydrogen atoms. Therefore this ratio is 3 TU. The dimensionless isotope ratio notation is different from activity units such as becquerels per volume.

Cross-check: 3/10¹⁸ divided by 1/10¹⁸ gives 3.

Exam pitfall: Do not equate 3 TU automatically with 3 Bq per liter.

Worked example 37. TU count in a hypothetical population

Problem: At 2 TU, what number of ³H atoms is expected in a sample containing 5×10¹⁸ hydrogen atoms?

Detailed solution: 2 TU represents 2 tritium atoms per 10¹⁸ hydrogen atoms. For five such blocks, expected count is 2×5=10 tritium atoms. For a tiny population, actual counts are discrete, but the ratio-based bookkeeping is simple.

Cross-check: Ten tritium atoms divided by 5×10¹⁸ total hydrogen gives 2×10⁻¹⁸, or 2 TU.

Exam pitfall: TU is a reference ratio, not a guarantee of a particular sample atom count.

Worked example 38. Nuclear spin comparison

Problem: Which of ¹H, ²H and ³H have nuclear spin 1/2?

Detailed solution: Protium ¹H and tritium ³H have nuclear spin one-half; deuterium ²H has nuclear spin one. This difference affects magnetic resonance behavior. The spin assignments are experimental nuclear properties, not inferable from A alone without nuclear theory/data.

Cross-check: All retain Z=1 despite different nuclear spins.

Exam pitfall: Do not assume all isotopes of one element have the same nuclear spin.

Worked example 39. Deuterium MS shift at z=+2

Problem: For one H→D substitution in a comparable +2 molecular ion, approximate m/z spacing.

Detailed solution: The neutral atomic mass substitution difference is about 1.006277 u. Divide by charge magnitude two to get Δ(m/z)≈0.5031385, assuming comparable ion chemistry and isotope positions. The isotope masses themselves did not become half as large.

Cross-check: 0.5031385×2≈1.006277.

Exam pitfall: Do not multiply the mass shift by two because the ion is doubly charged.

Worked example 40. A tracer does not establish synthesis rate

Problem: A protein contains deuterium after exposure to a labelled precursor. Can synthesis rate be computed from presence alone?

Detailed solution: No. The observation indicates isotope-label incorporation or exchange under the design. A synthesis or turnover estimate requires precursor enrichment, time, product patterns, exchangeable-site assessment and an appropriate kinetic model. The correct conclusion states the missing information rather than inventing a rate.

Cross-check: No explicit concentration-time or enrichment model data were supplied.

Exam pitfall: Do not equate mere label detection with an absolute biological flux.

1.A.1.18.34 Thirty misconception repairs: identify the exact confusion

This clinic distinguishes isotope mass from mass number, hydrogen nuclei from ions, stable from radioactive labels, fraction from ratio, kinetic from equilibrium isotope effects, and a tracer observation from a model-dependent biological conclusion.

1.A.1.18.36 Master revision: formula map, definitions and the route to Node 19

Core facts to retain:
  1. Protium ¹H: 1 proton, 0 neutrons, stable.
  2. Deuterium ²H / D: 1 proton, 1 neutron, stable.
  3. Tritium ³H / T: 1 proton, 2 neutrons, radioactive.
  4. All neutral hydrogen isotopes have one electron; ionization does not change nuclide identity.
  5. Mass number A counts nucleons; actual isotope masses differ from integers.
  6. Tritium beta-minus decays to helium-3, with half-life ≈12.32 years.
  7. D/H isotope ratios, δ²H and deuterium enrichment are sample metrics, not attributes of one nucleus.
  8. Hydrogen isotope effects arise from nuclear mass-dependent physics and can change reaction rates or equilibria.

A three-stage revision plan

Stage one: decode every H/D/T symbol and molecule on sight. Stage two: calculate reduced masses, isotope fractions, half-life populations and rate ratios without notes. Stage three: explain why a measured deuterium label in a biological product does not automatically prove one unique mechanism or turnover rate.

Glossary

Protium: ¹H. Deuterium: ²H, D. Deuteron: deuterium nucleus. Tritium: ³H, T. Triton: tritium nucleus. Isotopologue: molecule with a specified isotope composition. Isotope fraction: proportion of atoms in a sample that carry a nuclide. D/H: deuterium-to-protium ratio. δ²H: reference-relative ratio expressed in per mille. Zero-point energy: lowest vibrational energy. KIE: kinetic isotope effect. TU: tritium unit. Beta-minus decay: neutron-to-proton transformation with electron and antineutrino emission.
