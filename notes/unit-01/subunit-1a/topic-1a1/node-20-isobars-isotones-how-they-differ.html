1.A.1.20.1 The nuclear detective: two atoms with the same number but different identities

Imagine a nuclear-science student standing at a table with cards marked carbon-14, nitrogen-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16. Every card contains an element symbol and a mass number. Two cards display the number fourteen, but they are not the same element. A different pair share eight neutrons although their mass numbers differ. This puzzle introduces two powerful relationships: isobars and isotones.

The first investigation

Read ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N. Both contain fourteen nucleons, but carbon has six nuclear protons whereas nitrogen has seven. Their neutron counts are eight and seven. They are isobars. Now compare ¹⁴₆C with ¹⁵₇N: each has eight neutrons, although their total nucleon counts differ. They are isotones.

Why this is more than a vocabulary exercise

The distinction connects the periodic table, nuclear decay, the chart of nuclides, nuclear medicine, geochronology and mass spectrometry. It also prevents false conclusions: two isobars need not have identical measured atomic masses or similar chemical properties, and two isotones need not be equally stable.

How we will study it

Follow the chronological development of nuclides, learn the algebra before the examples, visualize families on an N–Z chart, examine beta-decay transformations, and then solve research-style problems. Our study rules are to state what is known, calculate what follows and explicitly identify what requires experimental nuclear or biological data.

Learning outcomes: Decode nuclide notation; distinguish isotopes, isobars and isotones by Z, A and N; build and classify nuclear families; reason through beta-minus, beta-plus, electron capture and gamma transitions; explain nuclear-isomer and isoelectronic distinctions; solve mass, charge, decay and nuclear-medicine interpretation problems; avoid overclaiming biological similarity.

1.A.1.20.2 Historical development: why atomic mass could not identify an element by itself

Dalton and early chemical reasoning

Classical chemical atomism linked each element with its own type of atom and characteristic mass. This was productive for explaining fixed chemical combining proportions, but later experiments revealed atoms of the same element could have different nuclear masses. Chemical identity and measured mass had to become separate ideas.

Nuclear discoveries changed the question

Electron research, Rutherford's nuclear model and Moseley's atomic-number work clarified that an element is identified by nuclear charge. Neutrons later explained the difference between mass number and proton number. These developments created a consistent language for nuclides with equal A but different Z and for nuclides with equal N but different Z.

Isotopes and the wider classification

The term isotope emerged from work on nuclear species occupying the same periodic-table place. The complementary relations isobar and isotone describe different coordinate matches on the nuclide chart. Modern standardized definitions are useful because informal ‘same mass’ language can otherwise confuse integer mass number with measured mass.

Modern measurements and transformations

Mass spectrometry distinguishes atomic or molecular ions by mass-to-charge properties; nuclear experiments determine stability and decay. A comparison between ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N is an isobaric classification whether or not a particular instrument separates their exact masses. Nuclear transformations give the relationship a deeper physical role.

1.A.1.20.3 Nuclide notation: read the symbol before making a classification

One nuclide is identified by its nuclear composition

In ᴬ_ZX, X is an element symbol, Z is the number of protons and A the number of total nucleons. The neutron number is N = A − Z. Atomic number is not a variable that can be chosen independently of a valid element symbol: if Z=6, the element is carbon.

Electron count is a different axis

For a neutral atom, electrons equal proton count Z; for a monatomic ion of charge q, electrons equal Z−q. This affects chemical charge, not the nuclear isotope classification. A calcium-40 cation and a neutral calcium-40 atom are the same nuclide in different electronic states.

Classify pairs, not isolated nuclei

One nuclide alone is not ‘an isobar’ without context specifying another nuclide with the same A. Likewise an isotone relationship compares at least two different nuclear species with the same N. One element contains isotope families if its nuclides have different A.

Do not substitute measured mass for A

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 are isobars because their integer mass number is fourteen. Their physical neutral-atomic masses are not necessarily equal; isotope stability, binding and electrons affect measured values.

P = Z;   N = A − Z;   A = Z + N;   neutral electrons = Z

1.A.1.20.4 Isotopes: the earlier concept that anchors the contrast

IUPAC definition

Isotopes are nuclides with the same atomic number and different mass numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 share Z=6 but have N=6 and N=7. Consequently they are isotopes of carbon, not different elements.

Chemistry and physical isotope effects

Neutral isotopes of an element generally have the same electron count and closely related electronic structure, but measured masses and nuclear properties can differ. This allows isotope fractionation, spectroscopy and sometimes differences in reaction rates. Their chemical behavior is not always numerically identical.

An isotope pair does not automatically form an isobar pair

Different mass numbers are required for distinct isotopes under the standard definition; equal mass number defines isobars only when nuclear species differ in Z. Thus ¹²C and ¹³C cannot be isobars.

A nuclear isomer is not simply another isotope

Two states such as ⁹⁹Tc and ⁹⁹ᵐTc have identical Z and A but different nuclear energy states. The metastable m indicates a nuclear isomeric state, not another neutron or an ordinary charge state.

1.A.1.20.5 Isobars: equal mass number, different nuclear charges

The rigorous definition

In nuclear terminology, different nuclides of equal mass number are called isobars. With different element identity, their Z values differ; because A=Z+N remains constant, the neutron counts must differ in the opposite direction. For example ¹⁴C has Z=6,N=8 and ¹⁴N has Z=7,N=7.

A mass number is not the same as measured neutral atomic mass

The phrase ‘same mass number’ means both nuclei contain the same total number of nucleons. Nuclear binding energies differ and measured isotope masses differ. Isobars can therefore have distinct masses even though both carry the integer label A=14.

A surprising biological connection

Carbon-14 beta-decays to nitrogen-14. The element changes, but A remains fourteen. The parent and daughter are isobars. This is relevant to understanding radiocarbon physics, although the isobar relationship alone does not supply radiocarbon age information.

Think of A as a constant-sum line

For a fixed A, if Z rises by one, N must fall by one. That makes isobaric neighbors natural participants in weak nuclear transformations such as beta-minus or electron capture when energetically allowed.

Isobars: A₁ = A₂, Z₁ ≠ Z₂;   then N₁ ≠ N₂

1.A.1.20.6 Isotones: equal neutron number, different proton numbers

The IUPAC definition

Isotones are nuclides having equal neutron number N but different atomic numbers Z. An easy example is ¹⁴C (Z=6,N=8), ¹⁵N (Z=7,N=8) and ¹⁶O (Z=8,N=8). These are different elements that share eight neutrons.

Why their mass numbers differ

If N is fixed and Z increases, total mass number A=Z+N also increases. Thus the sequence carbon-14, nitrogen-15, oxygen-16 has mass numbers 14,15,16 and proton numbers 6,7,8, but N=8 throughout.

Isotones are not ‘same chemistry’

Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen have different electronic structures as neutral atoms, different valences and different biochemical roles. Sharing eight neutrons creates a nuclear classification, not equivalent chemical behavior.

Neutron number cannot be judged from a label's last digit

Compare ²³Na and ²⁴Mg: sodium Z=11,N=12 and magnesium Z=12,N=12. They are isotones even though their mass numbers differ. A student must calculate A−Z rather than merely compare the written A values.

Isotones: N₁ = N₂;   Z₁ ≠ Z₂;   A₁ ≠ A₂

1.A.1.20.7 The three-way classification: an unambiguous exam decision method

The right order of operations

First read or derive Z for each nuclide. Next read A. Finally compute N=A−Z for each. If same Z with different A, classify isotopes. If same A with different Z, classify isobars. If same N with different Z, classify isotones. If none matches, say no such relationship among those three.

Examples show why the order matters

¹²C and ¹³C have equal Z and different A, so isotopes. ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N have equal A and differing Z, so isobars. ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N have neutron count eight, so isotones. ¹²C and ¹⁴N match none of these three because Z, A and N all differ (6 vs 7; 12 vs 14; 6 vs 7).

A pair of distinct nuclides cannot normally qualify as two of these relationships simultaneously

For two distinct nuclei, if A and Z both match, then N also matches, so they are the same nuclide (though possibly different nuclear states). If Z matches but A differs, N differs too; if A matches and Z differs, N differs; if N matches and Z differs, A differs. Algebra rules out the simultaneous isotope/isobar/isotone pair classifications of two distinct different-composition nuclei.

A complete answer names the matched variable

Instead of writing only ‘isotones’, write ‘isotones because N=8 for both, while Z differs’. That provides an independent calculation and reduces error risk.

1.A.1.20.8 The N–Z chart: visualize isotopes, isobars and isotones

A chart transforms three equations into geometry

A chart of nuclides can place neutron number N along the horizontal axis and proton number Z on the vertical axis. Then isotones share the same N coordinate and align vertically; isotopes share the same Z coordinate and align horizontally; isobars lie along diagonals where N+Z=A is constant.

The direction depends on the chosen axes

Some published nuclear charts place Z and N on opposite axes. The invariant idea is which coordinate is held constant, not whether a family happens to look like a horizontal or vertical line in a particular diagram.

A diagram is a reasoning tool, not the actual nuclear stability map

A pedagogical grid can include only selected example nuclides; actual charts contain many nuclear species with measured stability boundaries. The graphic below illustrates relations and should not be used as a complete classification of natural nuclides.

1.A.1.20.9 Isobar family with A = 14: carbon and nitrogen

Read the actual nuclei

Carbon-14 has Z=6 and N=8; nitrogen-14 has Z=7 and N=7. Their common mass number is A=14, so they are isobars. These two differ in elemental chemical identity, nuclear stability and neutral electronic structure.

The decay link gives a memorable narrative

Carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14. A neutron converts into a proton and the total nucleon count remains fourteen. Emission of an electron and an electron antineutrino accompanies the nuclear transformation. The relationship is isobaric, not isotopic.

One member may be stable while another is radioactive

Nitrogen-14 is stable, whereas carbon-14 is radioactive. Equal A therefore does not imply equal stability. Which decay is energetically possible depends on actual nuclear masses and energy levels, not merely the equality of mass number.

Biological significance lies in the distinct element and isotope properties

Carbon-14 is used in radiocarbon contexts and some research applications; nitrogen-14 is the predominant ordinary nitrogen nuclide in many samples. The term isobar explains the nuclear relation, not similarity of biological functions.

¹⁴₆C (P=6, N=8) → ¹⁴₇N (P=7, N=7) + β⁻ + antineutrino

1.A.1.20.10 Isotone family with N = 8: carbon-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16

Find the hidden invariant

Carbon-14: 14−6=8. Nitrogen-15: 15−7=8. Oxygen-16: 16−8=8. All three have eight neutrons, despite having different proton numbers and mass numbers. They form an isotone family.

Contrast stability

Carbon-14 is radioactive, but nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. A fixed neutron count does not determine whether all isotones share stability. Nuclear binding depends on the entire proton–neutron system and its quantum energy configuration.

Why eight is historically significant in nuclear structure

Neutron number eight is one of the familiar magic numbers in the nuclear shell model. Shell closure can influence stability patterns, but it is not a universal guarantee that every nucleus with N=8 is stable. This nuanced example avoids simplistic magic-number rules.

Avoid transferring chemical properties across an isotone family

Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen differ in chemistry and in their biological roles in proteins, nucleic acids, respiration and photosynthesis. Sharing N=8 does not make them interchangeable atoms in metabolism.

1.A.1.20.11 Isobar family A = 40: potassium, argon and calcium

Three different chemical elements share the number forty

Argon-40 has Z=18,N=22; potassium-40 has Z=19,N=21; calcium-40 has Z=20,N=20. All have A=40, so they are isobars. They are emphatically not isotopes of one element, and their neutral atom electron counts are 18,19,20.

Potassium-40 links a biological element with nuclear decay

Potassium is an essential biological element. A small naturally occurring portion is the radioactive isotope ⁴⁰K. Nuclear decay pathways include beta-minus transformation to ⁴⁰Ca and electron-capture transformation to ⁴⁰Ar, though their respective branching and detailed energetics require nuclear data.

Argon is a noble gas and calcium is a metal

Even with equal A, argon-40 and calcium-40 differ sharply in chemical behavior because their proton numbers and electronic structures differ. Equal integer nucleon count does not mean a similar Lewis structure, oxidation state or biological function.

Earth-science connection

Potassium–argon and argon–argon geochronology exploit radioactive potassium/argon-related nuclear history. This is conceptually distinct from the radiocarbon age of organic materials, and real dating needs analytical and geological assumptions.

1.A.1.20.12 Isotone family with N = 12: sodium-23 and magnesium-24

Calculate rather than memorize

Sodium-23 has Z=11, so N=23−11=12. Magnesium-24 has Z=12, so N=24−12=12. They are isotones because neutron counts match while atomic numbers differ.

A second useful relationship appears only for specified ions

Neutral ²³Na has eleven electrons and neutral ²⁴Mg has twelve, so they are not isoelectronic as neutral atoms. But Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each have ten electrons. These ions can be both isotonic in their nuclear composition and isoelectronic in their electronic count—two independent relations.

Do not conflate nuclei and ions

The words isotone and isoelectronic refer to different information. A nuclear isotope label remains the same after electron loss. A student should calculate N and electron count separately, particularly if the question supplies ionic charge.

²³Na: N=23−11=12; ²⁴Mg: N=24−12=12; Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ both have 10 electrons.

1.A.1.20.13 Isotope versus isobar versus isotone as mathematical constraints

Three variables, only two independent

The identity A=Z+N means knowing any two of A,Z,N determines the third. This prevents physically impossible combinations. For instance if two nuclei have A equal and Z different, then N must differ by exactly the negative of the Z difference.

If ΔA=0, then ΔN=−ΔZ

This is the isobaric condition. A beta-minus transformation changes Z by +1 and N by −1, so A is constant. That is why certain beta-decay parent/daughter nuclides are isobars.

If ΔN=0, then ΔA=ΔZ

This is the isotonic condition. Along an N-constant family, increasing Z by one also increases A by one. The carbon-14 → nitrogen-15 → oxygen-16 isotone sequence makes this equation visible.

If ΔZ=0, then ΔA=ΔN

This is the isotope condition. Carbon-12 → carbon-13 changes neutron count and mass number by one at fixed atomic number.

ΔA = ΔZ + ΔN;   isobars ΔA=0;   isotones ΔN=0;   isotopes ΔZ=0 (for distinct A).

1.A.1.20.14 Why isobars do not have equal exact atomic masses

Mass number A counts particles, not kilograms or atomic mass units

Nuclear mass and neutral atomic mass reflect proton, neutron and electron masses as well as nuclear binding energy. A=40 says forty nucleons in each of ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca; it does not say those atoms weigh exactly 40.000000 u or precisely the same measured mass.

Binding energy creates mass differences

In simple nuclear physics the mass defect is related to energy by E=Δmc². Two isobars can have different binding energies because their proton/neutron compositions differ. Their decay energetics depend on actual masses and accessible states.

Example without fabricated precision

The neutral atomic masses of ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N differ, and that difference permits carbon-14 beta-minus decay. A reliable computation of the Q-value must use evaluated atomic or nuclear masses with appropriate electron-mass bookkeeping. The integer A labels alone cannot provide the emitted energy.

Mass spectra may separate equal-A atomic species at high resolution

Nominal A matches are one possible source of isobaric interference in mass spectrometry, but ion charge, chemical interferences and instrumental resolution matter. For ion analysis, a mass-to-charge ratio is not identical to the integer mass number.

E = Δm c² (mass–energy relation);   equality of A does not imply equality of measured mass.

1.A.1.20.15 Beta-minus decay: an isobaric transformation

A neutron transforms within the nucleus

In beta-minus decay, a neutron becomes a proton, accompanied by emission of an electron and electron antineutrino. Thus Z increases by one, N decreases by one, and A remains unchanged. The parent and daughter have different element identities but share A.

Carbon-14 to nitrogen-14

The parent ¹⁴C has P=6,N=8, while the daughter ¹⁴N has P=7,N=7. Nuclear charge changes and emitted beta charge maintains the total electric-charge balance. The relation is isobaric.

Potassium-40 to calcium-40

A beta-minus branch of potassium-40 produces calcium-40. Again A stays forty while Z increases from nineteen to twenty. Nuclear and electron-antineutrino details are part of a complete nuclear reaction; merely moving an orbital electron is not the process.

The possibility of decay requires energetics

Algebra predicts which daughter Z and A follow beta-minus emission, but it does not tell you that a particular nuclide actually decays in this way. Whether the channel is open depends on nuclear energetics and the measured decay scheme.

β⁻: (A,Z,N) → (A,Z+1,N−1) + e⁻ + antineutrino

1.A.1.20.16 Beta-plus decay and electron capture: opposite direction across an A family

In beta-plus decay a proton converts to a neutron

A parent emits a positron and electron neutrino, decreasing nuclear Z by one and increasing N by one while A remains constant. Therefore an energetically allowed beta-plus parent and daughter are isobars.

Electron capture uses an atomic electron

In electron capture, a proton captures an electron and transforms into a neutron, with an electron neutrino emitted. The nucleus changes Z→Z−1, N→N+1, A unchanged. The captured electron is part of the atomic environment; the element changes, even though the mass number stays fixed.

Examples need the actual decay route

Beryllium-7 can decay to lithium-7 by electron capture, giving another isobaric parent–daughter pair. However one must not assume every nuclide with an isobaric partner undergoes positron emission or capture. Actual branching and rates come from nuclear data.

Radioactive pathways are not a classroom recipe

These are conceptual descriptions of nuclear transformations. Medical or laboratory radionuclide handling and production require professional facilities and regulatory controls, not instructions in a general teaching note.

β⁺: (A,Z,N) → (A,Z−1,N+1) + e⁺ + neutrino;   EC: (A,Z) + e⁻ → (A,Z−1) + neutrino

1.A.1.20.17 Gamma emission and nuclear isomers: neither an ordinary isotope nor a new isobar

Gamma rays change nuclear excitation energy, not nucleon composition

During ordinary gamma de-excitation, a nucleus releases electromagnetic radiation while keeping Z, N and A unchanged. The resulting nucleus remains the same nuclide in a lower energy state.

The superscript m means metastable nuclear state

Technetium-99m has the same Z=43 and A=99 as technetium-99, but is in a different nuclear energy state. Its transition toward the ground state can emit gamma radiation or undergo internal conversion pathways. It is not a separate isotope with another neutron.

An important nested relationship

Molybdenum-99 and technetium-99m have A=99 but Z=42 versus 43, so they are isobars. Technetium-99m and technetium-99 are nuclear isomers with the same A and Z. One sequence therefore illustrates two different kinds of relationship that must not be confused.

Isomer versus isobar in the exam

The letter m after mass number is not an ordinary molecular mass, proton count, electron charge, or definition of a ‘radioactive isotope’ in general. It indicates an excited nuclear energy state that can persist measurably.

⁹⁹Mo (Z=42) → ⁹⁹ᵐTc (Z=43) isobaric daughter; ⁹⁹ᵐTc → ⁹⁹Tc is isomeric transition.

1.A.1.20.18 The chart-of-nuclides geometry under beta and gamma transformations

Beta-minus moves diagonally in N–Z coordinates

If Z is vertical and N is horizontal, beta-minus raises Z by one and lowers N by one. Therefore it moves to a different point along the same A=N+Z diagonal. The parent/daughter A remains fixed, so the path is isobaric.

Beta-plus and electron capture move the other diagonal way

Both decrease Z by one and increase N by one, again along a constant-A line. The detailed weak-interaction products differ, but the family relation of the nuclei stays isobaric.

Gamma transition stays at the same coordinate

Because Z and N are unchanged, a gamma nuclear transition does not move the nuclide to a new position on the basic chart. Energy-state information requires a separate layer beyond just coordinates.

1.A.1.20.19 Nuclear stability: mass number alone cannot predict it

Equal A can contain both stable and unstable nuclides

For A=14, carbon-14 is radioactive and nitrogen-14 is stable. For A=40, potassium-40 is radioactive while argon-40 and calcium-40 are stable. So isobaric partners can show dramatically different half-lives or stable behavior.

Equal N does not guarantee equal stability

For N=8, carbon-14 is radioactive but nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. Nuclear stability reflects proton–neutron interactions, binding energy, nuclear level structure and decay energetics, not simply an equal neutron count.

The stable nuclei occupy a region of the N–Z chart

The balance of neutrons and protons that supports stability changes across the periodic table. Very light stable nuclides often have roughly similar N and Z; for heavier nuclei, stable neutron numbers commonly exceed proton numbers. This is a tendency rather than a universal arithmetic test.

Nuclear shell closures are additional clues

The shell-model magic numbers for protons or neutrons can contribute to greater stability in some nuclei. Yet a magic neutron count alone cannot certify a nuclide as stable. Use actual evaluated nuclear data for specific decay claims.

Exam checkpoint: ‘isobar’, ‘isotone’ and ‘isotope’ classify nuclear counts. None of these names alone proves that a nuclide is stable, radioactive, biologically useful, or safe.

1.A.1.20.20 The nuclear binding-energy picture behind isobars

The binding energy is not identical for every nucleus of mass number A

The balance of strong nuclear forces, Coulomb repulsion, asymmetry and pairing contributions influences nuclear binding. Isobars have the same nucleon total but different proton/neutron partitions, so their binding energies and masses can differ.

The mass parabola as a qualitative model

For a fixed A, simple semi-empirical nuclear mass models often represent mass versus Z by a parabola-like curve, possibly with pairing-dependent branches. Beta decay can sometimes move a nucleus toward energetically favored isobars. The exact scheme requires measured masses and allowed decay channels.

Not every isobaric neighbor can beta-decay

Energy conservation, quantum selection rules and nuclear states influence whether a given transition occurs. Merely writing ‘A fixed and Z changes by one’ gives a possible daughter identity but does not prove a spontaneous decay or calculate a half-life.

This is advanced context, not a calculation from A alone

A complete Q-value calculation must use actual evaluated atomic or nuclear masses and correct treatment of emitted leptons. Avoid inventing precise nuclear energies where only mass-number labels are provided.

For fixed A, a qualitative model uses nuclear mass M(A,Z) as a function of Z; the minimum generally relates to favored isobars.

1.A.1.20.21 Isobaric analog states: same A is a starting point, not the full story

An advanced nuclear-structure idea

Isobaric analog states describe related nuclear states in different isobaric nuclides connected through approximate isospin symmetry, where proton and neutron can be treated as related nucleon states in nuclear interactions. The concept is not equivalent to saying all isobars have identical spin or energy.

Why nuclear physicists compare isobaric systems

Such comparisons can help investigate strong-interaction properties, Coulomb effects and selection rules across nuclei sharing A. Yet real Coulomb forces and neutron/proton mass differences break perfect symmetry, so energy levels need not be equal.

What the life-science student should retain

For an introductory CSIR-NET life-science syllabus, correctly classify isobars and understand beta transformations first. Isobaric analog states provide an optional deeper nuclear-structure connection rather than a requirement to solve ordinary isotope-count numericals.

1.A.1.20.22 Mass spectrometry: nominal isobaric interference versus exact mass

Mass spectrometry sees m/z, not just printed A

Two atomic ions associated with different nuclides can share nominal mass number and similar m/z. Depending on resolving power, they may interfere or be separated. Their different exact isotope masses and different chemical species matter.

Argon-40 and calcium-40 illustrate a recognized issue

Argon-based ICP-MS can face interference from ⁴⁰Ar⁺ in measuring ⁴⁰Ca⁺ because both lie near nominal m/z 40. These are isobars by A=40 and distinct elements. Instrument designs may use higher resolution, collision/reaction technology or alternate isotopes and corrections.

Do not equate interference with indistinguishable atoms

An isotope label is not destroyed by an interfering ion. The issue is separation and attribution of instrument signals. A reported mass spectrum should state the charge state, formula, resolution and corrections where relevant.

Molecular polyatomic interferences are a related but distinct category

Ions built from several atoms can coincide in nominal mass with a single atomic ion. That is an analytical mass interference, but the molecules are not necessarily a pair of nuclear isobars: the term ‘isobaric interference’ is broader in instrumental contexts.

Measured spacing Δ(m/z) ≈ Δm / |z| for comparable ion charge states; integer A is not an m/z measurement.

1.A.1.20.23 Isotones and the science of nuclear shell patterns

Fix N and compare how proton number changes binding

Along an isotone chain, nuclear neutron count is constant while Z and A rise together. Such comparisons can reveal nuclear structure changes and shell effects across different elements. The shared neutrons do not imply identical nuclear excited states.

An N=8 family makes the limitations concrete

Carbon-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 all have eight neutrons. Yet carbon-14 radioactivity differs from the stability of nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16. Proton number affects Coulomb energy, nuclear state structure and allowed transformations.

An N=20 illustrative relationship

Calcium-40 has N=20 and potassium-39 has Z=19,N=20. Both form an isotone pair, with different elements and mass numbers. This also illustrates a commonly discussed shell closure, but it is not a rule that every possible N=20 nuclide is equally stable.

Nuclear categories cannot substitute for biology

An organism may contain both potassium and calcium compounds, but the presence of a fixed-neutron isotone relation among particular nuclei gives no immediate prediction of uptake, ionic function, toxicity or metabolism.

1.A.1.20.24 Isoelectronic, isodiaphers and nuclear isomers: nearby terms

Isoelectronic is an electronic description

Species are isoelectronic when they possess the same electron count (and sometimes an analogous electronic configuration, depending on the context). Neon, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each have ten electrons. Their nuclei have different proton counts; some specified isotopic choices can additionally form isotone pairs, but this is a separate calculation.

Isodiaphers share neutron excess N−Z

For deeper nuclear-chart terminology, isodiaphers are nuclides with equal N−Z, also called the neutron excess or isotopic difference. This is another line on an N–Z chart, distinct from equal A, equal N and equal Z. For example ¹⁴C has N−Z=2, and ¹⁸O has N−Z=2; they share neutron excess but do not form an isotope, isobar or isotone pair.

Nuclear isomers share the same Z, N and A but differ in energy state

⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc illustrate isomerism. Their metastable distinction is nuclear excitation. They are not different isotopes under the usual different-A definition, and ionization would not change their nuclear isomer state by itself.

Isotonic solution is an unrelated biological word

An isotonic solution in physiology concerns effective osmotic pressure relative to a reference and cell water movement; it is not a solution made of nuclear isotones. Similar word roots can mislead students when nuclear and cell biology terminology mix.

1.A.1.20.25 Three-way worked classification before the advanced exercises

Example: ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C

Each is carbon Z=6 with A=12,13,14, giving N=6,7,8. They are isotope members of one element. Only one of this set is radioactive, showing that isotope status is independent of decay behavior.

Example: ¹⁴C, ¹⁴N

The A values match at 14 while Z differs 6 versus 7, so isobars. Neutron values 8 versus 7 establish they are not isotones. The radioactive parent–daughter link adds a physical story but is not required for classification.

Example: ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁶O

Neutron count is eight in each: (14−6), (15−7), (16−8). They are isotones of different elements. Mass numbers differ so they cannot be isobars.

Example: ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K, ⁴⁰Ca

All have A=40 so the family is isobaric. Their Z values are 18,19,20 and N values are 22,21,20. The element labels and physical behavior differ. This illustrates isobaric families with more than two members.

Example: ²³Na⁺ and ²⁴Mg²⁺

The nuclei are isotones (N=12 for both). Their specified ions have ten electrons each, so they are also isoelectronic as ions. One word classifies nuclear neutrons, the other electronic charge states.

1.A.1.20.26 Biological elements are not interchangeable because some nuclei are isotones

The biological boundary

Carbon-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 have the same neutron number eight, but carbon, nitrogen and oxygen occupy different chemical groups and have different roles in metabolism. Neutron equality is a nuclear relationship and does not imply similar valence electrons, cell transport or toxicity.

Nitrogen-15 stable tracing

Nitrogen-15 may be used to study nitrogen incorporation in amino acids and other biological pools. Its isotope label remains nitrogen through ordinary chemistry; its N=8 isotone oxygen-16 does not replace it during a biological reaction.

Sodium and magnesium

Sodium-23 and magnesium-24 share twelve neutrons but Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ play different roles in transport and enzymatic chemistry. In their specified ionic forms they also happen to have ten electrons each; this is an isoelectronic relation, not the reason their nuclei are isotones.

Inference discipline

Equal A or equal N never determines biochemical function, flux, safety, ionic charge or environmental abundance. Those properties need distinct chemical and biological measurements.

1.A.1.20.27 Molybdenum-99 to technetium-99m: an isobaric nuclear medicine story

The nuclear arithmetic

⁹⁹Mo has Z=42,N=57. ⁹⁹ᵐTc has Z=43,N=56. Both have A=99, so they are isobars; the daughter is in a metastable nuclear energy state denoted m.

Physical half-lives

Molybdenum-99 has a half-life around 66 hours; technetium-99m has one around six hours. Their parent-daughter activity relationship depends on both rates, decay branches and sample history, not only the equality of mass number.

Medical relevance

Technetium-99m is used in professionally conducted SPECT imaging as part of suitable radiopharmaceuticals. Tissue distribution depends on compound chemistry and physiology; the isobar classification cannot predict scan results or individual clinical outcomes.

Isomerism is different

⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc share A,Z,N, but differ in nuclear energy state. Thus the Mo–Tc relation is isobaric, while the two Tc states are nuclear isomers; the latter does not describe two different mass-number isotopes.

1.A.1.20.28 The nuclear-medicine inference boundary: nuclide versus compound

An isotope name is not a whole medicine

A nuclide label gives nuclear identity and possibly energy state. A radiopharmaceutical is a chemical compound whose biological behavior depends on its ligands, receptors, transport pathways and preparation, none of which is determined by A alone.

Why measured timing matters

A radioactive daughter can be produced while it decays, making activity depend on coupled populations. Treating it as an isolated initial nuclide often gives wrong activity predictions.

Physical and biological half-lives

Radioactive physical decay and biological clearance are distinct processes. In an ideal model where both are independent first-order losses, 1/T_effective=1/T_physical+1/T_biological; real compartmental physiology may be more complex.

Scope and safety

This chapter teaches nuclear relationships and mathematical interpretation. Clinical radiopharmaceutical preparation, administration, exposure and interpretation belong to qualified regulated practice.

1.A.1.20.29 Potassium-40, argon-40 and calcium-40: an A=40 family

Three different elements

⁴⁰Ar has Z=18,N=22; ⁴⁰K has Z=19,N=21; ⁴⁰Ca has Z=20,N=20. All have equal mass number 40, making them isobars despite sharply different element-specific chemistry.

Decay changes the element

Radioactive potassium-40 can undergo beta-minus transformation to calcium-40 or electron capture to argon-40. In each nuclear change A remains 40 while Z and N change in opposite directions.

Potassium in living cells

Potassium ions are important in membrane potentials and homeostasis. Neither isobar status nor an isotope label alone determines a person's potassium physiology, and equal A does not make calcium, potassium and argon interchangeable.

Earth-science relevance

Potassium–argon and argon–argon dating use parent-daughter relations and geological context. Isobar identification alone cannot yield an age; decay parameters, measurements and source histories matter.

1.A.1.20.30 Carbon-14, nitrogen-14 and the distinction between decay and a tracer

Two relations for one nuclide

¹⁴C and ¹²C are isotopes, sharing Z=6 with different A. ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N are isobars, sharing A=14 with different Z. Nuclear relationships are always comparisons between specified species.

Beta-minus transmutation

Carbon-14 changes one neutron into a proton, producing nitrogen-14 and emitting an electron plus antineutrino. This transforms the element at fixed mass number; it is not ordinary chemical ionization.

A nuclear clock

Carbon-14 has a physical half-life near 5,730 years. The expected radioactive parent fraction follows (1/2)^(t/5730) in a suitable ideal model; this describes the parent population, not all carbon atoms in a sample.

Dating is additional inference

Real radiocarbon dating needs measured sample isotope composition, appropriate baselines, corrections and calibration. The isobar relationship does not itself establish a specimen's age.

1.A.1.20.31 Nuclear half-life versus biological clearance

Physical decay

N(t)=N₀e^(−λt) describes an isolated radioactive parent population. Its physical half-life is ln(2)/λ and is independent of the chemical name of the surrounding molecule.

Biological elimination

An isotope-bearing molecule can leave a biological compartment before its nucleus decays, or its nucleus can decay while the material remains there. These are independent kinds of process.

A conditional mathematical combination

Under independent single-exponential disappearance mechanisms, reciprocal half-lives add: 1/T_eff=1/T_phys+1/T_bio. This is an idealization and is not a universal law for every biological tracer system.

Avoid an unsupported result

Neither equal neutron number nor equal mass number supplies a biological clearance rate; biological and pharmacokinetic evidence is required.

1.A.1.20.32 Elemental mass spectrometry: isobaric ion interference

What the instrument actually measures

Mass spectrometry measures ion mass-to-charge ratio m/z. Equal nuclear mass number does not imply exactly equal atomic mass, and a nominal-mass overlap is not necessarily resolved by every instrument.

Argon-40 and calcium-40

Argon plasma-based ICP-MS measurements can face ⁴⁰Ar⁺ interference near ⁴⁰Ca⁺. These ions contain different element nuclei of equal A=40; depending on instrument resolution, the species can be difficult to separate.

A polyatomic caution

Molecular ions may also overlap in nominal mass with atomic analyte ions. This is an instrumental interference, but a molecule and a nucleus are not necessarily ‘nuclear isobars’ in the strict classification sense.

Evidence needed

A signal at nominal m/z 40 does not automatically demonstrate calcium concentration. Ion chemistry, controls, resolution and matrix effects must be evaluated.

1.A.1.20.33 Exact mass, mass defect and resolving power

Count versus mass

The integer mass number A counts nucleons. The measured mass of a nuclide or neutral atom also reflects constituent masses and nuclear binding energy, so different isobars can have distinct exact masses.

Resolving-power logic

An introductory resolving-power estimate is R≈m/Δm. To decide if two peaks can be separated one needs exact masses, charge states and the instrument's resolution or peak-width convention. Printed A values are insufficient.

A charge-state check

For comparable ions, Δ(m/z)≈Δm/|z|. A doubly charged ion can show half the m/z spacing of a singly charged ion for the same mass difference.

The scientific distinction

One cannot obtain the nuclear Q-value or exact spectral separation from the equality A₁=A₂ alone. Those require evaluated mass and instrument data.

1.A.1.20.34 Isotones and nuclear shell effects

The N=8 family

¹⁴C, ¹⁵N and ¹⁶O each have eight neutrons. Neutron number eight is a familiar nuclear shell-model magic number, yet ¹⁴C is radioactive while the other two are stable. Shell closure is a clue, not a universal stability guarantee.

Another clear pair

³⁹K has Z=19,A=39,N=20 and ⁴⁰Ca has Z=20,A=40,N=20. They are N=20 isotones and different chemical elements.

A fixed-N comparison

Nuclear physicists compare isotone chains across different Z to study binding and shell behavior. Nuclear Coulomb interactions and energy structures vary with proton number, so identical N does not imply identical mass or state.

Not electronic shells

Nuclear shell closures involve nucleon quantum states, not the electron valence shells controlling chemical bonds. These are related mathematical ideas in different physical systems.

1.A.1.20.35 Nuclear changes cannot be replaced by ordinary chemistry

Atoms move through biochemical pathways

A ¹⁵N label can move through nitrogen-containing metabolites without becoming oxygen-16, even if those two nuclides are isotones. Chemical exchange changes molecular position rather than nuclear proton number.

Nuclear decay changes element identity

A carbon-14 nucleus becomes nitrogen-14 through beta decay; A stays 14 but Z increases from 6 to 7. This is not an enzyme-catalyzed electron-transfer step.

Ionization is separate

A ²⁴Mg²⁺ ion and neutral ²⁴Mg have the same nucleus; only electron counts differ. The label ²⁴ remains the mass number.

Read the arrow carefully

Chemical equations move atoms between bonds. Nuclear equations can change a nuclide into a different element. Conservation bookkeeping and scientific interpretation must match the kind of process.

1.A.1.20.36 Conservation checks: beta, gamma, alpha and neutron emission

Beta-minus bookkeeping

A beta-minus nuclear daughter has Z+1,N−1 and the same A. Beta-plus and electron capture have Z−1,N+1 with A unchanged. The parent/daughter pair is isobaric when elemental identity changes.

Gamma de-excitation

Ordinary gamma transitions change nuclear energy without changing A,Z or N. Nuclear isomeric states may be distinguished despite identical nuclear composition.

Alpha decay

An alpha particle carries A=4 and Z=2, so the daughter has A−4 and Z−2. Parent and daughter are not isobars because their mass numbers differ.

Neutron emission

Emission of a neutron decreases A by one at constant Z. An existing resulting nucleus would be another isotope of the same element; charge/electron information is separate.

1.A.1.20.37 When the correct answer is 'not enough nuclear data'

Mathematical symbols are not existence proofs

An arbitrary pair (Z,N) may describe a hypothetical composition without guaranteeing a bound nucleus with measurable lifetime. Actual nuclear species are established experimentally and through evaluated models.

Beta daughter versus actual decay

If β⁻ is stipulated, calculate the daughter with A fixed and Z+1. If the question asks whether a specific nuclide spontaneously undergoes that decay, nuclear energetics and selection rules are required.

Binding-energy and Q-value claims

Mass number equality is not exact mass equality. Computing a precise beta Q-value requires measured nuclear or atomic masses and correct lepton/electron bookkeeping.

Nuclear data references

The IAEA LiveChart of Nuclides can support specific half-lives, levels and branching ratios. The IUPAC Gold Book supports definitions but not every nuclear decay inference.

1.A.1.20.38 A reliable classification decision tree

Read both nuclides

Identify their atomic numbers Z from the symbols and their integer mass numbers A. Do not treat an electron-charge superscript as part of the mass number.

Calculate N for each

Compute A−Z separately. If ions are specified, also compute E=Z−q, but do not let E determine isobar or isotone status.

Compare the invariants

Same Z with different A means isotopes; same A with different Z means isobars; same N with different Z means isotones. If none matches, say none of these three.

Perform an independent check

Use ΔA=ΔN for isotopes, ΔN=−ΔZ for isobars, and ΔA=ΔZ for isotones. Additional stability or biological claims require their own data.

1.A.1.20.40 Forty worked examples with complete solutions

Progressive worked workshop

The first examples decode Z,A,N,E; later examples classify isobars, isotones, isomers and isoelectronic ions, then follow weak nuclear transformations and real analytical scenarios. Each includes steps, independent verification and an exam trap.

Part I — Nuclear particle counts and exact family classification

Worked example 1. Carbon-12 particle count

Question: For neutral ¹²₆C, find P, N and E.

Detailed solution: The atomic number Z=6 fixes P=6. N=A−Z=12−6=6. A neutral atom has E=6 electrons. It is the carbon-12 isotope, with mass number twelve and stable nuclear status.

Verification: Check 6+6=12 and 6−6=0 net charge.

Exam trap: Do not interpret A=12 as electron count.

Worked example 2. Nitrogen-14 particle count

Question: For neutral ¹⁴₇N find P, N and E.

Detailed solution: Z=7 identifies seven protons. N=14−7=7. Neutral E=7. This species is nitrogen-14, not carbon-14, even though both share total nucleon count fourteen.

Verification: 7+7=14; zero ion charge means seven electrons.

Exam trap: Mass number alone does not identify the element.

Worked example 3. Carbon-14 as an isobar of nitrogen-14

Question: Classify ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N and compute both N.

Detailed solution: Carbon has Z=6 and N=14−6=8; nitrogen has Z=7 and N=14−7=7. Their common A=14 and different Z identify them as isobars. One nucleus has one fewer proton and one more neutron than the other.

Verification: ΔZ=+1 and ΔN=−1 at ΔA=0.

Exam trap: They are not isotopes, because Z is different.

Worked example 4. Carbon-14 as an isotone of nitrogen-15

Question: Classify ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N.

Detailed solution: For carbon-14, N=14−6=8. Nitrogen-15 has Z=7 and N=15−7=8. The neutron counts match while proton numbers and mass numbers differ. Therefore this pair is isotonic.

Verification: Check ΔZ=+1, ΔA=+1, so ΔN=0.

Exam trap: Do not use equality of total nucleons: A is different.

Worked example 5. Nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16

Question: Classify ¹⁵N and ¹⁶O by nuclear counts.

Detailed solution: Nitrogen has Z=7,A=15,N=8. Oxygen has Z=8,A=16,N=8. These are isotones because N=8 in both, not isotopes or isobars. Both nuclides are stable, but that fact is independent of isotony.

Verification: Check N=15−7=16−8=8.

Exam trap: Shared neutrons do not mean similar chemical valence.

Worked example 6. Carbon-12 versus carbon-13

Question: Are ¹²C and ¹³C isotopes, isobars or isotones?

Detailed solution: Both have Z=6; A differs 12 versus 13. Their neutron counts are six and seven, so the pair is isotopic only among these relationships. Neutral electron count is six for each.

Verification: ΔZ=0, ΔA=1, ΔN=1.

Exam trap: Do not call them isobars because their measured masses are near integer A.

Worked example 7. Oxygen-16 and oxygen-18

Question: Classify ¹⁶O and ¹⁸O.

Detailed solution: Both have Z=8 and mass numbers sixteen and eighteen. Their N values are 8 and 10. With equal Z but different A they are oxygen isotopes, not isobars or isotones.

Verification: ΔA=2=ΔN; ΔZ=0.

Exam trap: Sharing an element name is not the definition of equal N.

Worked example 8. Argon-40 versus calcium-40

Question: Show why ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca are isobars.

Detailed solution: Argon has Z=18,N=22. Calcium has Z=20,N=20. Both have A=40 with different Z and neutron counts, so they are isobars. Their neutral atoms have respectively 18 and 20 electrons and different chemistry.

Verification: ΔZ=2 and ΔN=−2, preserving A.

Exam trap: Do not assume equal A implies exactly equal measured atomic mass.

Worked example 9. Potassium-40 and argon-40

Question: Classify ⁴⁰K and ⁴⁰Ar.

Detailed solution: Potassium Z=19,A=40 gives N=21; argon Z=18,A=40 gives N=22. Their equal A means isobars. The nuclear relationship is relevant to potassium-40 decay history but does not, alone, establish a geologic age.

Verification: N changes opposite to Z at constant A.

Exam trap: Do not call this an isotope pair of potassium.

Worked example 10. Potassium-40 and calcium-40

Question: Classify ⁴⁰K and ⁴⁰Ca.

Detailed solution: Potassium has Z=19,N=21. Calcium Z=20,N=20. Equal A=40 and different Z establish isobars. Potassium-40 can undergo a beta-minus branch to calcium-40, but the decay mode is a separate physical property.

Verification: ΔA=0, ΔZ=+1, ΔN=−1.

Exam trap: Nuclear relation cannot determine potassium's physiological function.

Worked example 11. Sodium-23 and magnesium-24

Question: Find their relation and their neutron numbers.

Detailed solution: Sodium Z=11,A=23 gives N=12; magnesium Z=12,A=24 gives N=12. They are isotones. Both neutral atoms have different electron counts, but their specified Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions have ten electrons each.

Verification: N=23−11=24−12=12.

Worked example 12. Potassium-39 and calcium-40

Question: Determine the relationship of ³⁹K and ⁴⁰Ca.

Detailed solution: Potassium-39 has Z=19,N=39−19=20. Calcium-40 has Z=20,N=40−20=20. They are isotones of neutron number twenty. This does not say the two nuclides have identical nuclear levels.

Worked example 13. A sodium ion and magnesium ion

Question: For ²³Na⁺ and ²⁴Mg²⁺ calculate P, N, E and both nuclear/electronic relationships.

Detailed solution: Na⁺ has P=11,N=12,E=10. Mg²⁺ has P=12,N=12,E=10. Their nuclei are isotones (N=12) and their specified ions are isoelectronic (E=10), two separate relationships. The different ionic charges and elements remain meaningful.

Verification: Charges: 11−10=+1 and 12−10=+2.

Worked example 14. Same nuclide with different charges

Question: Are neutral ²⁴Mg and ²⁴Mg²⁺ distinct isotopes?

Detailed solution: No. Both share Z=12,A=24,N=12. Neutral magnesium has E=12 while Mg²⁺ has E=10. Changing electrons makes an ion, not a distinct nuclear isotope. The pair is the same nuclide in different charge states.

Verification: Check both have twelve protons and twelve neutrons.

Exam trap: An ionizing event cannot be substituted for neutron emission.

Worked example 15. Calculate a missing mass number in an isotone

Question: An isotope of phosphorus has Z=15 and is an isotone of ¹⁶O (N=8). Find its A.

Detailed solution: Oxygen-16 has N=16−8=8. A phosphorus nuclide with Z=15,N=8 would have A=15+8=23 and be labelled ²³P as a candidate nuclear composition. The calculation does not establish that such a nuclide is bound or exists experimentally; that needs nuclear data.

Verification: A−Z=23−15=8.

Exam trap: Do not claim physical stability from arithmetic alone.

Worked example 16. Find the isobar of a given candidate element

Question: A nucleus of element X with Z=8 is to be an isobar of ¹⁴C. What mass number and N would X have?

Detailed solution: The isobaric condition fixes A=14. Atomic number eight identifies oxygen, giving ¹⁴O with N=14−8=6. This is nuclear bookkeeping; whether and how ¹⁴O decays is an additional experimental question.

Verification: Z+N=8+6=14.

Exam trap: An isobar of carbon is not a carbon isotope.

Worked example 17. Isodiaphers versus isotones

Question: Compare ¹⁴C and ¹⁸O. Do they share N or neutron excess N−Z?

Detailed solution: Carbon-14 has N=8,Z=6, hence N−Z=2. Oxygen-18 has N=10,Z=8, hence N−Z=2. They share neutron excess and can be called isodiaphers, but they are not isotones (N differs), isobars (A differs) or isotopes (Z differs).

Verification: Check both neutron excess values equal two.

Exam trap: Do not classify equal N−Z as equal N.

Worked example 18. Same A and same Z with different m state

Question: Compare ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc.

Detailed solution: Both have Z=43,A=99,N=56. The metastable m refers to a different nuclear energy state of the same nuclide composition. These are nuclear isomers, not distinct-A isotope partners or different-element isobars.

Verification: N=99−43=56 for both.

Exam trap: Do not count m as an extra neutron.

Worked example 19. Molybdenum-99 and technetium-99m

Question: Classify ⁹⁹Mo and ⁹⁹ᵐTc by A,Z,N.

Detailed solution: Molybdenum has Z=42,N=57; technetium has Z=43,N=56; both have A=99. They are isobars. The technetium is in a metastable state, but its nuclear excitation does not alter the isobar classification relative to molybdenum.

Verification: ΔZ=+1 and ΔN=−1.

Exam trap: Do not confuse Mo/Tc isobars with Tc-99m/Tc-99 isomers.

Worked example 20. A=14 versus A=15 family check

Question: Classify ¹⁴N and ¹⁵N and check neutron count.

Detailed solution: Both nitrogen nuclei have Z=7. Their A values are 14 and 15, so neutron counts seven and eight. They are isotopes of nitrogen, not isobars or isotones, despite their close mass numbers.

Verification: ΔZ=0 and ΔA=ΔN=+1.

Exam trap: The different isotope mass is not a changed element.

Worked example 21. Beta-minus daughter of carbon-14

Question: Determine the daughter nuclide for β⁻ decay of ¹⁴₆C and classify parent/daughter.

Step-by-step solution: Beta-minus changes a neutron into a proton. The daughter has A=14 unchanged and Z=6+1=7, identifying ¹⁴₇N. Their neutron counts are eight and seven. The pair is isobaric and emitted electron plus antineutrino complete the reaction bookkeeping.

Independent check: A:14=14; electric charge +6=+7−1 for bare nuclei plus beta.

Common error: Do not say only an atomic electron was removed.

Worked example 22. Beta-minus daughter of potassium-40

Question: A ⁴⁰₁₉K nucleus undergoes β⁻ decay. Find daughter Z and N.

Step-by-step solution: Beta-minus keeps A=40 and raises Z from 19 to 20, which is calcium. The daughter ⁴⁰₂₀Ca has N=40−20=20, while parent has N=21. The transformation links isobars. This is a known branch of potassium-40 decay.

Independent check: Z change +1 and N change −1 conserve A=40.

Common error: Never conclude this decay branch exists for every A=40 isobar.

Worked example 23. Electron capture from potassium-40

Question: A ⁴⁰K nucleus transforms by EC. Which daughter is expected?

Step-by-step solution: Electron capture changes a nuclear proton into a neutron, leaving A=40 but decreasing Z from 19 to 18. Atomic number eighteen is argon, so daughter is ⁴⁰Ar with N=22. It is isobaric with ⁴⁰K, and a neutrino is emitted as part of the weak process.

Independent check: Parent N=21 and daughter N=22; A stays forty.

Common error: Do not treat the captured electron as a neutron that was already present.

Worked example 24. Fluorine-18 positron daughter

Question: A ¹⁸₉F nucleus undergoes β⁺ decay. What daughter results?

Step-by-step solution: Positron emission decreases nuclear Z by one and increases N by one while keeping A=18. The daughter is ¹⁸₈O with N=10, whereas fluorine-18 has N=9. They are isobars. Fluorine-18's clinical use in PET depends on radiopharmaceutical chemistry beyond this nuclear calculation.

Independent check: Parent/daughter both A=18; Z falls from nine to eight.

Common error: Do not claim positron emission changes A from 18 to 17.

Worked example 25. Electron capture beryllium-7

Question: If ⁷₄Be undergoes electron capture, identify daughter and classify.

Step-by-step solution: Electron capture reduces Z by one while leaving mass number seven. Daughter ⁷₃Li has N=7−3=4, while parent ⁷Be has N=3. The pair is isobaric; existence of the decay route is a known nuclear fact but cannot be deduced for arbitrary species from algebra alone.

Independent check: Z:4→3, N:3→4, A stays 7.

Common error: Electron capture is not an ordinary ionization event.

Worked example 26. Gamma de-excitation bookkeeping

Question: A metastable nucleus emits a gamma photon. How do A,Z,N change?

Step-by-step solution: A gamma transition changes nuclear energy state but not nucleon composition. Therefore ΔA=0, ΔZ=0 and ΔN=0. The initial and final states can be nuclear isomers rather than different-element isobars. Technetium-99m to lower-energy technetium-99 illustrates this conceptual distinction.

Independent check: A=Z+N remains satisfied without any count change.

Common error: Do not treat gamma emission as loss of a proton.

Worked example 27. Alpha decay of uranium-238

Question: A ²³⁸₉₂U nucleus emits an alpha. Find daughter A,Z and classify parent/daughter.

Step-by-step solution: An alpha carries A=4 and Z=2. Daughter A=238−4=234, Z=92−2=90, which is thorium-234. Parent/daughter differ in both A and Z, and their N counts differ by two; they are not isobars, isotopes or isotones of each other.

Independent check: 234+4=238 and 90+2=92.

Common error: An alpha has two protons, not four.

Worked example 28. Neutron emission and isotope identity

Question: A nucleus ¹⁶₈O emits one neutron. What nuclear label would remain under hypothetical bookkeeping?

Step-by-step solution: Removing a neutron decreases A from 16 to 15 while Z remains eight, giving oxygen-15 with N=7. Parent oxygen-16 and hypothetical daughter oxygen-15 would be isotopes. Whether the proposed emission occurs under the stated initial conditions is a separate energetic question.

Independent check: A drops one and Z stays eight.

Common error: Do not conclude all stable oxygen-16 nuclei spontaneously emit neutrons.

Worked example 29. Nuclear isomer versus isobar in one chain

Question: Distinguish ⁹⁹Mo→⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹ᵐTc→⁹⁹Tc.

Step-by-step solution: Mo-99 Z=42 and Tc-99m Z=43 share A=99, giving isobaric nuclear species. Tc-99m and Tc-99 share both A=99 and Z=43 but differ in energy state, giving nuclear isomers. Different relationships apply to the consecutive steps.

Independent check: For the first, ΔZ=1; for the second ΔZ=0 and A is unchanged.

Common error: Do not call both arrows the same type of isotope pair.

Worked example 30. Simple half-life arithmetic for radiocarbon

Question: An ideal isolated population has 6400 ¹⁴C nuclei. How many remain in expectation after two half-lives?

Step-by-step solution: Two half-lives reduce the parent fraction to (1/2)²=1/4. Thus 6400/4=1600 ¹⁴C parent nuclei remain in expectation. The daughter nitrogen-14 is an isobar, but its existence does not change the exponential parent calculation. This is a toy nuclear population, not a calibrated specimen date.

Independent check: 1600/6400=0.25.

Common error: Do not subtract half the original twice to get zero.

Worked example 31. Mo-99 physical half-life simplification

Question: Using a rounded Mo-99 half-life of 66 hours, what fraction remains from an ideal parent population after 132 hours?

Step-by-step solution: The elapsed time is 132/66=2 half-lives. Therefore the expected surviving Mo-99 fraction is one quarter. In an actual parent-daughter system, the Tc-99m daughter activity also depends on its creation and decay, so this calculation concerns only isolated parent survival.

Independent check: (1/2)²=0.25.

Common error: Do not infer the 99mTc amount is automatically 75% of original Mo atoms without considering branches and daughter decay.

Worked example 32. Compare radioactive and stable members at A=14

Question: Does equal A imply both ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N are radioactive?

Step-by-step solution: No. Carbon-14 is radioactive while nitrogen-14 is stable, although both have A=14. Nuclear stability depends on actual binding and allowed decay transitions, not the common integer mass number. The pair is still isobaric regardless of stability.

Independent check: Compare parent and daughter measured properties separately.

Common error: Do not infer half-life from A alone.

Worked example 33. Isotone group does not share stability

Question: Classify ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N and ¹⁶O and compare stability.

Step-by-step solution: All have N=8, making an isotone family. Carbon-14 is radioactive, while nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. The example illustrates that neutron shell closure is not a universal stability guarantee at every Z.

Independent check: Check N as 8 for all three.

Common error: Identical N does not force equal beta-decay energetics.

Worked example 34. Mass-number equality and exact mass

Question: Given only A=40 for Ar-40 and Ca-40, can an exact measured atomic-mass difference be calculated?

Step-by-step solution: No. Equal A establishes that both nuclei contain forty nucleons, but their actual atomic masses depend on constituent masses, binding and electrons. An exact mass difference requires evaluated mass data. Reporting zero because A is equal would be incorrect.

Independent check: Separate integer A from measured mass in u.

Common error: Do not equate nominal mass equality with exact mass equality.

Worked example 35. Resolving power toy calculation

Question: Two instrument peaks at m/z≈40 are separated by Δ(m/z)=0.004 under a stated convention. Compute R≈m/Δm.

Step-by-step solution: Under the deliberately simplified definition, R≈40/0.004=10000. This is a mathematical resolving-power estimate; whether peaks are separated also depends on the instrument's exact resolution definition and other conditions. The values are toy data, not claimed argon/calcium exact masses.

Independent check: 10000×0.004=40.

Common error: Do not treat a nominal mass number as a measured exact peak value without a model.

Worked example 36. Mass shift with charge magnitude two

Question: Two comparable ions differ in mass by 0.006 u and each carries charge magnitude 2. Estimate their m/z spacing.

Step-by-step solution: For the same charge magnitude, approximate separation is Δm/|z|=0.006/2=0.003. This analytical result describes ion mass-to-charge values. It does not alter the isobaric classification of the constituent atomic nuclei.

Independent check: 0.003×2=0.006.

Common error: Do not multiply by charge.

Worked example 37. Three A=40 members

Question: Classify ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K and ⁴⁰Ca and compute all N.

Step-by-step solution: Argon Z=18 gives N=22; potassium Z=19 gives N=21; calcium Z=20 gives N=20. Their A values are all 40, forming an isobar family. The family is not an isotone group because the neutron numbers differ.

Independent check: Z+N=40 for every member.

Common error: Do not infer identical chemical properties among three elements.

Worked example 38. Two different pairs from a small set

Question: Given ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴N and ¹⁵N, name isotope pairs and any isotones.

Step-by-step solution: ¹²C/¹³C are isotopes of carbon; ¹⁴N/¹⁵N are isotopes of nitrogen. ¹³C has N=13−6=7 and ¹⁴N has N=14−7=7, so those two are isotones. Classify each pair separately and do not assign one relationship to the entire mixed set.

Independent check: Check each pair's Z,A,N rather than reading only A labels.

Common error: Isotone relation does not imply the carbon and nitrogen are one element.

Worked example 39. An incomplete species identity

Question: A nucleus has A=28 and is an isobar of another unspecified A=28 nuclide. Can its element be determined?

Step-by-step solution: No. A=28 fixes the nucleon total but not how many are protons versus neutrons. Without Z or an element symbol, multiple nuclear compositions may have A=28. Isobar status with an unspecified partner does not identify the element.

Independent check: Need a second independent quantity Z or N to determine the third.

Common error: Do not choose an element from the number A alone.

Worked example 40. A physically underdetermined decay question

Question: A hypothetical A=50 nuclide has Z=24. Can its beta-minus Q-value be calculated from these two integers alone?

Step-by-step solution: No. The hypothetical daughter would have A=50 and Z=25 under β⁻ bookkeeping, but an actual decay energy needs evaluated atomic/nuclear masses and correct electron/weak-process accounting. The arithmetic identifies a possible isobaric daughter, not decay feasibility or Q.

Independent check: Parent has N=26; candidate daughter N=25, keeping A=50.

Common error: Do not substitute ΔA=0 into E=Δmc² and claim Q=0.

Incorrect idea: A signal near m/z=40 is definitely calcium.

Correct interpretation: Other elemental or polyatomic ions may interfere depending on instrument and matrix.

Verification: Argon plasma creates potential ⁴⁰Ar-based interference.

Misconception 26

Incorrect idea: Equal A means nuclear Q-value must be zero.

Correct interpretation: Decay Q-values depend on differences in actual nuclear/atomic masses, not integer A.

Verification: ¹⁴C can decay to ¹⁴N despite equal A.

Misconception 27

Incorrect idea: Sharing neutron count makes two radiotracers biologically interchangeable.

Correct interpretation: Different elements and chemical compounds have distinct biodistribution, uptake and pharmacology.

Verification: ¹⁵N and ¹⁶O sharing N=8 does not mean same metabolism.

Misconception 28

Incorrect idea: A radionuclide's physical half-life is identical to biological clearance time.

Correct interpretation: Physical decay and biological elimination are distinct processes.

Verification: Effective disappearance requires both processes and model assumptions.

Misconception 29

Incorrect idea: Isotonic solution is a mixture of nuclear isotones.

Correct interpretation: Physiological isotonicity concerns effective osmotic balance and water movement.

Verification: The word isotone in nuclear chemistry means equal neutron count.

Misconception 30

Incorrect idea: One nuclide label determines exact clinical imaging results.

Correct interpretation: A nuclide label states nuclear identity; imaging depends on compounds, physiology and measurements.

Verification: ⁹⁹ᵐTc is used in imaging, but its symbol alone does not specify a diagnosis.

1.A.1.20.43 Ten integrated interpretation problems

Multi-concept challenges

Each question combines two or more parts of the chapter: classification and decay, nuclear counts and ions, analytical interference and exact mass, or nuclear medicine and biological interpretation. Model answers identify missing assumptions where relevant.

Ten comprehensive integrated exam problems with worked model answers

Integrated challenge 1. A nuclear family with overlapping classifications

Question: Given ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C, ¹⁴N and ¹⁵N, identify one isotope pair, one isobar pair, and one isotone pair with counts.

Reasoned model solution: ¹²C/¹³C are isotopes because Z=6 in both and A differs. ¹⁴C/¹⁴N are isobars because A=14 in both and Z differs 6 versus 7. ¹⁴C/¹⁵N are isotones because N=14−6=8 and 15−7=8. State each comparison separately; one label does not assign one relationship to the entire set.

Integrated challenge 2. A pair with two different invariants

Question: For ²³Na⁺ and ²⁴Mg²⁺, classify nuclear and electronic relations and calculate P,N,E.

Reasoned model solution: Sodium-23 Z=11,N=12,E=10 as Na⁺; magnesium-24 Z=12,N=12,E=10 as Mg²⁺. Their nuclei are isotones, while the ions are isoelectronic. The two relations arise from different equalities: neutron count N and electron count E. They are not the same element.

Integrated challenge 3. Three A=40 isobars and two decay paths

Question: For ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K and ⁴⁰Ca give P,N and classify ⁴⁰K beta-minus and electron-capture daughters.

Reasoned model solution: Argon has P=18,N=22; potassium P=19,N=21; calcium P=20,N=20. All are isobars A=40. In the beta-minus branch ⁴⁰K produces ⁴⁰Ca through Z+1,N−1. In the electron-capture branch it produces ⁴⁰Ar through Z−1,N+1. Real branching is nuclear data, not deduced solely from arithmetic.

Integrated challenge 4. Medical imaging: parent, daughter and isomer

Question: A scheme shows ⁹⁹Mo → ⁹⁹ᵐTc → ⁹⁹Tc. Explain which pair is isobaric and which is isomeric.

Reasoned model solution: ⁹⁹Mo Z=42 and ⁹⁹ᵐTc Z=43 share A=99 but have different element identities: isobars. ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc share Z=43,A=99,N=56 but differ in nuclear energy: isomers. The sequence by itself cannot calculate daughter activity, tissue biodistribution or clinical imaging outcome.

Integrated challenge 5. An uncalibrated radiocarbon ‘age’

Question: A specimen is said to contain ¹⁴C; someone assigns 5730 years because that is its half-life. What can be concluded?

Reasoned model solution: The symbol identifies carbon-14 (Z=6,N=8), which is an isotope of carbon-12 and an isobar of nitrogen-14. Its half-life near 5730 years is a nuclide property. An age estimate needs a measured parent fraction or activity, appropriate baseline and radiocarbon calibration; no age follows from isotope presence alone.

Integrated challenge 6. An N=8 family and stability reasoning

Question: A student classifies ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁶O as isotones and says all should decay similarly. Evaluate.

Reasoned model solution: The classification is correct: all have N=8. The stability conclusion is false: carbon-14 is radioactive while nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. Nuclear structure depends on Z, masses and states as well as neutron count. An N magic number may be significant but is not a uniform stability guarantee.

Integrated challenge 7. An analytical interference near nominal m/z 40

Question: A sample yields signal around m/z 40 in argon-plasma MS. Can calcium be concluded from mass number alone?

Reasoned model solution: No. ⁴⁰Ar⁺ and ⁴⁰Ca⁺ have equal nominal A and may interfere; other species or matrix effects may also contribute. Actual signal assignment requires instrument resolution, exact mass/ion species or suitable method-specific controls. Equal A is a nuclear isobar relation, not sufficient analytical identification.

Integrated challenge 8. A hypothetical beta daughter without nuclear data

Question: A nuclide with A=50 and Z=24 is asked to undergo β⁻ hypothetically. Identify daughter, and explain what cannot be concluded.

Reasoned model solution: The stipulated transformation gives A=50,Z=25, which identifies manganese-50 as the bookkeeping daughter; parent chromium-50 has N=26 and daughter N=25. They are isobars. The arithmetic cannot establish that such beta-minus decay is an energetically allowed route or compute its Q-value; evaluated masses and decay data are needed.

Integrated challenge 9. A mass and charge calculation without exact isotope masses

Question: Two isobaric ions share A=40 and are both doubly charged. Can their exact m/z difference be calculated?

Reasoned model solution: No. Both ions carry the same integer mass number, but their measured isotope masses depend on nuclear binding and electron content. If an exact mass difference Δm were supplied, comparable charge +2 would give approximately Δ(m/z)=Δm/2. Without masses, an exact numerical spacing cannot be derived.

Integrated challenge 10. The same nucleus through a chemical reaction

Question: A ¹⁵N-labelled amino acid is incorporated into protein. Does its isotone relationship to ¹⁶O imply it becomes oxygen during incorporation?

Reasoned model solution: No. Nitrogen-15 remains Z=7,N=8 through ordinary protein synthesis; the same neutron count as oxygen-16 does not cause nuclear transmutation. A detected label supports nitrogen incorporation under the experiment, while a protein synthesis rate would require precursor labeling, time-course or other kinetic data.

1.A.1.20.44 Master summary, formula map and next chapter

The three invariants

Isotopes have equal Z; isobars equal A; isotones equal N. Use A=Z+N and N=A−Z. A nucleus's exact measured mass is not determined solely by its integer A.

Transformations

Beta-minus changes Z by +1, N by −1 at fixed A. Beta-plus and electron capture do the opposite. Gamma leaves composition unchanged, while alpha lowers A by four.

Memorable pairs

¹²C/¹³C isotopes; ¹⁴C/¹⁴N isobars; ¹⁴C/¹⁵N isotones; ⁴⁰Ar/⁴⁰K/⁴⁰Ca isobars; ²³Na/²⁴Mg isotones; ⁹⁹Mo/⁹⁹ᵐTc isobars and ⁹⁹ᵐTc/⁹⁹Tc nuclear isomers.
