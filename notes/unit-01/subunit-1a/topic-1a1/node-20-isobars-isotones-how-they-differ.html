1.A.1.20.1 The nuclear detective: two atoms with the same number but different identities

Imagine a nuclear-science student standing at a table with cards marked carbon-14, nitrogen-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16. Every card contains an element symbol and a mass number. Two cards display the number fourteen, but they are not the same element. A different pair share eight neutrons although their mass numbers differ. This puzzle introduces two powerful relationships: isobars and isotones.

The first investigation

Read ¹⁴₆C and ¹⁴₇N. Both contain fourteen nucleons, but carbon has six nuclear protons whereas nitrogen has seven. Their neutron counts are eight and seven. They are isobars. Now compare ¹⁴₆C with ¹⁵₇N: each has eight neutrons, although their total nucleon counts differ. They are isotones.

Why this is more than a vocabulary exercise

The distinction connects the periodic table, nuclear decay, the chart of nuclides, nuclear medicine, geochronology and mass spectrometry. It also prevents false conclusions: two isobars need not have identical measured atomic masses or similar chemical properties, and two isotones need not be equally stable.

How we will study it

Follow the chronological development of nuclides, learn the algebra before the examples, visualize families on an N–Z chart, examine beta-decay transformations, and then solve research-style problems. Our study rules are to state what is known, calculate what follows and explicitly identify what requires experimental nuclear or biological data.

Learning outcomes: Decode nuclide notation; distinguish isotopes, isobars and isotones by Z, A and N; build and classify nuclear families; reason through beta-minus, beta-plus, electron capture and gamma transitions; explain nuclear-isomer and isoelectronic distinctions; solve mass, charge, decay and nuclear-medicine interpretation problems; avoid overclaiming biological similarity.

1.A.1.20.2 Historical development: why atomic mass could not identify an element by itself

Dalton and early chemical reasoning

Classical chemical atomism linked each element with its own type of atom and characteristic mass. This was productive for explaining fixed chemical combining proportions, but later experiments revealed atoms of the same element could have different nuclear masses. Chemical identity and measured mass had to become separate ideas.

Nuclear discoveries changed the question

Electron research, Rutherford's nuclear model and Moseley's atomic-number work clarified that an element is identified by nuclear charge. Neutrons later explained the difference between mass number and proton number. These developments created a consistent language for nuclides with equal A but different Z and for nuclides with equal N but different Z.

Isotopes and the wider classification

The term isotope emerged from work on nuclear species occupying the same periodic-table place. The complementary relations isobar and isotone describe different coordinate matches on the nuclide chart. Modern standardized definitions are useful because informal ‘same mass’ language can otherwise confuse integer mass number with measured mass.

Modern measurements and transformations

Mass spectrometry distinguishes atomic or molecular ions by mass-to-charge properties; nuclear experiments determine stability and decay. A comparison between ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N is an isobaric classification whether or not a particular instrument separates their exact masses. Nuclear transformations give the relationship a deeper physical role.

1.A.1.20.3 Nuclide notation: read the symbol before making a classification

One nuclide is identified by its nuclear composition

In ᴬ_ZX, X is an element symbol, Z is the number of protons and A the number of total nucleons. The neutron number is N = A − Z. Atomic number is not a variable that can be chosen independently of a valid element symbol: if Z=6, the element is carbon.

Electron count is a different axis

For a neutral atom, electrons equal proton count Z; for a monatomic ion of charge q, electrons equal Z−q. This affects chemical charge, not the nuclear isotope classification. A calcium-40 cation and a neutral calcium-40 atom are the same nuclide in different electronic states.

Classify pairs, not isolated nuclei

One nuclide alone is not ‘an isobar’ without context specifying another nuclide with the same A. Likewise an isotone relationship compares at least two different nuclear species with the same N. One element contains isotope families if its nuclides have different A.

Do not substitute measured mass for A

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 are isobars because their integer mass number is fourteen. Their physical neutral-atomic masses are not necessarily equal; isotope stability, binding and electrons affect measured values.

P = Z;   N = A − Z;   A = Z + N;   neutral electrons = Z

1.A.1.20.4 Isotopes: the earlier concept that anchors the contrast

IUPAC definition

Isotopes are nuclides with the same atomic number and different mass numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 share Z=6 but have N=6 and N=7. Consequently they are isotopes of carbon, not different elements.

Chemistry and physical isotope effects

Neutral isotopes of an element generally have the same electron count and closely related electronic structure, but measured masses and nuclear properties can differ. This allows isotope fractionation, spectroscopy and sometimes differences in reaction rates. Their chemical behavior is not always numerically identical.

An isotope pair does not automatically form an isobar pair

Different mass numbers are required for distinct isotopes under the standard definition; equal mass number defines isobars only when nuclear species differ in Z. Thus ¹²C and ¹³C cannot be isobars.

A nuclear isomer is not simply another isotope

Two states such as ⁹⁹Tc and ⁹⁹ᵐTc have identical Z and A but different nuclear energy states. The metastable m indicates a nuclear isomeric state, not another neutron or an ordinary charge state.

1.A.1.20.5 Isobars: equal mass number, different nuclear charges

The rigorous definition

In nuclear terminology, different nuclides of equal mass number are called isobars. With different element identity, their Z values differ; because A=Z+N remains constant, the neutron counts must differ in the opposite direction. For example ¹⁴C has Z=6,N=8 and ¹⁴N has Z=7,N=7.

A mass number is not the same as measured neutral atomic mass

The phrase ‘same mass number’ means both nuclei contain the same total number of nucleons. Nuclear binding energies differ and measured isotope masses differ. Isobars can therefore have distinct masses even though both carry the integer label A=14.

A surprising biological connection

Carbon-14 beta-decays to nitrogen-14. The element changes, but A remains fourteen. The parent and daughter are isobars. This is relevant to understanding radiocarbon physics, although the isobar relationship alone does not supply radiocarbon age information.

Think of A as a constant-sum line

For a fixed A, if Z rises by one, N must fall by one. That makes isobaric neighbors natural participants in weak nuclear transformations such as beta-minus or electron capture when energetically allowed.

Isobars: A₁ = A₂, Z₁ ≠ Z₂;   then N₁ ≠ N₂

1.A.1.20.6 Isotones: equal neutron number, different proton numbers

The IUPAC definition

Isotones are nuclides having equal neutron number N but different atomic numbers Z. An easy example is ¹⁴C (Z=6,N=8), ¹⁵N (Z=7,N=8) and ¹⁶O (Z=8,N=8). These are different elements that share eight neutrons.

Why their mass numbers differ

If N is fixed and Z increases, total mass number A=Z+N also increases. Thus the sequence carbon-14, nitrogen-15, oxygen-16 has mass numbers 14,15,16 and proton numbers 6,7,8, but N=8 throughout.

Isotones are not ‘same chemistry’

Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen have different electronic structures as neutral atoms, different valences and different biochemical roles. Sharing eight neutrons creates a nuclear classification, not equivalent chemical behavior.

Neutron number cannot be judged from a label's last digit

Compare ²³Na and ²⁴Mg: sodium Z=11,N=12 and magnesium Z=12,N=12. They are isotones even though their mass numbers differ. A student must calculate A−Z rather than merely compare the written A values.

Isotones: N₁ = N₂;   Z₁ ≠ Z₂;   A₁ ≠ A₂

1.A.1.20.7 The three-way classification: an unambiguous exam decision method

The right order of operations

First read or derive Z for each nuclide. Next read A. Finally compute N=A−Z for each. If same Z with different A, classify isotopes. If same A with different Z, classify isobars. If same N with different Z, classify isotones. If none matches, say no such relationship among those three.

Examples show why the order matters

¹²C and ¹³C have equal Z and different A, so isotopes. ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N have equal A and differing Z, so isobars. ¹⁴C and ¹⁵N have neutron count eight, so isotones. ¹²C and ¹⁴N match none of these three because Z, A and N all differ (6 vs 7; 12 vs 14; 6 vs 7).

A pair of distinct nuclides cannot normally qualify as two of these relationships simultaneously

For two distinct nuclei, if A and Z both match, then N also matches, so they are the same nuclide (though possibly different nuclear states). If Z matches but A differs, N differs too; if A matches and Z differs, N differs; if N matches and Z differs, A differs. Algebra rules out the simultaneous isotope/isobar/isotone pair classifications of two distinct different-composition nuclei.

A complete answer names the matched variable

Instead of writing only ‘isotones’, write ‘isotones because N=8 for both, while Z differs’. That provides an independent calculation and reduces error risk.

1.A.1.20.8 The N–Z chart: visualize isotopes, isobars and isotones

A chart transforms three equations into geometry

A chart of nuclides can place neutron number N along the horizontal axis and proton number Z on the vertical axis. Then isotones share the same N coordinate and align vertically; isotopes share the same Z coordinate and align horizontally; isobars lie along diagonals where N+Z=A is constant.

The direction depends on the chosen axes

Some published nuclear charts place Z and N on opposite axes. The invariant idea is which coordinate is held constant, not whether a family happens to look like a horizontal or vertical line in a particular diagram.

A diagram is a reasoning tool, not the actual nuclear stability map

A pedagogical grid can include only selected example nuclides; actual charts contain many nuclear species with measured stability boundaries. The graphic below illustrates relations and should not be used as a complete classification of natural nuclides.

1.A.1.20.9 Isobar family with A = 14: carbon and nitrogen

Read the actual nuclei

Carbon-14 has Z=6 and N=8; nitrogen-14 has Z=7 and N=7. Their common mass number is A=14, so they are isobars. These two differ in elemental chemical identity, nuclear stability and neutral electronic structure.

The decay link gives a memorable narrative

Carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14. A neutron converts into a proton and the total nucleon count remains fourteen. Emission of an electron and an electron antineutrino accompanies the nuclear transformation. The relationship is isobaric, not isotopic.

One member may be stable while another is radioactive

Nitrogen-14 is stable, whereas carbon-14 is radioactive. Equal A therefore does not imply equal stability. Which decay is energetically possible depends on actual nuclear masses and energy levels, not merely the equality of mass number.

Biological significance lies in the distinct element and isotope properties

Carbon-14 is used in radiocarbon contexts and some research applications; nitrogen-14 is the predominant ordinary nitrogen nuclide in many samples. The term isobar explains the nuclear relation, not similarity of biological functions.

¹⁴₆C (P=6, N=8) → ¹⁴₇N (P=7, N=7) + β⁻ + antineutrino

1.A.1.20.10 Isotone family with N = 8: carbon-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16

Find the hidden invariant

Carbon-14: 14−6=8. Nitrogen-15: 15−7=8. Oxygen-16: 16−8=8. All three have eight neutrons, despite having different proton numbers and mass numbers. They form an isotone family.

Contrast stability

Carbon-14 is radioactive, but nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. A fixed neutron count does not determine whether all isotones share stability. Nuclear binding depends on the entire proton–neutron system and its quantum energy configuration.

Why eight is historically significant in nuclear structure

Neutron number eight is one of the familiar magic numbers in the nuclear shell model. Shell closure can influence stability patterns, but it is not a universal guarantee that every nucleus with N=8 is stable. This nuanced example avoids simplistic magic-number rules.

Avoid transferring chemical properties across an isotone family

Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen differ in chemistry and in their biological roles in proteins, nucleic acids, respiration and photosynthesis. Sharing N=8 does not make them interchangeable atoms in metabolism.

1.A.1.20.11 Isobar family A = 40: potassium, argon and calcium

Three different chemical elements share the number forty

Argon-40 has Z=18,N=22; potassium-40 has Z=19,N=21; calcium-40 has Z=20,N=20. All have A=40, so they are isobars. They are emphatically not isotopes of one element, and their neutral atom electron counts are 18,19,20.

Potassium-40 links a biological element with nuclear decay

Potassium is an essential biological element. A small naturally occurring portion is the radioactive isotope ⁴⁰K. Nuclear decay pathways include beta-minus transformation to ⁴⁰Ca and electron-capture transformation to ⁴⁰Ar, though their respective branching and detailed energetics require nuclear data.

Argon is a noble gas and calcium is a metal

Even with equal A, argon-40 and calcium-40 differ sharply in chemical behavior because their proton numbers and electronic structures differ. Equal integer nucleon count does not mean a similar Lewis structure, oxidation state or biological function.

Earth-science connection

Potassium–argon and argon–argon geochronology exploit radioactive potassium/argon-related nuclear history. This is conceptually distinct from the radiocarbon age of organic materials, and real dating needs analytical and geological assumptions.

1.A.1.20.12 Isotone family with N = 12: sodium-23 and magnesium-24

Calculate rather than memorize

Sodium-23 has Z=11, so N=23−11=12. Magnesium-24 has Z=12, so N=24−12=12. They are isotones because neutron counts match while atomic numbers differ.

A second useful relationship appears only for specified ions

Neutral ²³Na has eleven electrons and neutral ²⁴Mg has twelve, so they are not isoelectronic as neutral atoms. But Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each have ten electrons. These ions can be both isotonic in their nuclear composition and isoelectronic in their electronic count—two independent relations.

Do not conflate nuclei and ions

The words isotone and isoelectronic refer to different information. A nuclear isotope label remains the same after electron loss. A student should calculate N and electron count separately, particularly if the question supplies ionic charge.

²³Na: N=23−11=12; ²⁴Mg: N=24−12=12; Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ both have 10 electrons.

1.A.1.20.13 Isotope versus isobar versus isotone as mathematical constraints

Three variables, only two independent

The identity A=Z+N means knowing any two of A,Z,N determines the third. This prevents physically impossible combinations. For instance if two nuclei have A equal and Z different, then N must differ by exactly the negative of the Z difference.

If ΔA=0, then ΔN=−ΔZ

This is the isobaric condition. A beta-minus transformation changes Z by +1 and N by −1, so A is constant. That is why certain beta-decay parent/daughter nuclides are isobars.

If ΔN=0, then ΔA=ΔZ

This is the isotonic condition. Along an N-constant family, increasing Z by one also increases A by one. The carbon-14 → nitrogen-15 → oxygen-16 isotone sequence makes this equation visible.

If ΔZ=0, then ΔA=ΔN

This is the isotope condition. Carbon-12 → carbon-13 changes neutron count and mass number by one at fixed atomic number.

ΔA = ΔZ + ΔN;   isobars ΔA=0;   isotones ΔN=0;   isotopes ΔZ=0 (for distinct A).

1.A.1.20.14 Why isobars do not have equal exact atomic masses

Mass number A counts particles, not kilograms or atomic mass units

Nuclear mass and neutral atomic mass reflect proton, neutron and electron masses as well as nuclear binding energy. A=40 says forty nucleons in each of ⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca; it does not say those atoms weigh exactly 40.000000 u or precisely the same measured mass.

Binding energy creates mass differences

In simple nuclear physics the mass defect is related to energy by E=Δmc². Two isobars can have different binding energies because their proton/neutron compositions differ. Their decay energetics depend on actual masses and accessible states.

Example without fabricated precision

The neutral atomic masses of ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N differ, and that difference permits carbon-14 beta-minus decay. A reliable computation of the Q-value must use evaluated atomic or nuclear masses with appropriate electron-mass bookkeeping. The integer A labels alone cannot provide the emitted energy.

Mass spectra may separate equal-A atomic species at high resolution

Nominal A matches are one possible source of isobaric interference in mass spectrometry, but ion charge, chemical interferences and instrumental resolution matter. For ion analysis, a mass-to-charge ratio is not identical to the integer mass number.

E = Δm c² (mass–energy relation);   equality of A does not imply equality of measured mass.

1.A.1.20.15 Beta-minus decay: an isobaric transformation

A neutron transforms within the nucleus

In beta-minus decay, a neutron becomes a proton, accompanied by emission of an electron and electron antineutrino. Thus Z increases by one, N decreases by one, and A remains unchanged. The parent and daughter have different element identities but share A.

Carbon-14 to nitrogen-14

The parent ¹⁴C has P=6,N=8, while the daughter ¹⁴N has P=7,N=7. Nuclear charge changes and emitted beta charge maintains the total electric-charge balance. The relation is isobaric.

Potassium-40 to calcium-40

A beta-minus branch of potassium-40 produces calcium-40. Again A stays forty while Z increases from nineteen to twenty. Nuclear and electron-antineutrino details are part of a complete nuclear reaction; merely moving an orbital electron is not the process.

The possibility of decay requires energetics

Algebra predicts which daughter Z and A follow beta-minus emission, but it does not tell you that a particular nuclide actually decays in this way. Whether the channel is open depends on nuclear energetics and the measured decay scheme.

β⁻: (A,Z,N) → (A,Z+1,N−1) + e⁻ + antineutrino

1.A.1.20.16 Beta-plus decay and electron capture: opposite direction across an A family

In beta-plus decay a proton converts to a neutron

A parent emits a positron and electron neutrino, decreasing nuclear Z by one and increasing N by one while A remains constant. Therefore an energetically allowed beta-plus parent and daughter are isobars.

Electron capture uses an atomic electron

In electron capture, a proton captures an electron and transforms into a neutron, with an electron neutrino emitted. The nucleus changes Z→Z−1, N→N+1, A unchanged. The captured electron is part of the atomic environment; the element changes, even though the mass number stays fixed.

Examples need the actual decay route

Beryllium-7 can decay to lithium-7 by electron capture, giving another isobaric parent–daughter pair. However one must not assume every nuclide with an isobaric partner undergoes positron emission or capture. Actual branching and rates come from nuclear data.

Radioactive pathways are not a classroom recipe

These are conceptual descriptions of nuclear transformations. Medical or laboratory radionuclide handling and production require professional facilities and regulatory controls, not instructions in a general teaching note.

β⁺: (A,Z,N) → (A,Z−1,N+1) + e⁺ + neutrino;   EC: (A,Z) + e⁻ → (A,Z−1) + neutrino

1.A.1.20.17 Gamma emission and nuclear isomers: neither an ordinary isotope nor a new isobar

Gamma rays change nuclear excitation energy, not nucleon composition

During ordinary gamma de-excitation, a nucleus releases electromagnetic radiation while keeping Z, N and A unchanged. The resulting nucleus remains the same nuclide in a lower energy state.

The superscript m means metastable nuclear state

Technetium-99m has the same Z=43 and A=99 as technetium-99, but is in a different nuclear energy state. Its transition toward the ground state can emit gamma radiation or undergo internal conversion pathways. It is not a separate isotope with another neutron.

An important nested relationship

Molybdenum-99 and technetium-99m have A=99 but Z=42 versus 43, so they are isobars. Technetium-99m and technetium-99 are nuclear isomers with the same A and Z. One sequence therefore illustrates two different kinds of relationship that must not be confused.

Isomer versus isobar in the exam

The letter m after mass number is not an ordinary molecular mass, proton count, electron charge, or definition of a ‘radioactive isotope’ in general. It indicates an excited nuclear energy state that can persist measurably.

⁹⁹Mo (Z=42) → ⁹⁹ᵐTc (Z=43) isobaric daughter; ⁹⁹ᵐTc → ⁹⁹Tc is isomeric transition.

1.A.1.20.18 The chart-of-nuclides geometry under beta and gamma transformations

Beta-minus moves diagonally in N–Z coordinates

If Z is vertical and N is horizontal, beta-minus raises Z by one and lowers N by one. Therefore it moves to a different point along the same A=N+Z diagonal. The parent/daughter A remains fixed, so the path is isobaric.

Beta-plus and electron capture move the other diagonal way

Both decrease Z by one and increase N by one, again along a constant-A line. The detailed weak-interaction products differ, but the family relation of the nuclei stays isobaric.

Gamma transition stays at the same coordinate

Because Z and N are unchanged, a gamma nuclear transition does not move the nuclide to a new position on the basic chart. Energy-state information requires a separate layer beyond just coordinates.

Conceptual nuclear transformation map on constant-A lines. Beta changes Z and N oppositely; gamma retains both.

1.A.1.20.19 Nuclear stability: mass number alone cannot predict it

Equal A can contain both stable and unstable nuclides

For A=14, carbon-14 is radioactive and nitrogen-14 is stable. For A=40, potassium-40 is radioactive while argon-40 and calcium-40 are stable. So isobaric partners can show dramatically different half-lives or stable behavior.

Equal N does not guarantee equal stability

For N=8, carbon-14 is radioactive but nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 are stable. Nuclear stability reflects proton–neutron interactions, binding energy, nuclear level structure and decay energetics, not simply an equal neutron count.

The stable nuclei occupy a region of the N–Z chart

The balance of neutrons and protons that supports stability changes across the periodic table. Very light stable nuclides often have roughly similar N and Z; for heavier nuclei, stable neutron numbers commonly exceed proton numbers. This is a tendency rather than a universal arithmetic test.

Nuclear shell closures are additional clues

The shell-model magic numbers for protons or neutrons can contribute to greater stability in some nuclei. Yet a magic neutron count alone cannot certify a nuclide as stable. Use actual evaluated nuclear data for specific decay claims.

Exam checkpoint: ‘isobar’, ‘isotone’ and ‘isotope’ classify nuclear counts. None of these names alone proves that a nuclide is stable, radioactive, biologically useful, or safe.

1.A.1.20.20 The nuclear binding-energy picture behind isobars

The binding energy is not identical for every nucleus of mass number A

The balance of strong nuclear forces, Coulomb repulsion, asymmetry and pairing contributions influences nuclear binding. Isobars have the same nucleon total but different proton/neutron partitions, so their binding energies and masses can differ.

The mass parabola as a qualitative model

For a fixed A, simple semi-empirical nuclear mass models often represent mass versus Z by a parabola-like curve, possibly with pairing-dependent branches. Beta decay can sometimes move a nucleus toward energetically favored isobars. The exact scheme requires measured masses and allowed decay channels.

Not every isobaric neighbor can beta-decay

Energy conservation, quantum selection rules and nuclear states influence whether a given transition occurs. Merely writing ‘A fixed and Z changes by one’ gives a possible daughter identity but does not prove a spontaneous decay or calculate a half-life.

This is advanced context, not a calculation from A alone

A complete Q-value calculation must use actual evaluated atomic or nuclear masses and correct treatment of emitted leptons. Avoid inventing precise nuclear energies where only mass-number labels are provided.

For fixed A, a qualitative model uses nuclear mass M(A,Z) as a function of Z; the minimum generally relates to favored isobars.

1.A.1.20.21 Isobaric analog states: same A is a starting point, not the full story

An advanced nuclear-structure idea

Isobaric analog states describe related nuclear states in different isobaric nuclides connected through approximate isospin symmetry, where proton and neutron can be treated as related nucleon states in nuclear interactions. The concept is not equivalent to saying all isobars have identical spin or energy.

Why nuclear physicists compare isobaric systems

Such comparisons can help investigate strong-interaction properties, Coulomb effects and selection rules across nuclei sharing A. Yet real Coulomb forces and neutron/proton mass differences break perfect symmetry, so energy levels need not be equal.

What the life-science student should retain

For an introductory CSIR-NET life-science syllabus, correctly classify isobars and understand beta transformations first. Isobaric analog states provide an optional deeper nuclear-structure connection rather than a requirement to solve ordinary isotope-count numericals.

1.A.1.20.22 Mass spectrometry: nominal isobaric interference versus exact mass

Mass spectrometry sees m/z, not just printed A

Two atomic ions associated with different nuclides can share nominal mass number and similar m/z. Depending on resolving power, they may interfere or be separated. Their different exact isotope masses and different chemical species matter.

Argon-40 and calcium-40 illustrate a recognized issue

Argon-based ICP-MS can face interference from ⁴⁰Ar⁺ in measuring ⁴⁰Ca⁺ because both lie near nominal m/z 40. These are isobars by A=40 and distinct elements. Instrument designs may use higher resolution, collision/reaction technology or alternate isotopes and corrections.

Do not equate interference with indistinguishable atoms

An isotope label is not destroyed by an interfering ion. The issue is separation and attribution of instrument signals. A reported mass spectrum should state the charge state, formula, resolution and corrections where relevant.

Molecular polyatomic interferences are a related but distinct category

Ions built from several atoms can coincide in nominal mass with a single atomic ion. That is an analytical mass interference, but the molecules are not necessarily a pair of nuclear isobars: the term ‘isobaric interference’ is broader in instrumental contexts.

Measured spacing Δ(m/z) ≈ Δm / |z| for comparable ion charge states; integer A is not an m/z measurement.

1.A.1.20.23 Isotones and the science of nuclear shell patterns

Fix N and compare how proton number changes binding

Along an isotone chain, nuclear neutron count is constant while Z and A rise together. Such comparisons can reveal nuclear structure changes and shell effects across different elements. The shared neutrons do not imply identical nuclear excited states.

An N=8 family makes the limitations concrete

Carbon-14, nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16 all have eight neutrons. Yet carbon-14 radioactivity differs from the stability of nitrogen-15 and oxygen-16. Proton number affects Coulomb energy, nuclear state structure and allowed transformations.

An N=20 illustrative relationship

Calcium-40 has N=20 and potassium-39 has Z=19,N=20. Both form an isotone pair, with different elements and mass numbers. This also illustrates a commonly discussed shell closure, but it is not a rule that every possible N=20 nuclide is equally stable.

Nuclear categories cannot substitute for biology

An organism may contain both potassium and calcium compounds, but the presence of a fixed-neutron isotone relation among particular nuclei gives no immediate prediction of uptake, ionic function, toxicity or metabolism.

1.A.1.20.24 Isoelectronic, isodiaphers and nuclear isomers: nearby terms

Isoelectronic is an electronic description

Species are isoelectronic when they possess the same electron count (and sometimes an analogous electronic configuration, depending on the context). Neon, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ each have ten electrons. Their nuclei have different proton counts; some specified isotopic choices can additionally form isotone pairs, but this is a separate calculation.

Isodiaphers share neutron excess N−Z

For deeper nuclear-chart terminology, isodiaphers are nuclides with equal N−Z, also called the neutron excess or isotopic difference. This is another line on an N–Z chart, distinct from equal A, equal N and equal Z. For example ¹⁴C has N−Z=2, and ¹⁸O has N−Z=2; they share neutron excess but do not form an isotope, isobar or isotone pair.

Nuclear isomers share the same Z, N and A but differ in energy state

⁹⁹ᵐTc and ⁹⁹Tc illustrate isomerism. Their metastable distinction is nuclear excitation. They are not different isotopes under the usual different-A definition, and ionization would not change their nuclear isomer state by itself.

Isotonic solution is an unrelated biological word

An isotonic solution in physiology concerns effective osmotic pressure relative to a reference and cell water movement; it is not a solution made of nuclear isotones. Similar word roots can mislead students when nuclear and cell biology terminology mix.

1.A.1.20.25 Three-way worked classification before the advanced exercises

Example: ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C

Each is carbon Z=6 with A=12,13,14, giving N=6,7,8. They are isotope members of one element. Only one of this set is radioactive, showing that isotope status is independent of decay behavior.

Example: ¹⁴C, ¹⁴N

The A values match at 14 while Z differs 6 versus 7, so isobars. Neutron values 8 versus 7 establish they are not isotones. The radioactive parent–daughter link adds a physical story but is not required for classification.

Example: ¹⁴C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁶O

Neutron count is eight in each: (14−6), (15−7), (16−8). They are isotones of different elements. Mass numbers differ so they cannot be isobars.

Example: ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K, ⁴⁰Ca

All have A=40 so the family is isobaric. Their Z values are 18,19,20 and N values are 22,21,20. The element labels and physical behavior differ. This illustrates isobaric families with more than two members.

Example: ²³Na⁺ and ²⁴Mg²⁺

The nuclei are isotones (N=12 for both). Their specified ions have ten electrons each, so they are also isoelectronic as ions. One word classifies nuclear neutrons, the other electronic charge states.
