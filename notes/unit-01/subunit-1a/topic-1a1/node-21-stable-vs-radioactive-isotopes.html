1.A.1.21.1 A laboratory mystery: two hydrogen labels with different nuclear futures

One atom of hydrogen is not the whole story

Imagine comparing two water samples that contain different hydrogen isotope labels. Deuterium ²H contains one proton and one neutron; tritium ³H contains one proton and two neutrons. They belong to the same chemical element, yet deuterium is stable while tritium is radioactive. The question is not which is 'more hydrogen', but why nuclear composition affects spontaneous transformation.

The chemical reaction and nuclear event are distinct

Stable deuterium can enter water or a biomolecule and leave it through ordinary chemical or biological processes, retaining its nucleus. Tritium can also participate in hydrogen chemistry, but its nucleus additionally has a probability of beta-minus transformation into helium-3. The decay is a nuclear process rather than enzymatic hydrogen exchange.

A surprising contrast from carbon

Stable carbon-13 and radioactive carbon-14 both have six protons. Carbon-13 can be used for nonradioactive metabolite labeling, while carbon-14 can support radioactive tracer work or dating under specific conditions. Neither element name nor the fact of being heavier is enough to classify stability.

A study goal more rigorous than memorizing two definitions

We will reconstruct discoveries, examine nuclear structure and weak decay, derive activity and half-life equations, then move to isotope measurements, nutrition, photosynthesis, environmental clocks and nuclear medicine. At each step we distinguish what the isotope signal shows from what requires another instrument, measurement or model.

Study objective: identify stable and radioactive isotopes with proper nuclear counting, calculate decay and activity correctly, and interpret their biological use while separating nuclear physics from metabolic turnover.

1.A.1.21.2 From Becquerel to modern isotope science: chronological discoveries

Radioactivity challenged the permanent-atom picture

In 1896 Henri Becquerel observed spontaneous penetrating radiation associated with uranium. Scientists had understood many chemical changes as exchanges among atoms, but the observation revealed atomic matter could generate emissions through phenomena not explained by chemical bonding or light.

The Curies extended the evidence

Marie and Pierre Curie's work systematically investigated radioactive materials and expanded the understanding of their nuclear-related properties. Early experimentalists classified emissions by their interactions; alpha, beta and gamma distinctions developed before complete modern nuclear theory.

Nuclear and neutron discoveries made classification precise

Rutherford's nuclear model, Moseley's atomic-number work, the developing isotope concept and Chadwick's neutron discovery together clarified why an element can contain nuclides with different mass numbers. Nuclear stability could then be studied as a property of specific proton-neutron configurations.

Measurements evolved along two different branches

Mass spectrometry, isotope-ratio methods and nuclear magnetic resonance help characterize stable isotopes in materials. Radiation counters, scintillation methods, gamma spectroscopy and accelerator mass spectrometry serve some radionuclide studies. The instrument and the question determine which nuclear or molecular signal is measured.

1.A.1.21.3 A,Z,N first: radioactivity begins with a definite nuclide

Protons define the element

The nuclide symbol ᴬ_ZX specifies mass number A and proton number Z. Neutron count N=A−Z; a neutral atom has Z electrons. Chemical ionization changes electron count, not the nuclear isotope, so a positive ion is not a new radioactive nuclide.

Calculate before comparing status

For ¹³C, Z=6 and N=7. For ¹⁴C, Z=6 and N=8. The same Z means they are carbon isotopes. Carbon-13 is stable while carbon-14 is radioactive, a classification established from nuclear properties rather than inferred from the fact that N is one larger.

The N/Z ratio is a clue, not a formula for stability

Very light stable nuclides often have approximately similar proton and neutron numbers. Heavier stable nuclides often require more neutrons than protons. But N=Z is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for all stability, and simply checking N>Z cannot identify a radionuclide.

Nuclide composition and energy state are different

A nucleus can be in a metastable excited state with the same A and Z, such as technetium-99m versus technetium-99. Nuclear excitation and transitions are important even when the number of neutrons does not change.

P=Z;   N=A−Z;   E(neutral)=Z;   E(monatomic ion)=Z−q.

1.A.1.21.4 The definition of nuclear stability and its experimental limits

Stable means no spontaneous radioactive decay is observed

In standard usage, a stable nuclide has no known spontaneous radioactive decay. That does not mean it is chemically unreactive or exempt from nuclear transformations caused by an external physical interaction. The description applies to the nucleus, not to the food, solvent or organism containing it.

Extremely rare decay can complicate a strict binary label

Experiments have eventually identified extraordinarily long-lived decay of some species historically regarded as stable, such as bismuth-209. Therefore the epistemic distinction is that no spontaneous decay is observed or assigned within the relevant nuclear data, rather than a philosophical proof of zero probability for all time.

Stable isotopes can alter reactions through mass effects

Replacing a light isotope by a heavier stable isotope sometimes changes vibrational frequencies, equilibrium fractionation and reaction rates. Hydrogen-to-deuterium substitutions are especially mass-sensitive. A stable isotope label is not necessarily chemically inert at any amount or position.

The label stays with the nucleus during ordinary chemistry

A ¹³C nucleus in glucose stays ¹³C when the carbon atom enters another product in metabolism. The label may leave a measured pool through transport or exchange, but that disappearance is not radioactive nuclear decay.

1.A.1.21.5 Radioactive isotopes: what spontaneous nuclear transformation means

The radionuclide definition

A radioactive nuclide has a nuclear energy state or composition that spontaneously transforms with characteristic probability over time. A transformation can change the element or change only its nuclear energy level; emissions may include alpha or beta particles, gamma photons and other decay products.

Radioisotope and radionuclide are related words

Radioisotope typically emphasizes a radioactive isotope among an element's nuclides. Radionuclide more generally describes a radioactive nuclear species. Neither word means all isotopes of the element are radioactive.

An individual event is random in time

One cannot predict the exact moment an individual tritium nucleus will decay. For large populations, however, survival probabilities yield a reproducible characteristic decay constant and half-life. A half-life describes expected populations, not half of one atom.

The daughter can belong to another element

Tritium becomes helium-3 by beta-minus decay; carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14. By contrast, ordinary gamma de-excitation can retain the same elemental identity and nuclear composition. The emitted radiation and daughter must be identified per nuclide.

1.A.1.21.6 Reference comparison table: stable and radioactive representatives

Hydrogen, carbon and oxygen expose the pattern

Deuterium ²H is stable and tritium ³H radioactive. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable but carbon-14 radioactive. Oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 are stable. Thus neutron count affects nuclear physics without creating a monotonic ‘heavier equals radioactive’ rule.

Some useful radionuclide lifetimes

Approximate physical half-lives include tritium 12.32 years, carbon-14 5730 years, phosphorus-32 14.3 days, iodine-131 about eight days and fluorine-18 around 109.77 minutes. Precision and reference should be stated when performing professional nuclear calculations.

Do not treat stable isotope abundance as an activity

A material with 1% ¹³C has an atom-fraction property. It does not possess ¹³C radioactive activity because that isotope is stable. A very small quantity of tritium can produce measurable activity due to its decay probability.

Be cautious about stable isotope safety

The absence of radioactive decay does not prove the chemical compound is suitable for ingestion or clinical use at an arbitrary concentration. Research and medical applications follow study-specific safety practices.

1.A.1.21.7 Hydrogen isotopes: protium, deuterium and tritium

Three nuclei, one element

¹H contains one proton and no neutrons; ²H contains one proton and one neutron; ³H contains one proton and two neutrons. In neutral atoms each has one electron, because the shared atomic number is Z=1. Their nuclear stabilities differ despite their related chemistry.

Stable deuterium in living-water studies

Deuterium-labeled water can be followed through changes in isotope enrichment. In properly designed nutrition and body-water work, the dilution and elimination of a stable label may provide quantitative information with mass-balance and model assumptions. The isotope's nucleus does not radioactively vanish.

Tritium is a beta-minus emitter

Tritium spontaneously transforms into helium-3, with emission of an electron and an antineutrino. Its physical half-life near 12.32 years refers to parent tritium nuclear populations and is not equal to the water residence time in a body or the age of every groundwater sample.

The two types of measurement should not be mixed

Deuterium enrichment is an isotope ratio or atom fraction; tritium may additionally be reported as activity or tritium units. Without proper conversion and definitions these quantities cannot be compared as if they were the same number.

³₁H → ³₂He + e⁻ + antineutrino;   ²₁H is stable.

1.A.1.21.8 Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14: chemistry plus a nuclear clock

Three carbon mass numbers with shared proton identity

Carbon always has Z=6. ¹²C has six neutrons, ¹³C has seven and ¹⁴C has eight. The first two are stable; carbon-14 is radioactive. Carbon-13 nuclear spin makes ¹³C NMR possible, whereas carbon-12 has spin zero.

Metabolic carbon tracing with ¹³C

A labeled nutrient can supply ¹³C atoms to metabolites, and an instrument may measure their mass isotopologue distributions. The presence of a label supports incorporation or exchange under the study conditions but does not alone determine the exact metabolic flux.

Carbon-14 decays to nitrogen-14

A neutron becomes a proton in beta-minus decay, so A remains fourteen while Z rises from six to seven. Its physical half-life near 5730 years supports radiocarbon methods, which still require reference conditions, sample history, corrections and calibration.

A biological signal needs more than a nucleus label

Carbon isotope distributions in organisms reflect sources, fractionation, synthesis, respiration and turnover. A quantitative statement about one pathway cannot come only from observing ¹³C or ¹⁴C in one final specimen.

¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + antineutrino;   ¹³C remains stable under ordinary metabolism.

1.A.1.21.9 Stable nitrogen and oxygen isotopes in life-science research

Nitrogen-15

Stable ¹⁵N contains seven protons and eight neutrons. A label can track nitrogen from a defined source into amino acids and proteins. Measuring ¹⁵N enrichment tells us about label incorporation, not automatically the absolute synthesis rate of those molecules.

Oxygen-18

Stable ¹⁸O contains eight protons and ten neutrons. It is valuable for water isotope ratios and for tracing the oxygen atoms that form molecular oxygen during oxygenic photosynthesis. Its use does not rely on radioactive nuclear decay.

Stable isotope detection differs by nuclear spin

Carbon-13 and nitrogen-15 each have nuclear spin 1/2 and can be NMR-active. Oxygen-18 has spin zero, while oxygen-17 has spin 5/2 and can be studied with appropriate NMR methods. The magnetic resonance response is not a radioactive decay signal.

Environmental processes change isotope proportions without transmutation

Evaporation or condensation can fractionate hydrogen and oxygen stable isotopes; the nuclei remain the same chemical elements. The ratio change reflects physical partitioning, not conversion from oxygen-16 to oxygen-18.

1.A.1.21.11 Why nuclear stability differs: binding energy and nuclear forces

Two competing physical ingredients

Protons repel electrically because they are positively charged, while strong nuclear interactions among nearby nucleons contribute binding. The resulting nuclear stability depends on the full quantum configuration, available transformations and energy differences between initial and final nuclear states.

Neutrons are part of the binding pattern

Adding neutrons changes the nuclear interaction and total mass without increasing the nucleus's positive charge by one. In heavier stable nuclides, extra neutrons often help balance proton-related repulsion, but there is no universal straight-line condition for all elements.

Mass defect and energy

Nuclear binding energy relates to the difference between appropriate constituent masses and the measured bound-nucleus mass. The mass–energy principle E=Δmc² is useful, but exact decay energetics require actual masses rather than the integer A label.

Quantum structure can make selection rules decisive

Nuclear angular momentum, parity and available energy levels can make a transition allowed, suppressed or forbidden. Thus even two neighboring isotopes may have dramatically different half-lives, and knowing N/Z alone cannot calculate a decay constant.

1.A.1.21.12 Nuclear shell model, pairing and the valley of stability

Shell effects create recognizable structure

Nuclear shell closures are associated with commonly discussed magic proton or neutron counts such as 2, 8, 20, 28, 50 and 82, with 126 important for neutrons. Shell structure influences energetic stability, but an N=8 nucleus is not automatically stable at every possible Z.

Proton-rich and neutron-rich tendencies

Some neutron-rich nuclides can lower energy through beta-minus decay, while proton-rich nuclides may undergo positron emission or electron capture. These are broad nuclear-physics tendencies; actual spontaneous transformations require energetics and allowed nuclear states.

The chart of nuclides provides context

A plot of proton and neutron numbers shows stable nuclei concentrated in a band. The region is not a line defined by N=Z for all elements. Experimental nuclide tables are necessary for determining whether a particular species is stable, short-lived or extraordinarily long-lived.

An isobaric neighbor can differ in stability

Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 share mass number A=14 but have different proton counts; one is radioactive and the other stable. Equal total nucleons cannot imply equal binding, mass, decay paths or chemical properties.

1.A.1.21.13 Alpha decay and its conservation laws

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus

It has two protons and two neutrons, meaning A=4,Z=2 and charge +2. A parent nucleus emitting an alpha produces a daughter with A decreased by four and Z by two. This is a nuclear transformation distinct from ordinary chemical release of helium.

Work through uranium-238

A schematic reaction ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He conserves nucleon count (238=234+4) and nuclear electric charge (92=90+2). Real energies and associated emissions depend on the specific nuclear states.

Radiation interactions are type-specific

Alpha particles lose energy rapidly in many materials and can have short ranges. However external penetration does not fully determine risk; internal incorporation can create a different biological exposure situation. Radiation protection must be assessed professionally.

Not all radioactive isotopes are alpha emitters

Tritium and carbon-14 predominantly undergo beta-minus decay, while fluorine-18 is positron-emitting. Decay type and branch fractions are properties of each radionuclide, not of radioactivity as one universal category.

Alpha bookkeeping: ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ⁻⁴_{Z−2}Y + ⁴₂He.

1.A.1.21.14 Beta-minus decay: the neutron-to-proton transformation

A weak nuclear event

In beta-minus decay, one nuclear neutron converts to a proton. An electron and electron antineutrino are emitted, and the daughter has Z increased by one, N decreased by one, with the same A. This is not simply removing an orbital electron.

Carbon-14 and tritium examples

Carbon-14 transforms to nitrogen-14, and tritium to helium-3. Their decay rates are very different, but both follow the same beta-minus nuclear bookkeeping. Their emitted electron kinetic energies form spectra rather than one fixed value for every beta particle.

A and electric charge must be balanced separately

The daughter gains one unit of nuclear charge, balanced by the emitted negative electron. Lepton bookkeeping also requires the antineutrino. Checking only mass numbers is not enough to establish a complete nuclear equation.

Energetic feasibility is not given by algebra

If a question stipulates beta-minus decay, the daughter is found by Z+1 at constant A. Whether a specific nucleus actually decays by this mode and with what half-life requires nuclear data.

β⁻: ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ_{Z+1}Y + e⁻ + electron antineutrino.

1.A.1.21.15 Positron emission and electron capture: the proton-rich direction

Beta-plus emission

A proton in a nucleus changes to a neutron while a positron and electron neutrino are emitted. A remains unchanged, Z decreases by one and N increases by one. Fluorine-18 transforming to oxygen-18 provides an important positron-emitting example.

Positron annihilation and imaging

A positron can interact with an electron and annihilate, producing photons. Professionally designed PET systems detect related emissions to infer tracer distribution. The nuclide alone does not determine where a labeled compound travels.

Electron capture differs from beta-plus

An atomic electron can be captured by the nucleus, converting a proton to a neutron with neutrino emission. This also changes Z to Z−1 at constant A, but its emitted particles and energetic thresholds differ from positron emission.

The exact daughter and branch need evidence

Mass-number accounting identifies possible daughter nuclear composition. Branching fractions and decay energies require evaluated nuclear masses and state data; not every possible beta decay is actually open.

β⁺: ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ_{Z−1}Y + e⁺ + electron neutrino;   EC: ᴬ_ZX + e⁻ → ᴬ_{Z−1}Y + neutrino.

1.A.1.21.16 Gamma transitions and nuclear metastable isomers

Energy can be released without changing nucleon count

A nucleus in an excited state may transition to a lower state by gamma emission or internal conversion. Its proton number, neutron number and mass number may remain unchanged, so the emission is not necessarily elemental transmutation.

The meaning of the m symbol

Technetium-99m is a metastable excited state of technetium-99. Both have Z=43,A=99,N=56. The m denotes energy-state isomerism, not an additional neutron, a separate elemental identity or an ionization state.

A gamma photon is not a beta particle

Gamma emission involves electromagnetic radiation rather than a massive charged beta electron or positron. A radionuclide can also have associated photons after other nuclear transitions; one isotope's decay scheme may contain multiple emissions.

Half-life and radiation dose still need context

The physical half-life of a nuclear isomer describes the state-population transition. It does not alone determine the dose or clinical use of a radiopharmaceutical; emission energy, branching and distribution matter.

1.A.1.21.17 Beyond alpha and beta: conversion electrons and decay chains

Internal conversion

An excited nucleus can transfer transition energy directly to an atomic electron that is ejected. The electron is not created in the weak beta-minus transformation. The nuclear energy level changes but Z and A may remain fixed.

Radioactive daughters can continue decaying

Some parents produce unstable daughters, creating a decay chain with multiple nuclides and separate half-lives. The total activity detected from a mixed sample can be more complex than one exponential curve, especially when daughters accumulate.

Nuclear fission and other processes

Certain heavy radionuclides may undergo spontaneous fission, producing fragments and sometimes neutrons. Such processes require their own conservation and energy considerations; not every radioactive species decays by the same four textbook categories.

The safe conceptual limit

Our goal is to understand transformations and measurement interpretation, not to produce or handle radioactive materials. Actual research requires specialist authorization and radiation-protection practices.

1.A.1.21.18 Half-life explained through a growing and shrinking population

The population story

An initial collection of 8,000 radioactive parent nuclei has expected survivors 4,000 after one half-life, 2,000 after two, 1,000 after three and 500 after four. Each interval removes approximately half of what remains, not a fixed 4,000 nuclei every time.

Decay is statistical, not deterministic for individual nuclei

No individual nucleus gets split into fractions or is guaranteed to decay at the half-life instant. Each nucleus has a survival probability; the exponential law gives the expected fraction for a sufficiently characterized population.

A consistent mathematical statement

For one isolated radionuclide with constant decay constant λ, N(t)=N₀e^(−λt)=N₀(1/2)^(t/T½). The half-life T½ is the elapsed time that halves the expected surviving parent count or, for that isolated nuclide, its activity.

Do not apply radioactive half-life to stable labels

A falling deuterium concentration in saliva may represent biological washout, not a transformation of ²H nuclei. Isotope-label disappearance from one measured compartment and spontaneous nuclear decay are different physical processes.

N(t)/N₀ = e^(−λt) = (1/2)^(t/T½);   T½=ln2/λ.

1.A.1.21.19 Derive the exponential law and decay constant

Write the first-order decay relation

Each surviving parent nucleus has a constant total decay probability per unit time λ. The expected parent population obeys dN/dt=−λN, where λ has units of reciprocal time and N is an expected count of undecayed parent nuclei.

Integrate from an initial condition

Separating gives dN/N=−λdt. Between t=0 and t, integrate ln[N(t)/N₀]=−λt. Exponentiating yields N(t)=N₀e^(−λt), which explains the curved rather than linear decrease.

Find the half-life

Setting N(T½)/N₀=1/2 gives e^(−λT½)=1/2, so T½=ln2/λ. The mean lifetime τ=1/λ≈1.443T½, different from the half-life.

State the model's limitations

This expression applies to a defined parent radionuclide with a constant total decay constant. It does not describe every observed count stream when multiple daughter nuclides, transport processes or detector backgrounds are mixed in.

dN/dt=−λN ⇒ N=N₀e^(−λt);   λ=ln2/T½;   mean life τ=1/λ.

1.A.1.21.20 Activity and the becquerel: a nuclear-rate unit

Activity is proportional to the number of radioactive parents

For a pure radionuclide population, activity 𝒜=λN. Its SI unit is the becquerel (Bq), corresponding to one expected nuclear transformation per second. Activity can decline as the parent population declines even if individual nuclei retain the same λ.

Half-life and atom count both influence activity

Two radionuclide samples can contain equal numbers of parent atoms but have different activities because their decay constants differ. A shorter half-life implies a larger λ for that species, but dose to people still depends on emission, distribution and exposure pathways.

Detector counts are not always identical to disintegrations

A measured count rate depends on detection efficiency, branching, background and geometry. Raw counts per second do not automatically represent source activity without appropriate calibration and interpretation.

Avoid the letter A ambiguity

The mass number A=Z+N is an integer nuclear composition label; activity 𝒜 is a rate. They should be written distinctly even though different textbooks sometimes use the same letter A for both.

Activity 𝒜=λN;   1 Bq = one nuclear transformation per second.

1.A.1.21.21 Activity, absorbed dose and radiation-weighted dose are not synonyms

Becquerel: events per time

Activity in Bq quantifies how frequently nuclear transformations occur in a radionuclide population. It does not state how much energy is deposited in a particular human tissue.

Gray: absorbed energy per mass

Absorbed dose is measured in gray (Gy), with 1 Gy=1 joule per kilogram of absorbing material. Deriving this from a source requires emission energies, transport, absorption and geometry.

Sievert: specified radiation-weighted dose quantities

Equivalent and effective dose are defined in radiation protection using additional weighting and tissue concepts, with units of sievert (Sv). One cannot convert arbitrary Bq directly into Sv without source, route and biokinetic data.

Stable does not equal chemically harmless

Stable isotope compounds may still have chemical toxicity or altered biological effects at high isotopic enrichment. Safety must consider the compound and context, even though the label itself does not produce spontaneous nuclear radiation.

1.A.1.21.22 A physical half-life is not a biological clearance half-life

Nuclear decay occurs whether or not the molecule moves

A radionuclide has its intrinsic physical decay rate. A labeled compound can leave blood or a tissue through transport, metabolism or excretion even while its nuclei remain undecayed.

Combined rate under an explicitly simple model

If radioactive decay and biological elimination are independent first-order processes from one compartment, λ_eff=λ_phys+λ_bio. In half-life form, 1/T_eff=1/T_phys+1/T_bio. Multi-compartment behavior may not obey one effective exponential.

A stable label can have a measured disappearance half-time

Deuterium-containing water may become less enriched in a sampled body-water pool because label mixes and is eliminated. That observation is a biological isotope-turnover process even though the deuterium nucleus remains stable.

Define the measured endpoint before calculating

Is it parent activity, tracer concentration in blood, isotope ratio in urine, or a radioactive daughter signal? Each can vary in different ways. A number called half-life is ambiguous without identifying the physical process and compartment.

Independent first-order losses only: 1/T_eff = 1/T_phys + 1/T_bio.

1.A.1.21.23 Stable-isotope analytical measurements: mass and nuclear spin

Stable isotope ratios

A sample's ¹³C/¹²C or ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio can be measured by an appropriate isotope-sensitive method. Ratios compare counts of isotope-bearing atoms, not nuclear transformations per second. A fraction x=heavy/total and ratio R=heavy/light differ because of their denominators.

Delta notation and reference scales

For certain isotope measurements, δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰. Carbon δ¹³C is commonly expressed relative to VPDB; water δ¹⁸O relative to VSMOW. The negative sign indicates lower ratio than reference, not absence of stable isotope atoms.

NMR-active nuclides are not automatically radioactive

Carbon-13 and nitrogen-15 are stable and spin-1/2 NMR-active. Oxygen-17 is a stable spin-5/2 isotope. Nuclear magnetic resonance and radioactive decay are different physical phenomena.

Natural isotope abundance must be considered

A nominally unlabelled biological sample still contains naturally occurring stable heavy isotopes. Tracer-enrichment interpretation requires baseline, instrument calibration and the relevant isotope ratio or mass isotopologue distribution.

For a two-isotope sample x=R/(1+R);   δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰.

1.A.1.21.24 Stable isotope dilution and deuterium body-water studies

The fundamental mass-balance idea

A known amount of isotopically labelled water mixes into a body-water pool. Measurements of deuterium enrichment before and after mixing can, with the study's corrections and assumptions, help estimate distribution volume or total body water. The deuterium does not disappear by nuclear decay.

What the analytical instrument sees

Isotope-ratio mass spectrometry or appropriately validated infrared spectrometry can measure stable deuterium enrichment in sample material. The detector is characterizing composition, rather than counting nuclear beta particles or gamma photons.

Body composition requires extra physiology

A total-body-water estimate is one input to composition assessment. Converting it into fat-free mass or fat mass depends on assumptions about hydration and the population. The isotope result alone is not a complete nutritional or clinical assessment.

No self-experimentation protocol

Research involving human participants requires a suitable approved design, informed consent, reagent characterization and clinical or institutional supervision. This chapter teaches mass balance and interpretation but does not prescribe doses or sampling procedures.

1.A.1.21.25 Doubly labelled water: ²H and ¹⁸O measure biological turnover, not decay

A well-established nutrition research application

The doubly labelled water method uses nonradioactive deuterium and oxygen-18 in water. Both isotopes are stable. Their measured enrichment in body water changes over time, allowing the research design to investigate different elimination paths.

Two isotope signals have distinct elimination routes

Deuterium is eliminated from the body chiefly in water, while oxygen-18 participates in elimination through water and CO₂. With validated equations the difference in isotope disappearance can estimate CO₂ production and, with additional conversion assumptions, total energy expenditure.

Why half-lives here are not nuclear half-lives

If deuterium and oxygen-18 signals decline at different rates, neither nucleus is radioactively decaying. The loss describes movement of isotope-bearing water and CO₂ through biological compartments, an essential contrast with tritium or carbon-14 physical decay.

Measurement is not simply 'two labels equal calories'

Quantitative energy-expenditure inference requires isotope enrichment time courses, dilution-space estimates, fractionation and model choices. This method has robust scientific uses, but a single labelled sample's concentration is not enough to calculate calories burned.

1.A.1.21.26 ¹³C tracing in metabolism and the stable-isotope urea breath-test concept

Following stable carbon through metabolism

In a ¹³C-labelled substrate, the carbon nucleus remains ¹³C during ordinary chemical reactions. Isotope-sensitive instruments may detect its appearance in a metabolite. Flux inference requires precursor enrichment, carbon atom mapping, product distributions and a suitable biochemical model.

A clinically established ¹³C example

In the ¹³C-urea breath test, urease activity associated with Helicobacter pylori can convert appropriately administered isotope-labelled urea into ¹³CO₂ detectable in breath under standardized clinical conditions. The signal arises through chemistry and isotope mass, not nuclear decay radiation.

A label is not itself a diagnosis

Clinical tests rely on validated measurements and interpretation; the presence of ¹³C in breath alone is not sufficient to identify an infection without the test protocol and context. Medication and physiological conditions can also affect results.

Distinguish it from radiocarbon

¹³C is stable, so there is no radioactive activity correction for its nucleus. ¹⁴C is radioactive and brings additional decay, measurement and radiation-safety considerations if used as a research label.

1.A.1.21.27 ¹⁵N in proteins and natural abundance isotope ecology

Stable nitrogen labels can trace assimilation

A ¹⁵N-labelled nutrient or precursor can enter proteins and other nitrogenous molecules. Its appearance demonstrates labeled nitrogen contribution or exchange under the experiment but is not by itself a quantitative synthesis rate.

Ecological use of isotope ratios

Natural δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N can help constrain food-source and trophic hypotheses. Source endmembers, tissue discrimination and baseline variation are needed to avoid treating one isotope number as a unique species or diet label.

The distinction between source and flux persists

Two metabolic conditions can produce similar isotope enrichments with different nutrient uptake rates, because pool sizes, unlabeled inputs and exchange differ. A measured isotope fraction is not the same unit as material converted per unit time.

Stable versus radioactive interpretation

Stable isotope labels can wash out of a tissue because molecules are replaced, not because their nuclei radioactively vanish. If a radioactive tracer were used, both chemical turnover and physical decay might affect the measured signal.

1.A.1.21.28 Phosphorus-32 and sulfur-35: classic radioactive biochemical labels

Stable phosphorus-31 versus radioactive phosphorus-32

Both are phosphorus with Z=15. Phosphorus-31 is stable; phosphorus-32 is a beta-minus radionuclide with a physical half-life around 14.3 days. Both can be associated with phosphorus-containing chemistry, but their nuclear detection properties differ.

Biological connections

Phosphorus appears in nucleotides, nucleic acids and phosphorylation reactions. Historically, radioactive phosphorus labels have supported tracking of phosphorus-containing compounds in authorized research. A radioactive signal is not automatically a pathway rate or mechanistic proof.

Sulfur-35 context

Sulfur-35 is another radioactive nuclide associated with historical biochemical labeling, alongside stable sulfur nuclides. Detection and decay correction require isotope-specific knowledge, whereas stable sulfur isotope ratios depend on composition measurement and fractionation.

Boundaries of the chapter

This is a theoretical comparison and not a guide to radioactive reagent preparation, administration, shielding, exposure or waste handling. Those tasks require institutional radiation-safety infrastructure.

1.A.1.21.29 Technetium-99m and fluorine-18: why imaging uses radionuclides

Different radioactive processes yield different signals

Technetium-99m emits nuclear transition radiation suitable for certain SPECT applications; fluorine-18 is a positron emitter used in some PET radiopharmaceuticals. Both are radionuclides, but their decay modes differ.

The nuclide does not specify the biological target

The attached pharmaceutical chemistry determines much of the tracer's distribution and biological interactions. A radionuclide's physical half-life cannot, by itself, predict uptake in tissue or the meaning of a scan.

Timescales guide context, not clinical choice

Fluorine-18 has a physical half-life around 109.77 minutes; technetium-99m around six hours. Selection of imaging methods involves clinical indication, tracer properties, instrumentation and radiation protection, outside a simple nuclear-classification problem.

Distinguish activity and dose

A source activity describes transformations per second; the absorbed or effective dose received by tissues depends on emissions and exposure distribution. Neither a single activity nor a half-life number provides a personal risk calculation.

1.A.1.21.30 Iodine-127 and iodine-131: same element, different nuclear behavior

Iodine-127 is the principal stable naturally occurring iodine nuclide

It has Z=53 and A=127. Iodine compounds are important in thyroid physiology, but the nuclear label alone does not describe a person's nutritional iodine state or their hormonal function.

Iodine-131 is a radioisotope

It has the same Z=53 and a greater neutron count, A=131. Its physical half-life is about eight days and it can emit beta and gamma radiation. It is used in certain professionally supervised medical contexts.

Element-specific uptake does not erase the nuclear difference

Chemical iodine pathways can influence where iodine-containing substances go, while radioactive emission and biokinetics determine radiological considerations. Both kinds of information matter independently.

An exam-ready answer

Iodine-127 and iodine-131 are isotopes of the same element, not isobars. One is stable and one radioactive. No individualized diagnosis, dose or treatment recommendation can follow from those three facts alone.

1.A.1.21.31 Carbon-14 and tritium in environmental age studies

Radiocarbon has a long nuclear time scale

Carbon-14's half-life of about 5730 years supports dating in suitable carbon systems. A sample's inferred age requires measured isotope composition, knowledge or modeling of the initial carbon source, calibration and attention to reservoir effects and contamination.

Tritium can constrain water history

Hydrogen-3 has a physical half-life around 12.32 years. Measuring tritium in groundwater can inform source and transit hypotheses, but mixing and historical input concentrations prevent a universally unique water age from one result alone.

Stable isotopes record different evidence

Natural δ²H and δ¹⁸O in water can reflect precipitation sources, evaporation and mixing. Their stable nuclear labels are not radioactive clocks; the value is a sample composition property.

Match the question to the isotope system

A material's carbon-13 ratio, carbon-14 activity and tritium content answer different questions. Their units, model inputs and uncertainties cannot be interchanged.

1.A.1.21.32 The same falling graph may represent two completely different mechanisms

A radioactive parent population falls because nuclei transform

For pure isolated radionuclides N(t) follows an exponential radioactive decay law with constant λ. Changes in N describe actual loss of that specific nuclear species.

A stable label pool can fall because molecules move

If ²H-labelled water leaves a measured compartment or becomes diluted by unlabeled water, its enrichment can decline even though no deuterium nucleus decays. A superficially similar curve can arise from compartment exchange.

Observed detector signals contain more than one cause

Detector efficiency, background, daughter emissions and biochemical transport may affect measured radioactive counts. Stable isotope measurements can depend on sample mixing, fractionation and analytical baseline. A curve without a defined observable cannot determine its mechanism.

Ask for the necessary missing information

First name the nuclide and its stability, then the sampled material and instrument, then the mathematical model. Avoid assigning a radioactive half-life to a stable-isotope washout or using biological turnover to change nuclear decay constants.

1.A.1.21.33 From radioactive atoms to activity: why the units change

An isotope count is a population, not a rate

If a sample contains N radioactive parent atoms, their expected nuclear-transformation rate is 𝒜=λN. Two nuclides with equal N and different λ will have different activities, so reporting only a material mass or atom count cannot uniquely state its Bq value.

Half-life supplies λ in a chosen time unit

Given T½ in seconds, λ=ln2/T½ has units s⁻¹ and multiplication by N gives Bq. If half-life is in years while activity is requested in Bq, the time units must be converted consistently. A numerical answer without matching units can be wrong by orders of magnitude.

The instrument observes a filtered radiation signal

A detector registers only a fraction of actual nuclear emissions depending on branching, geometry, efficiency, absorption and background. A recorded count rate is not automatically the source activity. Counting statistics and calibration can be important, particularly when an isotope signal is small.

Stable isotope populations use a different descriptor

A stable ¹³C-labeled sample can be described by isotope atom fraction, isotopologue fractions, isotope ratio or mass-spectral enrichment. Since there is no observed radioactive decay of its nucleus, simply multiplying its atoms by a radionuclide λ is physically incorrect.

Activity 𝒜=λN (Bq); λ=ln2/T½. Compare with stable label atom fraction x=N_label/N_total.

1.A.1.21.34 Half-life, mean life and the difference between nuclear counts and measured signals

Half-life is not the mean waiting time

The exponential decay law N=N₀e^(−λt) implies T½=ln2/λ, while mean lifetime τ=1/λ=T½/ln2. Because ln2≈0.693, τ≈1.443T½. The two quantities describe related but different statistics of one radioactive population.

Parents and daughters do not follow identical curves

The surviving parent population decreases with its own λ. If it creates a radioactive daughter, that daughter's population can first rise and later decline, depending on its production and decay. A total gamma count may therefore not follow one parent-only exponential.

A measured tracer can disappear through chemical transport

In blood or water, isotope-bearing molecules can move between compartments. A stable isotope enrichment may decrease even while the nuclide remains stable; a radioactive tracer may be affected by both biology and physics. The data type and compartment must be specified before choosing an equation.

Statistical fluctuations and appropriate interpretation

For a very small population or low detector count rate, stochastic variation becomes important. A half-life is a probability-based constant in the ideal model, not a promise that exactly half of every tiny discrete sample must decay.

1.A.1.21.35 Natural abundance, isotope enrichment and mass isotopologue corrections

What natural abundance means

Most natural samples already contain multiple stable isotopes of an element. Carbon commonly includes ¹²C and ¹³C; water contains ¹H, ²H and several oxygen isotope species. A ‘natural abundance M+1’ mass peak may exist even when no deliberate tracer was introduced.

A labeled molecule is not one measured isotope fraction

Mass isotopologue categories such as M+0, M+1 and M+2 count the number or nominal mass contribution of heavy isotopes under a defined analysis. Distinct positional isotopomers may share one measured category. Correct inference may require natural-abundance adjustment and chemical atom-transition information.

Equal chemical elements, isotope-dependent rates

Stable isotope substitution can affect vibrational motion and chemical kinetics. Hydrogen–deuterium changes are often particularly influential, while isotope-ratio fractionation in water and carbon pools affects environmental or ecological interpretations.

Research statements should name the reference

A δ¹³C or δ¹⁸O value without the relevant ratio and reference scale is incomplete. Sample isotopic enrichment and radioactive activity use different units and should not be merged into one chart without explicit transformations.

1.A.1.21.36 Radioactive decay schemes and contamination versus irradiation

A radioactive substance is not the same as an irradiated object

Irradiation means a material is exposed to radiation from a source; radioactive contamination means radioactive material is present on or within it. An exposed object does not automatically become radioactive through every irradiation scenario. Activation is a distinct nuclear process under suitable radiation and target conditions.

A source can have multiple nuclear emissions

Some radionuclides decay to daughter states that emit additional photons or particles. Describing only a parent half-life does not enumerate all emissions, branching probabilities or resulting tissue dose. These details must come from evaluated nuclide data.

Environmental isotope measurements require source history

Tritium or carbon-14 in water may be measured as ratios or activities, but age inference also depends on initial concentrations, radioactive decay, mixing, reservoirs and contamination. Stable δ²H and δ¹⁸O may help constrain water origins without being nuclear clocks.

No general handling procedure follows from isotope classification

A textbook list of alpha or beta emissions does not provide the material, geometry, physical form or radiological assessment needed for real safety decisions. The appropriate procedures are regulated and study-specific.

1.A.1.21.37 Choosing a tracer for life-science research: the logic of the question

Define what you want to learn

If the question is whether glucose-derived carbon reaches a metabolite, stable ¹³C may suit mass-isotopologue tracing. If the question concerns body-water turnover or free-living energy expenditure, ²H and ¹⁸O are established stable isotope labels. A radioactive label is selected only when its signal properties and the study's regulatory framework are appropriate.

Separate the atom-source claim from the rate claim

Finding a labeled atom in a product supports contribution or exchange but does not uniquely determine a metabolic pathway or its flux. Rate estimation requires precursor enrichment, time, pool sizes, network models and analytical validation as relevant.

Plan the measurement with the label

IRMS, conventional MS, NMR, accelerator mass spectrometry or radiation detectors provide different observables. A short half-life, stable-isotope mass shift or nuclear spin cannot alone tell you which instrument is suitable without knowing the compound and scientific endpoint.

Plan ethical and safety review

For living participants or radioactive material, appropriate institutional oversight and qualified practice are required. Even a stable label should not be described as universally safe in any dose or molecular form. The study design governs the permissible use.

Scientific tracer inference: nucleus label → measured signal → model-supported biological interpretation.

1.A.1.21.38 Scientific QA and responsible interpretation: twelve checks before concluding

Nuclear and molecular identity

First state Z, A and N of the label and whether the decay status is supported by evaluated nuclide data. Then identify its chemical carrier: water, glucose, urea, a protein, a radionuclide-labeled tracer or an environmental carbonate. The nuclear and molecular labels answer different questions.

Data and calibrations

Specify which observable was actually measured: isotope atom fraction, δ ratio, mass isotopologue abundance, detector counts, Bq activity, dissolved gas or clinical imaging signal. Record reference scales, backgrounds, timing, detector corrections, decay constants and model assumptions where relevant.

Competing explanations

Check for stable isotope exchange, tracer dilution, biological transport, daughter buildup or mixed sources. A decreasing signal can reflect several mechanisms; do not automatically assign nuclear decay or a unique pathway.

The defensible conclusion

Write one sentence about direct observation and a second about model-based interpretation with its uncertainties. This habit is vital for reliable CSIR-NET and SET problem solving as well as real research.

The complete worked-problem ladder

We will progress through particle counts and isotope status, activity in Bq, conversion from physical half-life to λ, fractional survival and decay time, stable isotope dilution and δ values, effective half-life toy models, and the interpretation of nuclear medicine or ecological data.

Why verification matters

Each problem states its assumptions and includes arithmetic or physical checks. Units, conservation of A and Z, probability bounds and appropriate measurement types are checked before assigning a numerical result.

Part I — Twenty foundational isotope, decay and half-life numericals

Worked example 1. Count particles in stable deuterium

Problem: Neutral ²₁H is given. Determine protons, neutrons, electrons and stability.

Complete solution: Z=1 means one proton and one neutral-atom electron. A=2 gives N=2−1=1 neutron. Deuterium is a stable hydrogen isotope with no observed spontaneous radioactive decay. It can be present in water and organic molecules; the nuclear species does not become radioactive because it participates in chemistry.

Verification: P+N=1+1=2 and P−E=0.

Exam trap: Do not confuse deuterium with radioactive tritium.

Worked example 2. Count particles in radioactive tritium

Problem: For neutral ³₁H, calculate P,N,E and name its decay.

Complete solution: Hydrogen has Z=1 and A=3 gives N=2. Neutral E=1. Tritium is radioactive and beta-minus decays to helium-3, increasing nuclear proton number from one to two while keeping A=3. Its half-life is about 12.32 years.

Verification: 1 proton + 2 neutrons = 3 nucleons.

Exam trap: Do not treat the superscript 3 as three protons.

Worked example 3. Carbon-13 versus carbon-14

Problem: Count particles in ¹³C and ¹⁴C, then identify the stable one.

Complete solution: Both carbon nuclei have Z=6 and neutral atoms each have six electrons. Carbon-13 has N=13−6=7 and is stable; carbon-14 has N=14−6=8 and is radioactive. Their ordinary chemistry is carbon chemistry but nuclear behavior differs.

Verification: Both share Z=6; neutron count differs by one.

Exam trap: Do not conclude every heavier carbon isotope is necessarily radioactive.

Worked example 4. Nitrogen-15 nuclear census

Problem: Neutral ¹⁵N is used as a stable tracer. Determine P,N,E.

Complete solution: Nitrogen atomic number Z=7 means seven protons and, when neutral, seven electrons. N=15−7=8 neutrons. Nitrogen-15 is stable and can help trace nitrogen assimilation under an appropriate design; label presence alone does not determine a rate.

Verification: 7+8=15 and 7−7=0 charge.

Exam trap: Do not infer metabolic flux merely from the nuclear counts.

Worked example 5. Oxygen-18 as a stable label

Problem: Find P,N,E for neutral ¹⁸O and explain why it is not radioactive.

Complete solution: Oxygen Z=8 gives P=8 and neutral E=8, while N=18−8=10. Oxygen-18 is a stable nuclide and is used in water isotope studies and tracing oxygen atoms. Its mass number being larger than oxygen-16's does not imply it spontaneously decays.

Verification: 8+10=18; Z remains eight across oxygen isotopes.

Exam trap: An atom's mass number is not a radioactivity test.

Worked example 6. Phosphorus-32 particle arithmetic

Problem: For neutral ³²₁₅P, calculate particle numbers and its distinction from ³¹P.

Complete solution: P=15, N=32−15=17 and E=15. Phosphorus-32 is radioactive and predominantly beta-minus decays to sulfur-32. Phosphorus-31 is stable. They are isotopes of one element with a different nuclear future.

Verification: Z is 15 in both ³¹P and ³²P.

Worked example 7. Iodine isotope comparison

Problem: Count N in ¹²⁷I and ¹³¹I, and describe their status.

Complete solution: Iodine has Z=53. For ¹²⁷I, N=127−53=74 and it is stable. For ¹³¹I, N=131−53=78 and it is radioactive, with a physical half-life about eight days. Both remain iodine in chemical identity until any nuclear transmutation.

Verification: 53+74=127; 53+78=131.

Worked example 8. Fluorine-18 beta-plus daughter

Problem: Find Z,N of ¹⁸F and the β⁺ daughter, and classify the pair.

Complete solution: Fluorine has Z=9,A=18,N=9. Positron emission decreases Z to eight at constant A=18, giving oxygen-18 with N=10, a stable isotope. The two nuclei are isobars, not isotopes, because they have different proton numbers.

Verification: Parent and daughter A both equal eighteen.

Worked example 9. Technetium nuclear isomer

Problem: Count N for ⁹⁹ᵐTc and compare with ⁹⁹Tc.

Complete solution: Technetium has Z=43 and A=99, giving N=56. The metastable m identifies an excited nuclear state. Both states have identical nuclear composition, while gamma or internal-conversion transitions can connect energy states. They are nuclear isomers rather than different A isotopes.

Verification: 99−43=56 for both species.

Exam trap: The letter m does not add a neutron.

Worked example 10. Carbon-14 daughter conservation

Problem: Show the nuclear change in ¹⁴C beta-minus decay.

Complete solution: Carbon-14 begins with six protons and eight neutrons. Beta-minus changes one neutron to a proton, so daughter nitrogen-14 has seven protons and seven neutrons. A remains fourteen; an electron and electron antineutrino accompany the reaction. The parent and daughter are isobars.

Verification: Parent 6+8=14; daughter 7+7=14.

Exam trap: Chemical ionization is not the same as beta decay.

Worked example 11. Tritium after one half-life

Problem: An ideal population begins with 10,000 ³H nuclei. Find expected parents after 12.32 years.

Complete solution: One half-life has elapsed, so N=N₀(1/2)^1=10000/2=5000 tritium parent nuclei in expectation. The decayed part has transformed to helium-3; individual decay times remain random. The calculation assumes an isolated parent population.

Verification: Fraction surviving=5000/10000=0.5.

Exam trap: Half-life does not halve each individual atom.

Worked example 12. Tritium after two half-lives

Problem: From 10,000 tritium nuclei, how many expected parents remain after 24.64 years?

Complete solution: The elapsed time is two 12.32-year half-lives. Surviving fraction (1/2)²=1/4 gives 2500 parents. In an ideal closed population, expected number transformed is 7500. The decay calculation does not account for environmental or biological mixing.

Verification: 2500+7500=10000 in ideal parent/daughter counting.

Exam trap: Do not subtract 5000 again at the second half-life.

Worked example 13. Carbon-14 three-half-life problem

Problem: An ideal sample has 3200 radioactive ¹⁴C nuclei initially. How many survive after 17,190 years?

Complete solution: A carbon-14 half-life is approximately 5730 years. 17190/5730=3 half-lives. Expected surviving fraction=(1/2)³=1/8, giving 3200/8=400 radioactive parent nuclei. This is not the full radiocarbon calibration of a specimen.

Verification: 400/3200=0.125.

Exam trap: Stable carbon-12 atoms do not decay under the ¹⁴C law.

Worked example 14. Time for one-eighth radiocarbon survival

Problem: Under an ideal ¹⁴C closed-system model, what time corresponds to 12.5% survival?

Complete solution: One-eighth equals (1/2)³, corresponding to three half-lives. With an approximate 5730-year half-life, t=3×5730=17190 years. This calculates radioactive parent survival under the specified ideal model, not necessarily a calibrated calendar age.

Verification: 2^(−3)=1/8.

Exam trap: Do not use a linear decline to 12.5%.

Worked example 15. Find an unknown half-life from 25% survival

Problem: An isolated radionuclide has 25% of parents remaining after 16 days. What is T½?

Complete solution: 25%=0.25=(1/2)², so the time corresponds to two half-lives. Therefore T½=16/2=8 days. This is a mathematical exercise, not an identification of the particular radionuclide without extra properties.

Verification: Two 8-day half-lives make 16 days.

Exam trap: Do not assume the half-life is 4 days because 25% remains.

Worked example 16. Fraction after half a half-life

Problem: At time t=0.5T½, what fraction of a large ideal radioactive population remains?

Complete solution: The exponential law gives N/N₀=(1/2)^0.5=1/√2≈0.7071, or 70.71%. It is not 75%, because the decay curve is exponential rather than a line connecting 100% and 50%.

Verification: Squaring 0.7071 yields approximately 0.5.

Exam trap: Do not use linear interpolation for nuclear decay.

Worked example 17. Calculate λ from a 14-day half-life

Problem: A toy radionuclide has T½=14 days. Compute λ per day.

Complete solution: Use λ=ln2/T½. Substitute 0.693147/14=0.04951 day⁻¹ approximately. The unit is inverse day, because the model's time variable is measured in days. Convert to s⁻¹ only when an activity in Bq is required.

Verification: λ×14≈0.6931.

Exam trap: Do not invert the ratio as 14/ln2.

Worked example 18. Carbon-14 approximate λ per year

Problem: Using T½=5730 years, compute λ in year⁻¹.

Complete solution: λ=ln2/5730≈0.00012097 year⁻¹. This is the exponential decay constant for the rounded classroom half-life. The result is not a probability greater than one, nor a number of nuclei.

Verification: 0.00012097×5730≈0.693.

Exam trap: If Bq is requested, year⁻¹ must be converted into s⁻¹.

Worked example 19. Mean life versus half-life

Problem: A radioactive species has T½=8 days. What is its mean lifetime τ?

Complete solution: The mean lifetime is τ=1/λ=T½/ln2≈8/0.693147=11.54 days. This exceeds the half-life. The two terms both summarize the exponential lifetime distribution but do not refer to the same statistic.

Verification: 0.693147×11.54≈8.

Exam trap: Do not call mean life equal to half-life.

Worked example 20. Activity from known λ and atom count

Problem: In a toy model, N=1000 radioactive parent atoms and λ=0.002 s⁻¹. Find activity.

Complete solution: The expected activity is 𝒜=λN=0.002×1000=2 s⁻¹, so 2 Bq. This means an expected average of two nuclear transformations per second from the defined population. It does not state absorbed radiation dose or detector count efficiency.

Verification: 0.002 s⁻¹×1000=2 s⁻¹.

Exam trap: Bq is not interchangeable with gray or sievert.

Part II — Twenty advanced examples: activity, stable isotope ratios and biological inference

Worked example 21. Activity after three half-lives

Problem: An isolated radionuclide's activity starts at 80 Bq. What is expected after three physical half-lives?

Reasoned calculation: For one pure parent radionuclide, activity and parent population have the same exponential fraction. After three half-lives 𝒜/𝒜₀=(1/2)³=1/8, giving 80/8=10 Bq. The answer does not include daughter emissions or biological clearance.

Cross-check: 10×8=80 and 10/80=0.125.

Exam pitfall: Do not subtract a fixed 40 Bq each half-life.

Worked example 22. Recover parent atoms from activity

Problem: A hypothetical pure radionuclide has activity 300 Bq and λ=0.01 s⁻¹. What parent count does the model imply?

Reasoned calculation: Use 𝒜=λN. Rearranging gives N=𝒜/λ=300/0.01=30,000 radioactive parent atoms. Here Bq means s⁻¹ and λ is in s⁻¹, so the units cancel. This is an ideal relation and does not describe detector counts alone.

Cross-check: 30,000×0.01 s⁻¹=300 Bq.

Exam pitfall: Do not divide by a half-life without converting to λ.

Worked example 23. Detector count rate versus activity

Problem: A pure source has 2 Bq and the hypothetical effective event detection probability is 25% with one relevant detectable event per decay. What is expected recorded count rate?

Reasoned calculation: Under the explicitly stated simplified model, count rate=activity×detection fraction=2×0.25=0.5 counts per second in expectation. Real counting also depends on emission branching, background, geometry and calibration. Detector counts and Bq are not identical observables.

Cross-check: 0.5/2=0.25 detection fraction.

Exam pitfall: Do not report 0.5 Bq as though measured count rate equals source activity.

Worked example 24. Decay constant in seconds for a two-hour half-life

Problem: A model radionuclide has physical T½=2 hours. Find λ in s⁻¹.

Reasoned calculation: Convert 2 hours to 7200 seconds. Then λ=0.693147/7200=9.627×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ approximately. The conversion is essential because Bq calculations require per-second transformations. A calculation in h⁻¹ would instead be 0.3466 h⁻¹.

Cross-check: 9.627e-5×7200≈0.6931.

Exam pitfall: Do not use 2 directly when requesting per-second activity.

Worked example 25. Activity for a given nucleus count

Problem: For N=2,000,000 parent atoms and T½=2 hours, calculate activity in Bq.

Reasoned calculation: Using λ=ln2/7200≈9.627×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, activity 𝒜=λN≈9.627×10⁻⁵×2,000,000≈192.54 Bq. The result is an ideal expected decay rate and not a radiation dose or instrument count rate.

Cross-check: 192.54/2,000,000≈9.627e-5 s⁻¹.

Exam pitfall: Do not conflate number of atoms with number of Bq.

Worked example 26. Effective half-life: equal physical and biological rates

Problem: A radionuclide tracer has physical half-life 8 days and an independent modeled biological clearance half-life 8 days. What is T_eff?

Reasoned calculation: Under one-compartment independent first-order assumptions, 1/T_eff=1/8+1/8=1/4 day⁻¹, so T_eff=4 days. The compartment signal halves faster because two loss mechanisms act. Real tissue kinetics may not be single exponential.

Cross-check: Effective 4 days is shorter than both 8-day half-lives.

Exam pitfall: Do not add the half-lives directly.

Worked example 27. Effective half-life: different rate constants

Problem: A single-compartment tracer has physical half-life 6 hours and biological clearance half-life 12 hours. Find effective half-life.

Reasoned calculation: Use 1/T_eff=1/6+1/12=2/12+1/12=3/12=1/4 h⁻¹, giving T_eff=4 hours. This describes an ideal independent first-order model. It is not an actual patient-specific kinetic prediction.

Cross-check: 4 hours is shorter than both input half-lives.

Exam pitfall: Do not report six plus twelve equals eighteen hours.

Worked example 28. A stable label's concentration halves

Problem: Deuterium enrichment in a sample falls to 50% of its initial excess over 24 hours. Is the ²H radioactive half-life 24 hours?

Reasoned calculation: No. Deuterium ²H is stable. The measured drop likely reflects dilution, water turnover or exchange under the experiment. A biological signal can have a half-time even though its nucleus has no observed spontaneous radioactive decay. A compartment model and additional data are needed to interpret turnover.

Cross-check: The isotope identity stayed ²H throughout the measurement.

Exam pitfall: Do not fit radioactive λ to a stable isotope label.

Worked example 29. Carbon isotope delta conversion

Problem: For a sample with R_sample/R_VPDB=0.975, find δ¹³C.

Reasoned calculation: δ¹³C=[(R_sample/R_ref)−1]×1000=(0.975−1)×1000=−25‰. This means the sample's ¹³C/¹²C ratio is 2.5% below the reference ratio, not that 25% of carbon atoms are lost.

Cross-check: −0.025×1000=−25.

Exam pitfall: Do not equate per mille delta with atom fraction or activity.

Worked example 30. Oxygen isotope delta in water

Problem: If R_sample/R_VSMOW=0.992, what is δ¹⁸O?

Reasoned calculation: δ¹⁸O=(0.992−1)×1000=−8‰. This is a sample isotope-ratio comparison to VSMOW, and oxygen-18 remains a stable nucleus. The number cannot by itself determine water age or geographic origin without a model.

Cross-check: −0.008×1000=−8.

Exam pitfall: Do not interpret negative δ as negative oxygen-18 atoms.

Worked example 31. Atom fraction versus isotope ratio

Problem: A toy sample has ten ²H atoms and 990 ¹H atoms. Calculate deuterium atom fraction x and ratio R.

Reasoned calculation: Total H isotope atoms=1000, giving x_D=10/1000=0.01 or 1%. Heavy-to-light ratio R_D/H=10/990≈0.010101. The denominators differ. This is an artificial high-enrichment example, not a claim about natural water.

Cross-check: x=R/(1+R)≈0.01.

Exam pitfall: Do not use 1% as an exact heavy-to-light ratio.

Worked example 32. Convert a stable isotope ratio into atom fraction

Problem: Under a simplified two-isotope model R=0.005. Compute heavy atom fraction x.

Reasoned calculation: x=R/(1+R)=0.005/1.005≈0.0049751. In percentage terms the heavy fraction is roughly 0.4975%. The conversion assumes that only the specified heavy and light isotopic species make up the relevant elemental pool.

Cross-check: x/(1−x)=0.005 approximately.

Exam pitfall: Do not call R exactly equal to x when a precise answer is requested.

Worked example 33. Binomial mass isotopologue toy model

Problem: A two-carbon molecule has independent probability p=0.20 for each carbon to be ¹³C. Find M+0, M+1 and M+2 probabilities.

Reasoned calculation: Under the explicitly independent-site model, P(M+0)=0.8²=0.64, P(M+1)=2×0.2×0.8=0.32, P(M+2)=0.2²=0.04. The categories sum to one. A specifically position-labelled metabolic tracer need not follow this binomial assumption.

Cross-check: 0.64+0.32+0.04=1.

Exam pitfall: Do not claim isotope-count fractions uniquely identify metabolic pathways.

Worked example 34. Water isotopologue toy distribution

Problem: Under independent equal hydrogen-site probabilities p_D=0.10, estimate H₂O, HDO and D₂O percentages.

Reasoned calculation: P(H₂O)=(1−p)²=0.81; P(HDO)=2p(1−p)=0.18; P(D₂O)=p²=0.01, corresponding to 81%,18%,1%. These refer to molecule isotopologues under a random-site model; they are not the percent of deuterium atoms.

Cross-check: All probabilities total 100%.

Exam pitfall: Do not omit the two possible arrangements of HDO.

Worked example 35. Stable tracer dilution volume: illustrative arithmetic

Problem: In an ideal mixing model, 20 abstract tracer-mass units are uniformly diluted and the measured excess concentration is 1 mass unit per liter. Estimate pool volume.

Reasoned calculation: With complete mixing, no tracer loss and concentration defined as added tracer amount per liquid volume, V=tracer amount/excess concentration=20/(1 per liter)=20 liters. This is a toy mass balance, not a deuterium administration protocol or real body composition computation.

Cross-check: 20 liters×1 mass unit/liter=20 mass units.

Exam pitfall: Real biological isotope dilution needs baseline, equilibration and correction factors.

Worked example 36. Stable ¹³C incorporation versus absolute flux

Problem: A culture exposed to ¹³C glucose shows an M+3 lactate isotopologue. Can glycolytic flux be numerically determined from this alone?

Reasoned calculation: No. M+3 says the measured three-carbon lactate population contains a fully ¹³C-labelled isotopologue under the analysis. To estimate flux, the experiment must supply substrate enrichment, pool and timing data, network atom transitions and other constraints. No absolute rate units are given.

Cross-check: Three-carbon lactate can carry at most three ¹³C substitutions.

Exam pitfall: Do not present an isotope fraction as a conversion rate.

Worked example 37. The ¹³C-urea test signal is not radiation

Problem: Breath contains an increased ¹³CO₂ isotope signal after a standardized clinical test. Is the instrument detecting ¹³C radioactive decay?

Reasoned calculation: No. Carbon-13 is stable. The ¹³CO₂ signal reflects a stable isotope in a chemical product and is measured through a validated analytical method. The test's clinical meaning requires correct controls and interpretation; it is not a detector count of nuclear decay.

Cross-check: ¹³C and ¹⁴C are isotopes of carbon with different stability.

Exam pitfall: Do not claim isotope detection automatically equals a clinical diagnosis.

Worked example 38. Groundwater with a tritium isotope ratio

Problem: A report gives groundwater tritium concentration but not initial input or mixing. Can its unique age be calculated from 12.32 years alone?

Reasoned calculation: No. The tritium half-life constrains nuclear decay, but present concentration also depends on source history, initial isotope content, mixing, dilution and transport. A quantitative groundwater residence-time inference needs an appropriate environmental model and additional data.

Cross-check: A half-life is a nuclide property, not a universal sample timestamp.

Exam pitfall: Do not set sample age equal to one half-life from mere isotope presence.

Worked example 39. Absorbed dose unit, not activity

Problem: A hypothetical system deposits 0.5 joule of radiation energy uniformly into 2 kilograms of specified material. Calculate absorbed dose D.

Reasoned calculation: Absorbed dose D=E_absorbed/m=0.5 J/2 kg=0.25 J/kg=0.25 Gy. This does not automatically specify equivalent/effective dose in Sv because radiation and tissue weighting require additional definitions and context. No activity in Bq can be deduced from this number alone.

Cross-check: 0.25 Gy×2 kg=0.5 J deposited.

Exam pitfall: Gy and Bq have different physical dimensions.

Worked example 40. What can 100 Bq tell you?

Problem: A laboratory measurement reports 100 Bq of one radionuclide. Can you infer a person's dose or a stable isotope atom fraction?

Reasoned calculation: The observation specifies an expected 100 nuclear transformations per second from the defined source. To infer dose one needs radiation energy, geometry, uptake, residence and dosimetric models. A stable isotope atom fraction describes composition and is not obtained from Bq without other isotope-specific information.

Cross-check: Bq=s⁻¹, whereas Gy=J/kg and fraction has no units.

Exam pitfall: Do not call 100 Bq equal to 100 Gy, 100 Sv or 100 labelled atoms.

Sixty original explained practice questions — nuclei, half-life, stable tracers and responsible inference

These problems are original CSIR-NET/SET-oriented exercises, not reproduced official previous-year questions. Read each explanation: it states the scientific rule and prevents plausible but wrong conclusions.

Question 1. What is the key scientific property of a stable nuclide?

Answer: No observed spontaneous radioactive decay

Reasoning: Stable describes nuclear behavior, not whether an atom participates in chemical reactions or whether every compound containing it is safe. Very rare decays can be experimentally difficult to observe.

Question 2. What makes a nuclide radioactive?

Answer: Spontaneous nuclear transformation with a characteristic probability

Reasoning: The transformation may change proton/neutron composition or only nuclear energy state. It is different from ordinary exchange of atoms between molecules.

Question 3. Are all isotopes radioisotopes?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Isotope refers to nuclear species of one element with different N or A. Some are stable (¹³C), while others are radioactive (¹⁴C).

Question 4. Is deuterium ²H stable or radioactive?

Answer: Stable

Reasoning: It has one proton and one neutron and no observed spontaneous decay. In a biological water study its enrichment can change through exchange rather than nuclear transformation.

Question 5. Is tritium ³H stable?

Answer: No, it is radioactive

Reasoning: Tritium has one proton and two neutrons and beta-minus decays to helium-3. Its physical half-life is about 12.32 years.

Question 6. Which carbon isotope is used as a stable nuclear label: ¹³C or ¹⁴C?

Answer: ¹³C

Reasoning: Carbon-13 is stable. Carbon-14 is radioactive, despite both having the same carbon Z=6.

Question 7. Is carbon-14 stable because it is an isotope of carbon-12?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons and beta-minus decays to nitrogen-14. Sharing an element does not mean sharing nuclear lifetime.

Question 8. Which isotope is a stable nitrogen tracer?

Answer: ¹⁵N

Reasoning: Nitrogen-15 has Z=7,A=15,N=8. It can label nitrogen pools, but detection alone does not quantify a reaction rate.

Question 9. Is oxygen-18 radioactive?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Oxygen-18 is a stable oxygen nuclide with eight protons and ten neutrons. It is used in water isotope and atom-origin research.

Question 10. What is the radioactive counterpart of stable ³¹P in a common biochemical comparison?

Answer: ³²P

Reasoning: Phosphorus-32 undergoes beta-minus decay with a physical half-life around 14.3 days. It remains phosphorus in ordinary chemical reactions until nuclear decay occurs.

Question 11. Which commonly occurring iodine isotope is stable?

Answer: ¹²⁷I

Reasoning: Iodine-127 is the principal stable natural iodine nuclide. Iodine-131 is a radioactive isotope of the same element.

Question 12. What is the approximate physical half-life of ¹³¹I?

Answer: About eight days

Reasoning: This time measures nuclear parent-population decay, not how rapidly iodine compounds leave a particular tissue or whether a patient should receive a medical treatment.

Question 13. What does A count in ᴬ_ZX?

Answer: Protons plus neutrons

Reasoning: Mass number is the integer count of nucleons. It is not precise atomic mass, activity or total electrons.

Question 14. How many neutrons are present in ¹⁴C?

Answer: Eight

Reasoning: Carbon has Z=6; N=A−Z=14−6=8. The nuclear species is radioactive despite belonging to carbon.

Question 15. How many electrons does neutral tritium have?

Answer: One

Reasoning: Tritium is hydrogen with Z=1. The number three is its mass number, not an electron count.

Question 16. Does N=Z prove a nuclide is stable?

Answer: No

Reasoning: N=Z is not a universal stability criterion. Nuclear energetics, shell effects and decay possibilities determine observed stability.

Question 17. Does a larger mass number always imply greater radioactivity?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Oxygen-18 is heavier than oxygen-16 and remains stable. Comparisons of nuclear decay require isotope-specific evaluated data.

Question 18. Does the word stable guarantee a chemical has no toxic effects?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Nuclear stability concerns spontaneous decay. A molecule with stable isotope labels may still be chemically hazardous or biologically disruptive at certain concentrations.

Question 19. What is the beta-minus daughter of ¹⁴C?

Answer: ¹⁴N

Reasoning: Beta-minus converts a neutron into a proton: Z increases from six to seven while A remains fourteen. Electron and antineutrino are emitted.

Question 20. What is the beta-plus daughter of ¹⁸F?

Answer: ¹⁸O

Reasoning: Positron emission converts a nuclear proton to neutron. A=18 stays constant while Z changes from nine to eight.

Question 21. What is an alpha particle?

Answer: A helium-4 nucleus

Reasoning: It contains two protons and two neutrons and carries charge +2. Alpha emission lowers daughter mass number by four and Z by two.

Question 22. Does ordinary gamma de-excitation change Z and A?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Gamma emission changes nuclear energy state; ordinary de-excitation does not change nucleon composition. This differs from beta or alpha transformations.

Question 23. What does the m in ⁹⁹ᵐTc mean?

Answer: Metastable nuclear energy state

Reasoning: Technetium-99m and technetium-99 share Z and A but differ in excitation. The m is not an added neutron or electronic ion charge.

Question 24. Is an internal-conversion electron the same origin as a beta-minus electron?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Internal conversion ejects an atomic electron by transfer of nuclear excitation energy. Beta-minus creates an electron during the weak neutron-to-proton nuclear event.

Question 25. What nuclear change occurs in electron capture?

Answer: Z decreases by one and N increases by one

Reasoning: A proton captures an atomic electron and becomes a neutron, with a neutrino emitted. Mass number A remains constant.

Question 26. How does alpha emission change daughter A?

Answer: A decreases by four

Reasoning: The emitted ⁴He nucleus carries four nucleons. Parent and daughter generally differ in both proton and mass numbers.

Question 27. What happens to mass number in beta-minus decay?

Answer: It stays constant

Reasoning: A neutron transforms into a proton, preserving nucleon count but changing Z and N in opposite directions.

Question 28. What law describes the expected remaining radioactive parent count?

Answer: N(t)=N₀e^(−λt)

Reasoning: The first-order exponential law assumes a constant decay probability for the defined parent species. It does not describe arbitrary stable-label turnover.

Question 29. How are T½ and λ related?

Answer: T½=ln2/λ

Reasoning: λ has inverse-time units; use consistent units when calculating a decay fraction or activity in Bq.

Question 30. How does mean lifetime τ compare with T½?

Answer: τ=T½/ln2≈1.443T½

Reasoning: Mean life and half-life are different statistics of the same exponential lifetime distribution.

Question 31. What fraction survives after one half-life?

Answer: One half, in expectation

Reasoning: An individual atom is not split into halves. This is a statistical survival fraction of the parent population.

Question 32. What fraction survives after two half-lives?

Answer: One quarter

Reasoning: Successive intervals multiply remaining fraction by 1/2: (1/2)²=0.25.

Question 33. What fraction survives after three half-lives?

Answer: One eighth

Reasoning: (1/2)³=0.125 or 12.5%, for an ideal isolated parent population.

Question 34. What fraction survives after half a half-life?

Answer: 1/√2≈0.7071

Reasoning: Exponential decay is not a linear drop, so 75% is an incorrect interpolation.

Question 35. What are the units of λ?

Answer: Reciprocal time, such as s⁻¹

Reasoning: Activity in Bq requires a decay constant in s⁻¹ when multiplied by a nucleus count.

Question 36. How is radionuclide activity calculated from parent atoms?

Answer: 𝒜=λN

Reasoning: The expected transformations per unit time depend on both how many radioactive parents exist and their decay constant.

Question 37. What is one becquerel?

Answer: One expected nuclear transformation per second

Reasoning: Bq measures activity. The observed detector count rate may differ due to efficiency and branching.

Question 38. What does one gray measure?

Answer: One joule absorbed per kilogram

Reasoning: Gy measures absorbed dose, a different dimension from activity measured in Bq.

Question 39. What is the purpose of the sievert?

Answer: Radiation-weighted dosimetric quantities

Reasoning: Equivalent/effective dose quantities in Sv depend on specified weighting and exposure context, not simply the number of source decays.

Question 40. Can Bq be converted directly to Gy without exposure information?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Radiation energy, source geometry, absorption, time and tissue are required. The units represent different physical quantities.

Question 41. What does ¹³C/¹²C express?

Answer: A stable carbon heavy-to-light atom ratio

Reasoning: A ratio is not radioactive activity and is not identical to the heavy atom fraction because the denominator differs.

Question 42. What does a negative δ¹³C indicate?

Answer: Sample ¹³C/¹²C ratio below the named reference

Reasoning: The value does not imply negative isotope atoms or a decay signal. Carbon ratio values are commonly reported relative to VPDB.

Question 43. Which stable carbon isotope can be observed by ¹³C NMR?

Answer: ¹³C

Reasoning: Carbon-13 has nuclear spin one-half. Direct nuclear magnetic resonance is unrelated to spontaneous radioactive decay.

Question 44. Is ¹⁸O directly NMR-active with ordinary oxygen NMR?

Answer: No; its nuclear spin is zero

Reasoning: Stable oxygen-18 is useful in isotope mass/ratiometric studies; oxygen-17 has a distinct nonzero spin and may be NMR-active.

Question 45. Which stable oxygen nuclide has nuclear spin 5/2?

Answer: ¹⁷O

Reasoning: It can be studied with suitable NMR methods and has nuclear properties differing from stable spin-zero oxygen-16 and oxygen-18.

Question 46. What stable isotope is commonly used in water dilution studies?

Answer: Deuterium ²H

Reasoning: Deuterium oxide can provide isotope enrichment measurements in body water under validated research designs. The isotope is not decaying radioactively.

Question 47. Which two stable isotopes are used in doubly labelled water?

Answer: ²H and ¹⁸O

Reasoning: IAEA-described nutrition research uses their different measured elimination pathways, not radioactive half-lives, to infer CO₂ production with a model.

Question 48. Which label in doubly labelled water is also eliminated through CO₂?

Answer: Oxygen-18

Reasoning: Deuterium primarily tracks body-water elimination, while oxygen-18 participates in both water and CO₂ elimination paths under the method.

Question 49. Does a declining ²H isotope signal prove radioactive decay?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Deuterium is stable. Its enrichment can decrease because the labeled water pool mixes or leaves the sampled compartment.

Question 50. Which clinical test can use stable ¹³C-labelled urea?

Answer: ¹³C-urea breath test

Reasoning: Under standardized clinical conditions, urease-linked production of labelled CO₂ can be analyzed in a test associated with H. pylori. The isotope signal is not radioactivity.

Question 51. Can one ¹³C-labelled metabolite observation determine absolute flux?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Quantitative flux needs precursor enrichment, time information, atom mapping, network modeling and other constraints as appropriate.

Question 52. How many neutrons does ¹⁵N have?

Answer: Eight

Reasoning: N=15−7=8. Nitrogen-15 is a stable label and its use in biochemistry does not make it radioactive.

Question 53. Can carbon-14 presence alone give the calendar age of a specimen?

Answer: No

Reasoning: Radiocarbon dating needs measured isotope composition, initial-source history, contamination control, reservoir consideration and calibration.

Question 54. Can one tritium concentration uniquely date mixed groundwater?

Answer: No

Reasoning: The physical half-life constrains decay, but unknown initial input, mixing and transport can make unique age inference impossible.

1.A.1.21.42 Thirty common misconceptions with scientific corrections

The false shortcuts

Frequent errors include ‘every isotope is radioactive’, ‘heavier means more radioactive’, ‘stable means harmless at any amount’, ‘one half-life means exactly half of every small sample’, ‘Bq equals Gy’ and ‘a fading stable label must be decaying’.

How to repair a claim

For each incorrect statement, the correction identifies a counterexample, a relevant definition or an equation. The focus is scientific reasoning, not rote vocabulary.

Thirty misleading statements corrected with scientific counterexamples

Misconception 1

Incorrect claim: Every isotope is radioactive.

Correct scientific statement: An isotope is one nuclide of an element; stable isotopes exist alongside radioactive isotopes.

Independent check: Carbon-13 is stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive. Both are carbon Z=6.

Misconception 2

Incorrect claim: The heavier isotope of an element must be radioactive.

Correct scientific statement: Greater mass number alone does not determine nuclear stability. Binding, shell structure and decay energetics matter.

Independent check: Oxygen-18 is heavier than oxygen-16 and both are stable.

Misconception 3

Incorrect claim: Stable nuclei can never participate in any nuclear reaction.

Correct scientific statement: Stable means no observed spontaneous radioactive decay; externally induced nuclear interactions are separate physical processes.

Independent check: Ordinary oxygen-18 is stable, but nuclear reactions under appropriate high-energy conditions do not contradict that status.

Misconception 4

Incorrect claim: Anything labeled with stable isotopes is harmless at any concentration.

Correct scientific statement: Stable labels are nonradioactive, but compounds can have chemical toxicity or altered biological behavior at high isotope enrichment.

Independent check: The chemical identity, concentration, exposure pathway and study ethics all require assessment.

Misconception 5

Incorrect claim: Radioactive isotopes of an element always have unrelated chemistry.

Correct scientific statement: They share the same proton number and related electronic chemical identity, while mass and nuclear behavior differ.

Independent check: ¹³C and ¹⁴C are both carbon, but the latter has radioactive decay and different detection/safety issues.

Misconception 6

Incorrect claim: We can predict the exact decay time of one radioactive nucleus.

Correct scientific statement: Individual decay timing is stochastic. Half-life characterizes survival probability and expected large-population counts.

Independent check: After one half-life each parent nucleus has 50% survival probability under the exponential model.

Misconception 7

Incorrect claim: Half of a radioactive half-life means 75% of parents remain.

Correct scientific statement: Exponential decay gives (1/2)^0.5≈70.71%, not a straight-line interpolation.

Independent check: Square the surviving fraction: 0.7071²≈0.5.

Misconception 8

Incorrect claim: After one half-life, every small sample contains exactly half the parents.

Correct scientific statement: The half-life law gives a population expectation; small discrete populations exhibit statistical fluctuations.

Independent check: An individual atom cannot be half-decayed. The actual count from a few atoms can vary.

Misconception 9

Incorrect claim: A shorter half-life always means less activity per atom.

Correct scientific statement: A shorter half-life means larger λ and a higher decay probability per nucleus per unit time.

Independent check: 𝒜/N=λ=ln2/T½, which increases as T½ decreases.

Misconception 10

Incorrect claim: Radioactive atom count and activity in Bq are the same quantity.

Correct scientific statement: Atom count N is dimensionless, while activity 𝒜=λN is transformations per unit time.

Independent check: One radionuclide with N=1000 and λ=0.002 s⁻¹ has expected activity 2 Bq.

Misconception 11

Incorrect claim: A radiation detector's count rate always equals source activity.

Correct scientific statement: Detection efficiency, emission branching, geometry, absorption and background influence recorded counts.

Independent check: A source with 2 Bq may produce an average of 0.5 detected counts/s in a specified 25% toy-efficiency model.

Misconception 12

Incorrect claim: One becquerel equals one gray.

Correct scientific statement: Bq measures transformations per second; Gy measures absorbed joules per kilogram.

Independent check: Their units have different dimensions: s⁻¹ versus J/kg.

Misconception 13

Incorrect claim: One becquerel equals one sievert.

Correct scientific statement: Sievert is used for specified radiation-weighted dose quantities, not the count of nuclear transformations.

Independent check: Source emissions, distribution, exposure time and dosimetry are required for interpreting dose.

Misconception 14

Incorrect claim: A stable ¹³C isotope fraction of 10% means 10 Bq.

Correct scientific statement: Stable ¹³C has no observed spontaneous radioactive decay and its atom fraction is not an activity.

Independent check: 10% ¹³C is a composition ratio; Bq describes decays per second.

Misconception 15

Incorrect claim: Carbon-13 and carbon-14 are both radioactive.

Correct scientific statement: Carbon-13 is stable, whereas carbon-14 beta-minus decays to nitrogen-14.

Independent check: Their nuclear composition differs by one neutron but nuclear status must be checked per nuclide.

Misconception 16

Incorrect claim: Deuterium and tritium are both stable because both are hydrogen.

Correct scientific statement: Deuterium ²H is stable; tritium ³H is radioactive and transforms to helium-3.

Independent check: The labels share Z=1 but have N=1 and N=2 respectively.

Misconception 17

Incorrect claim: Oxygen-18 must emit radiation because it has two extra neutrons.

Correct scientific statement: Oxygen-18 is stable despite being heavier than oxygen-16.

Independent check: The stable oxygen family includes ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O.

Misconception 18

Incorrect claim: If stable isotope enrichment decreases, radioactive decay must have occurred.

Correct scientific statement: A stable label can leave or be diluted in the measured compartment through chemical and biological movement.

Independent check: ²H enrichment in body water can decrease without any ²H nucleus decaying.

Misconception 19

Incorrect claim: A ¹³C metabolite fraction automatically gives a pathway flux.

Correct scientific statement: A fraction reports isotope composition, while flux is material converted per time. Rates need models and additional measurements.

Independent check: Tracer position, precursor enrichment, pool turnover and sampling time constrain quantitative metabolic flux.

Misconception 20

Incorrect claim: A negative delta isotope value means the heavy isotope is absent.

Correct scientific statement: Negative delta only means the sample's heavy/light isotope ratio is below the specified reference ratio.

Independent check: δ¹³C=−25‰ corresponds to a positive ¹³C/¹²C ratio that is 2.5% below its reference.

Misconception 21

Incorrect claim: Doubly labelled water uses two radioactive nuclides.

Correct scientific statement: The established method uses stable deuterium ²H and stable oxygen-18 ¹⁸O.

Independent check: Their different biological elimination pathways are measured as enrichment, not as nuclear radioactivity.

Misconception 22

Incorrect claim: The ¹³C-urea breath test requires radioactive ¹³C decay.

Correct scientific statement: Carbon-13 is stable; the test detects isotope-labelled carbon dioxide produced through chemistry under controlled conditions.

Independent check: A clinical interpretation uses validated breath isotope changes, not beta or gamma counts from ¹³C.

Misconception 23

Incorrect claim: An M+2 stable-isotope signal uniquely proves one metabolic pathway.

Correct scientific statement: An isotopologue count can arise through several routes, positions, source mixtures or natural isotope contributions.

Independent check: ¹³C metabolic flux analysis uses atom maps, multiple measurements and network constraints.

Misconception 24

Incorrect claim: Any sample containing ¹⁴C must be exactly 5730 years old.

Correct scientific statement: 5730 years is an approximate physical half-life, not a specimen's timestamp.

Independent check: Real radiocarbon dating needs measured parent/reference fraction, historical context and calibration.

Misconception 25

Incorrect claim: A single tritium concentration determines groundwater age.

Correct scientific statement: A tritium value is influenced by source history, input time, mixing and decay; half-life alone does not determine age.

Independent check: Two waters with different recharge histories can have the same measured present tritium level.

Misconception 26

Incorrect claim: Phosphate chemistry makes phosphorus-32 radioactive.

Correct scientific statement: Radioactivity arises from the nucleus; stable ³¹P and radioactive ³²P share phosphorus chemical identity.

Independent check: ³²P's N=17 differs from stable ³¹P's N=16; ordinary phosphate bond formation does not cause beta decay.

Misconception 27

Incorrect claim: A beta-minus electron is simply an orbital electron removed by ionization.

Correct scientific statement: Beta-minus creates an electron in the weak nuclear neutron-to-proton transformation.

Independent check: ¹⁴C→¹⁴N changes the nuclear element at unchanged A, unlike ionization.

Misconception 28

Incorrect claim: Gamma emission always changes the element's proton number.

Correct scientific statement: Ordinary gamma de-excitation changes nuclear energy while Z and A remain unchanged.

Independent check: Technetium-99m can transition toward lower-energy technetium-99 without a neutron-count change.

Misconception 29

Incorrect claim: Alpha emission lowers the daughter mass number by two.

Correct scientific statement: An alpha particle is helium-4 with two protons and two neutrons, reducing daughter A by four and Z by two.

Independent check: ²³⁸U→²³⁴Th + ⁴He balances 238=234+4.

Misconception 30

Incorrect claim: The m in technetium-99m means an extra neutron or different isotope mass number.

Correct scientific statement: The m denotes metastable nuclear excitation with the same A=99, Z=43 and N=56 as technetium-99.

Independent check: The comparison is nuclear isomerism, not a pair of distinct mass-number isotopes.

1.A.1.21.43 Ten integrated higher-order exam problems

Combine nuclear composition with real-world interpretation

The final problems test multiple concepts at once: ¹³C versus ¹⁴C tracing, doubly labelled water, environmental radionuclide chronology, alpha/beta/gamma charge balance, decay activity and the difference between isotope detection and medical interpretation.

Model answers will name missing data

When half-life, source history, activity, tracer enrichment or clinical context are insufficient, the correct solution will identify the missing input instead of inventing a number.

Ten high-order integrated problems with complete model reasoning

Integrated challenge 1. Hydrogen isotope choice in a nutrition question

Problem: A student has ²H-labelled water and a tritium-labeled water example. Compare P,N, nuclear stability, and whether a falling body-water signal always means decay.

Model solution: Both nuclei have one proton. Deuterium ²H has N=1 and is stable; tritium ³H has N=2 and beta-minus decays to helium-3 with about 12.32-year physical half-life. Measured body-water enrichment can decline through dilution and clearance in both cases, but only tritium adds radioactive parent decay. Therefore a falling label curve is not enough to identify the mechanism.

Integrated challenge 2. A nuclear carbon pair and a decay equation

Problem: Compare ¹³C and ¹⁴C particle counts, then balance carbon-14's beta-minus daughter.

Model solution: Both have Z=6; N is seven for ¹³C and eight for ¹⁴C. Carbon-13 is stable, carbon-14 radioactive. In beta-minus decay, Z rises to seven and A stays fourteen, producing nitrogen-14 plus electron and antineutrino. A chemical carbon-tracer reaction does not itself perform this nuclear transformation.

Integrated challenge 3. A population after three half-lives

Problem: An isolated radioactive parent sample has 8000 nuclei and T½=5 days. Find expected survivors and decayed count after 15 days.

Model solution: Time is three half-lives (15/5=3). Remaining expected fraction=1/8, so N=8000/8=1000. Decayed nuclei=8000−1000=7000. This is a stochastic population expectation and assumes the defined parent population has no influx or mixing.

Integrated challenge 4. A fractional-half-life survival calculation

Problem: An isolated parent population has N₀=1600 and elapsed time 2.5 half-lives. Estimate survivor count.

Model solution: Surviving fraction=(1/2)^2.5=1/(4√2)≈0.1767767. Expected survivors≈1600×0.1767767≈282.84, about 283 nuclei. Since counts are discrete, actual small-population observations fluctuate around the expectation. Linear interpolation between whole half-lives is not appropriate.

Integrated challenge 5. Calculate activity but do not infer dose

Problem: A model source has N=100,000 radioactive parents and λ=0.0001 s⁻¹. Find Bq and explain why Gy cannot be derived.

Model solution: Activity 𝒜=λN=0.0001×100,000=10 Bq. This describes expected nuclear transformations per second. Absorbed dose requires emitted energies, absorption, material mass, source geometry and exposure conditions. A 10 Bq value alone cannot produce a unique gray or sievert estimate.

Integrated challenge 6. Physical and biological disappearance combined

Problem: A radionuclide has T_phys=12 days, T_bio=6 days, both as independent first-order processes in one model compartment. Find T_eff.

Model solution: 1/T_eff=1/12+1/6=1/12+2/12=3/12=1/4 day⁻¹, hence T_eff=4 days. The effective disappearance is faster than either individual mechanism. This model does not imply that nuclear physical half-life itself changed.

Integrated challenge 7. One stable isotope ratio and one delta value

Problem: A simplified two-isotope sample has heavy/light ratio R=0.005. Find heavy atom fraction, then interpret a different sample's δ=-20‰ relative to a named reference.

Model solution: The heavy atom fraction x=R/(1+R)=0.005/1.005≈0.004975 or 0.4975%. The separate δ=-20‰ says R_sample/R_ref=1−20/1000=0.98, so its ratio is 2% lower than reference. Neither quantity is Bq, and they should not be equated with one another.

Integrated challenge 8. Doubly labelled water with two measured rate constants

Problem: A research summary reports stable ²H washout rate k_H=0.08/day and ¹⁸O washout rate k_O=0.12/day. What can be concluded and what remains unknown?

Model solution: Both labels are stable. The difference k_O−k_H=0.04/day is consistent with an additional ¹⁸O elimination component associated with CO₂, under the technique's assumptions. A complete CO₂ production or energy expenditure calculation also requires distribution volumes, isotope fractionation corrections and metabolic conversion assumptions. There is no radioactive nuclear decay of either isotope.

Integrated challenge 9. Radiocarbon decay versus calibrated age

Problem: In a closed toy system, 25% of initial ¹⁴C parents remain. Find ideal elapsed half-life count and time, and qualify the result.

Model solution: Fraction 0.25=(1/2)² means two half-lives. With T½≈5730 years, ideal elapsed model time≈11,460 years. A real specimen's calendar age requires measured initial/reference conditions, reservoir/contamination assessment and calibration; this arithmetic alone is not a calibrated date.

Integrated challenge 10. A clinical label cannot determine personal risk

Problem: A study note compares stable ¹³C-urea, radioactive ¹⁸F PET tracer and an activity reading of 100 Bq. What can be stated and what cannot?

Model solution: Carbon-13 is stable and may provide a measurable breath isotope signal under a validated urea test. Fluorine-18 is a positron-emitting radionuclide used in some PET compounds, with isotope-specific half-life and radiological requirements. A value of 100 Bq refers to decays per second from a defined source; it does not specify individual dose or clinical outcomes. Those need qualified interpretation, compound chemistry and exposure modeling.

1.A.1.21.44 Master revision, glossary and bridge to Node 22

Read the nuclear identity first

Isotopes have equal Z but differing N. Stable means no observed spontaneous nuclear decay. Radioactive means a nuclear state transforms spontaneously, with a characteristic total decay constant and half-life.

Read the measurement unit second

Mass/isotope ratios and enrichment describe stable label composition; Bq measures nuclear activity; Gy measures energy absorbed per mass and Sv describes specified radiation-weighted dose quantities. These are not interchangeable.

Read the biological inference last

A labelled molecule may move, be metabolized, exchange atoms or be eliminated. Such changes are not nuclear transmutation. A radioactive tracer may also decay; real data may contain both processes.

Study order and glossary

Start with ²H/³H and ¹³C/¹⁴C, then alpha/beta/gamma and N(t), then measurements, stable-isotope nutrition, radioactive tracer studies and integrated practice. Nuclide: nuclear species; half-life: time for expected parent count/activity to halve; activity: expected transformations per second; isotope enrichment: relative amount of labelled atoms. Previous: Node 20 — Isobars and Isotones. The next published lesson is Node 22 — Atomic Mass Unit (u) and Relative Atomic Mass.

Essential equations:
  1. Protons P=Z; neutrons N=A−Z.
  2. N(t)=N₀e^(−λt)=N₀(1/2)^(t/T½).
  3. λ=ln2/T½; mean lifetime τ=1/λ.
  4. Radioactive activity 𝒜=λN in Bq; no Bq is assigned from stable isotope fraction alone.
  5. For an explicitly independent one-compartment model, 1/T_eff=1/T_phys+1/T_bio.
  6. For isotope ratios, R=heavy/light, δ=(R_sample/R_reference−1)×1000‰.
