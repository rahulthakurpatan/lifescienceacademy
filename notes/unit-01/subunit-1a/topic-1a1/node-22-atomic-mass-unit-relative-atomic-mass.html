1.A.1.22.1 The first puzzle: why does chlorine weigh approximately 35.45 when no single atom has that mass?

Imagine a student studying a periodic table. Beside chlorine (Cl) the student sees an atomic weight close to 35.45. Yet the familiar stable chlorine nuclides are chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Does a typical chlorine atom contain 35.45 nucleons? Can an individual nucleus contain part of a proton or part of a neutron? No. A nucleus has an integer mass number, and the value on the periodic table describes a sample-related average of isotope masses.

A balance from the chemical laboratory meets atoms that are too small to place on it

Ordinary measurements show that chlorine-containing substances have reproducible composition and chemical behavior. Atoms themselves are immensely light, so expressing their masses in kilograms gives inconvenient numbers. Chemistry therefore needs a reference mass on the atomic scale and a careful way to describe isotope mixtures.

Two different questions appear immediately

The first question asks for the physical mass of a particular nuclide, such as neutral chlorine-35. The second asks for the mean mass of chlorine atoms from a defined sample containing various isotopes. The first is an individual atomic mass; the second, when compared with a defined reference unit, leads to relative atomic mass or atomic weight.

Our learning journey

We will move chronologically from Dalton and early atomic-weight scales to the unified carbon-12 standard, distinguish mass number from actual mass, derive isotope-weighted averages, connect atomic masses to moles and biological molecules, and interpret modern isotope mass spectrometry. Later exercises will test both arithmetic and what cannot be inferred without an isotope composition.

Learning goals: define u and Da precisely, distinguish A, isotope atomic mass, relative isotopic mass and sample mean relative atomic mass; compute weighted means and isotope abundance; explain molecular masses, molar masses and mass spectral m/z without confusing their units.

1.A.1.22.2 Dalton's atomic reasoning: useful relative masses before precise nuclear physics

Why relative numbers came before absolute masses

John Dalton's early nineteenth-century atomic theory gave chemists a powerful framework for explaining fixed proportions in compounds. Scientists could compare how elements combined in chemical reactions long before they could reliably weigh one atom in kilograms. Choosing one element as a reference allowed relative atomic mass tables to be constructed.

An early reference was not our modern carbon-12 definition

Early hydrogen-based and oxygen-based conventions developed because their chemistry was central to stoichiometry. Historical values had limitations arising from uncertain formulas, isotope mixtures and differences in scientific conventions. Do not project the current exact carbon-12 definition backward onto Dalton's first tables.

Molecules made the challenge more complicated

A measured mass ratio depends on knowing how many atoms of each element occur in a formula. Improvements in atomic-molecular theory, gas measurements and chemical formulas helped make relative atomic masses mutually consistent. This history explains why the unit arose as a reference comparison instead of a direct weighing of an isolated atom.

One enduring insight

Relative mass is a ratio: compare a mass with the accepted reference mass and report the number. The reference itself may be given in an absolute unit such as the kilogram, but the ratio is dimensionless. This distinction remains essential in modern IUPAC terminology.

1.A.1.22.3 The oxygen-scale era: why two scientific communities could disagree

A historical chemistry problem

Before the unified carbon-12 convention, chemical and physical atomic-mass scales did not always use the same oxygen reference. Chemists commonly considered the average atomic mass of naturally occurring oxygen, whereas physicists used oxygen-16 as a nuclide reference. Those approaches differ because natural oxygen contains more than one stable isotope.

A tiny difference can matter in precise science

For many chemical calculations the two oxygen-based scales seemed close, but careful atomic mass measurements needed one unambiguous reference. An isotope-specific standard avoids silently substituting a changing natural sample average for an exact reference species.

The key historical transition

The unified scale based on a specified carbon-12 atom was adopted internationally around 1960–1961, replacing the conflicting older conventions. This did not change the chemical elements or the isotope masses; it standardized the denominator against which atomic masses are reported.

Why chronology supports understanding

Atomic mass measurement became more precise and the neutron/isotope picture matured. Once scientists recognized that samples contain multiple nuclides, the need for a particular reference isotope became unavoidable. The modern unit solves the reference problem; it does not make every isotope mass a whole number.

Historical conceptual shift: early relative scales, oxygen-based references and unified carbon-12 scale.

1.A.1.22.4 The unified atomic mass unit: one twelfth of a carbon-12 atom

The modern definition

The unified atomic mass unit, symbol u, equals one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state. The word atom matters: the reference includes the neutral atom's electrons, rather than referring only to a bare carbon nucleus. Its nuclear and electronic ground state are part of the formal definition.

Why carbon-12 is exactly 12 u on this scale

Let m(¹²C) denote the ground-state mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom. By definition, u=m(¹²C)/12. Therefore m(¹²C)/u=12 exactly. This is the reference identity, not a measured coincidence or a claim that every atom with mass number twelve weighs exactly 12 u.

What u measures

The unit u is a unit of mass, suitable for atoms, ions, molecules and subatomic particles. Its SI equivalent is approximately 1.66053906892 × 10⁻²⁷ kg according to 2022 CODATA recommended values. The kilogram equivalent is measured, not an exact decimal definition.

Use the correct reference object in answers

If an examiner says ‘one twelfth of a carbon-12 nucleus,’ the statement is not the exact unified atomic mass unit definition. A neutral carbon-12 atom contains six electrons and has a slightly different mass from its bare nucleus.

1 u = m(free neutral ground-state ¹²C atom)/12 ≈ 1.66053906892 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

1.A.1.22.5 Dalton (Da), unified atomic mass unit (u), and the legacy abbreviation amu

Dalton and u name the same modern unit

IUPAC recognizes the dalton, symbol Da, as equal to the unified atomic mass unit u. Thus 1 Da=1 u as units of mass. These symbols are especially convenient in biology, where protein masses may be described in daltons and kilodaltons.

Why the older term amu can be ambiguous

The abbreviation amu is widely seen in textbooks, but historically atomic mass unit conventions were not always unified. For precise modern communication, write u or Da and specify the current carbon-12 standard. An educational note may mention ‘amu’ as historical or colloquial terminology.

A protein mass in kilodaltons

A molecule described as 50 kDa has a mass of approximately 50,000 Da per molecule or specified species; it does not contain 50,000 atoms, weigh 50 kg or necessarily have a single sharp experimental mass peak. A protein sample can contain multiple chemical modifications or states.

Biology and physics use different preferred notations

Atomic isotope masses are commonly quoted in u, whereas protein and large complex masses often appear in Da or kDa. Both are mass units, not molar masses and not dimensionless relative molecular masses.

1 Da = 1 u;   1 kDa = 1000 Da;   50 kDa = 50,000 Da.

1.A.1.22.6 From kilograms to atomic-scale masses without losing dimensions

Why an atom's kilogram mass looks strange

One u is about 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁷ kilograms. A neutral carbon-12 atom therefore has mass exactly 12 u by definition, corresponding to approximately 1.99265 × 10⁻²⁶ kilograms. Such values are awkward for routine formulas, so u is more readable.

Conversion is ordinary dimensional analysis

If a particle has mass 16 u, multiply by kg/u to get approximately 16×1.660539×10⁻²⁷ = 2.65686×10⁻²⁶ kg. If a question instead gives kilograms, divide by the same conversion constant to obtain u.

The error check

A particle with a mass of a few tens of u should have a mass of order 10⁻²⁶ kg, not tens of kilograms. A protein at thousands of Da remains a molecule-scale mass, not a human-scale mass.

Precision should match the starting data

Using the 2022 CODATA constant to eleven significant digits does not justify reporting an answer to eleven digits when the supplied mass is a rounded integer. State approximations and preserve reasonable significant figures.

m(kg) = m(u) × (1.66053906892 × 10⁻²⁷ kg/u).

1.A.1.22.7 Mass number A versus actual isotopic atomic mass

The mass number is a count

For a nuclide ᴬ_ZX, A=Z+N counts whole protons and neutrons. It is dimensionless and always an integer for a definite nuclear composition. Oxygen-16 has A=16, while oxygen-18 has A=18.

An atomic mass is a physical quantity

The mass of a neutral ¹⁶O atom is approximately 15.994914620 u, not exactly 16 u. Carbon-13 has a neutral atomic mass close to 13.003354835 u, not exactly 13 u. These differences arise from the actual constituent masses and binding effects.

Why the approximation A u is useful but limited

At introductory level one may estimate an atomic isotope's mass by mass number multiplied by u because proton and neutron masses are close to one u. High-resolution measurements, mass spectrometry and nuclear binding calculations require measured atomic mass values, not integer approximations.

One nucleus is not a sample mean

A carbon-13 atom has a definite mass associated with that isotope, whereas ordinary carbon containing carbon-12 and carbon-13 has an abundance-weighted average near 12.01 u per atom. That sample mean is not the mass number of a new carbon isotope.

1.A.1.22.8 Why proton, neutron and electron masses do not simply add up to A u

Nucleons have slightly different rest masses

An isolated proton has a mass of approximately 1.007276 u, a neutron approximately 1.008665 u, and an electron approximately 0.00054858 u. These values are not exactly one u, and an atomic nucleus's mass is not simply the free constituent sum.

Binding energy changes a bound system's measured mass

When a nucleus forms, the bound system's mass is less than the total rest mass of widely separated constituent nucleons by an amount corresponding to binding energy through E=Δmc². Atomic electrons and their binding energies add smaller corrections when comparing neutral atomic and bare nuclear masses.

A useful carbon-12 reasoning check

Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons. Summing the free particle masses does not give the neutral carbon-12 atom's exact 12 u without accounting for nuclear binding and electrons. The exact 12 u is the chosen reference for the whole atom, not the direct sum of twelve particles each exactly one u.

What is not given by A, Z and N

Nuclear binding energy, neutral isotope mass, beta decay Q-value and separation energy require further evaluated physical data. Particle counting identifies composition but not the exact energy configuration of the system.

Nuclear binding energy B ≈ [Z mₚ + N mₙ − m_nucleus]c²; use nuclear, not neutral atomic masses.

1.A.1.22.9 Mass defect and the energy equivalence of one atomic mass unit

Einstein's mass–energy relation in nuclear science

The relation E=mc² connects a mass difference with an energy difference. In nuclear problems, a small mass defect expressed in u can correspond to a nuclear energy in MeV. It helps explain why isobars of equal A can differ in mass and why nuclear transitions release energy.

A useful approximate energy conversion

One unified atomic mass unit multiplied by c² corresponds to approximately 931.494 MeV of energy. Therefore a mass defect of 0.002 u corresponds to approximately 1.863 MeV, assuming that value is a genuine mass difference and the appropriate conversion applies.

A caution about nuclear mass versus neutral atomic mass

If using free protons and neutrons for a binding-energy expression, compare with a bare nuclear mass. If using neutral isotope atomic masses, electron-count and electron-binding contributions must be handled appropriately. Mixing conventions can introduce errors.

Why this chapter does not predict spontaneous decay from a mass number

A=14 for carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 does not mean they have equal physical masses or zero decay energy. Actual nuclide mass data and selection rules determine whether a particular nuclear transformation occurs.

1 u × c² ≈ 931.494 MeV;   ΔE (MeV) ≈ Δm (u) × 931.494.

1.A.1.22.10 Relative isotopic mass: a mass divided by the carbon-12 reference unit

The ratio definition

The relative isotopic mass Aᵣ of a specified isotope is the mass of its atom divided by the unified atomic mass constant mᵤ. If a neutral carbon-13 atom has mass 13.003354835 u, its relative isotopic mass has the numerical value 13.003354835 but no unit.

The distinction is not a small formatting preference

The statement ‘mass of isotope ¹³C is approximately 13.003355 u’ reports a mass. The statement ‘Aᵣ(¹³C)≈13.003355’ reports a dimensionless relative mass. Writing Aᵣ=13.003355 u mixes two different types of quantity.

Carbon-12 is the exact reference

Since m(¹²C)=12u, the relative isotopic mass Aᵣ(¹²C)=12 exactly. This does not force Aᵣ(¹³C)=13 exactly because physical particle masses and binding differ.

Useful for atomic mass calculations

Once each isotope's relative isotopic mass is known, average atomic weight can be calculated using amount fractions. The resulting mean Aᵣ for the defined element sample remains dimensionless.

Aᵣ(isotope) = m(atom of isotope)/mᵤ = m(atom in u), numerical value only.

1.A.1.22.11 Relative atomic mass of an element: an amount-fraction weighted mean

A real sample may contain more than one nuclide

Ordinary chlorine typically contains mostly chlorine-35 atoms and a smaller fraction of chlorine-37 atoms. Each isotope has a particular neutral atomic mass, and the sample's average mass per chlorine atom depends on how many atoms of each isotope are present.

Derive the weighted average

Let fᵢ be the amount fraction of isotope i, summing to one. Let mᵢ be the mass of one neutral atom of that isotope. The average atomic mass is m̄=Σ(fᵢ mᵢ). Divide by mᵤ to obtain Aᵣ(E)=Σ[fᵢ Aᵣ(isotope i)].

Why amount fractions, not arbitrary signal percentages

A mass spectrometer's raw peak intensity may depend on response or interference. The weighting formula requires corrected, appropriate isotope amount fractions, not any uncalibrated bar height. When a textbook explicitly gives isotope abundance percentages, convert them into fractions by dividing by 100.

A scientific scope note

The relative atomic mass is a property of the defined element sample. The standard atomic weight of the element is a recommended value or interval intended for normal materials, which need not describe isotopically enriched laboratory samples or every unusual environmental source.

Mean Aᵣ(E)=Σᵢ fᵢ Aᵣ(ⁱE), with fᵢ≥0 and Σᵢ fᵢ=1.

1.A.1.22.12 The chlorine story solved: 35 and 37 give a mean near 35.45

Why the classroom average is fractional

A toy sample with 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37 has approximate mean mass number 0.75×35+0.25×37=35.5. This is an approximation using integer mass numbers, not a claim that exact chlorine atomic weight in all samples is 35.5.

Use actual isotope masses when precision matters

Neutral atomic masses are approximately 34.968853 u for ³⁵Cl and 36.965903 u for ³⁷Cl. A natural-like sample with abundances around 75.76% and 24.24% has mean atomic mass close to 35.453 u. A specific sample's actual result depends on its measured fractions.

The current standardized view uses intervals for some elements

CIAAW provides chlorine standard atomic weight as an interval [35.446, 35.457] for normal materials, because natural isotope fractions vary. A periodic table may display a convenient abridged or conventional representative number such as 35.45, but it should not be treated as the exact mass of a single ³⁵Cl nucleus.

A quick plausibility check

A mixture of approximately 35 u and 37 u isotope masses must have an average between those values. With roughly three quarters of atoms near 35 u, its mean should lie closer to 35 than to 37. This provides a useful check before trusting arithmetic.

Illustrative m̄(Cl) ≈ 0.7576×34.968853 + 0.2424×36.965903 ≈ 35.453 u.

1.A.1.22.13 Carbon: why the periodic-table value is close to 12.011 rather than exactly 12

A standard with mass exactly twelve does not make every sample mean twelve

Every free neutral ground-state ¹²C atom has exactly 12 u by definition. Natural terrestrial carbon also includes carbon-13, so a typical carbon sample has an average mass per atom slightly above 12 u.

A simple toy calculation

Suppose 98.93% of carbon atoms are ¹²C and 1.07% are ¹³C, and ignore trace carbon-14. Using m(¹²C)=12u and m(¹³C)≈13.003355u gives m̄≈0.9893×12+0.0107×13.003355≈12.010736u. The corresponding Aᵣ is approximately 12.010736 without a unit.

This particular percentage is illustrative, not universal

Actual isotope abundances vary among sources. CIAAW reports carbon standard atomic weight as an interval [12.0096, 12.0116] for normal materials and also provides abridged values for routine chemical calculations.

The carbon-13 tracer connection

A laboratory sample enriched in stable ¹³C can have a substantially different average atomic mass from natural carbon. The enrichment does not create a new element or alter the definition of u.

Toy mean m̄(C)=0.9893×12u + 0.0107×13.003355u ≈12.010736u.

1.A.1.22.14 Oxygen: three stable nuclides contribute to an average

Why oxygen needs three terms

The common stable oxygen isotope family includes ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O. Their mass numbers are integers, but neutral atomic isotope masses differ slightly from those integers. A sample average requires all three isotope fractions for high accuracy.

A simple teaching mixture

If a hypothetical sample contains 90% oxygen-16 and 10% oxygen-18 with zero oxygen-17, using only rounded mass numbers yields 0.90×16+0.10×18=16.2. It is explicitly not a natural isotope distribution and not the exact isotope-mass result.

A more rigorous expression

Using isotope mass values m₁₆, m₁₇ and m₁₈ and fractions f₁₆, f₁₇ and f₁₈, write m̄=f₁₆m₁₆+f₁₇m₁₇+f₁₈m₁₈ with f₁₆+f₁₇+f₁₈=1. This makes clear why isotope measurement and sample context matter.

Biological and environmental significance

Water, carbon dioxide and biological tissues can vary in their stable oxygen isotope composition. Such fractionation can shift the sample's mean atomic mass slightly, but a δ¹⁸O measurement requires reference-ratio interpretation rather than a direct assumption about one exact atomic-weight value.

Aᵣ(O)=f₁₆ Aᵣ(¹⁶O)+f₁₇ Aᵣ(¹⁷O)+f₁₈ Aᵣ(¹⁸O).

1.A.1.22.15 Standard atomic weight, atomic weight of a sample, and a table's representative value

Three levels of description

An individual isotope has a specific atomic mass. A sample of an element has an abundance-weighted mean atomic mass and a relative atomic mass or atomic weight. A standard atomic weight is a CIAAW recommendation for normal materials, sometimes expressed as a range to reflect natural variability.

Why an interval does not mean the experiment is inaccurate

The interval [a,b] of some standard atomic weights reflects real variation in isotope composition among normal materials, not simply instrumental uncertainty around one universal true constant. Different rivers, rocks, plants or laboratory sources can have measurably different isotope mixes.

Examples with meaningful natural ranges

Current CIAAW tables show intervals for carbon, oxygen, chlorine and a set of other elements. The number on an abbreviated periodic table can be useful for routine calculations but should be interpreted as a recommended approximate representation.

When a sample is artificially enriched

A ¹³C-labelled reagent can be outside the normal-material range. For isotope-label mass calculations, use its stated isotope fractions and actual isotope masses rather than imposing the standard atomic weight of ordinary carbon.

1.A.1.22.16 The inverse problem: discover isotope abundance from an average

Two isotopes make one unknown fraction

Suppose a sample contains only isotopes with masses m₁ and m₂. Let x be the atom fraction of isotope 1 and 1−x the fraction of isotope 2. The mean mass is m̄=xm₁+(1−x)m₂. Rearrangement gives x=(m₂−m̄)/(m₂−m₁), assuming distinct isotope masses.

A classroom numerical

If isotope masses are approximated by 10u and 11u, and the measured average is 10.4u, then 10.4=x(10)+(1−x)(11)=11−x, so x=0.6. The sample contains 60% of the lighter isotope and 40% of the heavier isotope under the stated two-species model.

Bound checking protects the answer

A physically meaningful mean must lie between the two individual isotope masses, assuming both isotope fractions are nonnegative and sum to one. If an exam calculation produces x=1.3, either the data, assumptions or arithmetic are wrong.

Three or more isotopes need additional information

One average mass and the fact that fractions sum to one generally do not uniquely determine the abundance of three isotopes. At least one additional independent constraint or measurement is required. This is an identifiability issue, not an obstacle solved by a clever shortcut.

For two isotopes: x_light=(m_heavy−m̄)/(m_heavy−m_light).

1.A.1.22.17 Average atomic mass as a center of mass of isotope fractions

A visualization makes the weighted mean intuitive

Imagine each isotope represented by its actual neutral atomic mass on a horizontal axis, and the sample fractions by relative weights. The mean sits between them and is pulled toward the isotope that is more abundant. If the heavier isotope fraction rises, the mean shifts toward its mass.

A two-isotope expression shows the trend

For two isotope masses m_L and m_H, m̄=m_L+x_H(m_H−m_L). Increasing heavy isotope atom fraction x_H increases the average by (m_H−m_L) per unit change in fraction, holding the isotope masses fixed.

A small heavy fraction may still be measurable

Natural carbon-13 contributes only a small fraction of ordinary carbon atoms, yet its mass difference from carbon-12 shifts the average and creates detectable isotopic signals. An instrument that measures exact mass or isotope ratio can be sensitive to small composition changes.

Schematic weighted isotope mean between a light and heavy isotope; not a measured spectrum.

1.A.1.22.18 Precision and uncertainty: never turn rounded abundance into invented accuracy

Significant figures belong to the input

If an exam gives isotope abundances as 75% and 25% and approximate isotope masses as 35 and 37, a result like 35.5 is justified. Reporting fifteen decimal places would imply precision the question does not support.

Measured isotope masses and fraction uncertainties

A high-quality physical measurement can report isotope mass with uncertainty and isotope fractions with uncertainty. Their combination determines uncertainty in the calculated mean. In general, uncertainty in fractions may dominate in samples with variable isotope composition.

Reference data should be versioned

The CIAAW standard-weight tables are periodically updated when evaluated new evidence changes recommended values. A rigorous report names the data source or table version, rather than implying a printed periodic-table value is an immutable constant of the element.

Distinguish error from natural variability

Instrumental uncertainty describes limitations of a measurement; natural variation describes actual differences among valid samples. An atomic-weight interval for an element can reflect normal-material variation, not that every sample has an unknown measurement error of that width.

1.A.1.22.19 Protons, neutrons, electrons and the mass of a neutral atom versus an ion

An ion changes electron count, not A

A neutral ²³Na atom has eleven electrons, while Na⁺ has ten electrons. Their nuclear composition is unchanged. The ion's mass differs by approximately the mass of one electron, adjusted for ionization energy, rather than by one full atomic mass unit.

A proton mass is not the same as the neutral hydrogen atom's mass

A bare proton has a mass around 1.007276 u, whereas neutral ¹H has atomic mass around 1.007825 u because it includes an electron and binding-energy effects. A mass-spectrometry calculation that confuses these quantities can shift predicted ion m/z values.

The instrument needs an ion formula and charge state

A singly charged molecule [M+H]⁺ is usually heavier than the neutral molecule by about the proton mass, with mass-to-charge approximately m(M)+m(H⁺) divided by 1. Multiply charged ions divide mass by |z|, with proper accounting for added charges/adducts.

The isotope's nucleus is unchanged in routine MS ionization

Electrospray or another ordinary ionization process changes a molecule's charge state but does not transmute carbon-12 into carbon-13 or change mass number A. The observed spectrum therefore combines composition and ion state, not a nuclear reaction.

Approximate [M+H]⁺ m/z ≈ (m_neutral + 1.007276 u)/1, if singly charged and protonated.

1.A.1.22.20 From one atom to a mole: Avogadro's exact count after the 2019 SI revision

One mole is an exact specified number of entities

Since the 2019 SI redefinition, the mole is based on an exact Avogadro constant Nₐ=6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹. One mole of specified particles contains exactly that many particles, whether the entities are atoms, ions, molecules or another clearly named unit.

The carbon-12 reference still defines u

The atomic mass constant is defined from one free neutral ground-state ¹²C atom, while the modern mole is defined independently by an exact count. As a subtle consequence, the molar mass of unbound ¹²C is extremely close to 12 g/mol but is no longer exactly fixed at that value in the SI.

Why introductory chemistry remains simple

For ordinary laboratory calculations, relative atomic mass numerically corresponds extremely closely to molar mass in g/mol; the modern tiny correction is far below routine precision. An accurate graduate-level explanation should mention the distinction without making introductory problems unnecessarily complicated.

Never attach molar units to Aᵣ

Relative atomic mass Aᵣ(E) is dimensionless. Molar mass M(E) has units g/mol or kg/mol. A numerical correspondence near 12.011 does not make the two quantities identical.

Nₐ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ exactly;   M(entity)=Nₐ × m(one entity).

1.A.1.22.21 Relative molecular mass Mᵣ and the ordinary molecule

IUPAC's definition

Relative molecular mass Mᵣ is the mass of a molecule divided by the unified atomic mass constant. This ratio is dimensionless; it does not possess the units u, Da or g/mol. For a molecule with specified isotopic atoms, its mass can be calculated by adding appropriate atomic masses, allowing for chemical binding and charge where high precision is needed.

An elementary water calculation

For water H₂O in a normal isotope mixture, using H≈1.008 and O≈15.999 yields Mᵣ≈2×1.008+15.999≈18.015. That is an abundance-dependent average for ordinary water, not the exact mass of every possible individual H₂O isotopologue.

Why terminology matters

The average mass per water molecule can be expressed as about 18.015 Da, while the corresponding relative molecular mass is about 18.015 without a unit. The associated molar mass is approximately 18.015 g/mol. These numerical similarities come from shared reference conventions, not because the quantities have identical dimensions.

Chemical composition first

Calculate molecular atom counts from the molecular formula, select the correct isotope-specific or average atomic masses for the question, and then state the requested output type. A mass-spectrum question about [M+H]⁺ generally needs an ion mass, not only the mean molecular weight.

Mᵣ(molecule)=m(molecule)/mᵤ; introductory average Mᵣ≈Σ nᵢAᵣ(Eᵢ).

1.A.1.22.22 Formula mass: sodium chloride and biological salts

Formula unit versus discrete molecule

Solid sodium chloride is an ionic lattice. Its composition may be written NaCl as a formula unit even though one does not describe the entire crystal as independent neutral NaCl molecules. Formula mass is therefore a useful term for this kind of substance.

A numerical example

Using ordinary sodium Aᵣ≈22.990 and chlorine Aᵣ≈35.45 gives NaCl relative formula mass near 58.44. The approximate molar mass is 58.44 g/mol under normal isotope composition, and the different unit belongs explicitly to the molar mass.

The biological relevance

Salts are common in cell buffers and physiological solutions. Converting a weighed mass of reagent to a concentration depends on the correct chemical form—anhydrous salt, hydrate, acid, base, mixture or other preparation—not merely the name of one element.

Dissociation adds another layer

A solid salt may form solvated ions in water. Its formula mass supports material balance, while ionic strength, activities, effective osmotic pressure and transport behavior require separate chemical and biological treatment.

1.A.1.22.23 Water isotopologues: monoisotopic mass versus mean molecular mass

One molecule has a specific nuclear inventory

An individual molecule ¹H₂¹⁶O has neutral monoisotopic mass near 18.010565 u. A normal water sample also contains a smaller population of molecules involving ²H, ¹⁷O or ¹⁸O, so the sample's mean molecular mass is near 18.015 u. These statements are compatible.

One oxygen substitution is not exactly a two-u shift

Changing one oxygen-16 to oxygen-18 increases neutral molecular mass by about 2.004245 u, because actual isotope masses differ slightly from integer A. The label does not mean that a nucleus has gained two protons; oxygen Z stays eight.

One hydrogen substitution has another exact shift

Replacing ¹H with ²H increases mass by about 1.006277 u. This is close to, but not exactly, one u. Two deuterium substitutions would roughly double that isotope shift relative to ¹H₂¹⁶O, before very small chemical effects.

Three questions, three answers

For conventional water molar mass use about 18.015 g/mol. For all-light ¹H₂¹⁶O neutral mass use about 18.010565 Da. For δ¹⁸O of water use the isotope ratio compared with a specified reference scale, not a single molecular-mass number.

1.A.1.22.24 Glucose: exact mass is not conventional molecular weight

The molecule and its formula

Glucose has formula C₆H₁₂O₆. NIST chemical reference data report a conventional molecular weight near 180.1559 and a molar mass near 180.156 g/mol for the specified ordinary compound context.

The monoisotopic mass is different

A molecule composed of six ¹²C, twelve ¹H and six ¹⁶O atoms has an all-light monoisotopic neutral mass about 180.063388 Da. The difference from 180.156 occurs because average mass accounts for heavier isotope-containing molecular species.

The mass spectrometry trap

A high-resolution MS feature near monoisotopic m/z for protonated glucose should be compared with the appropriate exact ion mass and adduct/charge state. Substituting an average molar mass of 180.156 into a monoisotopic ion assignment can be misleading.

Stable tracer meaning

If all six carbon positions are labeled ¹³C, the neutral molecule mass is higher than the all-¹²C form by about 6.02013 Da. This mass shift supports isotope identity but is not itself the amount of glucose metabolized per second.

1.A.1.22.25 Proteins, daltons, kilodaltons and polymer chemistry

Kilodaltons scale the same mass unit

A 50 kDa molecular species has mass near 50,000 Da. The prefix kilo represents a factor of 1000; it does not change the underlying carbon-12 scale. Relative molecular mass around 50,000 is unitless, while mass in Da has units.

Residue mass is not free amino-acid mass

When amino acids form peptide bonds, the chemical combination loses elements corresponding to water. For an ordinary unmodified linear n-residue peptide, the mass is approximately the sum of free amino-acid molecular masses minus (n−1) water molecule masses.

A quick dipeptide illustration

Suppose two free amino acid masses are 75.07 Da and 89.09 Da. A normal dipeptide calculation gives about 75.07+89.09−18.015=146.145 Da. This rounded illustration is not an exact monoisotopic result.

Biological variability matters

Protein post-translational modifications, sequence variants, glycans, adducts, oxidation and isotope composition affect measured mass. A reported 50 kDa protein band or catalog value is not a guarantee of one precise isotope-specific ion peak.

Linear peptide: m≈Σm(free amino acids)−(n−1)m(H₂O), under specified chemical assumptions.

1.A.1.22.26 Gel electrophoresis provides apparent mass, not automatic exact mass

Migration is a measurement, not an identity label

SDS-PAGE estimates protein size by comparing gel mobility with mass standards under an electrophoretic model. Conformation, unusual composition, disulfide state, detergent binding and protein modifications can alter migration.

An apparent band may not represent one pure species

A band near 50 kDa can contain a mixture or a protein migrating atypically. Its exact sequence and monoisotopic mass generally require appropriate chemical characterization, often including proteomic mass spectrometry.

Do not infer function from mass alone

Two proteins can have similar molecular masses yet different amino-acid sequences and biological roles. Even a measured exact mass does not always uniquely identify a protein in a complex sample.

Use the right evidence combination

Gel mobility can support a size estimate; peptide MS/MS, sequence data and biochemical assays may support protein identity and function. These are different levels of inference.

1.A.1.22.27 Mass spectrometry reports ion mass-to-charge ratio m/z

Ion charge matters

A mass spectrometer measures mass-to-charge of ions, not simply integer mass number A or the neutral molecular weight. One analyte may produce singly charged, multiply charged or adduct-containing ions with different positions in the spectrum.

A protonated ion

For a positive singly charged molecule [M+H]⁺, the m/z value is approximately m(neutral)+1.007276, using masses in daltons and charge number one. That proton mass is not exactly the mass of a neutral hydrogen atom.

A doubly charged ion

For [M+2H]²⁺, the approximate reported m/z is [m(neutral)+2×1.007276]/2. The division by two can move a large protein ion to a much smaller m/z scale.

Ordinary ionization leaves nuclear identities unchanged

Producing an ion changes electrons and chemical association, not carbon-12 into carbon-13. A label-related spectral shift must be interpreted alongside charge and adduct information.

1.A.1.22.28 Isotope envelopes: why a molecule produces M, M+1 and M+2 peaks

Natural carbon-13 creates extra mass categories

Even an unlabelled molecule may contain one or more natural ¹³C nuclei. An ion with one ¹³C replacing one ¹²C has an exact mass increment around 1.003355 u, producing an M+1-like signal under usual naming conventions.

M+2 has multiple possible nuclear origins

One ¹⁸O substitution adds about 2.004245 u, whereas two ¹³C substitutions add about 2.006710 u. They both lie near nominal M+2 but differ in exact mass; other isotopes and elements may also contribute.

The number of atoms changes the pattern

A large molecule containing many carbon positions can exhibit a stronger M+1 category than a two-carbon molecule, even at the same natural ¹³C atom fraction. More positions provide more possible ways to contain one heavy carbon.

Peaks do not uniquely identify isotope positions

An M+1 signal may establish an isotope-count contribution under a validated formula, but parent ion mass alone often cannot distinguish positional isotopomers. Fragmentation or NMR can add positional information.

1.A.1.22.29 Probability model for a small carbon isotope envelope

Assume independent isotope incorporation

Suppose a molecule has n equivalent carbon sites with independent chance p that any site carries ¹³C. Then the probability of k ¹³C substitutions is P(k)=C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. This is a mathematical model, not a universal rule for deliberate tracer designs.

A two-carbon example

For n=2 and p=0.10, M+0 probability=(0.9)²=0.81, M+1=2(0.10)(0.90)=0.18, and M+2=(0.10)²=0.01. Probabilities sum to one.

Why the factor of two arises

One heavy carbon can occupy the first or second of two sites. The same nominal isotopologue category can therefore include different positional isotopomers.

Where the model stops

In [1-¹³C]glucose, the label is intentionally at one site; in [U-¹³C₆]glucose multiple labels are correlated. Biochemical pathway atom mapping and mixture sources can defeat an independent-site assumption.

P(k) = C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, when n sites are independent with identical p.

1.A.1.22.30 Exact ¹³C, ¹⁵N and ¹⁸O shifts in isotope-labelled metabolites

A carbon-13 substitution

Replacing ¹²C with ¹³C increases neutral molecular mass by about 1.003355 u. Six such replacements add about 6.02013 u to a molecule with six carbon atoms. A mass shift does not determine their biochemical pathway by itself.

A nitrogen-15 substitution

Replacing ¹⁴N by ¹⁵N increases mass by approximately 0.997035 u. In high-resolution experiments, a one-nitrogen substitution may be distinguished from one ¹³C substitution based on exact mass if the resolution and chemical formula permit.

An oxygen-18 substitution

Replacing ¹⁶O by ¹⁸O increases mass by about 2.004245 u. Two carbon-13 substitutions add approximately 2.006710 u, giving an exact-mass difference of 0.002465 u between these nominal M+2 possibilities.

Charge state changes apparent separation

At magnitude two ion charge, the observed m/z separation is approximately half the underlying neutral mass difference for comparable ions. Accurate identification uses isotope composition, adduct and charge state together.

Δ¹³C≈1.003355 u; Δ¹⁵N≈0.997035 u; Δ¹⁸O≈2.004245 u.

1.A.1.22.31 Average molecular mass and monoisotopic mass answer different questions

The mean represents a collection

If a molecule contains elements with natural isotope mixtures, an average molecular mass uses their average atom masses. It predicts the mean over molecular isotopologues when the assumed elemental isotope compositions and correlations are appropriate. It does not mean every molecule has exactly the mean mass.

A monoisotopic value specifies a particular composition

The all-light molecular species ¹²C₆¹H₁₂¹⁶O₆ has neutral mass around 180.063388 Da, while ordinary glucose has a conventional molar mass near 180.156 g/mol. The two values describe different quantities rather than an experimental contradiction.

A named monoisotopic choice requires care

For ordinary light-element organic compounds, selecting the most abundant isotope for each element often matches the light isotope, but that cannot be assumed as a universal law for every element or sample. State which isotope composition is being used.

Use the measurement context

For a routine mass-to-moles conversion, conventional molar mass is relevant. For high-resolution ion assignment, an isotopologue exact mass and charge are relevant. For tracer data, measured label composition and atom mapping matter.

1.A.1.22.32 Stable-isotope enrichment: mass changes without changing the chemical element

A heavier carbon nuclide still has six protons

A molecule enriched with ¹³C can undergo ordinary enzymatic reactions while each labeled carbon remains ¹³C. Its molecular mass spectrum changes in predictable increments, but the element stays carbon throughout chemistry.

One labelled glucose has multiple possible mass categories

[1-¹³C]glucose has one specified labeled carbon, while [U-¹³C₆]glucose has six labelled carbon positions in the indicated species. These differ in heavy carbon count and positional information, and can produce different downstream isotope patterns.

One peak is not a metabolic flux

A detected M+3 lactate fraction after labelled glucose input may support three ¹³C carbon atoms in the lactate pool, but absolute glycolytic flux needs substrate enrichment, pool size and/or time, and a network model. A spectrum tells us composition of measured species, not all pathway rates.

The connection to atomic weights

An enriched sample may have a sample-specific mean Aᵣ outside the CIAAW standard interval for normal carbon. That is expected: the carbon-12 definition of u has not changed, but the fractions of carbon isotopes in the sample have.

1.A.1.22.33 Isotope atom fraction, isotope ratio and delta notation

Two types of fraction are often confused

For two isotopes, the heavy atom fraction is x=N_heavy/(N_heavy+N_light), while isotope ratio R=N_heavy/N_light. They obey x=R/(1+R). A measured 1% atom fraction is not exactly an R of 0.01 because its denominator includes heavy atoms.

Delta compares two isotope ratios

δ=(R_sample/R_reference−1)×1000‰. Carbon δ¹³C is commonly expressed on the VPDB scale; water δ¹⁸O commonly uses VSMOW. These values reflect sample-reference comparison and need not be directly equal to standard atomic-weight shifts.

A three-isotope system needs more information

Oxygen contains ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O; one measured oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 ratio does not automatically determine the oxygen-17 fraction. An exact sample mean atomic weight may need all relevant isotope fractions.

The weighted mean remains the master formula

After converting available isotope ratios into complete normalized amount fractions, mean atomic mass is Σfᵢmᵢ, and Aᵣ is its ratio to u. Without sufficient isotope composition data, the exact mean remains underdetermined.

x=R/(1+R), for a two-isotope pool;   δ=(R_sample/R_reference−1)×1000‰.

1.A.1.22.34 Three isotopes and the identifiability problem

One mean does not reveal three unknown fractions

Suppose an element has three isotopes with masses m₁,m₂,m₃. Their unknown fractions satisfy f₁+f₂+f₃=1 and f₁m₁+f₂m₂+f₃m₃=m̄. Two equations generally cannot uniquely determine three unknown fractions.

A numerical toy demonstration

For illustrative mass numbers 10, 11 and 12 with average 11, the fractions (0,1,0) and (0.5,0,0.5) both give the same mean. These are distinct compositions with the same average. Many other combinations may also work.

What resolves the ambiguity

An independent isotope ratio, an additional measured abundance or a justified compositional constraint can allow the system to be solved. One average mass is therefore a compressed property of a distribution, not a full fingerprint.

Scientific interpretation

In mass spectrometry, an isotope envelope contains more information than one reported average. In biology, combining precursor isotope patterns, product distributions and time courses may be necessary before inferring how labeled material moved through metabolism.

1.A.1.22.35 Molar mass in biochemistry, clinical chemistry and laboratory units

Molar mass converts amount into mass

For one molecular species, M is the mass of one mole of specified entities, with units such as g/mol. Relative atomic mass Aᵣ has no units; molar mass has dimensions of mass per amount of substance.

A glucose numerical

Using M(glucose)≈180.156 g/mol, one mmol of glucose has mass about 180.156 mg. Therefore 1 mmol/L corresponds to 180.156 mg/L, or about 18.0156 mg/dL. This is a dimensional numerical, not a diagnosis or health interpretation.

The exact count basis

The Avogadro constant is exactly 6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹ in the post-2019 SI. The molar mass constant differs from 1 g/mol by an extremely tiny amount, so the familiar numerical relation between Da and g/mol remains accurate for ordinary laboratory calculations.

Sample chemistry matters

A measured analyte can occur as free form, salt, hydrate or a mixture. The correct molar mass depends on the entity whose amount is being described; applying the wrong molecular formula produces a concentration error regardless of nuclear isotope precision.

n=m/M;   N_entities=n×Nₐ;   1 mmol/L glucose ≈18.0156 mg/dL.

1.A.1.22.36 Measurement quality: uncertainty, resolution and traceable isotope standards

A reported atomic weight is not an immutable universal constant

For some elements, normal isotope abundance varies by source. CIAAW provides standard atomic-weight intervals; a selected abridged value is useful for routine problems, while a precise source-specific study must measure the actual isotopic composition.

High-resolution mass spectrometry adds its own uncertainty

Mass calibration, ion charge, adduct chemistry, overlapping peaks, natural abundance and background signals influence formula assignments. A nominal M+2 peak cannot automatically distinguish two ¹³C atoms from one ¹⁸O atom without sufficient resolution and supporting evidence.

The units of uncertainty should match the quantity

An isotope atomic mass may be given as m=...u with mass uncertainty. Aᵣ is dimensionless. A molar mass uncertainty is expressed in g/mol; a delta isotope ratio often in per mille. Combining unlike uncertainties without unit definitions is invalid.

Avoid invented precision

If input fractions are rounded to whole percentages and isotope masses to integer A, the final value should reflect those assumptions. More decimal places in the calculator do not create better data.

1.A.1.22.37 Where atomic mass joins biological mass spectrometry and isotope tracing

Metabolomics

Metabolite mass spectrometry uses precise masses and isotope patterns to help assign molecules and understand labelled nutrient incorporation. The distinction between monoisotopic mass, isotope envelope and conventional molar mass prevents incorrectly matching peaks.

Proteomics

Protein and peptide masses are reported in Da or kDa and can reflect sequence, terminal groups and modifications. Ion charge produces multiple m/z peaks in electrospray; inferred protein identity needs peptide fragments and database or other experimental support.

Nutrition and stable isotope studies

Deuterium and oxygen-18 labels can track water turnover and CO₂ pathways using isotope-ratio measurements. Their stable isotope status does not equate measured biological washout with radioactive decay or mean atomic mass change.

Biological inference limits

A label's mass increment demonstrates isotopic substitution in an identified species under suitable controls, but uptake, synthesis and metabolic flux are rate or mechanistic claims that require additional kinetic and biochemical evidence.

1.A.1.22.38 Final exam algorithm: identify the target quantity before solving

Identify the question's noun

Mass number A is a nucleon count. Atomic isotope mass m is expressed in u/Da. Relative isotopic or atomic mass Aᵣ is unitless. Relative molecular mass Mᵣ is unitless. Molar mass M is mass per mole. An ion's m/z is determined by ion mass and charge.

Pick the correct inputs

For a weighted average, require isotope fractions and isotope masses. For relative atomic mass, divide mean atomic mass by mᵤ. For a molecular exact mass, use specified isotope masses and formula. For m/z, include the charge/adduct state.

Check the basic bounds

Fractions must sum to 1, and the mean mass of a two-isotope mixture must lie between isotope masses. The difference between two nominal A values is not necessarily the exact measured mass difference.

Finish with scientific scope

If isotopic composition is not given, do not invent a precise sample weight. If a single M+1 peak is observed, do not assert a unique tracer pathway or an enzyme flux. State what's measured and which data would resolve uncertainty.

Master check: A = integer nucleon count; u/Da = mass unit; Aᵣ and Mᵣ = dimensionless; g/mol = molar mass; m/z = ion mass divided by charge number; isotope abundance = atom fractions; metabolic flux = amount/time.

Question: Given m(¹²C)=12u, find its relative isotopic mass Aᵣ.

Complete step-by-step answer: The relative isotopic mass is m(atom)/u. Thus Aᵣ(¹²C)=12u/u=12 exactly. The result is a dimensionless ratio, while the physical atomic mass 12u carries a mass unit. The number twelve is exact because ¹²C defines u.

Verification: Unit cancellation u/u yields a pure number.

Worked example 5. Convert a kilogram mass to u

Question: An atomic-scale particle has mass 3.321078×10⁻²⁷kg. Estimate its mass in u.

Complete step-by-step answer: Divide by 1.660539×10⁻²⁷kg/u. The ratio 3.321078/1.660539≈2.000000 gives approximately 2u. Because the supplied decimal is rounded, the result is an approximation rather than an exact statement about a particular isotope.

Verification: 2×1.660539×10⁻²⁷kg = 3.321078×10⁻²⁷kg.

Common exam error: Do not multiply when converting kg to u.

Worked example 6. Mass number versus physical mass of carbon-13

Question: Carbon-13 has A=13 and neutral atomic mass about 13.003355u. Which number is unitless?

Complete step-by-step answer: A=13 is the number of nucleons and is unitless. The actual atomic mass m≈13.003355u has the unit u. The relative isotopic mass Aᵣ≈13.003355 is unitless because the atomic mass is divided by 1u. Three similar-looking numbers describe different scientific quantities.

Verification: Mass(u)/u gives Aᵣ numerically.

Common exam error: Do not call mass number A=13.003355.

Worked example 7. Find neutrons without mistaking isotope mass

Question: A neutral ¹⁸O atom has measured mass near 17.99916u. How many neutrons does its nucleus contain?

Complete step-by-step answer: Oxygen's atomic number Z=8; ¹⁸O has integer A=18, so N=18−8=10 neutrons. Use mass number for neutron counting, not rounding a measured mass and treating decimals as fractional nucleons.

Verification: 8 protons+10 neutrons=18 nucleons.

Common exam error: 17.99916 is a mass in u, not the nuclear neutron count.

Worked example 8. Dalton and kilodalton conversion

Question: A protein is described as 48kDa. Convert to Da and u.

Complete step-by-step answer: By definition 1kDa=1000Da and 1Da=1u, so 48kDa=48,000Da=48,000u per specified molecular species. Its relative mass would have numerical value about 48,000 if measured against u, but without a unit. The label alone does not give exact sequence or residue count.

Verification: 48×1000=48,000.

Common exam error: Do not treat kDa as kilograms or atoms per molecule.

Worked example 9. Chlorine integer-mass classroom average

Question: A hypothetical chlorine mixture contains 75% isotope-35 and 25% isotope-37. Calculate approximate Aᵣ using integers.

Complete step-by-step answer: Convert percentages to amount fractions 0.75 and 0.25. The textbook approximation Aᵣ≈0.75×35+0.25×37=26.25+9.25=35.50. It is unitless. If exact isotope masses were given, use them for a more accurate sample mean.

Verification: The result 35.50 lies between 35 and 37.

Common exam error: Do not claim every atom has mass number 35.5.

Worked example 10. Chlorine average using actual isotope masses

Question: For 75% ³⁵Cl at 34.968853u and 25% ³⁷Cl at 36.965903u, find sample mean mass.

Complete step-by-step answer: Mean=0.75×34.968853+0.25×36.965903=26.22663975+9.24147575=35.4681155u. The relative atomic mass of this defined sample is Aᵣ≈35.4681155 without a unit. This differs from the integer approximation 35.5 because actual atomic masses are not integer A.

Common exam error: Do not mix exact isotope masses with unnormalized percentages.

Worked example 11. A natural-like chlorine mixture

Question: Using 75.76% ³⁵Cl and 24.24% ³⁷Cl with masses 34.968853u and 36.965903u, find the mean.

Complete step-by-step answer: f₁=0.7576 and f₂=0.2424. Mean=0.7576×34.968853+0.2424×36.965903≈35.452938u. Its relative atomic mass is approximately 35.452938, unitless, for the stated fractions. CIAAW's normal-material chlorine interval reflects source-dependent variability.

Worked example 14. Average mass of a simple oxygen toy sample

Question: Assume 90% ¹⁶O and 10% ¹⁸O, neglect ¹⁷O, and use their integer mass numbers as masses. Find the approximate mean.

Complete step-by-step answer: Using the explicitly approximate masses 16 and 18, mean=0.9×16+0.1×18=14.4+1.8=16.2u. The relative atomic mass in this toy model is 16.2. Natural oxygen does not generally contain 10% oxygen-18; actual accurate calculations use isotope-specific masses.

Worked example 15. Weighted average of three artificial isotopes

Question: Three isotopes have masses 10u,11u,12u and fractions 0.20,0.30,0.50. Find sample mean.

Complete step-by-step answer: The fractions sum to 1. Mean=0.20×10+0.30×11+0.50×12=2+3.3+6=11.3u. Its dimensionless relative atomic mass is 11.3. The answer is a sample mean, not the atomic mass of every constituent atom.

Verification: Weighted mean 11.3 is within the mass range 10–12.

Common exam error: Do not normalize fractions twice when they already total one.

Worked example 16. Find abundance from a two-isotope mean

Question: Isotopes of masses 10u and 11u give mean 10.40u. Find both fractions.

Complete step-by-step answer: Let x be the fraction of lighter mass 10. Then 10.4=10x+11(1−x)=11−x. Thus x=0.6 or 60% lighter, with 40% heavier. The inference assumes exactly two isotope species and the given masses.

Verification: 0.6×10+0.4×11=10.4.

Common exam error: One mean cannot uniquely recover three isotope fractions.

Worked example 17. A different inverse abundance calculation

Question: A sample has isotope masses 20u and 22u and mean 21.5u. Find heavier isotope fraction.

Complete step-by-step answer: Let x be the fraction of 22u. Then 21.5=(1−x)20+x22=20+2x. Thus x=(21.5−20)/2=0.75, giving 75% heavier and 25% lighter. Fractions are nonnegative and sum to one.

Verification: 0.25×20+0.75×22=5+16.5=21.5.

Common exam error: Do not confuse the heavy fraction with an absolute isotope mass.

Worked example 18. The non-identifiable three-isotope problem

Question: Three masses 10u, 11u and 12u have sample mean 11u. Determine all fractions uniquely.

Complete step-by-step answer: This problem is underdetermined. One valid composition is 100% of the 11u isotope. Another is 50% of 10u and 50% of 12u. Both obey fraction sum one and have mean 11u. At least one further independent measurement or assumption is required for a unique three-fraction solution.

Verification: 0×10+1×11+0×12=11 and .5×10+.5×12=11.

Common exam error: Do not invent a unique abundance vector.

Worked example 19. Mass number equality is not equal exact mass

Question: Two atoms are ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N. What is equal, and can their exact mass difference be inferred?

Complete step-by-step answer: Their nuclei have equal integer A=14 but different Z and neutron counts. They are isobars. Their actual neutral atomic masses differ due to binding and constituent contributions; numerical mass difference requires evaluated isotope mass measurements. The isotope names alone cannot yield a precise Q-value.

Verification: A(¹⁴C)=A(¹⁴N)=14 by nuclear bookkeeping.

Common exam error: Do not say the exact mass difference equals zero.

Worked example 20. Compute relative mass from a given atomic mass

Question: An atom's measured mass is 23.985042u. What is its relative isotopic mass?

Complete step-by-step answer: Relative isotopic mass equals m/u. Thus Aᵣ=23.985042u/u=23.985042, a dimensionless value. This does not, on its own, establish the element's natural isotope abundance or its sample-average relative atomic mass.

Verification: The numerical value is identical in u units, but the ratio has no unit.

Common exam error: Do not give the output as 23.985042g/mol without another calculation.

Thirty common mistakes, corrected using independent scientific checks

Misconception 1

Incorrect statement: One u is defined using a carbon-12 nucleus.

Accurate interpretation: One unified atomic mass unit is one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state.

Verification: The electrons belong to the reference atom; a bare nucleus has a different mass.

Misconception 2

Incorrect statement: Exactly one u equals 1.66053906892×10⁻²⁷kg by definition.

Accurate interpretation: The carbon-12 comparison defines u. Its SI kilogram equivalent is a measured CODATA recommended approximate value.

Verification: Carbon-12 neutral atomic mass is exactly 12u, but its kg mass has experimental uncertainty.

Misconception 3

Incorrect statement: Da and u are two incompatible mass scales.

Accurate interpretation: Dalton and unified atomic mass unit refer to the same unit; 1Da=1u.

Verification: Protein masses in Da and atomic isotope masses in u use one carbon-12 reference.

Misconception 4

Incorrect statement: The letters amu must always mean the modern unified scale in historical documents.

Accurate interpretation: Older atomic mass scales had differing reference conventions, so modern scientific work should use u or Da clearly.

Verification: The unified carbon-12 standard replaced older oxygen-related scale ambiguities.

Misconception 5

Incorrect statement: The atomic mass of every isotope exactly equals mass number A in u.

Accurate interpretation: A is an integer nucleon count; physical atomic masses differ from exact integers in general.

Verification: ¹⁶O has A=16 and neutral atomic mass about 15.994915u.

Misconception 6

Incorrect statement: Relative isotopic mass Aᵣ must be written with unit u.

Accurate interpretation: Aᵣ is atomic mass divided by u and is dimensionless.

Verification: m(¹³C)≈13.003355u but Aᵣ(¹³C)≈13.003355 with no unit.

Misconception 7

Incorrect statement: Relative atomic mass of natural carbon is exactly twelve.

Accurate interpretation: Only neutral ground-state ¹²C has exact reference mass 12u; a natural carbon sample contains some ¹³C.

Verification: Weighted average carbon Aᵣ is typically near 12.011, depending on isotope composition.

Misconception 8

Incorrect statement: A relative atomic mass of 35.45 means an atom has 35.45 nucleons.

Accurate interpretation: A is an integer for one nucleus; 35.45 describes an average isotope mixture in the appropriate periodic table context.

Verification: ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl are separate nuclides, with sample mean near 35.45.

Misconception 9

Incorrect statement: An isobar pair must have the same exact physical mass.

Accurate interpretation: Isobars share the integer mass number, not exact neutral atomic mass.

Verification: ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N have equal A=14 yet different nuclear binding and measured masses.

Misconception 10

Incorrect statement: Every particle inside an atom has exactly one atomic mass unit of mass.

Accurate interpretation: Protons, neutrons and electrons have distinct masses; nuclear binding changes the bound-system total.

Verification: Electron mass is only about 0.00054858u, not 1u.

Misconception 11

Incorrect statement: Atomic mass defect is an error in weighing an atom.

Accurate interpretation: Mass defect is a physical bound-system mass difference related to binding energy.

Verification: E=Δmc² gives the energy equivalence of a properly defined mass difference.

Misconception 12

Incorrect statement: One u corresponds to exactly 1MeV/c².

Accurate interpretation: The mass-energy equivalence of one u is about 931.494MeV/c².

Verification: 0.002u mass defect corresponds to about 1.863MeV, not 0.002MeV.

Misconception 13

Incorrect statement: You can average isotope percentages without dividing by 100.

Accurate interpretation: Use normalized amount fractions; if percentages are given, convert 75% to 0.75.

Verification: 0.75×35+0.25×37=35.5 rather than 75×35+25×37.

Misconception 14

Incorrect statement: A weighted mean of two positive isotope masses can fall beyond both.

Accurate interpretation: With nonnegative fractions summing to one, the mean lies between the component masses.

Verification: A 35/37 isotope mixture must have mean between 35 and 37 when those masses are assumed.

Misconception 15

Incorrect statement: Atomic weight of an element is the same in every sample.

Accurate interpretation: Isotope abundances can vary among sources, changing mean relative atomic mass.

Verification: CIAAW reports intervals for chlorine and some other elements in normal materials.

Misconception 16

Incorrect statement: A CIAAW atomic-weight interval means only poor laboratory accuracy.

Accurate interpretation: Intervals often express genuine natural isotope-composition variability among normal materials.

Verification: The width is not merely an instrument error bar around one universal chlorine value.

Misconception 17

Incorrect statement: A 90% ¹³C-enriched reagent must have ordinary carbon atomic weight.

Accurate interpretation: Isotope enrichment changes sample mean mass and can exceed normal-material ranges.

Verification: 90% ¹³C and 10% ¹²C give Aᵣ≈12.9030, not ordinary natural carbon.

Misconception 18

Incorrect statement: One average atomic mass uniquely determines every isotope fraction.

Accurate interpretation: For three or more isotopes, one mean and a fraction-sum condition typically leave multiple possible solutions.

Verification: A mean of 11u arises from either 100% isotope-11 or 50% each of masses 10 and 12u.

Misconception 19

Incorrect statement: Mᵣ and molar mass M are the same unit-bearing quantity.

Accurate interpretation: Mᵣ is a dimensionless molecular-to-u ratio; M is mass per mole in g/mol or kg/mol.

Verification: Water Mᵣ≈18.015 and M≈18.015g/mol for ordinary precision.

Misconception 20

Incorrect statement: Every water molecule weighs exactly 18.015Da.

Accurate interpretation: 18.015Da is an approximate sample mean; individual isotopologues have their own masses.

Verification: ¹H₂¹⁶O neutral monoisotopic mass is approximately 18.010565Da.

Misconception 21

Incorrect statement: Glucose molecular weight 180.156 and exact mass 180.063388 contradict each other.

Accurate interpretation: The former is a conventional average/molar-mass-related value; the latter is an all-light monoisotopic molecular mass.

Verification: Both apply to C₆H₁₂O₆ under different isotope-composition descriptions.

Misconception 22

Incorrect statement: A 50kDa protein must contain exactly 50,000 amino acids.

Accurate interpretation: kDa is a mass unit, not a residue count; residue masses vary and post-translational modifications matter.

Verification: A rough average residue may weigh near 110Da, so 50kDa is far fewer than 50,000 residues.

Misconception 23

Incorrect statement: A peptide mass equals the sum of free monomer amino-acid masses.

Accurate interpretation: Linear peptide bonds ordinarily form with loss of water, which must be subtracted when starting from free amino-acid masses.

Verification: An ordinary n-residue linear peptide has n−1 peptide bonds and corresponding water losses.

Misconception 24

Incorrect statement: A gel band's molecular-weight label is an exact monoisotopic protein mass.

Accurate interpretation: SDS-PAGE provides an apparent migration-derived size estimate under experimental assumptions.

Verification: MS sequencing or additional biochemical evidence is needed to identify exact mass and identity.

Misconception 25

Incorrect statement: Mass spectrometry always directly reports the neutral molecule's mass.

Accurate interpretation: Many instruments measure ion m/z; adducts, charge states and isotope patterns modify peak positions.

Misconception 26

Incorrect statement: A double-positive ion necessarily has double the m/z of a single-positive ion.

Accurate interpretation: For otherwise comparable mass, division by charge magnitude makes m/z lower for a doubly charged ion.

Verification: [M+2H]²⁺ m/z≈[M+2×1.007276]/2.

Misconception 27

Incorrect statement: One M+2 peak proves one oxygen-18 label.

Accurate interpretation: Two carbon-13 substitutions and other isotope combinations can contribute nominal M+2 peaks.

Verification: One ¹⁸O adds≈2.004245Da, two ¹³C add≈2.006710Da.

Misconception 28

Incorrect statement: Every molecular isotope envelope follows independent binomial label probabilities.

Accurate interpretation: Binomial predictions require appropriate random independent-site assumptions; deliberate labelled molecules may have positional or correlated labels.

Verification: [U-¹³C₆]glucose is deliberately fully labelled, not a random 1% carbon-13 mixture.

Misconception 29

Incorrect statement: The 2019 SI mole definition still fixes carbon-12's molar mass at exactly 12g/mol.

Accurate interpretation: The modern SI fixes Avogadro's constant exactly; carbon-12 molar mass remains extremely close to 12g/mol but no longer exactly fixed.

Verification: Molar mass constant is now an experimentally determined quantity at a tiny relative uncertainty.

Incorrect statement: Finding a ¹³C-labelled metabolite provides its absolute metabolic flux.

1.A.1.22.43 Ten integrated higher-order problems

Ten multi-concept problems with reasoned model solutions and stated assumptions.

Ten integrated higher-order problems with complete model answers

Integrated challenge 1. The atom-versus-sample contradiction

Problem: Carbon-12 is defined as exactly 12u. A carbon sample has 98.93% ¹²C and 1.07% ¹³C of mass 13.003355u. Find the mean and explain why it is not exactly 12u.

Model solution: The amount fractions are 0.9893 and 0.0107. Mean=0.9893×12+0.0107×13.003355=12.0107358985u. The sample's unitless Aᵣ≈12.010736. Carbon-12 itself remains exactly 12u, while the sample mean includes heavier carbon-13. There is no conflict with the definition of u.

Integrated challenge 2. Chlorine isotope abundance from average mass

Problem: A two-isotope chlorine-like model has masses 34.968853u and 36.965903u, with sample mean 35.4681155u. Find the fraction of the heavier isotope.

Model solution: Let x be heavier isotope fraction. Mean=34.968853(1−x)+36.965903x=34.968853+1.997050x. Therefore x=(35.4681155−34.968853)/1.997050=0.25, or 25%; lighter fraction is 75%. The result assumes only the two given nuclides and a correct atom-fraction-weighted mean.

Integrated challenge 3. Three isotopes and insufficient equations

Problem: Nuclides of mass 10u, 11u and 12u occur in a sample of mean mass 11u. Can the three abundance fractions be uniquely determined?

Model solution: No. The equations f₁+f₂+f₃=1 and 10f₁+11f₂+12f₃=11 permit multiple compositions. For example f=(0,1,0) and f=(0.5,0,0.5) are both valid. A further measured isotope ratio or independent fraction constraint is needed. A mean mass is not a full isotope fingerprint.

Integrated challenge 4. Glucose monoisotopic and protonated peak

Problem: Glucose has all-light neutral mass 180.063388Da and conventional average mass≈180.156Da. What is its simplified [M+H]⁺ m/z? Explain which mass to use.

Model solution: For a monoisotopic ion assignment use the all-light neutral mass and proton increment about 1.007276Da. For z=+1, m/z≈180.063388+1.007276=181.070664. The average molecular mass 180.156 belongs to an abundance-weighted or molar-mass context, not the exact all-light parent ion.

Integrated challenge 5. A fully carbon-13-labelled glucose molecule

Problem: An all-light glucose species has mass 180.063388Da. Replace all six ¹²C atoms with ¹³C. Estimate new mass and state one biological inference limit.

Model solution: Mass shift=6×1.003355=6.020130Da, so new neutral isotope mass≈186.083518Da. The molecule contains six ¹³C carbon atoms under this isotope specification. If such labeling appears in a metabolite, the label may support carbon-source contribution, but it does not alone give an absolute glycolysis or TCA flux.

Integrated challenge 6. Peptide formation and a protein-band claim

Problem: A linear peptide of five free amino acids has combined monomer mass 550Da. Estimate peptide mass using water loss, then explain why an SDS-PAGE band cannot verify it exactly.

Model solution: Five residues form four normal peptide bonds, so subtract 4×18.015=72.06Da from 550Da, giving about 477.94Da. This assumes standard unmodified peptide chemistry. Gel mobility offers an apparent mass estimate, not exact monoisotopic molecular formula or chemical modifications. Mass-spectral evidence may be needed.

Integrated challenge 7. Molar mass and the new SI mole

Problem: A glucose sample has conventional M≈180.156g/mol. Find mass of 2.00mmol glucose, then state whether the molar mass constant is exactly 1g/mol today.

Model solution: 2.00mmol=0.00200mol. Mass≈0.00200×180.156g=0.360312g=360.312mg, reported with appropriate significant figures. Since the 2019 SI the Avogadro constant is exactly defined while the molar mass constant is not exactly fixed at 1g/mol; its deviation is negligible for this classroom calculation.

Integrated challenge 8. Exact mass ambiguity at nominal M+2

Problem: One ¹⁸O substitution adds 2.004245Da; two ¹³C substitutions add 2.006710Da. Find their difference, then discuss instrument requirements.

Model solution: The exact-mass difference is 0.002465Da. A high-resolution experiment could distinguish the possibilities only if its resolving power, signal quality, peak definition and ion chemistry are sufficient. A single nominal M+2 peak without detailed data cannot identify the isotope or the label position.

Integrated challenge 9. Stable carbon fraction versus isotope ratio

Problem: A toy carbon sample has 10 heavy ¹³C atoms and 990 ¹²C atoms. Find x, R and explain why neither is Aᵣ.

Model solution: x=10/(10+990)=0.01 or 1%. Heavy-to-light ratio R=10/990≈0.010101. Mean Aᵣ would additionally require isotope masses and their normalized fractions. Neither x nor R has mass units; their definitions use different denominators.

Integrated challenge 10. A scientific conclusion from two numerical outputs

Problem: A report lists Mᵣ(glucose)=180.156 and ¹³C isotope enrichment in lactate after a tracer study. Can either number alone determine a patient's health or metabolic pathway rate?

Model solution: No. Mᵣ≈180.156 is a mass ratio for ordinary molecular composition, not a physiological finding. Lactate isotope enrichment supports measured label incorporation with appropriate controls, but a pathway rate requires precursor enrichment, uptake, time and biochemical network information. Clinical outcomes require validated clinical data, not these isolated mass descriptors.

1.A.1.22.44 Master revision and next node

The entire story in four steps

Chemists first needed relative masses to organize chemical composition. Competing historical oxygen scales highlighted the need for a single isotope-specific standard. Today one u equals one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral ground-state carbon-12 atom, and 1Da=1u. A single isotope's measured mass is then a physical value on that scale, whereas an element sample's relative atomic mass is an abundance-weighted, dimensionless mean.

A precise dictionary of quantities

Mass number A is integer protons plus neutrons, with no unit. Atomic isotope mass m is a measured physical mass in u or kg. Relative isotopic mass Aᵣ(isotope) equals m/u and is dimensionless. Sample relative atomic mass or atomic weight Aᵣ(E) is a weighted mean of those isotope ratios, also dimensionless. Standard atomic weight is a CIAAW-evaluated value or interval applicable to normal materials, subject to natural isotope composition.

From atom to biomolecule

Relative molecular mass Mᵣ is the mass of a molecule compared with u, without a unit. Mass in Da or kDa belongs to a specified molecule or species. Conventional molar mass M in g/mol is the mass per mole of specified entities. Proteins may have distributions of chemical forms, and high-resolution mass spectroscopy generally measures ion m/z rather than directly returning the neutral protein's exact mass.

Four numerical checks every student should memorize

Carbon-12 neutral ground-state atomic mass is exactly 12u. Natural-like carbon sample mean is near 12.011u per atom rather than exactly 12u. Chlorine is commonly represented near 35.45 because of ³⁵Cl/³⁷Cl isotope mixtures. Glucose ordinary molar mass is about 180.156g/mol, whereas all-light glucose monoisotopic neutral mass is about 180.063388Da. These are distinct statements, not contradictions.

A mass-spectral reasoning checklist

Before interpreting a peak, write molecular formula and isotope specification; name the adduct and charge; compute monoisotopic mass or mean as required; account for the ¹³C/¹⁵N/¹⁸O mass increments; and consider natural isotope envelopes. One nominal M+2 peak cannot prove one oxygen-18 substitution. In biological tracing, an isotope mass shift establishes compositional evidence only after identification and control checks; absolute flux requires further data.

How to solve almost any atomic-weight examination question

1. Identify whether the target is a nuclear count, mass, ratio, sample mean, molar mass or m/z. 2. Convert isotope percentages to normalized atom fractions. 3. Calculate Σfᵢmᵢ or ΣfᵢAᵣ,i. 4. Verify the average lies within component masses and fractions sum to one. 5. State units correctly: m uses u, Aᵣ does not. 6. Explain the assumption that the specified isotope fractions actually represent the sample being measured.

Master formula map
  1. Reference: 1 u=m(¹²C neutral ground-state atom)/12 and 1Da=1u.
  2. Nuclear identity: A=Z+N; A is dimensionless, not a measured mass.
  3. Relative isotope: Aᵣ(ⁱX)=m(ⁱX)/mᵤ, dimensionless.
  4. Mean atomic mass: m̄=Σ fᵢmᵢ with Σfᵢ=1.
  5. Mean relative atomic mass: Aᵣ(E)=Σ fᵢ Aᵣ(ⁱE), dimensionless.
  6. Two-isotope inverse: x_light=(m_heavy−m̄)/(m_heavy−m_light).
  7. Relative molecular mass: Mᵣ=m(molecule)/mᵤ, dimensionless.
  8. Amount of substance: n=m/M; N_entities=nNₐ; Nₐ=6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹ exactly.
  9. Mass-energy: ΔE=Δm c²; 1u×c²≈931.494MeV.
  10. Simple protonated ion: m/z≈(M_neutral+z×1.007276)/z for [M+zH]ᶻ⁺.
  11. Isotope ratio: for a two-isotope pool x=R/(1+R), and δ=(R_sample/R_reference−1)×1000‰.
