1.A.1.23.1 A natural puzzle: the same element with different isotope fingerprints

A classroom mystery

A student compares carbon atoms in a tree leaf with carbon atoms in an ocean carbonate sample. Every carbon nucleus has six protons. Yet a sensitive instrument reports different ¹³C/¹²C ratios in the two materials. How can one element have different isotope proportions without its elemental identity changing?

The answer begins with an atom population

Most ordinary carbon samples contain stable carbon-12 and carbon-13, with trace carbon-14 in some natural settings. The fractional amount of each isotope can change from sample to sample because materials have different origins and because chemical or biological processes sometimes sort isotopes differently.

A bigger conceptual shift than a new formula

Node 22 explained the carbon-12 atomic mass reference and isotope-weighted mean. Node 23 asks what the weights represent in nature, what produces natural isotope differences, and why a table value should not be mistaken for a universal constant of every sample.

A scientific promise to the learner

By the end, you should calculate isotope atom fractions, ratios and delta values; explain cosmic origin, radioactive production, equilibrium and kinetic fractionation; assess measurement limits; and connect isotope signatures to living organisms without claiming unmeasured biological rates.

Learning outcomes: Explain natural isotopic abundance and its sample dependence; interpret CIAAW 2024 composition ranges; calculate percentage, atom fraction, ratio, δ and source mixing; understand fractionation and biologically relevant stable isotope applications; solve evidence-limited exam problems.

1.A.1.23.2 Chronological development: Dalton, Soddy, Aston and modern reference laboratories

Bulk chemistry came before isotope science

Early atomic weights arose from proportions in chemical reactions. A value near 35.45 for chlorine made sense as a mean mass, but without isotope theory the underlying mixture of chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 was not directly visible.

Isotopes resolved the same-element mystery

The analysis of radioactive decay series and Frederick Soddy's isotope concept showed that chemically identical elements could have differing atomic masses. The isotope name reflects their shared place in the periodic table, not equal neutron count.

Mass spectrometers provided direct evidence

Mass spectrometric work by Francis Aston and successors revealed distinguishable atomic species and their proportions. Once the neutron and atomic number were understood, a nuclide could be specified by proton number Z and mass number A.

Standardized comparisons came next

Precise isotope-ratio measurements later required agreed reference materials and consistent reporting scales. CIAAW evaluates representative isotopic compositions and standard atomic weights; IUPAC defines terminology; reference scales such as VPDB and VSMOW support cross-study comparisons.

The chronology matters

Scientific thinking advanced from chemical average weights, to isotope recognition, to mass-resolved detection, to careful environmental and biological interpretation. An atom fraction answers a different question than a molecular formula, a decay half-life or a measured metabolic rate.

Conceptual history of isotope measurement and interpretation, not a year-scale timeline.

1.A.1.23.3 The definition of natural isotopic abundance: numerator and denominator

IUPAC's essential idea

Isotopic abundance is the relative number of atoms of a particular isotope among all the atoms of its element in a mixture. Natural isotopic abundance is the abundance of that isotope in the element as found in nature. It is a composition property of a specified material, not an unconditional exact percentage for the entire planet.

A carbon numerical

If a hypothetical sample contains 10,700 carbon-13 atoms per million carbon atoms, x(¹³C)=10,700/1,000,000=0.0107, equivalent to 1.07% of the sample's carbon atoms. This does not mean 1.07% of all atoms of all elements in the sample are carbon-13.

The fundamental closure rule

For a defined element, isotope atom fractions sum to one across the included species: Σxᵢ=1. For a two-isotope model x_light+x_heavy=1. For oxygen, ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O require three fractions.

Chemical reaction versus nucleus change

Evaporation, photosynthesis and mixing can shift relative isotope amounts in a pool while retaining each nucleus's identity. Nuclear production or decay is a separate physical mechanism capable of changing which isotope species exist.

A correct claim states the material

An abundance for water vapor, leaf tissue, seawater or soil nitrate should include where it came from and how it was measured. One familiar percentage cannot automatically be assigned to all of them.

x(ⁱE)=N(ⁱE)/N(all atoms of E); percent abundance=100x; Σxᵢ=1.

1.A.1.23.4 Natural composition versus representative tables versus enriched material

Natural is not synonymous with one fixed worldwide number

Normal natural materials show some real isotopic variability. Even though every oxygen-18 atom is the same nuclide, its fraction among oxygen atoms can differ between precipitation, seawater, minerals and biological tissues.

CIAAW representative values have a scope

The CIAAW Isotopic Compositions 2024 table supplies evaluated normal-material values or intervals. Some ranges reflect variations among samples; some measurements include uncertainty. A table entry is appropriate for approximate teaching but should not replace a measured fraction in a particular unusual sample.

The word standard belongs to a related but different quantity

Standard atomic weight describes the evaluated mean relative atomic mass of normal materials. It depends on isotope masses and their observed fraction distributions. Node 22 derived its weighted formula; here we explain why the fractions vary.

Enrichment changes composition without changing element

A tracer reagent reported as 99 atom % ¹³C has x(¹³C)=0.99, unlike ordinary terrestrial carbon near 1% carbon-13. The element is still carbon Z=6. Its sample mean mass and isotope envelope change, but the definition of 1u remains unchanged.

Commercial and natural samples can differ

Some processed commercial materials have modified isotope compositions. CIAAW marks certain elements with cautions; research reports should provide measured or certified composition instead of assuming every purchased substance is typical natural material.

1.A.1.23.5 Read CIAAW's 2024 ranges: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine

Always read fraction rather than percent

CIAAW lists ¹³C approximately in the range [0.0096,0.0116], corresponding to 0.96–1.16% of carbon atoms in the evaluated normal materials. A decimal point error would inflate the supposed abundance by tenfold.

Oxygen has three important stable species

For ¹⁶O the table lists [0.99738,0.99776], for ¹⁷O [0.000367,0.000400], and for ¹⁸O [0.00187,0.00222]. A real sample's fractions jointly sum to one; the independent range maxima are not necessarily from the same specimen.

Chlorine makes the fractions visible

The table lists ³⁵Cl [0.755,0.761] and ³⁷Cl [0.239,0.245]. In a two-isotope mixture a greater ³⁵Cl fraction necessarily means a lower ³⁷Cl fraction.

Use ranges as evidence, not a generator of arbitrary extremes

A reader cannot select the highest listed ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O fractions and claim they describe one sample. The closure rule and correlated isotope measurements must hold simultaneously.

The values have a dated evaluation context

The cited CIAAW online isotope-composition table incorporates updates through 2024 for selected elements. Actual normal-material evidence evolves. Precise research should cite an appropriate table version and sample data.

1.A.1.23.6 Where isotope inventories came from: from cosmology to stars

The universe has a nuclear history

Present terrestrial isotope compositions emerged from nuclear creation, survival and redistribution across cosmic history. Primordial nucleosynthesis produced much hydrogen and helium and smaller quantities of selected light nuclides, long before Earth or living organisms existed.

Stellar processes produced many heavier nuclides

Nuclear fusion and related stellar reaction pathways produced a large variety of isotopes. Explosive events and neutron capture contributed to heavy isotope inventories. The detailed reactions differ, but their common feature is genuine nuclear transformation rather than chemical sorting.

The Solar System inherited this material

Meteorites, planets and atmospheric reservoirs formed from materials with prior nuclear histories. Different isotopic signatures in extraterrestrial samples can reflect distinct source or subsequent processing; a terrestrial reference ratio must not be treated as exactly universal across space.

Time and nuclear lifetimes modify the inventory

Stable nuclides persist while long-lived radionuclides may decay into daughters over geological time. As a result, today’s isotope abundances are not determined by one event or a single nuclear stability rule.

History and measurement must connect

Nucleosynthesis explains possible isotopic origins, but a specific sample’s current ratio also depends on geological, physical and biological processes. A scientist should not infer an individual star or formation time solely from one familiar ¹³C percentage.

1.A.1.23.7 Primordial isotopes, long-lived radioactivity and the meaning of natural

Primordial is an origin label, not stability

Some nuclides present since early Solar System formation have survived because they are stable or because their half-lives are sufficiently long. Uranium-238 and uranium-235 are radioactive but still naturally present today.

An isotope may have several sources

A naturally occurring nuclide may be primordial, produced continuously through cosmic-ray reactions or formed as a daughter of another nuclide. 'Naturally occurring' does not imply 'stable', and 'natural' does not imply absence of human influence.

Carbon-14 makes the difference intuitive

Carbon-14 is continuously generated by nuclear reactions associated with cosmic rays and moves through the carbon cycle. Its atmospheric pre-weapons-test abundance was around 10⁻¹⁶ relative to carbon according to CIAAW. That rarity scarcely affects natural carbon atomic weight but is crucial in radiocarbon science.

The scale of a source matters

A cosmological abundance is not the same as an isotope fraction in a plant, a water sample or rock. Every ratio belongs to a specified population and time.

A robust exam distinction

Nuclear origin determines how a nuclide was created; nuclear stability determines whether it decays spontaneously; isotopic abundance says what share of the element’s atoms that nuclide represents in a defined material.

1.A.1.23.8 Primordial, cosmogenic and radiogenic labels: three different histories

Primordial inventory

Many stable nuclides and selected long-lived radioactive nuclides were incorporated in Earth's formation material. Their distribution can later be modified by geochemistry and biology.

Cosmogenic formation

Cosmic-ray-related nuclear interactions create certain nuclides, including carbon-14 in the atmosphere. The new radioactive nuclei enter environmental systems where carbon transport and turnover may alter measured concentrations.

Radiogenic daughter growth

Decay of a parent nuclide generates daughter atoms. For example, potassium-40 decay contributes to argon-40 and calcium-40 histories, and uranium/thorium decay is associated with particular lead isotopes. This can change an isotope inventory through a nuclear transformation rather than fractionation.

Radiogenic products can be stable

The daughter may be stable even though its parent is radioactive. Calling an atom radiogenic describes its formation history, not necessarily its own half-life.

Geochronology requires evidence

An abundance of radiogenic argon does not automatically yield rock age without isotope measurements, initial conditions, closed-system assessment and decay constants. Source mixing can mimic or complicate some abundance patterns.

1.A.1.23.9 Natural abundance is not a ranking of nuclear stability

A rare isotope may be stable

Carbon-13 is less abundant than carbon-12 but is also stable. Oxygen-17 is much rarer than oxygen-16 and remains stable. Their abundance differences do not imply that the less abundant isotope is currently decaying.

A small natural fraction can be radioactive

CIAAW representative potassium isotope composition includes ⁴⁰K fraction about 0.000117, or 0.0117% of potassium atoms, in a specified evaluated context. The presence of radioactive ⁴⁰K in nature does not make every potassium atom radioactive.

Half-life does not give the current fraction alone

A nuclide can have a known half-life yet an unknown abundance in a particular sample if its production history, initial inventory and mixing are not supplied.

Mass number A is not a percentage

A=40 means forty nucleons. It does not mean an abundance of 40% or forty atoms per 100 in a potassium sample.

Use data rather than a shortcut

Nuclide stability should come from evaluated nuclear data; natural isotope amounts from measured compositions. A periodic-table average or nuclide label alone is insufficient for either claim.

1.A.1.23.10 Creation versus redistribution: nuclear reactions and ordinary fractionation

One branch changes nuclear identity

Nucleosynthesis, neutron capture or radioactive decay can transform one nuclear species into another. For example, beta-minus decay changes a nucleus’s Z and N while preserving A. Such processes can create or eliminate isotope populations.

The other branch preserves each nucleus

Evaporation, condensation, molecular diffusion, enzyme-catalyzed reactions and mixing generally move existing isotope-bearing atoms between reservoirs. A carbon-13 atom remains carbon-13 even as its molecule changes.

The source-to-product partition

If one pool becomes depleted in a heavy isotope during a process, a linked reservoir or product may be relatively enriched; whole-system isotope counting must balance when no nuclear source or sink is present.

Why biology matters

Photosynthesis, respiration, nitrogen cycling and microbial sulfur processing can affect isotope ratios, often in process-dependent ways. An isotope shift alone is rarely sufficient to identify one pathway without other data.

Pick the governing model

Use material balance for mixing, fractionation factors for partitioning, decay laws for radioactivity and metabolic network models for flux. Applying an isotope half-life to stable carbon-13 photosynthetic fractionation is a category error.

Within a closed system without nuclear reactions: N_heavy,total=Σ N_heavy,reservoir j.

1.A.1.23.11 Equilibrium isotope fractionation: isotope preference between phases

A physical explanation

Heavy and light isotope substitutions slightly change vibrational energies, equilibrium constants and molecular partitioning. Two phases at equilibrium can have unequal heavy/light ratios even though both contain atoms of the same element.

The ratio-based factor

For defined isotope species and materials A and B, α_A/B=R_A/R_B. If α_A/B>1, A has a higher heavy/light ratio than B. α close to 1 indicates a small fractionation, not that the samples have identical absolute isotope contents.

An example from water

Under many equilibrium evaporation and condensation conditions, heavier stable water isotopes are relatively enriched in the liquid phase compared with coexisting vapor. The magnitude depends on temperature and details of the phase equilibrium.

A needed qualification

Open environmental evaporation also involves kinetic effects, humidity and turbulent transport, so one equilibrium α alone cannot reconstruct a lake’s isotope composition.

No transmutation occurs

Water molecules containing ²H or ¹⁸O are partitioned and transported; the nuclei remain those isotopes. Nuclear radioactivity is not responsible for an ordinary water isotope fractionation signature.

Fractionation factor α_A/B=R_A/R_B; R=N_heavy/N_light.

1.A.1.23.12 Kinetic fractionation: diffusion and biochemical rates

Equal chemistry is not exactly equal kinetics

Isotope substitution can change reaction rates, diffusion and transport. When products are removed faster than equilibrium can be established, kinetic isotope effects may alter the heavy/light isotope ratio of the products and residual pool.

Photosynthesis makes a biological example

Differences in carbon isotope discrimination can arise from CO₂ supply and biochemical carboxylation processes. Plant tissue δ¹³C therefore reflects physiology and environment together, not a single universal photosynthesis label.

Water vapor under natural conditions

Evaporation from open water depends on humidity, temperature and vapor mixing. Lighter water molecules can become relatively enriched in vapor, but the magnitude and isotope trajectory require a process-specific model.

Why simplistic shortcuts fail

'Heavy always remains behind' is not a universal law for every isotope system or reaction. Direction and magnitude depend on the process, isotope pair and whether exchange is at equilibrium.

Scientific conclusion discipline

A depleted heavy-isotope ratio may be consistent with one kinetic pathway but is not proof of a unique enzyme or environmental history without supporting measurements.

1.A.1.23.13 Atom fraction versus percent versus ppm and ppb

Name the denominator

If x(¹³C)=0.0107 in a carbon pool, 1.07% of carbon atoms are carbon-13, and 10,700 carbon-isotope atom ppm are carbon-13. It does not mean carbon-13 accounts for that fraction of the sample's mass or all-element atoms.

The conversion formula

Percentage is 100x, parts per million by atom number is 10⁶x, and parts per billion 10⁹x. A very rare nuclide can be meaningful while requiring scientific notation.

Oxygen-17 as a small fraction

NIST's representative oxygen-17 composition 0.00038 corresponds to 0.038% or about 380 ppm among oxygen atoms. Unit and denominator should accompany every value.

Radioactive carbon-14 is vastly rarer

A carbon-14 fraction of order 10⁻¹⁶ is 10⁻¹⁰ ppm of carbon atoms. This is an illustration of scale; actual amounts vary with source, time and anthropogenic effects.

A count check

Fractions are from zero to one, isotope percentages sum to 100% for a complete element mixture, and an omitted isotope species must be acknowledged.

Percentage=100x; ppm=10⁶x; ppb=10⁹x.

1.A.1.23.14 Heavy-to-light isotope ratio versus heavy atom fraction

Similar-looking but distinct numbers

For a two-isotope element, heavy atom fraction x_H=N_H/(N_H+N_L), while heavy/light ratio R=N_H/N_L. Therefore x_H=R/(1+R) and R=x_H/(1−x_H). The distinction is exact, though numbers are close for rare heavy isotopes.

A percentage arithmetic trap

If a mixture contains 1 heavy atom and 99 light atoms, the heavy fraction is 1/100=0.01 but R=1/99≈0.010101. Calling the ratio exactly 0.01 subtly changes the definition.

Reference ratios use an order

For carbon the common ratio is ¹³C/¹²C; nitrogen ¹⁵N/¹⁴N; oxygen ¹⁸O/¹⁶O; hydrogen ²H/¹H. Reversing the numerator changes the value and invalidates naive δ comparison.

A larger-ratio example

If R=0.25, the heavy fraction is 0.25/1.25=0.20 or 20%, not 25%. This is a useful exam problem because the difference is too large to ignore.

Three isotopes require additional information

The ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio alone does not specify ¹⁷O. Full fraction reconstruction requires an additional isotope ratio or justified constraint.

Two-isotope conversion: x_H=R/(1+R), R=x_H/(1−x_H).

1.A.1.23.15 Three-isotope closure: the oxygen example

Two independent fractions remain after normalization

For x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1, two independent measurements determine the third if all three isotopes are included. One ratio by itself is generally insufficient.

An illustrative complete composition

A hypothetical oxygen sample with x₁₆=0.99750, x₁₇=0.00040 and x₁₈=0.00210 has total one. Its ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio is 0.00210/0.99750≈0.00210526 and ¹⁷O/¹⁶O≈0.00040100.

The range-endpoint problem

CIAAW reports isotope ranges from evaluated normal materials; choosing the maximum ¹⁶O and maximum ¹⁸O independently might not be physically compatible with the fraction of ¹⁷O in a real sample.

Molecular consequences

Even water contains isotope choices for hydrogen and oxygen. Natural isotopologue frequencies can depend on multiple element-isotope fractions, correlations and exchange, not one universal isotope percentage.

The correct average

Given isotope-specific neutral atomic masses m_i and complete atom fractions x_i, mean m̄=Σx_i m_i. Insufficient composition leaves a precise sample mean underdetermined.

x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1; m̄(O)=x₁₆m₁₆+x₁₇m₁₇+x₁₈m₁₈.

1.A.1.23.16 Delta δ notation: turning subtle ratios into readable differences

Small ratios need a reference convention

Isotope ratio measurements may differ by a few parts per thousand across natural samples. Delta notation compares the sample ratio with a reference ratio and expresses their proportional difference in per mille.

Calculate it carefully

δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰. A ratio 0.98 of reference yields −20‰, meaning the sample heavy/light isotope ratio is 2% lower than the reference, not that the sample has negative isotope atoms.

A numeric illustration

If R_sample/R_ref=1.012, δ=+12‰. If the sample ratio is identical to the reference, δ=0‰. The sign depends on whether the sample is relatively enriched or depleted in the heavy isotope compared with the chosen standard.

δ is not an isotope atom percentage

To find atom fraction from δ, the reference absolute ratio must be known and other isotope species considered. A δ¹³C of −25‰ does not mean x(¹³C)=−0.025 or 25% of the sample is carbon-13.

The reference label cannot be omitted in research

VPDB for carbon, VSMOW for water hydrogen/oxygen and atmospheric N₂ conventions for nitrogen are common examples. Accurate comparison requires calibration, laboratory quality control and matched reporting scales.

δ(‰)=[R_sample/R_ref−1]×1000; R_sample=R_ref(1+δ/1000).

1.A.1.23.17 Natural isotope ratios and mixing are two different mathematical views

The physical mixing rule starts with counts

When two sources are mixed, the number of heavy and light isotope atoms adds separately. If sources have different elemental concentrations, their contribution weights depend on how much of the element each supplies, not merely the volume of water or material mixed.

For a common elemental pool use atom fractions

If source A supplies 30% of the carbon atoms and B supplies 70%, the mixed carbon-13 atom fraction is x_mix=0.30x_A+0.70x_B, assuming no post-mixing fractionation or nuclear transformation. The coefficients are contributions to carbon atoms.

Ratios are nonlinear under direct mixing

R=x/(1−x) for two isotopes. An arithmetic average of R values is generally not exact when one needs the total-count mixture. For small variations, approximate delta mixing may be used under carefully stated assumptions, but exact modeling begins with isotope counts.

A physical example

A mixture of two waters may have different amounts of dissolved carbon. Mixing them 50:50 by volume does not guarantee equal carbon-atom contributions if dissolved inorganic carbon concentrations differ.

What is required for source apportionment

At least source compositions, a suitable mixing model and independent constraints are needed. One intermediate δ measurement rarely identifies unique proportions among three unknown sources.

If w is fraction of the element atoms from source A: x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B.

1.A.1.23.18 Reference scales: VPDB, VSMOW, AIR and why reference identity matters

Carbon's VPDB convention

Carbon δ¹³C values in ecology and geochemistry are commonly reported relative to Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB). It is an isotope-ratio scale established through reference materials, not a claim that every actual carbonate sample has the same ratio.

Water hydrogen and oxygen

Water δ²H and δ¹⁸O are commonly referenced to Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW) conventions. Calibrated laboratory reference materials are needed to realize these scales; δ=0‰ is a comparison to a standard rather than 'no deuterium' or 'no oxygen-18'.

Nitrogen and sulfur have different standards

Nitrogen δ¹⁵N is commonly referenced to atmospheric N₂ (AIR). Sulfur δ³⁴S is commonly referenced to Vienna Cañon Diablo Troilite (VCDT). Without specifying the correct reference, values from different isotope systems are not directly comparable.

Oxygen ratios can use different scales in other materials

Oxygen isotope values in carbonates may be expressed relative to VPDB rather than the VSMOW water scale, so converting results requires correct conventions. A numerical δ¹⁸O value is incomplete without identifying its scale and material context.

Quality-control meaning

Two laboratories can produce inconsistent apparent delta values if standards, linearity corrections or scale normalization differ. The answer to a scientific comparison problem should identify both isotope pair and reference scale.

1.A.1.23.19 Hydrogen isotope abundance: ¹H, ²H and rare radioactive ³H

Hydrogen-1 dominates ordinary hydrogen

Most hydrogen atoms in normal terrestrial materials are protium ¹H. CIAAW lists an evaluated ¹H fraction range [0.99972,0.99999] and ²H [0.00001,0.00028] for normal materials, showing that deuterium is present at small but variable abundance.

Deuterium is a stable natural isotope

Hydrogen-2 has one proton and one neutron. Its abundance in water varies with evaporation, condensation, geographical source and mixing. Its nuclear stability is not altered when a water molecule moves from ocean to vapor.

Tritium is a different type of trace species

Hydrogen-3 has one proton and two neutrons and is radioactive, with physical half-life near 12.32 years. Its natural and anthropogenic sources can contribute to environmental water measurements, but it is not typically represented by an ordinary stable hydrogen percentage table in the same way as ¹H and ²H.

Why a nutrition study might use ²H

Deuterium oxide labeling can help study water pools and energy expenditure with calibrated stable-isotope methods. The normal background level, deliberate enrichment and post-administration enrichment are different quantities; a decrease in a sampled pool need not mean radioactive decay.

A useful comparison

A reported sample δ²H of −50‰ indicates a D/H ratio 5% below the named reference ratio, not a deuterium percentage of −5% or a decrease in the global hydrogen atom inventory.

1.A.1.23.20 Oxygen isotopes in natural water: the moving water fingerprint

Three stable oxygen isotopes

Oxygen-16 is dominant, with small amounts of oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. In the CIAAW normal-material table, oxygen-18's atom fraction is about 0.00187–0.00222; slight variation is scientifically informative even though its absolute fraction is small.

Evaporation can fractionate water

Water molecules containing heavier oxygen isotopes can partition differently into liquid and vapor. Under many conditions, residual evaporating water becomes relatively enriched in heavy oxygen isotope while escaping vapor is depleted relative to it. The actual pattern depends on humidity, temperature and transport.

Condensation also changes the isotope signature

As moist air cools and precipitation forms, isotope ratios may vary across a sequence of events. Latitude, altitude, season and precipitation history can influence values. The δ¹⁸O or δ²H of one rain sample is not a unique location fingerprint by itself.

Meteoric water lines

The widely used global meteoric water line approximation δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10‰ describes a broad empirical relationship for precipitation in a conventional reference framework. Local meteoric water lines can differ, and evaporated water may deviate from this pattern.

Why hydrogeologists combine multiple measurements

Water isotopes, chemistry, geological context and hydrological observations jointly support source and mixing inference. Neither oxygen-18's natural fraction nor one δ value proves a groundwater age or exact recharge elevation.

An illustrative global meteoric water line: δ²H (‰) ≈ 8 × δ¹⁸O (‰) + 10, not universal for every local sample.
Conceptual water isotope enrichment across evaporation and condensation; not a quantitative model.

1.A.1.23.21 Carbon natural abundance: carbon-12, carbon-13 and rare carbon-14

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 dominate average-mass calculations

Normal carbon isotope abundance is mostly stable carbon-12, while stable carbon-13 is about 1% of carbon atoms in many terrestrial sources. CIAAW reports normal carbon-13 fraction [0.0096,0.0116], reflecting real sample-to-sample variation.

Carbon-14 is much rarer and radioactive

Radiocarbon originates largely through nuclear processes associated with cosmic rays and then enters the carbon cycle. It is too rare in normal carbon to materially shift carbon's standard atomic weight, but the radioactive signal can support isotope chronology under validated dating models.

Photosynthesis can shift the carbon-13 ratio

During carbon fixation, isotope discrimination often leads plant organic carbon to become relatively ¹³C-depleted compared with source inorganic carbon. The amount of depletion reflects pathways, diffusion and environmental conditions.

Do not infer isotope fraction from all-carbon abundance

A leaf can have high carbon content by dry mass and still exhibit approximately ordinary ¹³C fraction among the carbon atoms. The percent of carbon in a tissue and the percent of carbon atoms that are ¹³C are separate denominators.

The sample's mean atomic weight can shift

An enriched ¹³C laboratory reagent can have a mean carbon atomic mass appreciably above ordinary standard weights. Such isotope enrichment is deliberate composition change, not a new periodic element.

1.A.1.23.22 C₃, C₄ and CAM photosynthesis: isotope discrimination is biological evidence

Different pathways can leave different carbon isotope signatures

Many C₃ plants show lower δ¹³C values than typical C₄ plants due to differences in CO₂ supply and carboxylation mechanisms. IAEA agricultural isotope resources discuss broad characteristic ranges, but these vary with species, environment and measurement convention.

C₃ carbon fixation

Rubisco-mediated fixation and diffusion through stomata contribute to the observed carbon isotope discrimination in C₃ photosynthesis. Photosynthetic water-use conditions and atmospheric source composition influence a plant's tissue ratio; one δ¹³C value is not a complete photosynthetic rate measurement.

C₄ carbon fixation

C₄ plants concentrate CO₂ using an additional fixation and transport mechanism, frequently resulting in less depleted δ¹³C tissue values than C₃ plants. A range comparison can be informative when baselines are known, but overlapping cases and processing effects limit certainty.

CAM plants require special care

Crassulacean acid metabolism may produce δ¹³C values reflecting variable proportions of nocturnal and daytime CO₂ fixation. A CAM species cannot always be classified unambiguously from one carbon isotope number alone.

A defensible lab interpretation

A negative δ¹³C difference may support hypotheses about plant pathway or dietary source when cross-checked against local baselines. It does not uniquely identify a plant species, enzyme activity or exact water-use efficiency.

Keep source and discrimination separate

If atmospheric CO₂ isotope composition shifts, plant δ¹³C may change even without a fundamental biochemical change. Comparing sources and tissues or discrimination factors helps avoid misinterpretation.

1.A.1.23.23 Nitrogen-14 and nitrogen-15: natural abundance in food webs and soils

Two stable nitrogen isotopes

Nitrogen-14 is dominant and nitrogen-15 a minor stable isotope. CIAAW gives representative normal-material ¹⁵N fraction in the range [0.00337,0.00422], so the ¹⁵N abundance is roughly a few tenths of one percent, not fifteen percent.

The atmospheric nitrogen reference

δ¹⁵N values are typically referenced to atmospheric N₂, giving a common comparison scale. Positive δ¹⁵N means the sample's ¹⁵N/¹⁴N is above the chosen reference ratio, not that the sample contains a positive concentration of a new element.

Nitrogen cycling changes signatures

Biological nitrogen fixation, assimilation, nitrification, denitrification and volatilization can alter nitrogen isotope ratios in particular pools, with process- and environment-dependent fractionations. No one isotope ratio uniquely proves which nitrogen cycle process dominated.

Food-web ecology

Tissues of consumers may become relatively ¹⁵N-enriched compared with diet under many trophic settings, supporting broad food-web inferences when baselines and tissue-specific discrimination are known. A single δ¹⁵N value cannot uniquely establish precise trophic level or a complete diet.

Agriculture and pollution source questions

Nitrogen isotope ratios of soil nitrate and water can contribute evidence about fertilizer, manure, wastewater and atmospheric sources, but environmental processing and overlapping source ranges require multiple tracers and additional measurements.

1.A.1.23.24 Sulfur isotopes: a microbial and environmental chemistry connection

Several naturally occurring stable sulfur isotope species

Sulfur has common stable nuclides ³²S, ³³S, ³⁴S and ³⁶S. CIAAW 2024 lists ³²S normal-material range [0.9441,0.9529], ³⁴S [0.0396,0.0477], with smaller amounts of ³³S and ³⁶S. One average weight does not reconstruct the four fractions uniquely.

Microbial sulfur transformations

Processes such as microbial sulfate reduction can fractionate sulfur isotopes. A sulfide product can differ in δ³⁴S from its sulfate source because biochemical pathways and kinetic conditions select isotope-bearing chemical species differently.

The sulfur delta standard

δ³⁴S is commonly given relative to VCDT conventions. As with other systems, delta expresses a heavy/light ratio difference, not isotope atom percent, sulfur concentration or a direct microbial cell count.

Environmental interpretation

Multiple sulfur reservoirs and redox reactions can generate similar signatures. A rock or water sample's sulfur isotope result must be interpreted with chemistry, source context, microbial ecology and often oxygen isotope data as well.

Biological limits

A δ³⁴S result may provide supporting evidence for sulfur cycle processes, but it does not uniquely identify one bacterial species or quantify sulfate reduction rate without additional data.

1.A.1.23.25 Boron and lithium: pronounced natural isotope variability with chemical context

Boron has two stable isotope species

Boron-10 and boron-11 are naturally present. CIAAW 2024 gives boron-10 fraction [0.189,0.204] and boron-11 [0.796,0.811] for evaluated normal materials. These fractions are complementary in a two-isotope model.

Boron chemistry contributes fractionation

The proportions of boric acid and borate forms in water depend on solution chemistry, and the different species can fractionate stable boron isotopes. An observed δ¹¹B may help constrain geochemical conditions in a calibrated model but is not a direct universal pH measurement.

Lithium abundance can be strongly source dependent

CIAAW normal-material lithium-6 fraction range [0.019,0.078] and lithium-7 [0.922,0.981] show that isotopic composition variation or modification can be substantial. Laboratory material may differ from natural reference populations.

Biological and industrial context

Lithium isotopes and boron isotopes are studied across geochemistry, environmental transport and specialized scientific settings. A fraction does not by itself establish toxicity, biological uptake or a clinical conclusion.

A mass-weighting implication

Source-dependent isotope fractions influence the corresponding sample mean atomic mass. If a question asks for a precise lithium mean, the source's stated isotopic composition is essential.

1.A.1.23.26 Chlorine and bromine: a clear isotope envelope in mass spectra

Chlorine's stable isotope pair

Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 have typical normal-material fractions near three-quarters and one-quarter respectively. Their mass difference is near two u, producing recognizable nominal M and M+2 contributions in many singly charged chlorine-containing molecular ions.

One chlorine atom's pattern

Under an appropriate one-chlorine molecule and detector model, the ³⁵Cl-containing and ³⁷Cl-containing isotope-related peaks are often roughly in a 3:1 intensity ratio. This rule of thumb assumes comparable ion transmission and neglects other contributions.

Two chlorine atoms change the combinatorics

With two independent chlorine sites and approximate isotope probabilities p=0.75 for ³⁵Cl and q=0.25 for ³⁷Cl, categories with zero, one or two heavy-chlorine atoms have probabilities p²=0.5625, 2pq=0.375 and q²=0.0625, approximately a 9:6:1 pattern.

Bromine also produces a conspicuous pattern

Bromine has major stable nuclides bromine-79 and bromine-81 with broadly comparable natural proportions, which can make M and M+2 isotope categories of a one-bromine ion similarly strong. Exact peak behavior depends on formula and instrument.

An analytical caution

An M+2 feature does not uniquely prove chlorine or bromine; sulfur-34, oxygen-18 and other isotope combinations may also contribute. Use exact mass, fragment patterns and chemical context.

1.A.1.23.27 Magnesium, silicon and iron: multiple isotopes in biological materials

Magnesium illustrates three-stable-isotope mixtures

Normal magnesium contains ²⁴Mg, ²⁵Mg and ²⁶Mg. Representative NIST fractions are about 0.7899, 0.1000 and 0.1101. Every magnesium nucleus has Z=12, but the fractions and exact isotope masses differ.

Silicon and environmental signatures

Silicon has ²⁸Si, ²⁹Si and ³⁰Si naturally present. Silicon isotope fractionation is studied in Earth science and biological silica contexts, but one isotope ratio alone does not identify an organism or its silicon uptake rate.

Iron isotopes

Natural iron includes ⁵⁴Fe, ⁵⁶Fe, ⁵⁷Fe and ⁵⁸Fe. Fractionation among iron isotope species can reflect physical, chemical and biological processes, but redox conditions, multiple reservoirs and analytical corrections can complicate interpretation.

Life-science relevance and limits

Iron participates in heme and redox biology, magnesium in enzymes and nucleic acid interactions, and silicon in some biological silica systems. Equal chemical element does not guarantee the isotopic ratio of every tissue matches the average normal reference material.

Measurement versus function

A tissue's isotope ratio may carry source or processing information. It cannot alone prove a deficiency, diagnose a disease or establish enzyme function.

1.A.1.23.28 Potassium-40 abundance: a natural isotope that also decays

Three important potassium nuclides

Potassium-39, potassium-40 and potassium-41 occur naturally. CIAAW representative fractions are approximately 0.932581, 0.000117 and 0.067302, respectively; the tiny middle fraction belongs to radioactive potassium-40.

Chemical potassium and nuclear potassium are different layers

Potassium ions participate in membrane physiology, but the isotope's nuclear decay is a separate process. The presence of ⁴⁰K in natural potassium does not make all K atoms radioactive or determine a person's potassium concentration.

Decay changes isotope inventories over time

Potassium-40 can transform into calcium-40 by beta-minus decay and argon-40 through electron capture-related decay history. Radiogenic argon can accumulate in a mineral depending on geological conditions.

An age estimate needs more than a percentage

A simple natural ⁴⁰K fraction in a mineral does not tell us its closure time or how much radiogenic argon was retained. Isotopic dating requires measured parent-daughter conditions and a geological model.

A precise exam response

Distinguish potassium isotope amount fraction, physical half-life, isotope-specific activity and cellular potassium concentration. They are independent measurements with different units and inference requirements.

1.A.1.23.29 Lead isotope abundance: a record of geological ancestry

Lead has multiple stable isotope species

Natural lead may contain ²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁶Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb and ²⁰⁸Pb. Their ratios vary substantially with geological context because selected isotopes receive radiogenic contributions from long-lived uranium and thorium series.

The daughter connection

Uranium-238 eventually contributes to lead-206, uranium-235 to lead-207, and thorium-232 to lead-208 in their decay chains. Lead-204 provides a different comparison role because it is not the terminal radiogenic daughter of those same series.

Natural isotope range can be broad

CIAAW tabulates wide lead isotope-composition ranges for normal-material categories, demonstrating why a single ‘natural lead percentage’ may be a poor approximation for particular ores or environmental sources.

Source fingerprinting under uncertainty

Lead isotope ratios can assist in distinguishing source hypotheses when reference compositions are known. Mixed sources, weathering, contamination and analytical error can cause overlap. A lead isotope signature alone does not provide a unique mining location or a person's exposure history.

Biological caution

Lead is toxic regardless of the isotope-ratio measurement. Isotopic composition does not substitute for measuring exposure, health effects or appropriate clinical evaluation.

1.A.1.23.30 Natural isotope composition and standard atomic weight variation

The standard atomic weight is a downstream average

The mean atom mass of an element in a sample is m̄=Σxᵢmᵢ. Relative atomic mass Aᵣ=m̄/u. Thus normal isotope-composition variation can slightly change the sample Aᵣ without changing the identity of the element.

CIAAW ranges are scientifically meaningful

CIAAW gives standard atomic weight intervals for carbon [12.0096,12.0116], oxygen [15.99903,15.99977], chlorine [35.446,35.457] and other elements. Their ranges reflect evaluated normal-material variation, not a claim that one atom has fractional nucleon count.

An accurate statement names the sample

A ¹³C-enriched reagent may have Aᵣ well outside normal carbon's interval, which is expected. An environmental water sample may differ only slightly from a typical oxygen mean, requiring high-precision isotope measurement.

Don't reconstruct a sample by combining unrelated endpoints

The extremes of individual isotope fraction ranges may represent different source materials. The actual distribution must be internally consistent and normalized before any weighted-average atomic mass is computed.

Connection to the next questions

The important biological inference comes from measured isotope ratios and process history, not solely from one weighted atomic weight. The next sections deepen measurement, mixing and model assumptions.

Mean atomic mass m̄=Σxᵢmᵢ; relative atomic mass Aᵣ=m̄/u, with Σxᵢ=1.

1.A.1.23.31 Exact mixing of isotope sources: begin with conserved atom counts

The correct weighting principle

When two sources contribute an element to a mixture, heavy isotope atom counts add. If source A contributes fraction w of the element atoms and source B contributes 1−w, the heavy isotope atom fraction is x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B, provided no other source, isotope fractionation or nuclear transformation occurs.

A carbon example

Suppose 30% of carbon atoms in a mixture come from source A with x_A(¹³C)=0.012, and 70% from source B with x_B=0.010. Then x_mix=0.30×0.012+0.70×0.010=0.0106, or 1.06% ¹³C of the mixture's carbon atoms.

Equal volume is not equal carbon contribution

If two waters differ in dissolved carbon concentration, mixing equal volumes does not mean the same number of carbon atoms came from each. The weights must represent the element's atom contributions; volume alone can give a wrong source fraction.

What about delta notation?

δ is derived from heavy/light isotope ratio R, which itself is related nonlinearly to atom fraction x. A weighted average of delta values can approximate close compositions in some contexts, but exact conservation should mix the isotope atom counts before converting to R and δ.

A uniqueness limit

If two sources have identical isotope composition, one mixed isotope measurement cannot reveal the fraction from either source. Distinguishing them requires another property or tracer.

x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B; w=(x_mix−x_B)/(x_A−x_B) for x_A≠x_B.

1.A.1.23.32 Fractionation during continuing removal: the Rayleigh-model idea

The dynamic-reservoir story

When a reservoir continuously loses material and the removed product has a different isotope ratio, the isotope composition of what remains can change progressively. This is distinct from simply mixing two static reservoirs.

A conditional formula

A commonly used idealized Rayleigh expression can be written R_remaining/R_initial=f^(α−1), where f is the fraction of reservoir remaining and α is defined consistently for the removal process. Different texts use different α conventions, so the definition must accompany the equation.

A toy numerical

With f=0.50 and α=1.010 under this stated convention, the ratio multiplier is 0.5^0.010≈0.99309. The residual ratio is about 0.691% lower than initially under this hypothetical model; this does not establish the direction of all real evaporation processes.

Where natural systems deviate

Rainout, open-lake evaporation and soil nutrient movement may include source addition, exchange, temperature variation or nonuniform reaction rates. A one-equation Rayleigh curve may not capture those conditions.

Scientific exam behavior

Do not insert a δ value into a Rayleigh expression without identifying whether the quantity is R, the appropriate f, the source/product convention for α, and whether a closed-reservoir assumption is justified.

Idealized model: R_residual/R_initial=f^(α−1), with α and the system defined explicitly.

1.A.1.23.33 Isotope-ratio mass spectrometry and reference-normalized measurements

Why IRMS is useful

Natural isotope fractionations can be small compared with total atom abundance, so instruments need high ratio precision. Isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) measures appropriately prepared species and compares isotope-sensitive ion currents.

Calibration is part of measurement

The raw instrument signal can suffer background, drift, ionization fractionation or mass overlap. Laboratories use reference materials, blanks, replicates and normalization to report results on defined scales such as VPDB or VSMOW.

Precision versus accuracy

Repeatable detector values are not automatically correct if standards or chemical preparation are wrong. A robust report includes the isotope ratio, reference scale and realistic uncertainty rather than treating its delta value as an exact property.

Direct observations versus inferred source

An IRMS δ¹³C value states a reference-relative carbon isotope ratio. It does not, by itself, identify the unique species of plant, age of water, complete diet or metabolic reaction.

Complementary analytical systems

For different elements and samples, IRMS, thermal ionization mass spectrometry, plasma mass spectrometry and high-resolution instruments may provide complementary information. Each method has its own corrections.

1.A.1.23.34 Elemental mass spectrometry: why raw peaks are not guaranteed atom fractions

The detector measures ions

Ion signals depend on mass-to-charge, charge state, ionization efficiency, transmission and potential molecular or isobaric interferences. A raw peak area cannot universally be divided by the total peak area to obtain exact isotope fractions.

Interferences require validation

Certain elements encounter interference from molecular ions or other species near the same nominal mass. Resolution, standards, isotope dilution, collision/reaction approaches or alternative isotope channels may be used in professionally validated methods.

NIST versus CIAAW data roles

NIST provides isotope-specific atomic masses and tabulated compositions; CIAAW evaluates recommended natural abundance and standard atomic weight data. A mass isotope value is different from its atom fraction and from the instrument response.

Uncertainty and contaminants

Trace environmental samples may be sensitive to blanks, contamination, instrument memory and source heterogeneity. Apparent rare-isotope enrichment should be checked before making geological or biological claims.

Quantitative conclusion

A properly calibrated ion ratio can support isotope composition. A single uncorrected MS peak does not prove sample provenance, medical condition or absolute metabolic flux.

1.A.1.23.35 Compound-specific isotope analysis: from a bulk tissue to its metabolites

Bulk tissue averages different compounds

A carbon isotope ratio for whole leaf or muscle reflects the measured carbon across multiple molecular species. Lipids, proteins and carbohydrates may have differing isotope compositions because their sources and biosynthesis differ.

Separate chemistry from isotope ratio

Compound-specific analysis can resolve molecules before determining their isotope signatures, helping distinguish hypotheses about source contributions. The exact sample preparation and calibrations determine which compounds are represented.

Position-specific isotope questions need additional tools

A molecule's total ¹³C/¹²C ratio cannot usually state which carbon position carries a heavier isotope. Suitable NMR, fragmentation or other site-specific methods may be needed.

Natural versus labeled research designs

Natural-abundance variations may be reported as δ in per mille; deliberately enriched tracers may use atom percent excess or isotope isotopologue distributions. These data representations are not interchangeable without conversion.

Do not turn a molecular signature into a rate

Identifying ¹³C enrichment in one amino acid can inform source hypotheses, but a synthesis rate needs time, pool and precursor data and a model.

1.A.1.23.36 Natural isotope fingerprinting versus deliberate tracer enrichment

Two complementary strategies

Natural-abundance studies inspect small isotope ratio differences already present among organisms or environments. A tracer study deliberately introduces a specified isotope-enriched precursor or labeled water to follow atom movement or turnover.

Photosynthetic natural signature

A leaf's δ¹³C may differ from local carbonate or atmospheric CO₂ because of source and fractionation. That is an environmental and biological signature; no tracer addition is necessary.

A labeled-glucose experiment

A culture supplied [U-¹³C₆]glucose can produce different ¹³C-containing metabolite isotopologues. Their patterns reflect atom incorporation under biochemical processing. They are not simply variations of natural unlabelled ¹³C around one percent.

A measurement distinction

Tracer studies may report atom percent excess above baseline, while natural isotope ecology often uses δ values tied to reference standards. Both require calibration, controls and appropriate units.

A crucial inference limit

Observed labeled atoms do not by themselves determine absolute carbon flux. Rates need precursor labeling, time courses, measured pools and network models.

1.A.1.23.37 Ecological food-web reasoning: sources, trophic shifts and baselines

Carbon and nitrogen often complement each other

In ecological studies δ¹³C can help evaluate basal carbon-source hypotheses, while δ¹⁵N can support questions about trophic processes. The strength of each inference depends on the local source signatures and tissue-specific fractionation.

The baseline problem

A high consumer δ¹⁵N may reflect trophic enrichment, a high-δ¹⁵N environmental nitrogen source, or both. Without local baseline measurements a precise trophic level cannot generally be assigned.

Multiple diet sources

Consumers may receive carbon and nitrogen from several prey or foods with overlapping isotope signatures. A mixing model with additional biomarkers and independent observations may help distinguish contributions.

Discrimination varies

There is no universally exact δ¹⁵N enrichment per trophic step across all species, tissues, growth states and diets. Literature-derived factors are model assumptions, not directly measured constants for each individual.

A disciplined conclusion

State the observed consumer and source isotope ratios, uncertainty and any model assumptions. A natural abundance signature is evidence, not proof of one unique feeding event.

1.A.1.23.38 Scientific interpretation of isotope maps, water and biology

Water isotopes need two measurements and a source model

δ²H and δ¹⁸O may show covariation through precipitation, evaporation and mixing. The empirical global meteoric water line can provide context, but local lines and climate conditions vary.

An example of scale and inference

With δ¹⁸O=−5‰, a global illustrative relation δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10 gives −30‰. If a sample has δ²H=−45‰ instead, it deviates below that idealized line, but one deviation is not a unique explanation of lake evaporation or precise recharge location.

Biological source discrimination

Paired δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N can help compare diet and trophic source hypotheses if appropriate baselines and discrimination are measured. One isolated isotope value is not a complete ecological history.

Age is a separate question

Stable isotope signatures can describe sources and processes; radioactive systems may supply age constraints given input histories and decay parameters. A δ¹⁸O number alone is not a nuclear clock.

Evidence ladder

Name isotope and material, record method and reference scale, identify direct measurement, specify the fractionation/mixing model and provide a qualified inference with uncertainties.

Four inference levels: Isotope identity → calibrated ratio → modeled fractionation/mixing → contextual ecological or environmental explanation.

Case study 6. Two streams mix but one carries four times as much carbon

The stable isotope carbon-13 does not become a new element; it simply occupies a much greater fraction of specified carbon positions in the reagent. A metabolite M+3 category after labeling can support incorporation of three heavy carbon atoms under validated molecule identification, but an absolute glycolysis rate is not available from one peak height alone. Precursor enrichment, carbon mapping, time, pool sizes and other data are required. A natural-abundance δ¹³C measurement and a strongly enriched tracer isotopologue analysis therefore answer related but different scientific questions.

Case study 12. A laboratory reports impossible independent isotope maxima

A researcher reads CIAAW separate normal-material intervals for oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 and combines all three maximum fractions into one alleged oxygen sample. The sum exceeds one, so the combination cannot describe one physical element pool. Published intervals represent evaluated material variation; they do not guarantee that independent extremes are present simultaneously. An actual oxygen sample must have x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1, and its isotope ratios must be measured or constrained consistently. A report that treats representative ranges as mutually independent outputs risks impossible normalization and an incorrect weighted atomic mass. A defensible computation begins with one internally consistent set of fractions, includes appropriate isotope masses, and reports the sample/reference convention. The lesson is about data provenance as much as arithmetic.
