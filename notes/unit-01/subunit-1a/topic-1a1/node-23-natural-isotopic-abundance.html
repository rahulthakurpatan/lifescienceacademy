1.A.1.23.1 A natural puzzle: the same element with different isotope fingerprints

A classroom mystery

A student compares carbon atoms in a tree leaf with carbon atoms in an ocean carbonate sample. Every carbon nucleus has six protons. Yet a sensitive instrument reports different ¹³C/¹²C ratios in the two materials. How can one element have different isotope proportions without its elemental identity changing?

The answer begins with an atom population

Most ordinary carbon samples contain stable carbon-12 and carbon-13, with trace carbon-14 in some natural settings. The fractional amount of each isotope can change from sample to sample because materials have different origins and because chemical or biological processes sometimes sort isotopes differently.

A bigger conceptual shift than a new formula

Node 22 explained the carbon-12 atomic mass reference and isotope-weighted mean. Node 23 asks what the weights represent in nature, what produces natural isotope differences, and why a table value should not be mistaken for a universal constant of every sample.

A scientific promise to the learner

By the end, you should calculate isotope atom fractions, ratios and delta values; explain cosmic origin, radioactive production, equilibrium and kinetic fractionation; assess measurement limits; and connect isotope signatures to living organisms without claiming unmeasured biological rates.

Learning outcomes: Explain natural isotopic abundance and its sample dependence; interpret CIAAW 2024 composition ranges; calculate percentage, atom fraction, ratio, δ and source mixing; understand fractionation and biologically relevant stable isotope applications; solve evidence-limited exam problems.

1.A.1.23.2 Chronological development: Dalton, Soddy, Aston and modern reference laboratories

Bulk chemistry came before isotope science

Early atomic weights arose from proportions in chemical reactions. A value near 35.45 for chlorine made sense as a mean mass, but without isotope theory the underlying mixture of chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 was not directly visible.

Isotopes resolved the same-element mystery

The analysis of radioactive decay series and Frederick Soddy's isotope concept showed that chemically identical elements could have differing atomic masses. The isotope name reflects their shared place in the periodic table, not equal neutron count.

Mass spectrometers provided direct evidence

Mass spectrometric work by Francis Aston and successors revealed distinguishable atomic species and their proportions. Once the neutron and atomic number were understood, a nuclide could be specified by proton number Z and mass number A.

Standardized comparisons came next

Precise isotope-ratio measurements later required agreed reference materials and consistent reporting scales. CIAAW evaluates representative isotopic compositions and standard atomic weights; IUPAC defines terminology; reference scales such as VPDB and VSMOW support cross-study comparisons.

The chronology matters

Scientific thinking advanced from chemical average weights, to isotope recognition, to mass-resolved detection, to careful environmental and biological interpretation. An atom fraction answers a different question than a molecular formula, a decay half-life or a measured metabolic rate.

Conceptual history of isotope measurement and interpretation, not a year-scale timeline.

1.A.1.23.3 The definition of natural isotopic abundance: numerator and denominator

IUPAC's essential idea

Isotopic abundance is the relative number of atoms of a particular isotope among all the atoms of its element in a mixture. Natural isotopic abundance is the abundance of that isotope in the element as found in nature. It is a composition property of a specified material, not an unconditional exact percentage for the entire planet.

A carbon numerical

If a hypothetical sample contains 10,700 carbon-13 atoms per million carbon atoms, x(¹³C)=10,700/1,000,000=0.0107, equivalent to 1.07% of the sample's carbon atoms. This does not mean 1.07% of all atoms of all elements in the sample are carbon-13.

The fundamental closure rule

For a defined element, isotope atom fractions sum to one across the included species: Σxᵢ=1. For a two-isotope model x_light+x_heavy=1. For oxygen, ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O require three fractions.

Chemical reaction versus nucleus change

Evaporation, photosynthesis and mixing can shift relative isotope amounts in a pool while retaining each nucleus's identity. Nuclear production or decay is a separate physical mechanism capable of changing which isotope species exist.

A correct claim states the material

An abundance for water vapor, leaf tissue, seawater or soil nitrate should include where it came from and how it was measured. One familiar percentage cannot automatically be assigned to all of them.

x(ⁱE)=N(ⁱE)/N(all atoms of E); percent abundance=100x; Σxᵢ=1.

1.A.1.23.4 Natural composition versus representative tables versus enriched material

Natural is not synonymous with one fixed worldwide number

Normal natural materials show some real isotopic variability. Even though every oxygen-18 atom is the same nuclide, its fraction among oxygen atoms can differ between precipitation, seawater, minerals and biological tissues.

CIAAW representative values have a scope

The CIAAW Isotopic Compositions 2024 table supplies evaluated normal-material values or intervals. Some ranges reflect variations among samples; some measurements include uncertainty. A table entry is appropriate for approximate teaching but should not replace a measured fraction in a particular unusual sample.

The word standard belongs to a related but different quantity

Standard atomic weight describes the evaluated mean relative atomic mass of normal materials. It depends on isotope masses and their observed fraction distributions. Node 22 derived its weighted formula; here we explain why the fractions vary.

Enrichment changes composition without changing element

A tracer reagent reported as 99 atom % ¹³C has x(¹³C)=0.99, unlike ordinary terrestrial carbon near 1% carbon-13. The element is still carbon Z=6. Its sample mean mass and isotope envelope change, but the definition of 1u remains unchanged.

Commercial and natural samples can differ

Some processed commercial materials have modified isotope compositions. CIAAW marks certain elements with cautions; research reports should provide measured or certified composition instead of assuming every purchased substance is typical natural material.

1.A.1.23.5 Read CIAAW's 2024 ranges: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine

Always read fraction rather than percent

CIAAW lists ¹³C approximately in the range [0.0096,0.0116], corresponding to 0.96–1.16% of carbon atoms in the evaluated normal materials. A decimal point error would inflate the supposed abundance by tenfold.

Oxygen has three important stable species

For ¹⁶O the table lists [0.99738,0.99776], for ¹⁷O [0.000367,0.000400], and for ¹⁸O [0.00187,0.00222]. A real sample's fractions jointly sum to one; the independent range maxima are not necessarily from the same specimen.

Chlorine makes the fractions visible

The table lists ³⁵Cl [0.755,0.761] and ³⁷Cl [0.239,0.245]. In a two-isotope mixture a greater ³⁵Cl fraction necessarily means a lower ³⁷Cl fraction.

Use ranges as evidence, not a generator of arbitrary extremes

A reader cannot select the highest listed ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O fractions and claim they describe one sample. The closure rule and correlated isotope measurements must hold simultaneously.

The values have a dated evaluation context

The cited CIAAW online isotope-composition table incorporates updates through 2024 for selected elements. Actual normal-material evidence evolves. Precise research should cite an appropriate table version and sample data.

1.A.1.23.6 Where isotope inventories came from: from cosmology to stars

The universe has a nuclear history

Present terrestrial isotope compositions emerged from nuclear creation, survival and redistribution across cosmic history. Primordial nucleosynthesis produced much hydrogen and helium and smaller quantities of selected light nuclides, long before Earth or living organisms existed.

Stellar processes produced many heavier nuclides

Nuclear fusion and related stellar reaction pathways produced a large variety of isotopes. Explosive events and neutron capture contributed to heavy isotope inventories. The detailed reactions differ, but their common feature is genuine nuclear transformation rather than chemical sorting.

The Solar System inherited this material

Meteorites, planets and atmospheric reservoirs formed from materials with prior nuclear histories. Different isotopic signatures in extraterrestrial samples can reflect distinct source or subsequent processing; a terrestrial reference ratio must not be treated as exactly universal across space.

Time and nuclear lifetimes modify the inventory

Stable nuclides persist while long-lived radionuclides may decay into daughters over geological time. As a result, today’s isotope abundances are not determined by one event or a single nuclear stability rule.

History and measurement must connect

Nucleosynthesis explains possible isotopic origins, but a specific sample’s current ratio also depends on geological, physical and biological processes. A scientist should not infer an individual star or formation time solely from one familiar ¹³C percentage.

1.A.1.23.7 Primordial isotopes, long-lived radioactivity and the meaning of natural

Primordial is an origin label, not stability

Some nuclides present since early Solar System formation have survived because they are stable or because their half-lives are sufficiently long. Uranium-238 and uranium-235 are radioactive but still naturally present today.

An isotope may have several sources

A naturally occurring nuclide may be primordial, produced continuously through cosmic-ray reactions or formed as a daughter of another nuclide. 'Naturally occurring' does not imply 'stable', and 'natural' does not imply absence of human influence.

Carbon-14 makes the difference intuitive

Carbon-14 is continuously generated by nuclear reactions associated with cosmic rays and moves through the carbon cycle. Its atmospheric pre-weapons-test abundance was around 10⁻¹⁶ relative to carbon according to CIAAW. That rarity scarcely affects natural carbon atomic weight but is crucial in radiocarbon science.

The scale of a source matters

A cosmological abundance is not the same as an isotope fraction in a plant, a water sample or rock. Every ratio belongs to a specified population and time.

A robust exam distinction

Nuclear origin determines how a nuclide was created; nuclear stability determines whether it decays spontaneously; isotopic abundance says what share of the element’s atoms that nuclide represents in a defined material.

1.A.1.23.8 Primordial, cosmogenic and radiogenic labels: three different histories

Primordial inventory

Many stable nuclides and selected long-lived radioactive nuclides were incorporated in Earth's formation material. Their distribution can later be modified by geochemistry and biology.

Cosmogenic formation

Cosmic-ray-related nuclear interactions create certain nuclides, including carbon-14 in the atmosphere. The new radioactive nuclei enter environmental systems where carbon transport and turnover may alter measured concentrations.

Radiogenic daughter growth

Decay of a parent nuclide generates daughter atoms. For example, potassium-40 decay contributes to argon-40 and calcium-40 histories, and uranium/thorium decay is associated with particular lead isotopes. This can change an isotope inventory through a nuclear transformation rather than fractionation.

Radiogenic products can be stable

The daughter may be stable even though its parent is radioactive. Calling an atom radiogenic describes its formation history, not necessarily its own half-life.

Geochronology requires evidence

An abundance of radiogenic argon does not automatically yield rock age without isotope measurements, initial conditions, closed-system assessment and decay constants. Source mixing can mimic or complicate some abundance patterns.

1.A.1.23.9 Natural abundance is not a ranking of nuclear stability

A rare isotope may be stable

Carbon-13 is less abundant than carbon-12 but is also stable. Oxygen-17 is much rarer than oxygen-16 and remains stable. Their abundance differences do not imply that the less abundant isotope is currently decaying.

A small natural fraction can be radioactive

CIAAW representative potassium isotope composition includes ⁴⁰K fraction about 0.000117, or 0.0117% of potassium atoms, in a specified evaluated context. The presence of radioactive ⁴⁰K in nature does not make every potassium atom radioactive.

Half-life does not give the current fraction alone

A nuclide can have a known half-life yet an unknown abundance in a particular sample if its production history, initial inventory and mixing are not supplied.

Mass number A is not a percentage

A=40 means forty nucleons. It does not mean an abundance of 40% or forty atoms per 100 in a potassium sample.

Use data rather than a shortcut

Nuclide stability should come from evaluated nuclear data; natural isotope amounts from measured compositions. A periodic-table average or nuclide label alone is insufficient for either claim.

1.A.1.23.10 Creation versus redistribution: nuclear reactions and ordinary fractionation

One branch changes nuclear identity

Nucleosynthesis, neutron capture or radioactive decay can transform one nuclear species into another. For example, beta-minus decay changes a nucleus’s Z and N while preserving A. Such processes can create or eliminate isotope populations.

The other branch preserves each nucleus

Evaporation, condensation, molecular diffusion, enzyme-catalyzed reactions and mixing generally move existing isotope-bearing atoms between reservoirs. A carbon-13 atom remains carbon-13 even as its molecule changes.

The source-to-product partition

If one pool becomes depleted in a heavy isotope during a process, a linked reservoir or product may be relatively enriched; whole-system isotope counting must balance when no nuclear source or sink is present.

Why biology matters

Photosynthesis, respiration, nitrogen cycling and microbial sulfur processing can affect isotope ratios, often in process-dependent ways. An isotope shift alone is rarely sufficient to identify one pathway without other data.

Pick the governing model

Use material balance for mixing, fractionation factors for partitioning, decay laws for radioactivity and metabolic network models for flux. Applying an isotope half-life to stable carbon-13 photosynthetic fractionation is a category error.

Within a closed system without nuclear reactions: N_heavy,total=Σ N_heavy,reservoir j.

1.A.1.23.11 Equilibrium isotope fractionation: isotope preference between phases

A physical explanation

Heavy and light isotope substitutions slightly change vibrational energies, equilibrium constants and molecular partitioning. Two phases at equilibrium can have unequal heavy/light ratios even though both contain atoms of the same element.

The ratio-based factor

For defined isotope species and materials A and B, α_A/B=R_A/R_B. If α_A/B>1, A has a higher heavy/light ratio than B. α close to 1 indicates a small fractionation, not that the samples have identical absolute isotope contents.

An example from water

Under many equilibrium evaporation and condensation conditions, heavier stable water isotopes are relatively enriched in the liquid phase compared with coexisting vapor. The magnitude depends on temperature and details of the phase equilibrium.

A needed qualification

Open environmental evaporation also involves kinetic effects, humidity and turbulent transport, so one equilibrium α alone cannot reconstruct a lake’s isotope composition.

No transmutation occurs

Water molecules containing ²H or ¹⁸O are partitioned and transported; the nuclei remain those isotopes. Nuclear radioactivity is not responsible for an ordinary water isotope fractionation signature.

Fractionation factor α_A/B=R_A/R_B; R=N_heavy/N_light.

1.A.1.23.12 Kinetic fractionation: diffusion and biochemical rates

Equal chemistry is not exactly equal kinetics

Isotope substitution can change reaction rates, diffusion and transport. When products are removed faster than equilibrium can be established, kinetic isotope effects may alter the heavy/light isotope ratio of the products and residual pool.

Photosynthesis makes a biological example

Differences in carbon isotope discrimination can arise from CO₂ supply and biochemical carboxylation processes. Plant tissue δ¹³C therefore reflects physiology and environment together, not a single universal photosynthesis label.

Water vapor under natural conditions

Evaporation from open water depends on humidity, temperature and vapor mixing. Lighter water molecules can become relatively enriched in vapor, but the magnitude and isotope trajectory require a process-specific model.

Why simplistic shortcuts fail

'Heavy always remains behind' is not a universal law for every isotope system or reaction. Direction and magnitude depend on the process, isotope pair and whether exchange is at equilibrium.

Scientific conclusion discipline

A depleted heavy-isotope ratio may be consistent with one kinetic pathway but is not proof of a unique enzyme or environmental history without supporting measurements.

1.A.1.23.13 Atom fraction versus percent versus ppm and ppb

Name the denominator

If x(¹³C)=0.0107 in a carbon pool, 1.07% of carbon atoms are carbon-13, and 10,700 carbon-isotope atom ppm are carbon-13. It does not mean carbon-13 accounts for that fraction of the sample's mass or all-element atoms.

The conversion formula

Percentage is 100x, parts per million by atom number is 10⁶x, and parts per billion 10⁹x. A very rare nuclide can be meaningful while requiring scientific notation.

Oxygen-17 as a small fraction

NIST's representative oxygen-17 composition 0.00038 corresponds to 0.038% or about 380 ppm among oxygen atoms. Unit and denominator should accompany every value.

Radioactive carbon-14 is vastly rarer

A carbon-14 fraction of order 10⁻¹⁶ is 10⁻¹⁰ ppm of carbon atoms. This is an illustration of scale; actual amounts vary with source, time and anthropogenic effects.

A count check

Fractions are from zero to one, isotope percentages sum to 100% for a complete element mixture, and an omitted isotope species must be acknowledged.

Percentage=100x; ppm=10⁶x; ppb=10⁹x.

1.A.1.23.14 Heavy-to-light isotope ratio versus heavy atom fraction

Similar-looking but distinct numbers

For a two-isotope element, heavy atom fraction x_H=N_H/(N_H+N_L), while heavy/light ratio R=N_H/N_L. Therefore x_H=R/(1+R) and R=x_H/(1−x_H). The distinction is exact, though numbers are close for rare heavy isotopes.

A percentage arithmetic trap

If a mixture contains 1 heavy atom and 99 light atoms, the heavy fraction is 1/100=0.01 but R=1/99≈0.010101. Calling the ratio exactly 0.01 subtly changes the definition.

Reference ratios use an order

For carbon the common ratio is ¹³C/¹²C; nitrogen ¹⁵N/¹⁴N; oxygen ¹⁸O/¹⁶O; hydrogen ²H/¹H. Reversing the numerator changes the value and invalidates naive δ comparison.

A larger-ratio example

If R=0.25, the heavy fraction is 0.25/1.25=0.20 or 20%, not 25%. This is a useful exam problem because the difference is too large to ignore.

Three isotopes require additional information

The ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio alone does not specify ¹⁷O. Full fraction reconstruction requires an additional isotope ratio or justified constraint.

Two-isotope conversion: x_H=R/(1+R), R=x_H/(1−x_H).

1.A.1.23.15 Three-isotope closure: the oxygen example

Two independent fractions remain after normalization

For x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1, two independent measurements determine the third if all three isotopes are included. One ratio by itself is generally insufficient.

An illustrative complete composition

A hypothetical oxygen sample with x₁₆=0.99750, x₁₇=0.00040 and x₁₈=0.00210 has total one. Its ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio is 0.00210/0.99750≈0.00210526 and ¹⁷O/¹⁶O≈0.00040100.

The range-endpoint problem

CIAAW reports isotope ranges from evaluated normal materials; choosing the maximum ¹⁶O and maximum ¹⁸O independently might not be physically compatible with the fraction of ¹⁷O in a real sample.

Molecular consequences

Even water contains isotope choices for hydrogen and oxygen. Natural isotopologue frequencies can depend on multiple element-isotope fractions, correlations and exchange, not one universal isotope percentage.

The correct average

Given isotope-specific neutral atomic masses m_i and complete atom fractions x_i, mean m̄=Σx_i m_i. Insufficient composition leaves a precise sample mean underdetermined.

x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1; m̄(O)=x₁₆m₁₆+x₁₇m₁₇+x₁₈m₁₈.

1.A.1.23.16 Delta δ notation: turning subtle ratios into readable differences

Small ratios need a reference convention

Isotope ratio measurements may differ by a few parts per thousand across natural samples. Delta notation compares the sample ratio with a reference ratio and expresses their proportional difference in per mille.

Calculate it carefully

δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰. A ratio 0.98 of reference yields −20‰, meaning the sample heavy/light isotope ratio is 2% lower than the reference, not that the sample has negative isotope atoms.

A numeric illustration

If R_sample/R_ref=1.012, δ=+12‰. If the sample ratio is identical to the reference, δ=0‰. The sign depends on whether the sample is relatively enriched or depleted in the heavy isotope compared with the chosen standard.

δ is not an isotope atom percentage

To find atom fraction from δ, the reference absolute ratio must be known and other isotope species considered. A δ¹³C of −25‰ does not mean x(¹³C)=−0.025 or 25% of the sample is carbon-13.

The reference label cannot be omitted in research

VPDB for carbon, VSMOW for water hydrogen/oxygen and atmospheric N₂ conventions for nitrogen are common examples. Accurate comparison requires calibration, laboratory quality control and matched reporting scales.

δ(‰)=[R_sample/R_ref−1]×1000; R_sample=R_ref(1+δ/1000).

1.A.1.23.17 Natural isotope ratios and mixing are two different mathematical views

The physical mixing rule starts with counts

When two sources are mixed, the number of heavy and light isotope atoms adds separately. If sources have different elemental concentrations, their contribution weights depend on how much of the element each supplies, not merely the volume of water or material mixed.

For a common elemental pool use atom fractions

If source A supplies 30% of the carbon atoms and B supplies 70%, the mixed carbon-13 atom fraction is x_mix=0.30x_A+0.70x_B, assuming no post-mixing fractionation or nuclear transformation. The coefficients are contributions to carbon atoms.

Ratios are nonlinear under direct mixing

R=x/(1−x) for two isotopes. An arithmetic average of R values is generally not exact when one needs the total-count mixture. For small variations, approximate delta mixing may be used under carefully stated assumptions, but exact modeling begins with isotope counts.

A physical example

A mixture of two waters may have different amounts of dissolved carbon. Mixing them 50:50 by volume does not guarantee equal carbon-atom contributions if dissolved inorganic carbon concentrations differ.

What is required for source apportionment

At least source compositions, a suitable mixing model and independent constraints are needed. One intermediate δ measurement rarely identifies unique proportions among three unknown sources.

If w is fraction of the element atoms from source A: x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B.

1.A.1.23.18 Reference scales: VPDB, VSMOW, AIR and why reference identity matters

Carbon's VPDB convention

Carbon δ¹³C values in ecology and geochemistry are commonly reported relative to Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB). It is an isotope-ratio scale established through reference materials, not a claim that every actual carbonate sample has the same ratio.

Water hydrogen and oxygen

Water δ²H and δ¹⁸O are commonly referenced to Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW) conventions. Calibrated laboratory reference materials are needed to realize these scales; δ=0‰ is a comparison to a standard rather than 'no deuterium' or 'no oxygen-18'.

Nitrogen and sulfur have different standards

Nitrogen δ¹⁵N is commonly referenced to atmospheric N₂ (AIR). Sulfur δ³⁴S is commonly referenced to Vienna Cañon Diablo Troilite (VCDT). Without specifying the correct reference, values from different isotope systems are not directly comparable.

Oxygen ratios can use different scales in other materials

Oxygen isotope values in carbonates may be expressed relative to VPDB rather than the VSMOW water scale, so converting results requires correct conventions. A numerical δ¹⁸O value is incomplete without identifying its scale and material context.

Quality-control meaning

Two laboratories can produce inconsistent apparent delta values if standards, linearity corrections or scale normalization differ. The answer to a scientific comparison problem should identify both isotope pair and reference scale.

1.A.1.23.19 Hydrogen isotope abundance: ¹H, ²H and rare radioactive ³H

Hydrogen-1 dominates ordinary hydrogen

Most hydrogen atoms in normal terrestrial materials are protium ¹H. CIAAW lists an evaluated ¹H fraction range [0.99972,0.99999] and ²H [0.00001,0.00028] for normal materials, showing that deuterium is present at small but variable abundance.

Deuterium is a stable natural isotope

Hydrogen-2 has one proton and one neutron. Its abundance in water varies with evaporation, condensation, geographical source and mixing. Its nuclear stability is not altered when a water molecule moves from ocean to vapor.

Tritium is a different type of trace species

Hydrogen-3 has one proton and two neutrons and is radioactive, with physical half-life near 12.32 years. Its natural and anthropogenic sources can contribute to environmental water measurements, but it is not typically represented by an ordinary stable hydrogen percentage table in the same way as ¹H and ²H.

Why a nutrition study might use ²H

Deuterium oxide labeling can help study water pools and energy expenditure with calibrated stable-isotope methods. The normal background level, deliberate enrichment and post-administration enrichment are different quantities; a decrease in a sampled pool need not mean radioactive decay.

A useful comparison

A reported sample δ²H of −50‰ indicates a D/H ratio 5% below the named reference ratio, not a deuterium percentage of −5% or a decrease in the global hydrogen atom inventory.

1.A.1.23.20 Oxygen isotopes in natural water: the moving water fingerprint

Three stable oxygen isotopes

Oxygen-16 is dominant, with small amounts of oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. In the CIAAW normal-material table, oxygen-18's atom fraction is about 0.00187–0.00222; slight variation is scientifically informative even though its absolute fraction is small.

Evaporation can fractionate water

Water molecules containing heavier oxygen isotopes can partition differently into liquid and vapor. Under many conditions, residual evaporating water becomes relatively enriched in heavy oxygen isotope while escaping vapor is depleted relative to it. The actual pattern depends on humidity, temperature and transport.

Condensation also changes the isotope signature

As moist air cools and precipitation forms, isotope ratios may vary across a sequence of events. Latitude, altitude, season and precipitation history can influence values. The δ¹⁸O or δ²H of one rain sample is not a unique location fingerprint by itself.

Meteoric water lines

The widely used global meteoric water line approximation δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10‰ describes a broad empirical relationship for precipitation in a conventional reference framework. Local meteoric water lines can differ, and evaporated water may deviate from this pattern.

Why hydrogeologists combine multiple measurements

Water isotopes, chemistry, geological context and hydrological observations jointly support source and mixing inference. Neither oxygen-18's natural fraction nor one δ value proves a groundwater age or exact recharge elevation.

An illustrative global meteoric water line: δ²H (‰) ≈ 8 × δ¹⁸O (‰) + 10, not universal for every local sample.

1.A.1.23.21 Carbon natural abundance: carbon-12, carbon-13 and rare carbon-14

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 dominate average-mass calculations

Normal carbon isotope abundance is mostly stable carbon-12, while stable carbon-13 is about 1% of carbon atoms in many terrestrial sources. CIAAW reports normal carbon-13 fraction [0.0096,0.0116], reflecting real sample-to-sample variation.

Carbon-14 is much rarer and radioactive

Radiocarbon originates largely through nuclear processes associated with cosmic rays and then enters the carbon cycle. It is too rare in normal carbon to materially shift carbon's standard atomic weight, but the radioactive signal can support isotope chronology under validated dating models.

Photosynthesis can shift the carbon-13 ratio

During carbon fixation, isotope discrimination often leads plant organic carbon to become relatively ¹³C-depleted compared with source inorganic carbon. The amount of depletion reflects pathways, diffusion and environmental conditions.

Do not infer isotope fraction from all-carbon abundance

A leaf can have high carbon content by dry mass and still exhibit approximately ordinary ¹³C fraction among the carbon atoms. The percent of carbon in a tissue and the percent of carbon atoms that are ¹³C are separate denominators.

The sample's mean atomic weight can shift

An enriched ¹³C laboratory reagent can have a mean carbon atomic mass appreciably above ordinary standard weights. Such isotope enrichment is deliberate composition change, not a new periodic element.

1.A.1.23.22 C₃, C₄ and CAM photosynthesis: isotope discrimination is biological evidence

Different pathways can leave different carbon isotope signatures

Many C₃ plants show lower δ¹³C values than typical C₄ plants due to differences in CO₂ supply and carboxylation mechanisms. IAEA agricultural isotope resources discuss broad characteristic ranges, but these vary with species, environment and measurement convention.

C₃ carbon fixation

Rubisco-mediated fixation and diffusion through stomata contribute to the observed carbon isotope discrimination in C₃ photosynthesis. Photosynthetic water-use conditions and atmospheric source composition influence a plant's tissue ratio; one δ¹³C value is not a complete photosynthetic rate measurement.

C₄ carbon fixation

CAM plants require special care

Crassulacean acid metabolism may produce δ¹³C values reflecting variable proportions of nocturnal and daytime CO₂ fixation. A CAM species cannot always be classified unambiguously from one carbon isotope number alone.

A defensible lab interpretation

A negative δ¹³C difference may support hypotheses about plant pathway or dietary source when cross-checked against local baselines. It does not uniquely identify a plant species, enzyme activity or exact water-use efficiency.

Keep source and discrimination separate

If atmospheric CO₂ isotope composition shifts, plant δ¹³C may change even without a fundamental biochemical change. Comparing sources and tissues or discrimination factors helps avoid misinterpretation.

Two stable nitrogen isotopes

The atmospheric nitrogen reference

Nitrogen cycling changes signatures

Biological nitrogen fixation, assimilation, nitrification, denitrification and volatilization can alter nitrogen isotope ratios in particular pools, with process- and environment-dependent fractionations. No one isotope ratio uniquely proves which nitrogen cycle process dominated.

Food-web ecology

Agriculture and pollution source questions

1.A.1.23.24 Sulfur isotopes: a microbial and environmental chemistry connection

Several naturally occurring stable sulfur isotope species

Sulfur has common stable nuclides ³²S, ³³S, ³⁴S and ³⁶S. CIAAW 2024 lists ³²S normal-material range [0.9441,0.9529], ³⁴S [0.0396,0.0477], with smaller amounts of ³³S and ³⁶S. One average weight does not reconstruct the four fractions uniquely.

Microbial sulfur transformations

Processes such as microbial sulfate reduction can fractionate sulfur isotopes. A sulfide product can differ in δ³⁴S from its sulfate source because biochemical pathways and kinetic conditions select isotope-bearing chemical species differently.

The sulfur delta standard

δ³⁴S is commonly given relative to VCDT conventions. As with other systems, delta expresses a heavy/light ratio difference, not isotope atom percent, sulfur concentration or a direct microbial cell count.

Environmental interpretation

Multiple sulfur reservoirs and redox reactions can generate similar signatures. A rock or water sample's sulfur isotope result must be interpreted with chemistry, source context, microbial ecology and often oxygen isotope data as well.

Biological limits

A δ³⁴S result may provide supporting evidence for sulfur cycle processes, but it does not uniquely identify one bacterial species or quantify sulfate reduction rate without additional data.

1.A.1.23.27 Magnesium, silicon and iron: multiple isotopes in biological materials

Magnesium illustrates three-stable-isotope mixtures

Normal magnesium contains ²⁴Mg, ²⁵Mg and ²⁶Mg. Representative NIST fractions are about 0.7899, 0.1000 and 0.1101. Every magnesium nucleus has Z=12, but the fractions and exact isotope masses differ.

Silicon and environmental signatures

Silicon has ²⁸Si, ²⁹Si and ³⁰Si naturally present. Silicon isotope fractionation is studied in Earth science and biological silica contexts, but one isotope ratio alone does not identify an organism or its silicon uptake rate.

Iron isotopes

Natural iron includes ⁵⁴Fe, ⁵⁶Fe, ⁵⁷Fe and ⁵⁸Fe. Fractionation among iron isotope species can reflect physical, chemical and biological processes, but redox conditions, multiple reservoirs and analytical corrections can complicate interpretation.

Measurement versus function

1.A.1.23.28 Potassium-40 abundance: a natural isotope that also decays

Three important potassium nuclides

Potassium-39, potassium-40 and potassium-41 occur naturally. CIAAW representative fractions are approximately 0.932581, 0.000117 and 0.067302, respectively; the tiny middle fraction belongs to radioactive potassium-40.

Chemical potassium and nuclear potassium are different layers

Potassium ions participate in membrane physiology, but the isotope's nuclear decay is a separate process. The presence of ⁴⁰K in natural potassium does not make all K atoms radioactive or determine a person's potassium concentration.

Decay changes isotope inventories over time

Potassium-40 can transform into calcium-40 by beta-minus decay and argon-40 through electron capture-related decay history. Radiogenic argon can accumulate in a mineral depending on geological conditions.

An age estimate needs more than a percentage

A simple natural ⁴⁰K fraction in a mineral does not tell us its closure time or how much radiogenic argon was retained. Isotopic dating requires measured parent-daughter conditions and a geological model.

A precise exam response

Distinguish potassium isotope amount fraction, physical half-life, isotope-specific activity and cellular potassium concentration. They are independent measurements with different units and inference requirements.

1.A.1.23.29 Lead isotope abundance: a record of geological ancestry

Lead has multiple stable isotope species

Natural lead may contain ²⁰⁴Pb, ²⁰⁶Pb, ²⁰⁷Pb and ²⁰⁸Pb. Their ratios vary substantially with geological context because selected isotopes receive radiogenic contributions from long-lived uranium and thorium series.

The daughter connection

Uranium-238 eventually contributes to lead-206, uranium-235 to lead-207, and thorium-232 to lead-208 in their decay chains. Lead-204 provides a different comparison role because it is not the terminal radiogenic daughter of those same series.

Natural isotope range can be broad

CIAAW tabulates wide lead isotope-composition ranges for normal-material categories, demonstrating why a single ‘natural lead percentage’ may be a poor approximation for particular ores or environmental sources.

Source fingerprinting under uncertainty

Lead isotope ratios can assist in distinguishing source hypotheses when reference compositions are known. Mixed sources, weathering, contamination and analytical error can cause overlap. A lead isotope signature alone does not provide a unique mining location or a person's exposure history.

Biological caution

Lead is toxic regardless of the isotope-ratio measurement. Isotopic composition does not substitute for measuring exposure, health effects or appropriate clinical evaluation.

1.A.1.23.30 Natural isotope composition and standard atomic weight variation

The standard atomic weight is a downstream average

The mean atom mass of an element in a sample is m̄=Σxᵢmᵢ. Relative atomic mass Aᵣ=m̄/u. Thus normal isotope-composition variation can slightly change the sample Aᵣ without changing the identity of the element.

CIAAW ranges are scientifically meaningful

CIAAW gives standard atomic weight intervals for carbon [12.0096,12.0116], oxygen [15.99903,15.99977], chlorine [35.446,35.457] and other elements. Their ranges reflect evaluated normal-material variation, not a claim that one atom has fractional nucleon count.

An accurate statement names the sample

A ¹³C-enriched reagent may have Aᵣ well outside normal carbon's interval, which is expected. An environmental water sample may differ only slightly from a typical oxygen mean, requiring high-precision isotope measurement.

Don't reconstruct a sample by combining unrelated endpoints

The extremes of individual isotope fraction ranges may represent different source materials. The actual distribution must be internally consistent and normalized before any weighted-average atomic mass is computed.

Connection to the next questions

The important biological inference comes from measured isotope ratios and process history, not solely from one weighted atomic weight. The next sections deepen measurement, mixing and model assumptions.

1.A.1.23.31 Exact mixing of isotope sources: begin with conserved atom counts

The correct weighting principle

When two sources contribute an element to a mixture, heavy isotope atom counts add. If source A contributes fraction w of the element atoms and source B contributes 1−w, the heavy isotope atom fraction is x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B, provided no other source, isotope fractionation or nuclear transformation occurs.

A carbon example

Suppose 30% of carbon atoms in a mixture come from source A with x_A(¹³C)=0.012, and 70% from source B with x_B=0.010. Then x_mix=0.30×0.012+0.70×0.010=0.0106, or 1.06% ¹³C of the mixture's carbon atoms.

Equal volume is not equal carbon contribution

If two waters differ in dissolved carbon concentration, mixing equal volumes does not mean the same number of carbon atoms came from each. The weights must represent the element's atom contributions; volume alone can give a wrong source fraction.

What about delta notation?

δ is derived from heavy/light isotope ratio R, which itself is related nonlinearly to atom fraction x. A weighted average of delta values can approximate close compositions in some contexts, but exact conservation should mix the isotope atom counts before converting to R and δ.

A uniqueness limit

If two sources have identical isotope composition, one mixed isotope measurement cannot reveal the fraction from either source. Distinguishing them requires another property or tracer.

1.A.1.23.32 Fractionation during continuing removal: the Rayleigh-model idea

The dynamic-reservoir story

When a reservoir continuously loses material and the removed product has a different isotope ratio, the isotope composition of what remains can change progressively. This is distinct from simply mixing two static reservoirs.

A conditional formula

A commonly used idealized Rayleigh expression can be written R_remaining/R_initial=f^(α−1), where f is the fraction of reservoir remaining and α is defined consistently for the removal process. Different texts use different α conventions, so the definition must accompany the equation.

A toy numerical

With f=0.50 and α=1.010 under this stated convention, the ratio multiplier is 0.5^0.010≈0.99309. The residual ratio is about 0.691% lower than initially under this hypothetical model; this does not establish the direction of all real evaporation processes.

Where natural systems deviate

Rainout, open-lake evaporation and soil nutrient movement may include source addition, exchange, temperature variation or nonuniform reaction rates. A one-equation Rayleigh curve may not capture those conditions.

Scientific exam behavior

Do not insert a δ value into a Rayleigh expression without identifying whether the quantity is R, the appropriate f, the source/product convention for α, and whether a closed-reservoir assumption is justified.

1.A.1.23.33 Isotope-ratio mass spectrometry and reference-normalized measurements

Why IRMS is useful

Natural isotope fractionations can be small compared with total atom abundance, so instruments need high ratio precision. Isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) measures appropriately prepared species and compares isotope-sensitive ion currents.

Calibration is part of measurement

The raw instrument signal can suffer background, drift, ionization fractionation or mass overlap. Laboratories use reference materials, blanks, replicates and normalization to report results on defined scales such as VPDB or VSMOW.

Precision versus accuracy

Repeatable detector values are not automatically correct if standards or chemical preparation are wrong. A robust report includes the isotope ratio, reference scale and realistic uncertainty rather than treating its delta value as an exact property.

Direct observations versus inferred source

An IRMS δ¹³C value states a reference-relative carbon isotope ratio. It does not, by itself, identify the unique species of plant, age of water, complete diet or metabolic reaction.

Complementary analytical systems

For different elements and samples, IRMS, thermal ionization mass spectrometry, plasma mass spectrometry and high-resolution instruments may provide complementary information. Each method has its own corrections.

1.A.1.23.34 Elemental mass spectrometry: why raw peaks are not guaranteed atom fractions

The detector measures ions

Ion signals depend on mass-to-charge, charge state, ionization efficiency, transmission and potential molecular or isobaric interferences. A raw peak area cannot universally be divided by the total peak area to obtain exact isotope fractions.

Interferences require validation

Certain elements encounter interference from molecular ions or other species near the same nominal mass. Resolution, standards, isotope dilution, collision/reaction approaches or alternative isotope channels may be used in professionally validated methods.

NIST versus CIAAW data roles

NIST provides isotope-specific atomic masses and tabulated compositions; CIAAW evaluates recommended natural abundance and standard atomic weight data. A mass isotope value is different from its atom fraction and from the instrument response.

Uncertainty and contaminants

Trace environmental samples may be sensitive to blanks, contamination, instrument memory and source heterogeneity. Apparent rare-isotope enrichment should be checked before making geological or biological claims.

Quantitative conclusion

A properly calibrated ion ratio can support isotope composition. A single uncorrected MS peak does not prove sample provenance, medical condition or absolute metabolic flux.

1.A.1.23.35 Compound-specific isotope analysis: from a bulk tissue to its metabolites

Bulk tissue averages different compounds

A carbon isotope ratio for whole leaf or muscle reflects the measured carbon across multiple molecular species. Lipids, proteins and carbohydrates may have differing isotope compositions because their sources and biosynthesis differ.

Separate chemistry from isotope ratio

Compound-specific analysis can resolve molecules before determining their isotope signatures, helping distinguish hypotheses about source contributions. The exact sample preparation and calibrations determine which compounds are represented.

Position-specific isotope questions need additional tools

A molecule's total ¹³C/¹²C ratio cannot usually state which carbon position carries a heavier isotope. Suitable NMR, fragmentation or other site-specific methods may be needed.

Natural versus labeled research designs

Natural-abundance variations may be reported as δ in per mille; deliberately enriched tracers may use atom percent excess or isotope isotopologue distributions. These data representations are not interchangeable without conversion.

Do not turn a molecular signature into a rate

Identifying ¹³C enrichment in one amino acid can inform source hypotheses, but a synthesis rate needs time, pool and precursor data and a model.

1.A.1.23.36 Natural isotope fingerprinting versus deliberate tracer enrichment

Two complementary strategies

Natural-abundance studies inspect small isotope ratio differences already present among organisms or environments. A tracer study deliberately introduces a specified isotope-enriched precursor or labeled water to follow atom movement or turnover.

Photosynthetic natural signature

A leaf's δ¹³C may differ from local carbonate or atmospheric CO₂ because of source and fractionation. That is an environmental and biological signature; no tracer addition is necessary.

A labeled-glucose experiment

A culture supplied [U-¹³C₆]glucose can produce different ¹³C-containing metabolite isotopologues. Their patterns reflect atom incorporation under biochemical processing. They are not simply variations of natural unlabelled ¹³C around one percent.

A measurement distinction

Tracer studies may report atom percent excess above baseline, while natural isotope ecology often uses δ values tied to reference standards. Both require calibration, controls and appropriate units.

A crucial inference limit

Observed labeled atoms do not by themselves determine absolute carbon flux. Rates need precursor labeling, time courses, measured pools and network models.

1.A.1.23.37 Ecological food-web reasoning: sources, trophic shifts and baselines

Carbon and nitrogen often complement each other

In ecological studies δ¹³C can help evaluate basal carbon-source hypotheses, while δ¹⁵N can support questions about trophic processes. The strength of each inference depends on the local source signatures and tissue-specific fractionation.

The baseline problem

A high consumer δ¹⁵N may reflect trophic enrichment, a high-δ¹⁵N environmental nitrogen source, or both. Without local baseline measurements a precise trophic level cannot generally be assigned.

Multiple diet sources

Consumers may receive carbon and nitrogen from several prey or foods with overlapping isotope signatures. A mixing model with additional biomarkers and independent observations may help distinguish contributions.

Discrimination varies

There is no universally exact δ¹⁵N enrichment per trophic step across all species, tissues, growth states and diets. Literature-derived factors are model assumptions, not directly measured constants for each individual.

A disciplined conclusion

State the observed consumer and source isotope ratios, uncertainty and any model assumptions. A natural abundance signature is evidence, not proof of one unique feeding event.

1.A.1.23.38 Scientific interpretation of isotope maps, water and biology

Water isotopes need two measurements and a source model

δ²H and δ¹⁸O may show covariation through precipitation, evaporation and mixing. The empirical global meteoric water line can provide context, but local lines and climate conditions vary.

An example of scale and inference

With δ¹⁸O=−5‰, a global illustrative relation δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10 gives −30‰. If a sample has δ²H=−45‰ instead, it deviates below that idealized line, but one deviation is not a unique explanation of lake evaporation or precise recharge location.

Biological source discrimination

Paired δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N can help compare diet and trophic source hypotheses if appropriate baselines and discrimination are measured. One isolated isotope value is not a complete ecological history.

Age is a separate question

Stable isotope signatures can describe sources and processes; radioactive systems may supply age constraints given input histories and decay parameters. A δ¹⁸O number alone is not a nuclear clock.

Evidence ladder

Name isotope and material, record method and reference scale, identify direct measurement, specify the fractionation/mixing model and provide a qualified inference with uncertainties.

If an analyst assumes every sample contains only the approximate natural carbon-13 fraction of one percent, they misinterpret the reagent and downstream mass isotopologue patterns. The stable isotope carbon-13 does not become a new element; it simply occupies a much greater fraction of specified carbon positions in the reagent. A metabolite M+3 category after labeling can support incorporation of three heavy carbon atoms under validated molecule identification, but an absolute glycolysis rate is not available from one peak height alone. Precursor enrichment, carbon mapping, time, pool sizes and other data are required. A natural-abundance δ¹³C measurement and a strongly enriched tracer isotopologue analysis therefore answer related but different scientific questions.

Scientific checkpoint: State the isotope and fraction/ratio measured, the relevant reference or source, the applicable physical model, and any additional data needed before a causal, age or rate inference.

Case study 12. A laboratory reports impossible independent isotope maxima

A researcher reads CIAAW separate normal-material intervals for oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 and combines all three maximum fractions into one alleged oxygen sample. The sum exceeds one, so the combination cannot describe one physical element pool. Published intervals represent evaluated material variation; they do not guarantee that independent extremes are present simultaneously. An actual oxygen sample must have x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1, and its isotope ratios must be measured or constrained consistently. A report that treats representative ranges as mutually independent outputs risks impossible normalization and an incorrect weighted atomic mass. A defensible computation begins with one internally consistent set of fractions, includes appropriate isotope masses, and reports the sample/reference convention. The lesson is about data provenance as much as arithmetic.

Part I — Twenty complete isotope-count, abundance, ratio and delta calculations

Worked example 1. Atom fraction from observed isotope counts

Question: A toy carbon sample contains 10,700 ¹³C atoms among one million carbon atoms. Find x(¹³C).

Step-by-step solution: Use the IUPAC atom fraction definition: x=N(¹³C)/N(all C)=10,700/1,000,000=0.0107. This is dimensionless and means 1.07% of the carbon atoms in the sampled population are ¹³C. The other 989,300 carbon atoms are not automatically a clinical or ecological measure; in this two-stable-isotope toy model they are assigned to ¹²C.

Verification: 0.0107×1,000,000=10,700.

Exam trap: Never divide by the total atoms of all elements unless that is the expressly defined denominator.

Worked example 2. Percentage from a natural-like isotope fraction

Question: A sample reports x(¹³C)=0.0107. Write percent abundance.

Step-by-step solution: Percent=100x=100×0.0107=1.07%. This is a fraction of carbon atoms, not the percentage of carbon mass in the sample. It is a typical teaching example rather than an exact composition of every natural carbon material; CIAAW publishes a range.

Verification: 1.07/100=0.0107.

Exam trap: Do not call 0.0107 a percentage of 0.0107%.

Worked example 3. Parts per million from carbon-13 fraction

Question: Convert x(¹³C)=0.0107 into atom-based ppm.

Step-by-step solution: Parts per million by carbon atom count are x×10⁶=0.0107×1,000,000=10,700 ppm of carbon isotope atoms. The statement is not 10,700 mg/kg of the sample because the denominator is carbon atoms rather than sample mass.

Verification: 10,700 ppm/10⁶=0.0107.

Exam trap: Do not silently treat isotope atom-ppm as a solute concentration.

Worked example 4. Convert CIAAW's carbon-13 normal range to percentage

Question: CIAAW lists x(¹³C)=[0.0096,0.0116] for its normal-material scope. Express the percentage range.

Step-by-step solution: Multiply endpoints by 100: [0.96%,1.16%]. This is an evaluated interval representing isotope-composition variation, not a random measurement uncertainty or an exact universal distribution. A sample-specific result is needed for a precise calculation.

Verification: 0.96/100=0.0096 and 1.16/100=0.0116.

Exam trap: The table shows fractions, not an already-percent notation.

Worked example 5. Nitrogen-15 range in percent

Question: Translate the normal-material ¹⁵N fraction [0.00337,0.00422] to percent.

Step-by-step solution: Multiply each value by 100: 0.337% to 0.422%. These are fractions of nitrogen atoms belonging to ¹⁵N. They do not state the nitrogen content of a plant or assign a trophic level.

Verification: 0.00337×100=0.337; 0.00422×100=0.422.

Exam trap: Do not read mass number fifteen as 15%.

Worked example 6. Oxygen-18 fraction in ppm

Question: A representative water-oxygen population has x(¹⁸O)=0.00205. Compute percent and atom-ppm.

Step-by-step solution: Percentage=0.00205×100=0.205%. Atom-ppm=0.00205×1,000,000=2050 ppm of oxygen atoms. Neither number is a δ¹⁸O value; δ compares isotope ratios against VSMOW or another named reference.

Verification: 2050/10,000=0.205% because 1%=10,000 ppm.

Exam trap: Do not call this 2050 mg/L oxygen-18 without the total oxygen amount.

Worked example 7. Oxygen-17's rarity on three scales

Question: NIST gives representative x(¹⁷O)=0.00038. Find percentage, ppm and ppb.

Step-by-step solution: Percentage=0.038%; ppm=380; ppb=380,000 among oxygen atoms. These numerical scales correspond to the same isotope fraction. The isotope is stable, even though its abundance is small.

Verification: 0.038%=0.00038 as a fraction.

Exam trap: A large ppb number does not mean the isotope becomes chemically dominant.

Worked example 8. Three oxygen fractions should close

Question: Suppose oxygen sample fractions are x₁₆=0.99757, x₁₇=0.00038 and x₁₈=0.00205. Verify normalization.

Step-by-step solution: Add 0.99757+0.00038+0.00205=1.00000. Thus the three isotope fractions form one internally consistent oxygen pool. The given values are representative teaching numbers; a different natural water reservoir may have different measured fractions.

Verification: 1−0.99757−0.00038=0.00205.

Exam trap: Do not combine unrelated published endpoint maxima without normalization.

Worked example 9. Calculate heavy/light ratio from carbon fraction

Question: In a simplified two-carbon-isotope pool x(¹³C)=0.0107. Find R=¹³C/¹²C.

Step-by-step solution: Under the two-isotope model, x_L=1−0.0107=0.9893. Thus R=0.0107/0.9893≈0.0108157. The ratio is slightly larger than 0.0107 because its denominator excludes the ¹³C atoms.

Verification: R/(1+R)≈0.0107.

Exam trap: Do not use isotope fraction directly as R when precision matters.

Worked example 10. Recover heavy fraction from a supplied ratio

Question: A hypothetical two-isotope sample has R=0.0200. Find heavy isotope atom fraction.

Step-by-step solution: Use x_H=R/(1+R)=0.02/1.02≈0.01960784 or 1.960784%. The heavy fraction is slightly below 2% because the denominator includes both heavy and light atoms, while R's denominator is only light atoms.

Verification: x/(1−x)≈0.0200.

Exam trap: Do not report 2.00% as the exact heavy atom fraction.

Worked example 11. One atom in ninety-nine gives a nonidentical ratio

Question: A hypothetical pool contains 1 heavy atom and 99 light atoms. Find x and R.

Step-by-step solution: There are 100 atoms in total, so x=1/100=0.01. The heavy/light ratio is R=1/99≈0.0101010. The distinction is simple but important for delta-ratio calculations and reference measurements.

Verification: R/(1+R)=0.01.

Exam trap: Do not say the heavy/light ratio equals the heavy/total fraction.

Worked example 12. A simple negative δ example

Question: If R_sample/R_ref=0.975, calculate δ.

Step-by-step solution: δ=(0.975−1)×1000=−25‰. The sample ratio is 2.5% lower than the defined reference ratio. No statement is made that the sample has negative heavy-isotope atoms or that 25% of its carbon has decayed.

Verification: −25/1000+1=0.975.

Exam trap: Per mille (‰) differs from percent (%).

Worked example 13. A positive δ example

Question: A sample has R_sample/R_ref=1.012. Find δ.

Step-by-step solution: δ=(1.012−1)×1000=+12‰. Its heavy/light isotope ratio is 1.2% above the reference ratio; the atom fraction cannot be obtained numerically without the reference ratio itself.

Verification: 1.012=1+12/1000.

Exam trap: A +12‰ result is not +12% isotope atom abundance.

Worked example 14. Recover ratio from δ and reference ratio

Question: R_ref=0.0100 in a toy carbon system; δ=−20‰. Find R_sample.

Step-by-step solution: R_sample=R_ref(1+δ/1000)=0.0100(1−0.020)=0.00980. The measured sample ratio is 2% lower than the specified reference. To obtain heavy atom fraction use x=R/(1+R) afterward.

Verification: (0.00980/0.0100−1)×1000=−20‰.

Exam trap: Do not subtract 20 directly from R_ref.

Worked example 15. Difference in δ is not a percentage difference in atom fraction

Question: Two samples have δ¹³C=−28‰ and δ¹³C=−13‰ relative to the same reference. How far apart are they in delta units?

Step-by-step solution: Subtract the values: (−13)−(−28)=+15‰, meaning the second sample is 15 per mille units higher in the reported isotope-ratio scale. This is not proof that the second has 15% more carbon-13 atoms or that its photosynthesis rate is 15 units greater.

Verification: −28+15=−13.

Exam trap: δ differences must not be presented as element concentration differences.

Worked example 16. Chlorine abundance and integer-weight mean

Question: A hypothetical chlorine sample contains 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl. Estimate its mean mass number using integer masses.

Step-by-step solution: Mean≈0.75×35+0.25×37=26.25+9.25=35.50. This is a teaching approximation for average mass; an individual chlorine atom has integer A=35 or A=37, not 35.5. Exact mass calculations would use measured isotope masses.

Verification: The mean lies between 35 and 37 and closer to 35.

Exam trap: A mean mass number approximation is not an isotope's exact atomic mass.

Worked example 17. Chlorine weighted mean with measured isotope masses

Question: Use fractions 0.7576,0.2424 and masses 34.968853u,36.965903u for ³⁵Cl,³⁷Cl. Find mean atomic mass.

Step-by-step solution: m̄=0.7576×34.968853+0.2424×36.965903≈35.452938u. The corresponding relative atomic mass is ≈35.452938 without units. This sample-specific mean is consistent with an ordinary chlorine context under the stated illustrative proportions.

Verification: Fractions sum to 1 and mean lies strictly between isotope masses.

Exam trap: A single numerical example does not prove this precise fraction in every chlorine source.

Worked example 18. Carbon mean mass from illustrative isotope fractions

Question: Carbon fractions are x(¹²C)=0.9893 and x(¹³C)=0.0107; masses 12u and 13.003355u. Calculate m̄.

Step-by-step solution: m̄=0.9893×12+0.0107×13.003355=12.0107358985u≈12.010736u. Relative atomic mass has the same numerical value without units. The mass shift relative to 12u arises from the heavier isotope population.

Verification: 0.9893+0.0107=1; m̄ is slightly above 12.

Exam trap: Do not call the carbon-12 reference itself 12.010736u.

Worked example 19. Infer a two-isotope fraction from mean mass

Question: Isotope masses 10u and 11u have mean 10.4u. Find fraction of the lighter isotope.

Step-by-step solution: Let x be lighter fraction. 10.4=10x+11(1−x)=11−x. Thus x=0.60 and heavier fraction 0.40. The inference assumes exactly those two isotope species and correctly specified masses.

Verification: 0.6×10+0.4×11=10.4.

Exam trap: One measured mean is not enough for three unknown fractions.

Worked example 20. A three-isotope normalizing problem

Question: An oxygen sample reports x₁₆=0.99750, x₁₇=0.00040; find x₁₈ and its ratio to ¹⁶O.

Step-by-step solution: Closure gives x₁₈=1−0.99750−0.00040=0.00210. Ratio R₁₈/₁₆=0.00210/0.99750≈0.00210526. Both fractions and ratio are dimensionless; a δ value needs a specified reference ratio.

Verification: 0.99750+0.00040+0.00210=1.

Exam trap: Do not assume x₁₈ itself equals R₁₈/₁₆.

Worked example 21. Two-source carbon-atom mixing

Question: Source A contributes 30% of mixture carbon atoms with x(¹³C)=0.012; source B contributes 70% with x=0.010. Find x_mix.

Detailed solution: Conservation of heavy carbon atoms gives x_mix=0.30×0.012+0.70×0.010=0.0036+0.0070=0.0106. This corresponds to 1.06% carbon-13 of the mixed carbon. The weights refer to carbon atoms, not necessarily the same fraction of liquid volume or total mass.

Cross-check: 0.0106 lies between 0.010 and 0.012.

Common error: Do not treat fractions of total water volume as carbon contributions without concentration data.

Worked example 22. Equal water volumes but unequal carbon concentration

Question: One liter of A carries four times the carbon atoms of one liter of B. A has x=0.012 and B x=0.010. Find mixed x for equal volumes.

Detailed solution: Carbon-atom contributions are 4/(4+1)=0.8 from A and 0.2 from B. Therefore x_mix=0.8×0.012+0.2×0.010=0.0096+0.0020=0.0116. This is different from the naive 50:50 average 0.0110 because equal liquid volumes carry unequal numbers of carbon atoms.

Cross-check: 0.0116 is closer to A's 0.012 than to B's 0.010.

Common error: Do not ignore the concentration of the element being traced.

Worked example 23. Inverse isotope-mixing fraction

Question: A two-source mixture has x_A=0.014, x_B=0.010, and x_mix=0.011. Find A's fraction of element atoms.

Detailed solution: Let w be source A's contribution. 0.011=w(0.014)+(1−w)(0.010)=0.010+0.004w, so w=0.001/0.004=0.25 or 25%. The other 75% of element atoms came from B in this ideal no-fractionation two-source model.

Cross-check: 0.25×0.014+0.75×0.010=0.011.

Common error: The proportion concerns element atoms, not necessarily mass or volume.

Worked example 24. An isotope signature cannot separate identical sources

Question: Two sources have identical carbon-13 fraction x_A=x_B=0.0107. A mixture also gives x_mix=0.0107. Can w be recovered?

Detailed solution: No. The mixing equation x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B simplifies to 0.0107 for every w between zero and one. The inverse formula has zero denominator x_A−x_B. Another independent tracer or source difference is essential for attribution.

Cross-check: Both w=0 and w=1 give the same observed isotope fraction.

Common error: Do not divide by zero or invent an arbitrary source share.

Worked example 25. Three-source mixing is underdetermined

Question: Three isotope sources have x values 0.010,0.012,0.014. A mixture has x=0.012. Is source composition unique?

Detailed solution: No. One valid combination is 100% of the middle source, whereas another is 50% of the first and 50% of the third. Both give 0.012. Their normalized source contributions differ; one measured isotope fraction plus a sum-to-one condition generally cannot resolve three unknown contributions.

Cross-check: 0.5×0.010+0.5×0.014=0.012.

Common error: A mean signature is not a full source fingerprint.

Worked example 26. Fractionation factor from two isotope ratios

Question: An equilibrium toy system has R_A=0.0110 and R_B=0.0100 for the same isotope pair. Find α_A/B.

Detailed solution: α_A/B=R_A/R_B=0.0110/0.0100=1.10. Phase A's heavy/light ratio is 10% greater than phase B's under the model. The magnitude is deliberately large for arithmetic clarity, not a claimed typical natural C isotope fractionation.

Cross-check: R_B×α=0.0110.

Common error: Do not report α in per mille; it is a ratio without units.

Worked example 27. Convert α into relative per-mille fractionation

Question: If α_A/B=1.003 under a specified convention, compute 1000(α−1).

Detailed solution: The approximate per-mille difference is 1000×0.003=3‰. This is a useful description of ratio fractionation, but the exact relation between differences of reported δ values depends on reference ratios and which expression is defined. α itself stays dimensionless.

Cross-check: 3‰ corresponds to a 0.3% ratio difference.

Common error: Do not equate a 3‰ ratio effect with a 3% atom-fraction increase.

Worked example 28. Ideal Rayleigh fractionation calculation

Question: Use the explicitly defined toy model R_rem/R₀=f^(α−1), with f=0.5 and α=1.010. Compute ratio multiplier.

Detailed solution: R_rem/R₀=0.5^0.010=exp[0.010 ln(0.5)]≈exp(−0.0069315)≈0.9930925. The residual isotope ratio is about 0.69075% lower in this mathematical convention. This is not a universal evaporation rule: α definition and physical conditions determine application.

Cross-check: ln(0.9930925)≈−0.0069315.

Common error: Do not use a Rayleigh formula without specifying α and the reservoir.

Worked example 29. A point on the global meteoric-water approximation

Question: A hypothetical precipitation sample has δ¹⁸O=−5‰. Evaluate δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10.

Detailed solution: δ²H_pred≈8(−5)+10=−40+10=−30‰. This is a broad empirical comparison, not a universal formula that determines the actual δ²H for every rain sample or provides a location or age.

Cross-check: 8(−5)+10=−30.

Common error: Do not present the line as a nuclear decay equation.

Worked example 30. A point below the illustrative meteoric-water line

Question: A sample measures δ¹⁸O=−5‰ and δ²H=−45‰. Relative to the global approximation, what is the vertical offset?

Detailed solution: Predicted δ²H=−30‰. Observed minus predicted=−45−(−30)=−15‰. The point is 15 per-mille units below the illustrative global line. Evaporation or source differences could be hypotheses; this single point does not prove one physical history.

Cross-check: −30−15=−45.

Common error: An offset is not a unique recharge or evaporation diagnosis.

Worked example 31. One chlorine atom: expected two isotope peak proportions

Question: Under an ideal model with ³⁵Cl fraction 0.75 and ³⁷Cl fraction 0.25, what is one-chlorine M:M+2 category ratio?

Detailed solution: The lighter chlorine isotope appears with probability 0.75 and heavier with 0.25. Under a comparable detection and other-isotopes-negligible model, the ratio is 0.75:0.25=3:1. Real spectra also have natural carbon, sulfur and other isotope contributions and response factors.

Cross-check: Peak category proportions sum to one.

Common error: Do not assert any M+2 peak proves chlorine without molecular formula evidence.

Worked example 32. Two chlorine atoms: 9:6:1 pattern

Question: Under independent sites with p(³⁵Cl)=0.75 and q(³⁷Cl)=0.25, find M, M+2 and M+4 class probabilities.

Detailed solution: P(0 heavier)=p²=0.5625. P(1 heavier)=2pq=2×0.75×0.25=0.375. P(2 heavier)=q²=0.0625. These are 56.25%, 37.5%, 6.25%, giving 9:6:1. The categories reflect chlorine count under a toy independent-site model.

Cross-check: 0.5625+0.375+0.0625=1.

Common error: For one heavy atom there are two arrangements, hence factor two.

Worked example 33. Two carbon sites: natural-like binomial categories

Question: A molecule has two independent carbon sites, each with illustrative ¹³C probability p=0.01. Compute M+0, M+1 and M+2 carbon-only probabilities.

Detailed solution: P₀=(0.99)²=0.9801, P₁=2(0.01)(0.99)=0.0198, P₂=(0.01)²=0.0001. The fractions sum to one. Real mass spectra also involve other isotopes, charge states and detection artifacts.

Cross-check: 0.9801+0.0198+0.0001=1.

Common error: Do not claim the binomial model applies to position-labelled metabolic tracers.

Worked example 34. Three carbon sites: exactly one heavy isotope

Question: For n=3 independent carbon sites with p(¹³C)=0.01, calculate P(k=1).

Detailed solution: Use binomial P(k=1)=C(3,1)p(1−p)²=3×0.01×0.99²=0.029403, or 2.9403%. This is an ideal three-site natural-abundance-like model; deliberate labeling correlations would change the distribution.

Cross-check: 3×0.01×0.9801=0.029403.

Common error: Do not replace C(3,1) with 1.

Worked example 35. Potassium-40 as percent and ppm

Question: CIAAW representative x(⁴⁰K)=0.000117. Convert to percent and atom-ppm.

Detailed solution: Percent=0.000117×100=0.0117%. Atom-ppm=0.000117×10⁶=117 ppm of potassium isotope atoms. This value does not specify the concentration of potassium in an organism or the total activity in a mineral without total parent atoms and nuclear decay information.

Cross-check: 117 ppm /10,000=0.0117%.

Common error: Do not call all natural potassium radioactive.

Worked example 36. Carbon-14 at an order-of-magnitude fraction

Question: Suppose ¹⁴C fraction is 10⁻¹⁶ of carbon atoms in a toy premodern context. Convert to atom-ppm and percent.

Detailed solution: Percent=100×10⁻¹⁶=10⁻¹⁴%, while ppm=10⁶×10⁻¹⁶=10⁻¹⁰ ppm of carbon atoms. The extreme rarity helps explain why radiocarbon barely affects mean atomic weight; activity and age calculations need separate decay data.

Cross-check: 10⁻¹⁰ppm/10⁶=10⁻¹⁶.

Worked example 37. Tracer enrichment relative to background

Question: Background x(¹³C)=0.0107; a labeled sample has x=0.2107. Find atom-percent excess and enrichment factor in the simple IUPAC sense.

Detailed solution: Atom fraction excess is 0.2107−0.0107=0.20, or 20 atom percentage points. The abundance ratio x_labeled/x_background=0.2107/0.0107≈19.6916. These are different enrichment metrics; label the chosen definition. The sample is not a normal natural carbon mixture.

Cross-check: Background + excess=0.0107+0.20=0.2107.

Worked example 38. Expected carbon-13 count in a large sample

Question: A hypothetical pool contains 500,000 carbon atoms and x(¹³C)=0.0107. How many ¹³C atoms are expected under the composition?

Detailed solution: Expected count=500,000×0.0107=5350 carbon-13 atoms, with 494,650 carbon atoms assigned to the remaining isotopes in the simplified two-isotope model. Actual sampling introduces statistical variation and isotope composition is sample-dependent.

Cross-check: 5350/500,000=0.0107.

Common error: Do not apply this carbon fraction to every element's atoms.

Worked example 39. From mixed fraction to δ in a stated reference model

Question: Two-source carbon mixing yields x_mix=0.0106. Find R_mix, and δ given an explicitly hypothetical R_ref=0.0108.

Detailed solution: First compute R_mix=x/(1−x)=0.0106/0.9894≈0.01071356. Then δ=[0.01071356/0.0108−1]×1000≈−8.00335‰. This result depends on the supplied reference ratio; it is not a universal VPDB value and uses a simplified two-isotope pool.

Cross-check: R_mix/(1+R_mix)=0.0106.

Common error: Do not compute δ directly from isotope atom fraction without conversion.

Worked example 40. A measured ratio cannot prove an exact metabolic flux

Question: A leaf has δ¹³C=−27‰ and a root has δ¹³C=−25‰. Calculate their δ difference and identify the limits of inference.

Detailed solution: Root minus leaf=−25−(−27)=+2‰. The root is 2 per-mille units higher in the reported reference-relative isotope ratio, but the data alone cannot identify a unique pathway, growth condition or numerical carbon transport flux. Tissue chemistry, source signatures, time, fractionation and independent measures are needed.

Cross-check: −27+2=−25.

1.A.1.23.41 Sixty explained original examination questions

Sixty original CSIR-NET/SET concept checks with answers and scientific explanations. These are not represented as official past exam questions.

Sixty original CSIR-NET and SET-oriented questions with explanations

These are original exam-style problems, not copied official past-year questions. Their answers distinguish direct measurements from model-based inference, and all numerical values have explicit denominators or reference scales.

Question 1. What is natural isotopic abundance?

Answer: The isotope's atom fraction in the element as found in nature

Why: The denominator is all atoms of the same element in a specified natural material, not all atoms in a compound or a universal planetary sample.

Question 2. How is isotope atom fraction x defined?

Answer: N(isotope)/N(all atoms of its element)

Why: IUPAC abundance is a number fraction and has no unit; isotope atom fractions sum to one for a complete element population.

Question 3. How do you convert an isotope fraction to percent?

Answer: Multiply x by 100

Why: A fraction of 0.0107 corresponds to 1.07%, not 0.0107%.

Question 4. What does x=0.002 mean in atom-ppm?

Answer: 2,000 ppm among the element's atoms

Why: Multiplication by 10⁶ converts an atom fraction into parts per million of the selected element's atom population.

Question 5. Is every natural carbon sample exactly 1.07% ¹³C?

Answer: No

Why: CIAAW gives evaluated normal-material carbon-13 composition ranges; sample sources and fractionation can change the actual value.

Question 6. What is the CIAAW normal-material range for carbon-13?

Answer: Approximately 0.0096–0.0116 atom fraction

Why: This is 0.96%–1.16% of carbon atoms in the evaluated normal-material range, not a single universal fraction.

Question 7. Which normal nitrogen isotope is much more abundant?

Answer: ¹⁴N

Why: Nitrogen-14 dominates normal nitrogen atom fractions, while stable nitrogen-15 is a few tenths of a percent.

Question 8. Which is the dominant oxygen isotope in normal terrestrial material?

Answer: ¹⁶O

Why: Its normal-material fraction is close to 0.9975; oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 are smaller stable components.

Question 9. What is a sample's isotope closure condition?

Answer: All isotope atom fractions of that element sum to one

Why: Values must be compatible within one specimen. Independently selecting maxima from different CIAAW ranges can violate this rule.

Question 10. Does isotope mass number 13 mean a 13% abundance?

Answer: No

Why: Mass number A counts nucleons; abundance is a number fraction among atoms of an element. These are independent quantities.

Question 11. What did the isotope concept solve historically?

Answer: Atoms of one element can differ in nuclear mass

Why: Chemical identity can remain the same even when neutron count and mass number differ; this helped explain nonintegral sample atomic weights.

Question 12. Why was mass spectrometry important for isotope science?

Answer: It distinguished isotope species and their mass-related signals

Why: Mass-resolved measurements made isotope mixtures directly observable, beyond bulk chemical average mass alone.

Question 13. Did primordial nucleosynthesis produce all present isotopic compositions unchanged?

Answer: No

Why: Later stellar nuclear production, radioactive decay, geological and biological processes modify inventories and sample ratios.

Question 14. What is a radiogenic isotope?

Answer: A nuclide produced through radioactive decay history

Why: Radiogenic describes origin; the daughter may itself be stable, such as some lead isotopes receiving uranium/thorium decay contributions.

Question 15. What is a cosmogenic isotope?

Answer: A nuclide produced by cosmic-ray-associated nuclear processes

Why: Carbon-14 is a familiar environmental example. Its creation is nuclear rather than ordinary carbon fixation.

Question 16. Are all primordial nuclides stable?

Answer: No

Why: Some long-lived radioactive nuclides, such as uranium isotopes, survive from early Solar System material.

Question 17. Can chemical evaporation change ¹⁸O into ¹⁶O?

Answer: No

Why: Evaporation fractionates stable isotope-bearing molecules by physical partitioning; it does not change nuclear proton or neutron counts.

Question 18. Does a rare isotope have to be radioactive?

Answer: No

Why: Stable oxygen-17 is comparatively rare, while abundant carbon-12 and less abundant carbon-13 are both stable.

Question 19. Does one radionuclide half-life determine its abundance in every rock?

Answer: No

Why: Production, initial amount, decay duration, mixing and daughter retention influence the isotope inventory of a particular sample.

Question 20. What is a naturally occurring rare radioactive potassium isotope?

Answer: ⁴⁰K

Why: CIAAW gives an illustrative fraction near 0.000117 among potassium atoms, not 40% or 0.000117%.

Question 21. What is the heavy-to-light isotope ratio R?

Answer: N_heavy/N_light

Why: It is not identical to heavy atom fraction x=N_heavy/(N_heavy+N_light).

Question 22. For a two-isotope system, how is x related to R?

Answer: x=R/(1+R)

Why: The result accounts for the heavy isotope atoms in the total atom denominator.

Question 23. If x=0.20, what is R?

Answer: 0.25

Why: R=x/(1−x)=0.20/0.80=0.25.

Question 24. What does isotope δ measure?

Answer: Relative difference in isotope ratio compared with a reference

Why: δ=(R_sample/R_reference−1)×1000‰; a negative delta does not mean negative isotope atoms.

Question 25. If R_sample/R_ref=0.98, what is δ?

Answer: −20‰

Why: (0.98−1)×1000=−20‰ means a sample ratio 2% below the reference ratio.

Question 26. Which reference is widely used for δ¹³C?

Answer: VPDB

Why: Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite is a ratio reference scale, not the ratio of all carbon atoms in every carbonate.

Question 27. What reference is widely used for δ¹⁵N?

Answer: Atmospheric N₂ (AIR)

Why: The reference should be specified before comparing isotope delta values across studies.

Question 28. What reference is widely used for δ²H and δ¹⁸O in water?

Answer: VSMOW

Why: Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water conventions underpin common water stable isotope ratio comparisons.

Question 29. What is α_A/B in a defined isotope fractionation model?

Answer: R_A/R_B

Why: The fractionation factor compares heavy-to-light isotope ratios in specified chemical species or phases and is dimensionless.

Question 30. Does α>1 imply radioactive decay?

Answer: No

Why: It means that the defined phase A has relatively higher heavy/light isotope ratio than B; ordinary fractionation preserves nuclei.

Question 31. Can C₃ and C₄ plants show different δ¹³C signatures?

Answer: Often yes

Why: Their carbon acquisition and carboxylation processes may discriminate against heavy carbon differently; species, environment and source CO₂ also influence values.

Question 32. Does δ¹³C=−27‰ uniquely prove a plant species is C₃?

Answer: No

Why: It can be consistent with a C₃-type signature but does not uniquely identify species or fixation pathway without context.

Question 33. Why can CAM plants be harder to identify from δ¹³C alone?

Answer: Their fixation modes and contributions can vary

Why: CAM physiology can generate broad or overlapping isotope signatures depending on conditions and plant behavior.

Question 34. What can a higher consumer δ¹⁵N suggest in a food web?

Answer: Possible trophic enrichment relative to an appropriate baseline

Why: It is not a guaranteed one-step trophic shift because source baselines and tissue discrimination vary.

Question 35. Does one δ¹⁵N value identify a nitrate pollutant source uniquely?

Answer: No

Why: Fertilizer, manure, atmospheric and soil signatures can overlap, and microbial processing can fractionate the isotope ratio.

Question 36. Can microbial sulfate reduction affect δ³⁴S?

Answer: Yes

Why: Biochemical sulfur transformations can fractionate stable sulfur isotopes, but one sulfur ratio does not uniquely identify a microbial species or rate.

Question 37. Which stable isotope pair is central to water delta studies?

Answer: ²H/¹H and ¹⁸O/¹⁶O

Why: Paired ratios may inform evaporation, precipitation and source mixing without being nuclear clocks.

Question 38. What is a broad empirical global meteoric water relationship?

Answer: δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10‰

Why: It is an approximate global precipitation trend, not an exact line for every local rainfall or water sample.

Question 39. Can water δ¹⁸O alone give exact groundwater age?

Answer: No

Why: Stable isotopes support source and fractionation hypotheses. Age needs additional time-sensitive data and a valid environmental model.

Question 40. Are natural δ¹³C values a direct measurement of photosynthetic rate?

Answer: No

Why: A ratio difference reflects isotope composition; carbon fixation rates require separate gas-exchange, time or kinetic evidence.

Question 41. What is isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) used for?

Answer: Precisely comparing isotope ratios in appropriately prepared samples

Why: Its raw ion signals require calibration, reference materials, background correction and quality checks.

Question 42. Are all uncorrected mass spectral peak heights exact isotope fractions?

Answer: No

Why: Ionization, detector response, molecular overlaps, charge and matrix effects can bias apparent proportions.

Question 43. Why can chlorine-containing molecules show M and M+2 categories?

Answer: ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl differ by approximately two mass units

Why: A one-chlorine species often has approximately 3:1 categories in a simple normal-abundance toy model.

Question 44. What is the ideal two-chlorine isotope pattern for 0.75/0.25 fractions?

Answer: 9:6:1 for M:M+2:M+4

Why: The categories are p², 2pq and q² for two independent chlorine sites.

Question 45. Does any M+2 feature prove chlorine?

Answer: No

Why: Oxygen-18, sulfur-34, two carbon-13 substitutions and other chemical species can contribute nominal M+2.

Question 46. Does stable ¹³C enrichment change carbon into another element?

Answer: No

Why: The label has Z=6 throughout ordinary metabolism and affects mass and isotope ratios, not chemical element identity.

Question 47. Does a ¹³C-labelled metabolite peak prove an absolute enzyme flux?

Answer: No

Why: A quantitative rate needs precursor enrichment, time, pools, network constraints and controls, not just one isotope mass peak.

Question 48. What must a good δ¹³C laboratory report identify?

Answer: Isotope pair, reference scale, sample and uncertainty

Why: VPDB normalization, calibration, sample preparation and analytical precision influence interpretation.

Question 49. How does a CIAAW isotope-composition range differ from a particular sample value?

Answer: It summarizes an evaluated material context rather than one specimen

Why: A specific sample measurement is needed to assign an exact isotope fraction or ratio.

Question 50. Can a chemical concentration assay be replaced by an isotope fraction?

Answer: No

Why: Isotope fraction answers what share of element atoms have a given nuclide; concentration answers how much of the element or analyte is present.

Question 51. What is the correct exact mixing weight for isotope atom fractions?

Answer: The fraction of element atoms contributed by each source

Why: Water volume, bulk mass or flow fraction may be inappropriate if element concentrations differ.

Question 52. If sources have identical isotope fractions, can one mixed fraction reveal their proportions?

Answer: No

Why: The mixing equation becomes independent of source weights; another tracer or difference is needed.

Question 53. Can one isotope value uniquely determine three source shares?

Answer: Generally no

Why: Multiple mixtures can satisfy one abundance equation plus normalization. Independent constraints or more tracers are required.

Question 54. What does a sample mean relative atomic mass require?

Answer: Isotope masses and normalized abundance fractions

Why: A standard atomic weight may be suitable for ordinary work but cannot describe every enriched or unusual sample.

Question 55. What does Rayleigh-type fractionation model?

Answer: Progressive isotope evolution of a reservoir as material is removed

Why: The equation requires a stated fractionation convention, reservoir definition and physical assumptions.

Question 56. Does δ=−25‰ mean 25% of heavy isotope atoms disappeared?

Answer: No

Why: It is a 2.5% relative deficit of an isotope ratio compared with the specified reference, not a mass loss.

Question 57. What does the term atom-percent excess mean in a tracer study?

Answer: Label atom percentage above the stated baseline

Why: It is different from an isotope delta value or the isotope enrichment factor defined as an abundance ratio.

Question 58. What biological evidence could help interpret δ¹⁵N in an animal?

Answer: Local source baselines, diet records, tissue discrimination and other isotope tracers

Why: One tissue δ¹⁵N alone does not give a precise trophic level or species-specific feeding history.

Question 59. Which next chapter follows Node 23 in this academy syllabus?

Answer: Node 24 — Calculating Weighted Average Atomic Mass

Why: Node 23 explains abundance origin and measurements; Node 24 will develop multi-step weighted mean calculations in greater depth.

Question 60. What makes a natural isotope inference scientifically defensible?

Answer: Correct isotope/units, measured reference, suitable process model and honest uncertainty

Why: A numerical ratio is evidence, not by itself proof of one unique cause, age, location or biological rate.

1.A.1.23.42 Thirty common misconception corrections

Thirty errors are repaired using counterexamples, equations and scientific inference limits.

Thirty scientifically corrected statements with evidence and calculation checks

Misconception 1

Incorrect idea: A natural isotope abundance is exactly the same everywhere on Earth.

Correction: Normal-material isotope fractions can vary by environment, source and physical or biological fractionation.

Evidence check: CIAAW gives carbon-13, oxygen-18 and chlorine-37 ranges rather than one immutable exact atom fraction.

Misconception 2

Incorrect idea: Every line in a CIAAW isotope table is a percentage.

Correction: Many CIAAW composition tables give atom fractions; convert to percent by multiplying by 100.

Evidence check: x(¹³C)=0.0107 is 1.07%, not 0.0107%.

Misconception 3

Incorrect idea: Isotope atom fraction uses the total atoms of all elements as denominator.

Correction: The denominator is all atoms of the same chemical element in the defined sample.

Evidence check: ¹³C among carbon atoms is not the fraction of all hydrogen, oxygen and carbon atoms in a leaf.

Misconception 4

Incorrect idea: Mass number 13 means carbon-13 is 13% abundant.

Correction: Mass number A counts nuclear protons plus neutrons; isotope abundance is an independent population fraction.

Evidence check: ¹³C has A=13 while typical carbon-13 atom fraction is around one percent.

Misconception 5

Incorrect idea: A less abundant isotope is necessarily radioactive.

Correction: Abundance and nuclear stability are distinct properties.

Evidence check: Oxygen-17 and carbon-13 are stable despite being much less abundant than their major stable isotopes.

Misconception 6

Incorrect idea: All naturally occurring isotopes must be stable.

Correction: Some naturally occurring nuclides are radioactive, such as ⁴⁰K and ¹⁴C.

Evidence check: Potassium-40 is a small natural fraction of potassium and has nuclear decay.

Misconception 7

Incorrect idea: Primordial automatically means stable.

Correction: Primordial labels origin and survival, not the absence of radioactive transformation.

Evidence check: Uranium-238 and uranium-235 are naturally present long-lived radioactive nuclides.

Misconception 8

Incorrect idea: Cosmogenic carbon-14 is produced by the plant's photosynthesis.

Correction: Nuclear reactions associated with cosmic rays produce ¹⁴C, and environmental carbon cycling subsequently transports it.

Evidence check: Photosynthesis usually redistributes carbon atoms and affects stable isotope ratios; it does not create ¹⁴C nuclei.

Misconception 9

Incorrect idea: An isotope becomes another isotope when ordinary water evaporates.

Correction: Evaporation partitions molecules with existing nuclei; it does not change Z or N.

Evidence check: ²H remains deuterium and ¹⁸O remains oxygen-18 when water changes phase.

Misconception 10

Incorrect idea: Fractionation always makes every product heavier-isotope enriched.

Correction: Direction and magnitude depend on equilibrium, kinetic processes, isotope pair and environment.

Evidence check: Vapor often becomes relatively lighter than liquid under particular evaporation conditions, but process assumptions matter.

Misconception 11

Incorrect idea: An equilibrium α factor describes every outdoor evaporation condition perfectly.

Correction: Open systems can involve humidity, wind, non-equilibrium diffusion and source mixing.

Evidence check: One temperature-dependent equilibrium factor cannot fully specify an evaporating lake.

Misconception 12

Incorrect idea: α_A/B=1.003 means 3% difference in isotope ratio.

Correction: A ratio of 1.003 represents 0.3% or approximately 3 per-mille difference.

Evidence check: (1.003−1)×1000=3‰.

Misconception 13

Incorrect idea: The heavy-to-light isotope ratio R equals heavy atom fraction x exactly.

Correction: R=heavy/light; x=heavy/(heavy+light), so x=R/(1+R) for two isotopes.

Evidence check: One heavy and 99 light atoms yield x=0.01 but R≈0.010101.

Misconception 14

Incorrect idea: A negative isotope δ means the sample has a negative number of heavy atoms.

Correction: Negative δ means the heavy/light ratio is below a specified reference ratio.

Evidence check: δ¹³C=−25‰ means R_sample=0.975 R_ref, not negative carbon-13.

Misconception 15

Incorrect idea: δ¹³C=−25‰ means 25% of carbon is missing.

Correction: The value is −2.5% relative in isotope ratio, expressed as −25 per mille.

Evidence check: (−25/1000)+1=0.975.

Misconception 16

Incorrect idea: Delta isotope values can be compared without stating a reference.

Correction: Correct interpretation requires an isotope pair and appropriate reference scale such as VPDB, VSMOW, AIR or VCDT.

Evidence check: δ¹⁸O of water on VSMOW and δ¹⁸O of carbonate on VPDB require explicit scale context.

Misconception 17

Incorrect idea: One δ¹³C value proves a plant is a particular C₃ species.

Correction: Plant carbon signatures are influenced by physiology, sources and environments with overlapping ranges.

Evidence check: A δ¹³C near −27‰ may be consistent with C₃ but does not uniquely identify one plant species.

Misconception 18

Incorrect idea: CAM plants must have one fixed carbon isotope signature.

Correction: CAM pathway expression and environmental conditions can vary, generating a broader signature pattern.

Evidence check: One isotopic value cannot uniquely establish daily fixation strategy of a CAM plant.

Misconception 19

Incorrect idea: Higher predator δ¹⁵N always proves exactly one trophic step.

Correction: Source baselines and tissue-specific trophic discrimination vary with species and physiology.

Evidence check: A 3‰ difference is evidence to interpret, not an exact universal rung in a food web.

Misconception 20

Incorrect idea: A high nitrate δ¹⁵N uniquely identifies a manure pollution source.

Correction: Sources overlap and nitrification/denitrification can alter ratios after entry.

Evidence check: Hydrological, chemical and multiple-isotope evidence is needed for responsible attribution.

Misconception 21

Incorrect idea: Any δ¹⁸O water value is a direct groundwater age.

Correction: Stable oxygen ratios reflect source, mixing and fractionation, not radioactive time by themselves.

Evidence check: Groundwater age questions need additional time-sensitive tracers and environmental modeling.

Misconception 22

Incorrect idea: Every rain sample must lie exactly on the global meteoric water line.

Correction: The global relation is empirical and approximate; local precipitation and evaporated water can differ.

Evidence check: δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10 is contextual, not a required equality for every measured water.

Misconception 23

Incorrect idea: Equal water volumes always contribute equal amounts of isotope-bearing carbon.

Correction: Different dissolved carbon concentrations can make atom contributions unequal.

Evidence check: Equal liters with a 4:1 carbon concentration ratio give 80% versus 20% carbon atom contributions.

Misconception 24

Incorrect idea: A mixture's δ is always exactly the volume-weighted mean δ.

Correction: Conserve isotope atoms, derive fractions and ratios, then calculate δ using a reference.

Evidence check: Mixing by volume can be wrong when element concentrations differ, and δ is nonlinear in x.

Misconception 25

Incorrect idea: One average mass or one isotope ratio uniquely resolves three source fractions.

Correction: Several source mixtures may yield the same mean or ratio without extra constraints.

Evidence check: A mean of 11u can represent pure mass-11 nuclides or equal parts 10u and 12u.

Misconception 26

Incorrect idea: All listed CIAAW isotope abundance maxima can describe one sample simultaneously.

Correction: Each actual element pool must obey the fraction-sum rule; range extremes may come from different samples.

Evidence check: Maximum ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O fractions are not automatically a valid normalized composition together.

Misconception 27

Incorrect idea: One chlorine-containing molecular ion has equal M and M+2 peaks.

Correction: A typical single chlorine atom has ³⁵Cl/³⁷Cl proportions near 3:1, not 1:1.

Evidence check: One normal-like chlorine atom gives approximate probabilities 0.75 and 0.25 under ideal detector assumptions.

Misconception 28

Incorrect idea: Two chlorine atoms must still show the same 3:1 M/M+2 pattern.

Correction: Two independent chlorine sites produce three categories with approximate probabilities 9:6:1.

Evidence check: p², 2pq, q² with p=0.75 and q=0.25.

Misconception 29

Incorrect idea: Raw mass spectral peak intensity is always the true atom fraction.

Correction: Instrument response, ion chemistry, background and isobaric or molecular interferences can bias signals.

Evidence check: Calibrated standards and validated corrections are needed for precise isotope composition.

Misconception 30

Incorrect idea: Detection of stable ¹³C in a metabolite directly gives absolute glycolytic flux.

Correction: A detected label supports incorporation but flux requires precursor enrichment, time, atom maps and kinetic constraints.

Evidence check: One M+3 lactate isotopologue signal cannot independently establish a rate in mol/s.

1.A.1.23.43 Ten integrated higher-order reasoning problems

Ten multi-step problems combine isotope ratio mathematics, source mixing, environmental evidence and biological interpretation.

Ten original integrated problems with fully reasoned model answers

Integrated challenge 1. A carbon abundance range with a correct scientific interpretation

Problem: CIAAW lists ¹³C atom fraction for normal carbon as 0.0096–0.0116. Convert to percentage; explain what range and source context mean.

Complete solution: The percent range is 0.96–1.16% of carbon atoms. These are evaluated values for normal materials, reflecting source-dependent variability. They are not the percent carbon content of a sample or a guarantee that every leaf has the same value. A precise isotope-weighted mass calculation for a particular reagent needs the reagent's measured or certified composition.

Integrated challenge 2. Two isotope ratios and the delta reference

Problem: A carbon sample has R_sample=0.0100 and an explicitly hypothetical R_ref=0.0104. Find δ, then explain why the isotope atom fraction is not immediately 1%.

Complete solution: δ=(0.0100/0.0104−1)×1000≈−38.4615‰. Under a two-isotope carbon model x=R/(1+R)=0.0100/1.0100≈0.00990099, or 0.990099%. Delta is a relative ratio difference; the atom fraction uses a different denominator. The reference is a toy value, not claimed VPDB absolute ratio.

Integrated challenge 3. A two-source water and carbon mixing puzzle

Problem: Two water samples are mixed at equal volume. Source A contains four times B's carbon atoms per liter. The ¹³C fractions are 0.012 in A and 0.010 in B. Find mixed isotope fraction.

Complete solution: Equal volume does not imply equal carbon atom contributions: A contributes 4/5=0.8 and B 1/5=0.2. Therefore x_mix=0.8×0.012+0.2×0.010=0.0116, or 1.16% of mixed carbon atoms. A naive average 0.011 is incorrect. The result assumes no fractionation or reaction after mixing.

Integrated challenge 4. An inverse mixing problem with a unique solution

Problem: Sources A and B have x_A=0.014 and x_B=0.010. The mixture is x=0.011. Under ideal two-source conditions, what percentage of element atoms came from source A?

Complete solution: x_mix=w×0.014+(1−w)×0.010=0.010+0.004w. Solving w=(0.011−0.010)/0.004=0.25 yields 25% of the element atoms from A and 75% from B. This is not necessarily 25% of fluid volume; element concentrations may differ.

Integrated challenge 5. Isotopically distinct sources but insufficient biology

Problem: A predator tissue has δ¹⁵N=+9‰ and a prey composite δ¹⁵N=+6‰. What difference is observed, and can its exact trophic level be calculated?

Complete solution: The observed tissue-prey delta difference is +3‰. One can discuss trophic discrimination as a possible influence, but a precise trophic level needs local baseline species, tissue discrimination assumptions, diet composition and ecological context. The isotope difference is evidence, not a universal one-level rule.

Integrated challenge 6. A meteoric-water-line example and uncertainty

Problem: A water sample has δ¹⁸O=−6‰ and measured δ²H=−52‰. Use δ²H≈8δ¹⁸O+10 and interpret the offset.

Complete solution: The global approximate line predicts 8(−6)+10=−38‰. Measured −52‰ differs by −14‰ below the illustrative relation. It could be compatible with several evaporation or source histories; one point does not uniquely establish the actual water origin or recharge age. A local reference and hydrological data are needed.

Integrated challenge 7. A natural chloride isotope envelope with two chlorine atoms

Problem: Using ideal p(³⁵Cl)=0.75 and q(³⁷Cl)=0.25, calculate the three chlorine-only categories for a two-chlorine molecular ion.

Complete solution: M category p²=0.5625, M+2 2pq=0.375, M+4 q²=0.0625, or 56.25%,37.5%,6.25%, giving 9:6:1. The assumption is independent chlorine sites, equal detection and other isotopic influences ignored. An actual spectrum may contain overlapping peaks from other elements or adducts.

Integrated challenge 8. The three-isotope abundance ambiguity

Problem: Oxygen-16 and oxygen-17 fractions are 0.99750 and 0.00040. Find oxygen-18 fraction and explain what additional measurement is needed to compute δ¹⁸O.

Complete solution: Closure gives x₁₈=1−0.99750−0.00040=0.00210. The isotope ratio R₁₈/₁₆=0.00210/0.99750≈0.00210526. To compute δ¹⁸O, specify the reference ratio (e.g., appropriate VSMOW realization); ratio alone is not δ.

Integrated challenge 9. Natural abundance and an artificial ¹³C tracer experiment

Problem: Background carbon fraction x₀=0.0107, while a sample after deliberate labeling reports x=0.2107. Compute atom-percent excess and explain whether it determines metabolic flux.

Complete solution: Atom-percent excess=(0.2107−0.0107)×100=20.0 percentage points. The sample is deliberately enriched well beyond ordinary normal carbon. This does not provide absolute flux; precursor atom labeling, time, metabolite pool sizes and reaction network constraints are needed.

Integrated challenge 10. A nuclear geochemistry and environmental age comparison

Problem: A mineral has a measured potassium-40 fraction and an argon isotope signal; a groundwater sample has δ¹⁸O and tritium. Which observations describe composition and what further information is needed for age?

Complete solution: All listed measurements describe compositions or isotope signals. ⁴⁰K/⁴⁰Ar mineral age interpretation requires parent/daughter amounts, decay parameters, argon retention, initial daughter assessment and geological closure. Groundwater δ¹⁸O mainly informs sources/fractionation; tritium can add a time-related constraint only with historical input, mixing and transport models. Neither sample age follows from one natural isotope percentage alone.

1.A.1.23.44 Master revision and forward syllabus navigation

Follow the chronological story

Atomic weight puzzles led to isotope discovery and mass spectrometry. Astrophysical nucleosynthesis created many nuclide inventories. Natural reservoirs then evolved under radioactive decay, geochemical separation, isotope fractionation, mixing and biological cycling. Reference materials and modern instruments now allow tiny isotope-ratio differences to be compared with scientific traceability.

The one-sentence definition

Natural isotopic abundance is the fraction of a specified isotope among all atoms of its element in a specified natural material. It is sample-dependent; a CIAAW representative value or range should never be mistaken for a universally exact percentage.

The quantities that must remain distinct

Mass number A is an integer nucleon count. Isotope atom fraction x is an atom share. Isotope ratio R compares two isotope atom counts. Delta δ measures a reference-relative isotope-ratio difference. Fractionation factor α compares isotope ratios between phases. Standard atomic weight is a sample-context-dependent weighted mass ratio. A metabolic flux is material per time, requiring additional kinetic information.

Natural production versus fractionation

Cosmogenic and radiogenic processes involve nuclear formation or decay. Fractionation, respiration, photosynthesis, water evaporation and mixing mostly redistribute isotope-bearing molecules without transmuting the nuclei. An isotope ratio difference alone does not reveal which process occurred.

Biological map of the chapter

Carbon-13 differences can inform photosynthetic carbon-source hypotheses; nitrogen-15 can contribute to food-web and nitrogen-cycle analysis; sulfur isotope variations can inform microbial sulfur-process hypotheses; deuterium and oxygen-18 can constrain water source and evaporation; natural potassium-40 and radiogenic lead illustrate nuclear histories. Each inference must include appropriate sample baselines and model assumptions.

Seven checks for a correct exam answer

Identify the element and isotope pair; name the sampled pool; confirm fraction versus ratio versus delta; normalize sample fractions; use isotope atom contributions for mixing; state the analytical reference and the physical model; then separate direct observations from hypotheses requiring additional evidence.

What a measured isotope number does not tell you

A single δ¹³C value cannot uniquely identify a plant species or quantify photosynthesis. One δ¹⁵N measurement cannot assign an exact trophic level. A chlorine M+2 peak cannot by itself prove chlorine. A water δ¹⁸O number is not a groundwater age. A natural isotope fraction alone cannot establish a physiological diagnosis or an environmental source with certainty.

Essential quantitative formula map
  1. Isotope atom fraction: xᵢ=Nᵢ/N_element, 0≤xᵢ≤1, Σxᵢ=1.
  2. Percent abundance: 100xᵢ; atom-ppm: 10⁶xᵢ.
  3. For two isotopes: R=N_heavy/N_light=x_H/(1−x_H), and x_H=R/(1+R).
  4. Relative isotope-ratio difference: δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰.
  5. Fractionation factor for defined phases: α_A/B=R_A/R_B.
  6. Two-source element-atom mixing without fractionation: x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B.
  7. Two-source inverse: w=(x_mix−x_B)/(x_A−x_B) if the signatures differ.
  8. Weighted mean atomic mass: m̄=Σxᵢmᵢ; relative atomic mass is m̄/u.
  9. Two chlorine sites with heavy-isotope fraction q: P(0)=p²; P(1)=2pq; P(2)=q², where p+q=1.
  10. Only with a stated Rayleigh convention: R_res/R_initial=f^(α−1), for a modeled residual fraction f.
