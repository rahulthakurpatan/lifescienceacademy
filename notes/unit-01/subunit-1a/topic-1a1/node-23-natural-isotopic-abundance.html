1.A.1.23.1 A natural puzzle: the same element with different isotope fingerprints

A classroom mystery

A student compares carbon atoms in a tree leaf with carbon atoms in an ocean carbonate sample. Every carbon nucleus has six protons. Yet a sensitive instrument reports different ¹³C/¹²C ratios in the two materials. How can one element have different isotope proportions without its elemental identity changing?

The answer begins with an atom population

Most ordinary carbon samples contain stable carbon-12 and carbon-13, with trace carbon-14 in some natural settings. The fractional amount of each isotope can change from sample to sample because materials have different origins and because chemical or biological processes sometimes sort isotopes differently.

A bigger conceptual shift than a new formula

Node 22 explained the carbon-12 atomic mass reference and isotope-weighted mean. Node 23 asks what the weights represent in nature, what produces natural isotope differences, and why a table value should not be mistaken for a universal constant of every sample.

A scientific promise to the learner

By the end, you should calculate isotope atom fractions, ratios and delta values; explain cosmic origin, radioactive production, equilibrium and kinetic fractionation; assess measurement limits; and connect isotope signatures to living organisms without claiming unmeasured biological rates.

Learning outcomes: Explain natural isotopic abundance and its sample dependence; interpret CIAAW 2024 composition ranges; calculate percentage, atom fraction, ratio, δ and source mixing; understand fractionation and biologically relevant stable isotope applications; solve evidence-limited exam problems.

1.A.1.23.2 Chronological development: Dalton, Soddy, Aston and modern reference laboratories

Bulk chemistry came before isotope science

Early atomic weights arose from proportions in chemical reactions. A value near 35.45 for chlorine made sense as a mean mass, but without isotope theory the underlying mixture of chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 was not directly visible.

Isotopes resolved the same-element mystery

The analysis of radioactive decay series and Frederick Soddy's isotope concept showed that chemically identical elements could have differing atomic masses. The isotope name reflects their shared place in the periodic table, not equal neutron count.

Mass spectrometers provided direct evidence

Mass spectrometric work by Francis Aston and successors revealed distinguishable atomic species and their proportions. Once the neutron and atomic number were understood, a nuclide could be specified by proton number Z and mass number A.

Standardized comparisons came next

Precise isotope-ratio measurements later required agreed reference materials and consistent reporting scales. CIAAW evaluates representative isotopic compositions and standard atomic weights; IUPAC defines terminology; reference scales such as VPDB and VSMOW support cross-study comparisons.

The chronology matters

Scientific thinking advanced from chemical average weights, to isotope recognition, to mass-resolved detection, to careful environmental and biological interpretation. An atom fraction answers a different question than a molecular formula, a decay half-life or a measured metabolic rate.

Conceptual history of isotope measurement and interpretation, not a year-scale timeline.

1.A.1.23.3 The definition of natural isotopic abundance: numerator and denominator

IUPAC's essential idea

Isotopic abundance is the relative number of atoms of a particular isotope among all the atoms of its element in a mixture. Natural isotopic abundance is the abundance of that isotope in the element as found in nature. It is a composition property of a specified material, not an unconditional exact percentage for the entire planet.

A carbon numerical

If a hypothetical sample contains 10,700 carbon-13 atoms per million carbon atoms, x(¹³C)=10,700/1,000,000=0.0107, equivalent to 1.07% of the sample's carbon atoms. This does not mean 1.07% of all atoms of all elements in the sample are carbon-13.

The fundamental closure rule

For a defined element, isotope atom fractions sum to one across the included species: Σxᵢ=1. For a two-isotope model x_light+x_heavy=1. For oxygen, ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O require three fractions.

Chemical reaction versus nucleus change

Evaporation, photosynthesis and mixing can shift relative isotope amounts in a pool while retaining each nucleus's identity. Nuclear production or decay is a separate physical mechanism capable of changing which isotope species exist.

A correct claim states the material

An abundance for water vapor, leaf tissue, seawater or soil nitrate should include where it came from and how it was measured. One familiar percentage cannot automatically be assigned to all of them.

x(ⁱE)=N(ⁱE)/N(all atoms of E); percent abundance=100x; Σxᵢ=1.

1.A.1.23.4 Natural composition versus representative tables versus enriched material

Natural is not synonymous with one fixed worldwide number

Normal natural materials show some real isotopic variability. Even though every oxygen-18 atom is the same nuclide, its fraction among oxygen atoms can differ between precipitation, seawater, minerals and biological tissues.

CIAAW representative values have a scope

The CIAAW Isotopic Compositions 2024 table supplies evaluated normal-material values or intervals. Some ranges reflect variations among samples; some measurements include uncertainty. A table entry is appropriate for approximate teaching but should not replace a measured fraction in a particular unusual sample.

The word standard belongs to a related but different quantity

Standard atomic weight describes the evaluated mean relative atomic mass of normal materials. It depends on isotope masses and their observed fraction distributions. Node 22 derived its weighted formula; here we explain why the fractions vary.

Enrichment changes composition without changing element

A tracer reagent reported as 99 atom % ¹³C has x(¹³C)=0.99, unlike ordinary terrestrial carbon near 1% carbon-13. The element is still carbon Z=6. Its sample mean mass and isotope envelope change, but the definition of 1u remains unchanged.

Commercial and natural samples can differ

Some processed commercial materials have modified isotope compositions. CIAAW marks certain elements with cautions; research reports should provide measured or certified composition instead of assuming every purchased substance is typical natural material.

1.A.1.23.5 Read CIAAW's 2024 ranges: carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine

Always read fraction rather than percent

CIAAW lists ¹³C approximately in the range [0.0096,0.0116], corresponding to 0.96–1.16% of carbon atoms in the evaluated normal materials. A decimal point error would inflate the supposed abundance by tenfold.

Oxygen has three important stable species

For ¹⁶O the table lists [0.99738,0.99776], for ¹⁷O [0.000367,0.000400], and for ¹⁸O [0.00187,0.00222]. A real sample's fractions jointly sum to one; the independent range maxima are not necessarily from the same specimen.

Chlorine makes the fractions visible

The table lists ³⁵Cl [0.755,0.761] and ³⁷Cl [0.239,0.245]. In a two-isotope mixture a greater ³⁵Cl fraction necessarily means a lower ³⁷Cl fraction.

Use ranges as evidence, not a generator of arbitrary extremes

A reader cannot select the highest listed ¹⁶O, ¹⁷O and ¹⁸O fractions and claim they describe one sample. The closure rule and correlated isotope measurements must hold simultaneously.

The values have a dated evaluation context

The cited CIAAW online isotope-composition table incorporates updates through 2024 for selected elements. Actual normal-material evidence evolves. Precise research should cite an appropriate table version and sample data.

1.A.1.23.6 Where isotope inventories came from: from cosmology to stars

The universe has a nuclear history

Present terrestrial isotope compositions emerged from nuclear creation, survival and redistribution across cosmic history. Primordial nucleosynthesis produced much hydrogen and helium and smaller quantities of selected light nuclides, long before Earth or living organisms existed.

Stellar processes produced many heavier nuclides

Nuclear fusion and related stellar reaction pathways produced a large variety of isotopes. Explosive events and neutron capture contributed to heavy isotope inventories. The detailed reactions differ, but their common feature is genuine nuclear transformation rather than chemical sorting.

The Solar System inherited this material

Meteorites, planets and atmospheric reservoirs formed from materials with prior nuclear histories. Different isotopic signatures in extraterrestrial samples can reflect distinct source or subsequent processing; a terrestrial reference ratio must not be treated as exactly universal across space.

Time and nuclear lifetimes modify the inventory

Stable nuclides persist while long-lived radionuclides may decay into daughters over geological time. As a result, today’s isotope abundances are not determined by one event or a single nuclear stability rule.

History and measurement must connect

Nucleosynthesis explains possible isotopic origins, but a specific sample’s current ratio also depends on geological, physical and biological processes. A scientist should not infer an individual star or formation time solely from one familiar ¹³C percentage.

1.A.1.23.7 Primordial isotopes, long-lived radioactivity and the meaning of natural

Primordial is an origin label, not stability

Some nuclides present since early Solar System formation have survived because they are stable or because their half-lives are sufficiently long. Uranium-238 and uranium-235 are radioactive but still naturally present today.

An isotope may have several sources

A naturally occurring nuclide may be primordial, produced continuously through cosmic-ray reactions or formed as a daughter of another nuclide. 'Naturally occurring' does not imply 'stable', and 'natural' does not imply absence of human influence.

Carbon-14 makes the difference intuitive

Carbon-14 is continuously generated by nuclear reactions associated with cosmic rays and moves through the carbon cycle. Its atmospheric pre-weapons-test abundance was around 10⁻¹⁶ relative to carbon according to CIAAW. That rarity scarcely affects natural carbon atomic weight but is crucial in radiocarbon science.

The scale of a source matters

A cosmological abundance is not the same as an isotope fraction in a plant, a water sample or rock. Every ratio belongs to a specified population and time.

A robust exam distinction

Nuclear origin determines how a nuclide was created; nuclear stability determines whether it decays spontaneously; isotopic abundance says what share of the element’s atoms that nuclide represents in a defined material.

1.A.1.23.8 Primordial, cosmogenic and radiogenic labels: three different histories

Primordial inventory

Many stable nuclides and selected long-lived radioactive nuclides were incorporated in Earth's formation material. Their distribution can later be modified by geochemistry and biology.

Cosmogenic formation

Cosmic-ray-related nuclear interactions create certain nuclides, including carbon-14 in the atmosphere. The new radioactive nuclei enter environmental systems where carbon transport and turnover may alter measured concentrations.

Radiogenic daughter growth

Decay of a parent nuclide generates daughter atoms. For example, potassium-40 decay contributes to argon-40 and calcium-40 histories, and uranium/thorium decay is associated with particular lead isotopes. This can change an isotope inventory through a nuclear transformation rather than fractionation.

Radiogenic products can be stable

The daughter may be stable even though its parent is radioactive. Calling an atom radiogenic describes its formation history, not necessarily its own half-life.

Geochronology requires evidence

An abundance of radiogenic argon does not automatically yield rock age without isotope measurements, initial conditions, closed-system assessment and decay constants. Source mixing can mimic or complicate some abundance patterns.

1.A.1.23.9 Natural abundance is not a ranking of nuclear stability

A rare isotope may be stable

Carbon-13 is less abundant than carbon-12 but is also stable. Oxygen-17 is much rarer than oxygen-16 and remains stable. Their abundance differences do not imply that the less abundant isotope is currently decaying.

A small natural fraction can be radioactive

CIAAW representative potassium isotope composition includes ⁴⁰K fraction about 0.000117, or 0.0117% of potassium atoms, in a specified evaluated context. The presence of radioactive ⁴⁰K in nature does not make every potassium atom radioactive.

Half-life does not give the current fraction alone

A nuclide can have a known half-life yet an unknown abundance in a particular sample if its production history, initial inventory and mixing are not supplied.

Mass number A is not a percentage

A=40 means forty nucleons. It does not mean an abundance of 40% or forty atoms per 100 in a potassium sample.

Use data rather than a shortcut

Nuclide stability should come from evaluated nuclear data; natural isotope amounts from measured compositions. A periodic-table average or nuclide label alone is insufficient for either claim.

1.A.1.23.10 Creation versus redistribution: nuclear reactions and ordinary fractionation

One branch changes nuclear identity

Nucleosynthesis, neutron capture or radioactive decay can transform one nuclear species into another. For example, beta-minus decay changes a nucleus’s Z and N while preserving A. Such processes can create or eliminate isotope populations.

The other branch preserves each nucleus

Evaporation, condensation, molecular diffusion, enzyme-catalyzed reactions and mixing generally move existing isotope-bearing atoms between reservoirs. A carbon-13 atom remains carbon-13 even as its molecule changes.

The source-to-product partition

If one pool becomes depleted in a heavy isotope during a process, a linked reservoir or product may be relatively enriched; whole-system isotope counting must balance when no nuclear source or sink is present.

Why biology matters

Photosynthesis, respiration, nitrogen cycling and microbial sulfur processing can affect isotope ratios, often in process-dependent ways. An isotope shift alone is rarely sufficient to identify one pathway without other data.

Pick the governing model

Use material balance for mixing, fractionation factors for partitioning, decay laws for radioactivity and metabolic network models for flux. Applying an isotope half-life to stable carbon-13 photosynthetic fractionation is a category error.

Within a closed system without nuclear reactions: N_heavy,total=Σ N_heavy,reservoir j.

1.A.1.23.11 Equilibrium isotope fractionation: isotope preference between phases

A physical explanation

Heavy and light isotope substitutions slightly change vibrational energies, equilibrium constants and molecular partitioning. Two phases at equilibrium can have unequal heavy/light ratios even though both contain atoms of the same element.

The ratio-based factor

For defined isotope species and materials A and B, α_A/B=R_A/R_B. If α_A/B>1, A has a higher heavy/light ratio than B. α close to 1 indicates a small fractionation, not that the samples have identical absolute isotope contents.

An example from water

Under many equilibrium evaporation and condensation conditions, heavier stable water isotopes are relatively enriched in the liquid phase compared with coexisting vapor. The magnitude depends on temperature and details of the phase equilibrium.

A needed qualification

Open environmental evaporation also involves kinetic effects, humidity and turbulent transport, so one equilibrium α alone cannot reconstruct a lake’s isotope composition.

No transmutation occurs

Water molecules containing ²H or ¹⁸O are partitioned and transported; the nuclei remain those isotopes. Nuclear radioactivity is not responsible for an ordinary water isotope fractionation signature.

Fractionation factor α_A/B=R_A/R_B; R=N_heavy/N_light.

1.A.1.23.12 Kinetic fractionation: diffusion and biochemical rates

Equal chemistry is not exactly equal kinetics

Isotope substitution can change reaction rates, diffusion and transport. When products are removed faster than equilibrium can be established, kinetic isotope effects may alter the heavy/light isotope ratio of the products and residual pool.

Photosynthesis makes a biological example

Differences in carbon isotope discrimination can arise from CO₂ supply and biochemical carboxylation processes. Plant tissue δ¹³C therefore reflects physiology and environment together, not a single universal photosynthesis label.

Water vapor under natural conditions

Evaporation from open water depends on humidity, temperature and vapor mixing. Lighter water molecules can become relatively enriched in vapor, but the magnitude and isotope trajectory require a process-specific model.

Why simplistic shortcuts fail

'Heavy always remains behind' is not a universal law for every isotope system or reaction. Direction and magnitude depend on the process, isotope pair and whether exchange is at equilibrium.

Scientific conclusion discipline

A depleted heavy-isotope ratio may be consistent with one kinetic pathway but is not proof of a unique enzyme or environmental history without supporting measurements.

1.A.1.23.13 Atom fraction versus percent versus ppm and ppb

Name the denominator

If x(¹³C)=0.0107 in a carbon pool, 1.07% of carbon atoms are carbon-13, and 10,700 carbon-isotope atom ppm are carbon-13. It does not mean carbon-13 accounts for that fraction of the sample's mass or all-element atoms.

The conversion formula

Percentage is 100x, parts per million by atom number is 10⁶x, and parts per billion 10⁹x. A very rare nuclide can be meaningful while requiring scientific notation.

Oxygen-17 as a small fraction

NIST's representative oxygen-17 composition 0.00038 corresponds to 0.038% or about 380 ppm among oxygen atoms. Unit and denominator should accompany every value.

Radioactive carbon-14 is vastly rarer

A carbon-14 fraction of order 10⁻¹⁶ is 10⁻¹⁰ ppm of carbon atoms. This is an illustration of scale; actual amounts vary with source, time and anthropogenic effects.

A count check

Fractions are from zero to one, isotope percentages sum to 100% for a complete element mixture, and an omitted isotope species must be acknowledged.

Percentage=100x; ppm=10⁶x; ppb=10⁹x.

1.A.1.23.14 Heavy-to-light isotope ratio versus heavy atom fraction

Similar-looking but distinct numbers

For a two-isotope element, heavy atom fraction x_H=N_H/(N_H+N_L), while heavy/light ratio R=N_H/N_L. Therefore x_H=R/(1+R) and R=x_H/(1−x_H). The distinction is exact, though numbers are close for rare heavy isotopes.

A percentage arithmetic trap

If a mixture contains 1 heavy atom and 99 light atoms, the heavy fraction is 1/100=0.01 but R=1/99≈0.010101. Calling the ratio exactly 0.01 subtly changes the definition.

Reference ratios use an order

For carbon the common ratio is ¹³C/¹²C; nitrogen ¹⁵N/¹⁴N; oxygen ¹⁸O/¹⁶O; hydrogen ²H/¹H. Reversing the numerator changes the value and invalidates naive δ comparison.

A larger-ratio example

If R=0.25, the heavy fraction is 0.25/1.25=0.20 or 20%, not 25%. This is a useful exam problem because the difference is too large to ignore.

Three isotopes require additional information

The ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio alone does not specify ¹⁷O. Full fraction reconstruction requires an additional isotope ratio or justified constraint.

Two-isotope conversion: x_H=R/(1+R), R=x_H/(1−x_H).

1.A.1.23.15 Three-isotope closure: the oxygen example

Two independent fractions remain after normalization

For x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1, two independent measurements determine the third if all three isotopes are included. One ratio by itself is generally insufficient.

An illustrative complete composition

A hypothetical oxygen sample with x₁₆=0.99750, x₁₇=0.00040 and x₁₈=0.00210 has total one. Its ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio is 0.00210/0.99750≈0.00210526 and ¹⁷O/¹⁶O≈0.00040100.

The range-endpoint problem

CIAAW reports isotope ranges from evaluated normal materials; choosing the maximum ¹⁶O and maximum ¹⁸O independently might not be physically compatible with the fraction of ¹⁷O in a real sample.

Molecular consequences

Even water contains isotope choices for hydrogen and oxygen. Natural isotopologue frequencies can depend on multiple element-isotope fractions, correlations and exchange, not one universal isotope percentage.

The correct average

Given isotope-specific neutral atomic masses m_i and complete atom fractions x_i, mean m̄=Σx_i m_i. Insufficient composition leaves a precise sample mean underdetermined.

x₁₆+x₁₇+x₁₈=1; m̄(O)=x₁₆m₁₆+x₁₇m₁₇+x₁₈m₁₈.

1.A.1.23.16 Delta δ notation: turning subtle ratios into readable differences

Small ratios need a reference convention

Isotope ratio measurements may differ by a few parts per thousand across natural samples. Delta notation compares the sample ratio with a reference ratio and expresses their proportional difference in per mille.

Calculate it carefully

δ=(R_sample/R_ref−1)×1000‰. A ratio 0.98 of reference yields −20‰, meaning the sample heavy/light isotope ratio is 2% lower than the reference, not that the sample has negative isotope atoms.

A numeric illustration

If R_sample/R_ref=1.012, δ=+12‰. If the sample ratio is identical to the reference, δ=0‰. The sign depends on whether the sample is relatively enriched or depleted in the heavy isotope compared with the chosen standard.

δ is not an isotope atom percentage

To find atom fraction from δ, the reference absolute ratio must be known and other isotope species considered. A δ¹³C of −25‰ does not mean x(¹³C)=−0.025 or 25% of the sample is carbon-13.

The reference label cannot be omitted in research

VPDB for carbon, VSMOW for water hydrogen/oxygen and atmospheric N₂ conventions for nitrogen are common examples. Accurate comparison requires calibration, laboratory quality control and matched reporting scales.

δ(‰)=[R_sample/R_ref−1]×1000; R_sample=R_ref(1+δ/1000).

1.A.1.23.17 Natural isotope ratios and mixing are two different mathematical views

The physical mixing rule starts with counts

When two sources are mixed, the number of heavy and light isotope atoms adds separately. If sources have different elemental concentrations, their contribution weights depend on how much of the element each supplies, not merely the volume of water or material mixed.

For a common elemental pool use atom fractions

If source A supplies 30% of the carbon atoms and B supplies 70%, the mixed carbon-13 atom fraction is x_mix=0.30x_A+0.70x_B, assuming no post-mixing fractionation or nuclear transformation. The coefficients are contributions to carbon atoms.

Ratios are nonlinear under direct mixing

R=x/(1−x) for two isotopes. An arithmetic average of R values is generally not exact when one needs the total-count mixture. For small variations, approximate delta mixing may be used under carefully stated assumptions, but exact modeling begins with isotope counts.

A physical example

A mixture of two waters may have different amounts of dissolved carbon. Mixing them 50:50 by volume does not guarantee equal carbon-atom contributions if dissolved inorganic carbon concentrations differ.

What is required for source apportionment

At least source compositions, a suitable mixing model and independent constraints are needed. One intermediate δ measurement rarely identifies unique proportions among three unknown sources.

If w is fraction of the element atoms from source A: x_mix=w x_A+(1−w)x_B.
