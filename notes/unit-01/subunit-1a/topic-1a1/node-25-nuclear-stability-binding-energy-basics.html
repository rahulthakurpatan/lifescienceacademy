1.A.1.25.1 The missing mass puzzle: why a helium atom weighs less than its separated ingredients

A measurement that changes everything

Imagine a young scientist assembling the mass of one helium-4 atom on paper. Its nucleus contains two protons and two neutrons, and the neutral atom also has two electrons. By adding the masses of the corresponding separated particles, the scientist predicts a value greater than the measured mass of an ordinary neutral helium-4 atom. The scale has not malfunctioned: the difference contains information about nuclear binding.

The same atoms but two physical states

A bound nucleus and a collection of widely separated free nucleons are different energy states. The bound system has less total rest mass because energy would have to be supplied to separate it into its constituents. The missing mass corresponds to this energy difference through E=mc².

The surprising numerical result

With compatible neutral-atom masses, the helium-4 mass defect is about 0.0303766 u. Multiplying by about 931.494 MeV/u gives approximately 28.30 MeV of nuclear binding energy, or 7.07 MeV per nucleon.

A life-science student should ask one more question

If helium-4 is tightly bound, does that alone prove that all other nuclei are unstable? No. Nuclear stability requires studying energetically allowed transformations, conservation laws and decay probabilities. A large binding energy is part of the story, not a complete verdict about radioactive half-life.

The complete journey

We begin with the discovery of the nucleus and neutron, uncover residual strong nuclear binding and electrostatic repulsion, derive a scientifically consistent mass defect equation, interpret the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, then examine the band of nuclear stability, beta decay, shell structure, kinetic barriers and biological applications.

Learning objectives: Distinguish chemical and nuclear binding; derive mass defect from atomic or nuclear masses; calculate B and B/A with reliable units; explain neutron–proton balance, pairing and shell effects; distinguish binding energy from stability, decay Q-value, half-life, activity and dose; connect nuclear properties to biological tracers and nuclear medicine without unsafe assumptions.

1.A.1.25.2 A chronology of discovery: Rutherford to modern nuclear structure

Rutherford locates the compact nucleus

The gold-foil scattering experiment revealed that an atom contains a tiny dense positively charged nucleus rather than uniformly distributed positive matter. It was an essential step toward asking what holds multiple positive nuclear charges together.

The proton clarifies nuclear charge

Atomic number Z became linked to the nuclear positive charge and chemical identity. Yet for many measured atomic masses, the nucleus could not be explained solely by Z positively charged protons and surrounding electrons. Nuclear mass and charge needed a more complete particle inventory.

Chadwick's neutron breakthrough

James Chadwick's 1932 discovery of the neutron provided the missing neutral nucleon. It helped explain isotope families with the same proton count but different neutron counts. A=Z+N became a precise way to organize nuclides.

Einstein's mass–energy principle makes the missing mass meaningful

Mass-energy equivalence explained why the bound system's measured mass could be smaller than the sum of separated ingredients. Nuclear binding energy could be quantitatively inferred from precise mass measurements rather than merely imagined as an invisible force.

From mass measurements to nuclear models

Subsequent nuclear measurements revealed trends of binding energy per nucleon, stability along particular neutron–proton combinations, shell closures and radioactive decay. Liquid-drop and shell-model pictures capture complementary aspects; neither alone predicts every nuclide's behavior perfectly.

Why the chronology matters educationally

Each discovery resolved an earlier puzzle: the nucleus explained scattering, the neutron explained isotope masses, mass defect measured nuclear cohesion, and nuclide charts revealed why some neutron–proton combinations persist while others change.

1.A.1.25.3 Why does a nucleus need a binding interaction?

The electric repulsion puzzle

Every proton has positive electric charge. The Coulomb force between any two protons is repulsive, so simply placing multiple protons close together would not explain how the nucleus remains compact.

An attractive nuclear interaction must be present

A residual interaction arising from the strong interaction binds nucleons at nuclear distances. It is powerful over short ranges, with properties different from the long-range electromagnetic interaction. Neutrons participate in the attraction without adding proton–proton electrostatic repulsion.

Protons and neutrons are not identical charges

Protons and neutrons have comparable but slightly different masses, and neutrons are electrically neutral. Strong-interaction physics, quantum states and proton–neutron correlations determine how particular nuclei behave.

The binding is not a chemical bond

Chemical bonding mainly involves electrons and electromagnetic energy scales of order eV. Nuclear binding typically involves millions of eV, expressed conveniently as MeV. A change in oxidation state or protein folding cannot be treated as nuclear fission, and ordinary metabolism does not change nuclide identity.

A balanced conceptual statement

The nucleus is stabilized by residual strong interaction in competition with Coulomb repulsion and quantum effects. Gravity is negligible for ordinary nuclei. The precise binding of a nuclide depends on its proton and neutron numbers and their quantum arrangement.

1.A.1.25.4 Strong interaction versus the residual nuclear force

Quarks, gluons and nucleons

Protons and neutrons are composite particles containing quarks bound by the fundamental strong interaction mediated by gluons. The force binding separate nucleons in the nucleus is commonly described as the residual strong interaction, not merely the same color force inside one proton.

Short-range attraction and a repulsive core

At typical internucleon separations, nuclear interactions can provide strong attraction; their detailed short-distance behavior includes repulsive components. Textbook language like 'the strong force always attracts' is therefore an oversimplification.

Saturation helps explain the nuclear energy scale

Because nuclear forces are short range, a nucleon interacts most strongly with nearby nucleons rather than equally with all other nucleons. This contributes to the approximate saturation of nuclear density and why binding energy generally grows roughly with nucleon count for many nuclei.

Why neutrons matter

Adding neutrons can increase binding by contributing strong-interaction relationships without adding electrostatic charge. But too many neutrons can also make a nucleus energetically unfavorable; there is no universal rule that extra neutrons always stabilize it.

Quantum nuclear physics is more complex than marbles

Nucleons occupy quantum states; shell effects, pairing and interactions influence binding. Our diagrams are conceptual aids, not literal small balls held by visible springs.

1.A.1.25.5 The size of the nuclear world: femtometres and density

A nucleus is much smaller than its atom

A typical nuclear radius is on the order of femtometres (1 fm=10⁻¹⁵m), whereas the full atom extends over roughly ångström scales near 10⁻¹⁰m. Chemical electron clouds are thus vastly more extended than the nucleus.

A common radius scaling

For a roughly spherical nucleus, a useful empirical estimate is R≈r₀A^(1/3), where r₀ is about 1.2fm. This captures approximate nuclear size scaling rather than the exact shape of each nuclide.

Why the cube root appears

If nuclear matter has roughly similar density among many nuclei, volume is proportional to nucleon number A. A sphere's volume scales with R³, so R should scale like A^(1/3). This is a geometric relation, not a proof that all nuclei are perfect uniform spheres.

A comparison calculation

For A=64, A^(1/3)=4, giving R≈1.2×4=4.8fm. For A=8, A^(1/3)=2, giving R≈2.4fm. Doubling the radius would increase a sphere's volume by factor eight.

What this does and does not imply

Nuclear density is high relative to ordinary matter, but this does not make every heavy nucleus stable. Stability depends on competing interactions and quantum structure, not density alone.

R ≈ r₀A^(1/3), with r₀≈1.2 fm and 1 fm=10⁻¹⁵m.

1.A.1.25.6 From separated nucleons to a bound nucleus: define the mass defect

The reference state must be explicit

Consider Z free protons and N free neutrons at rest and widely separated. Let their rest masses be m_p and m_n. Their total free mass is Zm_p+Nm_n. After forming one bound nucleus, its mass is M_nucleus.

The mass difference

Define the positive nuclear mass defect Δm=Zm_p+Nm_n−M_nucleus for a bound nuclide relative to separated nucleons. Its binding energy is B=Δmc², the energy required to disassemble the nucleus into separated nucleons in the idealized nuclear model.

Why the bound object is lighter

Forming a more tightly bound state releases energy to the surroundings. When energy leaves the system, the final bound nucleus has less rest energy and therefore less rest mass than the corresponding separated nucleons.

The sign convention protects understanding

With B defined as positive energy required for separation, a bound nucleus has Δm>0. If a problem uses a different sign convention for mass defect or reaction Q, carefully translate rather than treating a negative sign as a physically negative binding energy.

The equation has assumptions

The constituent and bound masses must refer to compatible particles and states. Do not mix the mass of a bare nucleus with an atom containing electrons without accounting for electronic contributions.

Nuclear-mass form: Δm=Z m_p+N m_n−M_nucleus;   B=Δmc².

1.A.1.25.7 Atomic mass data versus nuclear mass data: the electron bookkeeping rule

What a typical isotope mass table reports

NIST tables commonly list the mass of a neutral ground-state atom, including its electrons. Nuclear binding energy formulas using free proton masses require bare nuclear masses, so blindly inserting a neutral atom mass without adjustment causes an electron mismatch.

A useful near-cancellation

For neutral atoms, replace Z separated protons plus Z electrons with Z neutral hydrogen atoms. The nuclear mass defect can then be approximated by Δm≈Z m(¹H atom)+N m_n−M(⁽ᴬ⁾X neutral atom).

Why this works extremely well

The Z electrons on the parent atom and on the separated hydrogen atoms largely cancel. Small electronic binding-energy differences remain and matter for high-precision nuclear metrology, but are usually negligible in introductory MeV-scale calculations.

One formula cannot serve incompatible tables

Use the proton formula with nuclear masses; use the hydrogen-atom formula with neutral atomic masses. If an excited nuclear state is specified, account for its additional excitation energy. Do not mix parent atomic mass with proton mass and ignore the electrons.

The hydrogen-1 atom is not a proton

Neutral hydrogen includes one electron and its atomic binding contribution. Its mass near 1.007825032u is larger than the free proton's mass near 1.007276467u; choosing the wrong value would introduce about an electron mass per proton.

1.A.1.25.8 Convert u into MeV and joules without losing c²

The meaning of E=mc²

One atomic mass unit corresponds to a rest-energy equivalent near 931.494 MeV. If a mass defect is expressed as a number in u, multiply by 931.494 MeV/u to obtain binding energy. It is a conversion of mass-energy, not a claim that the mass unit is itself energy.

Separate MeV from MeV/c²

A mass may be stated as an energy-equivalent mass in MeV/c², while energy is stated in MeV. Writing 1u≈931.494MeV is shorthand for the energy equivalent 1u×c²≈931.494MeV; strict dimensional notation is important.

A quick calculation

If Δm=0.0100u, B≈0.0100×931.494=9.31494MeV. Converting energy to joules uses 1eV=1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹J exactly, so 1MeV=1.602176634×10⁻¹³J.

Orders of magnitude

Typical molecular chemical bonds are measured in electronvolts per bond, while nuclear binding involves MeV per nucleon. This energy scale gap helps explain why ordinary biological reactions cannot be understood as nuclear changes.

Significant figures

When input defect is only 0.0100u, report about 9.31MeV according to input precision; do not imply that the last digits of a more precise constant increase measurement accuracy.

B(MeV)=Δm(u)×931.49410372;   E(J)=E(MeV)×1.602176634×10⁻¹³.

1.A.1.25.9 The full helium-4 calculation: a nucleus that explains itself

Identify nuclide composition

Helium-4 has Z=2 protons, N=2 neutrons and A=4 nucleons. Use the neutral atomic mass of helium-4, approximately 4.00260325413u, with two neutral hydrogen-1 atomic masses and two neutron masses.

Write the correct atom-mass equation

Δm≈2(1.00782503223)+2(1.00866491595)−4.00260325413≈0.03037664223u. The separated hydrogen and neutron reference masses exceed the bound helium atom mass.

Convert the defect to binding energy

B≈0.03037664223×931.49410372≈28.29566MeV. Divide by four nucleons to get B/A≈7.07392MeV per nucleon. The 28.30 MeV is the total binding, not the energy of one specific emitted gamma photon.

The subtle distinction from chemistry

The calculation compares an atomic isotope to specified separated constituents and neglects tiny electron binding corrections in an introductory formulation. It does not mean ordinary helium atoms spontaneously release 28MeV when they form a chemical bond.

An interpretation checkpoint

Helium-4 is strongly bound compared with its separated nucleons. Its stability against possible nuclear transformations is evaluated using other final states and allowed decays, not just by noticing that B is positive.

⁴He: Δm≈0.03037664223u; B≈28.29566MeV; B/A≈7.07392MeV.
Conceptual mass deficit: separated nucleons have more rest mass than the helium-4 bound nucleus.

1.A.1.25.10 The deuteron: a two-nucleon laboratory of nuclear binding

Hydrogen-2 changes the nuclear inventory

Deuterium ²H has one proton and one neutron. Its nucleus is called the deuteron, and its neutral atom is a hydrogen isotope with one electron.

A neutral-atom calculation

Use m(¹H)=1.00782503223u, m_n=1.00866491595u and neutral ²H atomic mass≈2.01410177812u. Mass defect≈1.00782503223+1.00866491595−2.01410177812≈0.00238817006u.

Energy and per-nucleon value

B≈0.00238817006×931.49410372≈2.22457MeV total. Dividing by two gives B/A≈1.11228MeV per nucleon. This is much lower than helium-4's 7.07MeV per nucleon, illustrating the strong increase in binding among selected light nuclei.

A process distinction

Stable deuterium is not radioactive and does not decay spontaneously merely because its B/A is lower than helium-4. One must consider whether any particular transformation is allowed and its reaction context.

Biological isotope use

Deuterium-labelled water and nutrients appear in stable-isotope biological studies. Their label can be followed through normal chemistry, but binding energy is a nuclear property and is not the mechanism of ordinary deuterium exchange.

²H: Δm≈0.00238817006u; B≈2.22457MeV; B/A≈1.11228MeV.

1.A.1.25.11 Carbon-12: exact reference mass, nonzero nuclear binding

A common conceptual trap

Carbon-12 has an exact neutral-atom mass of 12u by definition. That does not mean its nuclear binding energy is zero or that the sum of its six free protons and six free neutrons equals exactly 12u.

The correct neutral-atom formula

For Z=6 and N=6, Δm≈6m(¹H)+6m_n−12u. Using the constants above gives Δm≈0.09893968908u. The carbon-12 atom's 12u reference mass is fully compatible with a positive mass defect.

Calculate binding

B≈0.09893968908×931.49410372≈92.16174MeV total. Per nucleon B/A≈7.68014MeV. The exact 12u mass fixes the scale, while the nuclear binding value follows from independently measured constituent masses.

Stable carbon-13 is a different nucleus

Carbon-13 has one more neutron and is also stable, with different total binding and mass. The periodic table's average carbon atomic weight describes isotope proportions, not one nucleus's binding energy.

Biological relevance with a boundary

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 participate in ordinary carbon biochemistry because their electrons support carbon chemical bonding. Slight isotope effects can appear in reaction rates, but neither isotope changes nuclear composition during ordinary metabolism.

¹²C: B≈92.16174MeV; B/A≈7.68014MeV.

1.A.1.25.12 Iron-56: follow the numbers toward the broad binding maximum

An important heavy benchmark

Iron-56 has Z=26 protons, N=30 neutrons, A=56. Its neutral atomic mass is approximately 55.93493633u. The binding calculation uses 26 neutral hydrogen-1 masses and 30 neutron masses against this atom mass.

Compute the mass defect

Δm≈26(1.00782503223)+30(1.00866491595)−55.93493633≈0.52846198648u. This is substantially larger total defect than helium-4 because it contains many more nucleons.

Total energy and energy per nucleon

B≈492.25922MeV, while B/A≈492.25922/56≈8.79034MeV per nucleon. The per-nucleon figure, rather than total B alone, makes comparisons across nuclear size meaningful.

Iron is near, not uniquely the highest point

The empirical B/A curve has a broad maximum around iron- and nickel-region nuclei. Some nuclei, including nickel-62, have slightly greater B/A than iron-56. Saying iron-56 is exactly the single most tightly bound nuclide is therefore an inaccurate oversimplification.

The survival question is still separate

A high binding energy per nucleon is not equivalent to a complete decay-lifetime prediction. To decide whether a nuclide can transform, evaluate the energy and selection rules of specific channels.

⁵⁶Fe: Δm≈0.52846198648u; B≈492.25922MeV; B/A≈8.79034MeV.

1.A.1.25.13 What binding energy per nucleon means—and what it does not

Why divide by A?

Total B often grows with the number of nucleons simply because bigger nuclei contain more nucleons. Dividing by A gives an average energy per constituent nucleon, useful for comparing how strongly different-size nuclei are bound on average.

A comparison table

The deuteron is around 1.11MeV per nucleon; helium-4 around 7.07; carbon-12 around 7.68; oxygen-16 around 7.98; iron-56 around 8.79. These values come from a consistent approximate atomic-mass formula.

One nucleon need not have equal individual removal energy

B/A is an average, not the exact energy to remove any chosen neutron or proton. Separation energies depend on differences between neighboring nuclide masses and may differ from B/A because of shell, pairing and other structure.

Don't use B/A as a radioactive half-life calculator

A nucleus can have high B/A and still have an energetically allowed decay; some decay modes can be extremely slow because of selection rules or quantum barriers. A nuclide's half-life needs decay probability information.

Energy scales and biology

MeV per nucleon is vastly larger than ordinary molecular bond energy. Biological ionization by radiation is about interactions of emitted particles or photons with molecules, not about direct release of all nuclear B during routine cellular reactions.

1.A.1.25.14 The famous binding-energy-per-nucleon curve

Follow the rise among light nuclei

Very light nuclei often gain binding per nucleon as nucleons form more favorable interactions. Helium-4 has particularly high binding relative to small mass numbers, reflecting nuclear structure and pairing.

The broad maximum

The B/A curve rises to a plateau and maximum in the iron/nickel region, around A near 50–65. Precise rankings require actual evaluated nuclear masses; nickel-62 has a slightly higher B/A than iron-56.

Then comes a gradual decrease

For much heavier nuclei, the increasing Coulomb repulsion among many protons reduces B/A relative to the peak region, even though total binding remains large. Heavy nuclei may have energetically favorable fission possibilities under suitable conditions.

Fusion and fission are about differences in total energy

Processes that move nuclear configurations toward more tightly bound final products can release energy if total final rest mass is lower than initial mass, respecting conservation laws. The simple curve suggests trends, not the guaranteed outcome, reaction rate or practicality of any particular process.

A biological scientist's interpretation

The curve explains why some nuclides release nuclear energy by transformation; it does not imply that ordinary respiration, protein synthesis or ATP hydrolysis are miniature nuclear reactions.

1.A.1.25.15 The high-binding peak is not a single magical stability verdict

Different definitions of 'stable'

A positive nuclear binding energy means separated free nucleons require added energy to recreate from that bound nucleus. Stability against beta decay means no energetically allowed accessible beta transformation from that state, or none observed within relevant timescales. They are distinct statements.

A nucleus may have channels other than full nucleon separation

Even a strongly bound nucleus could transform into a different nucleus plus emitted particles if the final state's total energy is lower and conservation laws allow the process. Alpha decay, beta decay and other channels compare specific initial and final states.

Kinetics can block an energetically favored change

Quantum tunneling probabilities and selection rules can make allowed decays extremely slow. A nucleus may have a high binding energy per nucleon and a finite radioactive half-life extending beyond human observational times.

Stable with respect to which process and over which timescale?

Physicists use stable in specific senses, including stable against spontaneous observed radioactive modes. Some nuclides considered stable historically were later found to undergo extremely rare decay modes, requiring precise nuclear measurements.

Better wording

‘Iron-region nuclides are very tightly bound on average’ is defensible; ‘the highest B/A isotope must always have the longest half-life and cannot decay’ is not.

1.A.1.25.16 The valley of stability: a map of proton and neutron combinations

Plot nuclides using Z and N

Instead of reading a periodic table, imagine a map with proton number Z on one axis and neutron number N on the other. Stable and very long-lived nuclei occupy a curved band of energetically favorable nuclide combinations, sometimes called the valley of stability.

For light stable nuclei

Neutron and proton numbers are often similar: helium-4 has 2 and 2; carbon-12 has 6 and 6; oxygen-16 has 8 and 8. The pattern is a useful observation, not a rule that all N=Z nuclides are stable.

For heavier stable nuclei

As Z increases, proton-proton Coulomb repulsion becomes more consequential. Heavier stable nuclei generally contain more neutrons than protons; iron-56 has N=30,Z=26, and lead-208 has N=126,Z=82.

Outside the band

A neutron-rich nuclide may lower its energy through beta-minus decay while a proton-rich one may undergo positron emission or electron capture, provided the specified channel is energetically accessible and permitted. The full nuclide chart matters, not just one ratio.

What is not predicted by a stability map alone

The band gives broad tendencies; detailed nuclear mass surfaces, quantum states and transition probabilities determine individual lifetimes. It is not safe to calculate radioactivity by simply inspecting a neutron/proton ratio.

1.A.1.25.17 Neutron-to-proton ratio: a trend, not a universal stability equation

Why additional neutrons can help

Protons repel one another electrically. Neutrons contribute to residual strong interactions but do not add positive charge, so heavier bound nuclei frequently need neutron excess.

An opposing cost

An extreme excess of neutrons also carries a nuclear energy penalty from quantum occupations and neutron-proton imbalance. The stable region reflects competition among attraction, Coulomb energy, asymmetry and pairing.

Three illustrative ratios

Carbon-12 has N/Z=6/6=1.00. Iron-56 has 30/26≈1.154. Lead-208 has 126/82≈1.537. The increasing ratio is a trend among selected stable species, not an exact mass-number cutoff.

What a high ratio can suggest

For a specific nuclide, a strongly neutron-rich composition may suggest a beta-minus pathway to a lower-energy isobar. The hypothesis must be tested with mass differences and nuclear selection rules.

A critical exam distinction

N/Z and nuclear binding energy are distinct quantities. No single N/Z value gives total binding energy, gamma transition energies or radioactive half-life.

N/Z = (A−Z)/Z, for Z>0; it is descriptive, not a complete decay law.

1.A.1.25.18 Pairing: the even-even preference and odd-odd exceptions

Quantum correlations favor pairs in many nuclei

Nucleons are fermions occupying nuclear quantum states. Pairing correlations often give additional binding when protons can pair with protons and neutrons with neutrons, favoring even-even configurations.

Three classes

Even-even nuclei have even Z and N. Odd-A nuclei have one odd count. Odd-odd nuclei have odd Z and N. Different classes display observable shifts in nearby nuclear masses and beta stability patterns.

Why even A is not enough

An even mass number can correspond to even-even or odd-odd. Carbon-12 (Z6,N6) is even-even, while nitrogen-14 (Z7,N7) is odd-odd, despite both having even A.

Stable odd-odd examples

Deuterium-2, lithium-6, boron-10 and nitrogen-14 are classic stable odd-odd examples. Pairing is a trend rather than a prohibition against every odd-odd nucleus.

The broader explanation

Pairing interacts with shell closures, proton-neutron asymmetry and Coulomb repulsion. The existence of stable or long-lived nuclides requires checking all relevant energetic channels.

1.A.1.25.19 Closed shells and magic numbers in nuclear structure

A nuclear shell model

Protons and neutrons occupy quantum nuclear levels. Shell closures at certain numbers can produce increased resistance to nucleon removal and distinctive nuclear excitation patterns.

Common major magic numbers

2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 and 126 are widely cited shell closures in relevant nuclear regimes, including effects of strong spin–orbit coupling. These are structural observations, not a magic formula for every nuclear property.

Doubly magic examples

Helium-4 has Z2,N2; oxygen-16 Z8,N8; calcium-40 Z20,N20; lead-208 Z82,N126. Each illustrates a configuration with closed shells in the familiar shell-model picture.

Why this matters to binding calculations

Binding-energy-per-nucleon curves show broad trends, while shell effects cause local deviations and jumps in separation energies. Two neighboring nuclei can therefore have surprising relative stability.

No absolute invulnerability

A magic number can enhance binding or inhibit transitions; it cannot replace checking nuclear mass differences and possible decay modes.

Major shell closures often discussed: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126.

1.A.1.25.20 The liquid-drop model and semi-empirical mass formula

A helpful coarse-grained picture

Treat the nucleus as an approximately saturated nuclear fluid with bulk attraction, surface cost, Coulomb repulsion, neutron-proton asymmetry and a pairing correction. This model explains large-scale patterns in nuclear binding.

Volume versus surface

The attractive volume term approximately scales with A, while the surface term subtracts binding because nucleons near the boundary have fewer near neighbors. This resembles, but is not identical to, behavior of ordinary macroscopic liquids.

Coulomb contribution

A term proportional roughly to Z(Z−1)/A^(1/3) penalizes mutual positive charge as nuclei grow. Its growth is one reason heavy stable nuclei tend to be neutron-rich.

Asymmetry and pairing

A term penalizes large N−Z imbalance in a many-nucleon system. Pairing adds a correction depending on odd-even proton and neutron composition.

Know model limitations

Different semi-empirical formula versions fit different coefficients. The model captures systematics but not precise shell effects, excitation spectra, transition rules or decay half-lives.

SEMF concept: B≈aᵥA−aₛA^(2/3)−a꜀Z(Z−1)/A^(1/3)−aₐ(A−2Z)²/A+δ_pair.

1.A.1.25.21 Beta stability: compare specific parent and daughter isobars

At fixed A, changing Z changes the nuclide

An isobaric family contains species with equal mass number but different proton count. Their different atomic masses represent different nuclear energies, and beta processes can move among them.

Beta-minus tendency

When allowed, a neutron-rich nucleus may convert a neutron into a proton with emission of an electron and electron antineutrino: Z increases by one, N decreases by one, A stays fixed.

Positron and electron-capture tendency

Some proton-rich nuclides may transform a proton into a neutron through beta-plus emission or electron capture, decreasing Z by one at fixed A.

Atomic mass Q-values

For neutral atomic mass tables, Q_beta_minus≈(M_parent−M_daughter)c². For beta-plus, Q≈(M_parent−M_daughter−2m_e)c². Electron capture uses a different electron accounting; the available energy is close to the atomic mass difference before atomic binding and excitation corrections.

The major biological bridge

Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope used in tracer and dating contexts, unlike stable carbon-13. The chemical carbon skeleton behaves similarly in many reactions, while nuclear beta decay is a separate physical event.

Never use Q as a half-life

Positive Q means energy is available, not that decay occurs within seconds. The weak nuclear transition probability, nuclear states and selection rules influence its rate.

Neutral atomic masses: Q(β−)≈[M_parent−M_daughter]c²; Q(β+)≈[M_parent−M_daughter−2mₑ]c².

1.A.1.25.22 Alpha decay: tightly bound helium-4 and quantum tunneling

Alpha is helium-4 nuclear matter

An alpha particle contains two protons and two neutrons. Its comparatively high binding energy for a light nucleus makes it a favorable cluster product in certain heavy nuclear decays.

Conservation

An alpha-emitting parent decreases Z by 2 and A by 4. The daughter plus alpha must preserve total nuclear charge and nucleon number.

A positive Q is not sufficient for an immediate emission

The outgoing alpha faces an electrostatic Coulomb barrier. Quantum tunneling allows some emission even when classical barrier crossing would be forbidden at the given energy.

Very different lifetimes

Barrier width and alpha energy, along with nuclear structure, can alter tunneling probabilities enormously. A parent nucleus can be strongly bound and still have an energetically allowed alpha decay with a long half-life.

Model-based interpretation

A broad B/A trend suggests possible favorability for heavy-nucleus transformations; specific parent/daughter masses and tunneling probabilities determine the actual channel and rate.

1.A.1.25.23 Thermodynamics versus kinetics: an essential nuclear distinction

Energetically favorable does not mean fast

If a specific reaction has positive Q, the final rest-mass configuration is energetically lower. The transition can still be very slow when barriers, weak coupling or quantum selection rules suppress it.

Coulomb barriers

Alpha particles and charged nuclear reactants face electrostatic repulsion. Tunneling probability helps explain alpha decay and some low-energy fusion behavior.

Weak interaction and beta decay

Beta processes require weak-interaction transitions. Their decay rates depend on nuclear matrix elements, allowedness, available energy and phase space, not simply the ratio B/A.

Gamma-ray transitions

Excited nuclear states can emit photons, but transition probabilities depend on angular momentum, parity and electromagnetic multipolarity. Some excited states can therefore be metastable.

A conceptual analogy with a limit

A ball separated from a lower valley by a hill illustrates energy barriers, but quantum tunneling has no exact classical rolling-ball mechanism. Use the analogy only for the distinction between energy permission and transition likelihood.

1.A.1.25.24 Nucleon separation energies versus average binding per nucleon

Total B is not one-nucleon removal energy

B is energy required to separate a nuclide into all free nucleons, while S_n is energy required to remove one neutron and leave a particular neighboring residual nucleus.

The neutron equation

S_n=[M(Z,N−1)+m_n−M(Z,N)]c² with compatible nuclear masses, or appropriate atomic masses where electrons cancel. A low S_n indicates the final neutron is comparatively weakly bound under that channel.

Proton removal

The analogous proton separation energy requires a daughter with Z−1 and a free proton and consistent electrons. Since charge changes, atomic mass bookkeeping must be especially careful.

Shell effects

Neutron separation energies often reveal discontinuities around shell closures and pairing patterns. A single B/A average can conceal those detailed structural features.

Practical question recognition

If the prompt says 'binding energy per nucleon', divide B by A. If it asks 'remove one neutron', compare neighboring isotope masses. Do not substitute one formula for the other.

Sₙ=[M(Z,N−1)+mₙ−M(Z,N)]c².

1.A.1.25.25 Fission energy: moving toward more strongly bound products

The mass difference is the source of energy

Some heavy nuclei can split into lighter nuclide products with greater total binding. The combined final rest mass can be lower, with energy released as motion, radiation and excited states.

Use a specified complete reaction

A fission Q-value depends on the exact products, including emitted neutrons and nuclear excitation. The smooth B/A curve cannot by itself calculate a particular event's energy.

A barrier can delay spontaneous fission

Nuclear deformation and potential barriers influence spontaneous fission rates. Heavy, bound nuclei are not compelled to split at once simply because a lower-energy fragment configuration exists.

Conservation still applies

Across a fully written nuclear reaction, total nucleon count and electric charge must balance. Any emitted particles must be included.

Scope for life sciences

Fission is a nuclear process distinct from metabolism. This section explains energy reasoning without instructions on operating, acquiring or assembling nuclear materials.

1.A.1.25.26 Fusion energy: light nuclei and the Coulomb barrier

In some light-nucleus reactions, total binding increases

The binding curve suggests that combining suitable light nuclides can produce a more tightly bound final configuration and release energy. The exact Q-value is determined by reactant and product nuclear masses.

Favorable Q is not enough for fusion to happen frequently

Positively charged nuclei repel; to reach the short-range nuclear interaction region they must overcome or tunnel through a Coulomb barrier. The rate depends on temperature, quantum physics and the particular reaction.

Stellar context

Nuclear reactions in stars form heavier nuclei through networks of individual steps with particles and radiation. One binding comparison is not a complete stellar reaction chain.

No biochemical confusion

ATP hydrolysis and cellular respiration release chemical free energy rather than transforming nuclear nuclides. A large nuclear energy scale does not imply normal enzyme activity triggers fusion.

Interpret the curve narrowly

Moving up in B/A may motivate a hypothesis about favorable energy; use actual total masses and conservation laws to test a specific reaction.

1.A.1.25.27 The nuclear Q-value: a reaction-specific mass comparison

State every participant

For a specified nuclear reaction, Q=[Σm_initial−Σm_final]c² using compatible species and energy states. If initial total rest mass is larger, positive Q indicates net energy released in the fully accounted reaction.

Positive and negative Q

Positive Q is exoergic. Negative Q indicates an endoergic reaction requiring energy input, with laboratory collision thresholds potentially larger than |Q| due to momentum constraints.

Not the same as the binding energy of one nucleus

A nucleus's B is defined for full separation into free nucleons. A decay Q compares one specified initial state with one specified final particle set.

Neutral atoms may simplify bookkeeping

When atomic electrons cancel on both sides, neutral atomic masses are convenient. For beta-plus and electron-capture processes, electron mass and electron binding need explicit attention.

Probability remains independent

Q>0 does not alone establish a decay branching ratio or half-life. Angular momentum, lepton number, charge, baryon number and applicable selection rules constrain transitions.

Q=[Σm_initial−Σm_final]c²; Q>0 is energetic permission, not a rate law.

1.A.1.25.28 Nuclear excited states, gamma emissions and metastable isomers

Energy states beyond the ground state

A nucleus can occupy an excited quantum state with the same Z and A but additional internal energy. Excited-state nuclear mass is greater than ground-state mass by approximately E_exc/c².

Gamma transitions

A transition to a lower nuclear energy level may emit a gamma photon. Recoil also carries energy, so photon energy can be slightly below the simple level difference.

Some transitions are slow

Nuclear spin and parity restrictions can suppress transitions, creating metastable isomers. An m suffix indicates an isomeric nuclear state, not a new chemical element or neutron count.

Technetium-99m is a life-science connection

Technetium-99m is used extensively in nuclear medicine imaging and has an approximately six-hour physical half-life. Its detectable emissions and radiochemical behavior are chosen and managed through clinical and radiation-safety protocols.

Nuance of gamma versus beta or alpha

Gamma transitions usually retain Z and A, while alpha or beta transformations change nuclear composition. Real decay schemes may involve daughter excitation and several successive emissions.

1.A.1.25.29 What nuclear stability means in experimentally testable terms

Stable against separation is not stable against all decays

A positive nuclear binding energy shows that complete separation into free nucleons requires energy. A strongly bound nucleus may still be unstable toward a specific beta, alpha or other transition.

The nuclear energy landscape matters

Different daughters and products provide different possible final energies. A positive decay Q is necessary for spontaneous energy-releasing transformation but may not be sufficient for a measurable rate.

Long-lived can still mean radioactive

Some decays are energetically favorable but extraordinarily slow because of barriers or transition restrictions. Measurements may discover ultra-rare decays in nuclides once assumed stable.

What a textbook may reasonably call stable

A stable nuclide is one without observed spontaneous radioactive decay in relevant conditions and observation ranges, subject to evolving evaluated nuclear data.

The student's final model

Assess composition (Z,N), binding (B,B/A), neighboring masses (Q), shell and pairing effects, and probability (half-life). Only then claim nuclear stability.

1.A.1.25.30 Radioactive transformation as a statistical process

One nucleus does not carry a countdown clock

For a given radioactive nuclide under ordinary assumptions, a nucleus has a characteristic probability per unit time of decay, but the exact moment at which one individual nucleus decays cannot generally be predicted. Large ensembles show reproducible statistical behavior.

Define the decay constant

A simplified single-channel decay constant λ has units of inverse time and describes probability rate in an exponential model. For an ensemble of N undecayed nuclei, dN/dt=−λN, giving N(t)=N₀e^(−λt).

Expected populations, not guaranteed exact counts

If 100 nuclei have an expected surviving fraction one half after one half-life, the expected surviving number is 50, but an actual small sample can fluctuate. The exponential law is a statistical model accurate for large numbers under stated physical conditions.

Why binding is not a decay rate

Binding energy compares nuclear mass states. Decay constant depends on the transition interaction, matrix elements, available energy, quantum barriers and selection rules. A nuclide with high B/A may be radioactive or stable for immensely long times.

Physical assumptions

The simple law applies to a specified radionuclide in a suitable closed parent population with constant decay constant. Production, mixing or daughter feed-in require additional source terms.

dN/dt=−λN; N(t)=N₀e^(−λt); λ has units time⁻¹.

1.A.1.25.31 Half-life: why it comes from λ rather than from the mass number

A half-life is a statistical interval

The physical half-life t½ is the time for the expected number of undecayed nuclei—or the activity of a pure single-species source—to fall to half its initial value under standard decay assumptions.

Derive the familiar formula

Set N(t½)/N₀=1/2=e^(−λt½). Taking logarithms gives t½=ln2/λ≈0.693147/λ. Rearranging gives λ=ln2/t½.

An example with time units

If t½=6hours, after 18hours (three half-lives) the expected remaining parent fraction is (1/2)³=1/8=12.5%, assuming no new production. That is not the fraction of activity that must have been absorbed by the body.

Short and long lifetimes

Technetium-99m has a physical half-life near 6hours; carbon-14 is about 5730years; iodine-131 about eight days. These times are nuclide-specific, not implied by nucleus mass number alone.

An important precision note

Half-life is not the mean lifetime τ=1/λ; they differ by ln2. Nor is it the time required for every nucleus in a large sample to decay.

t½=ln(2)/λ; N(t)/N₀=2^(−t/t½).

1.A.1.25.32 Activity in becquerels: count of transformations, not absorbed dose

Define the rate observable

Activity A_act is the expected number of spontaneous radioactive nuclear transformations per second in a specified sample. In a single-exponential model, A_act(t)=λN(t).

The SI unit

One becquerel (Bq) is one nuclear transformation per second. If 10⁶ parent nuclei have decay constant 0.001s⁻¹, their activity is 1000Bq at that instant. This is a rate, not energy per transformation.

Activity decreases with the same half-life for a pure source

When λ remains constant, A_act(t)=A₀e^(−λt). This describes parent activity; a decay chain with daughters can behave differently because daughter production and decay are additional processes.

Radiation energy is another dimension

Two sources with equal activity can emit different radiation types and energies, leading to different exposure and dose conditions. Activity alone cannot rank their biological effects.

A student should not call A mass number here

The symbol A is used for nuclear mass number and sometimes for activity in different contexts. Write A_act for activity in this chapter to avoid confusing a dimensionless nucleon count with Bq.

A_act=λN; 1Bq=1 transformation·s⁻¹; activity is not absorbed dose.

1.A.1.25.33 Physical half-life, biological clearance and effective half-life

A nuclide may leave a biological pool without decaying

After a radionuclide enters a biological system, its physical radioactive decay continues while transport, metabolism and excretion can remove it from a chosen compartment. The measured count in that compartment can decrease for two different reasons.

Define each timescale

Physical half-life is the nuclide's radioactive decay property. Biological half-life describes removal from a defined biological compartment under a particular kinetic model and experimental conditions; it is not automatically a universal constant of that nuclide.

The simple first-order combined model

If radioactive decay and independent first-order biological clearance both contribute exponential loss from the measured compartment, λ_eff=λ_phys+λ_bio, hence 1/t½,eff=1/t½,phys+1/t½,bio.

A numerical illustration

For physical half-life 6h and independent biological half-life 3h, the effective compartment half-life is 1/(1/6+1/3)=2h. This model assumes no redistribution, reuptake, complex compartments or continued labeling.

Important limits

Real radiopharmaceutical kinetics may be multi-compartmental. The physical decay half-life remains the same even though compartment activity or tracer concentration changes faster. Stable isotopes have no radioactive decay term.

Independent first-order processes: 1/t½,eff=1/t½,phys+1/t½,bio.

1.A.1.25.34 Activity, absorbed dose, equivalent dose and why units cannot be swapped

Becquerel counts transformations

Activity in Bq answers how often nuclear transformations occur. It does not directly express energy transferred to tissue or expected biological harm.

Gray measures absorbed energy per mass

Absorbed dose D is energy deposited per unit mass of material, with 1Gy=1J/kg. An absorbed dose calculation requires radiation transport, energy deposition and geometry, not only activity.

Sievert incorporates radiation weighting

Equivalent dose and effective dose are radiation protection quantities expressed in Sv, incorporating radiation weighting and, for effective dose, tissue weighting in specified models. Sv is not simply a different name for Gy.

Biological effects need more context

Different radiation types, energies, exposure distributions, dose rates, cell types and repair capacities influence outcomes. One Bq value cannot be converted to Sv without a detailed exposure scenario and dose coefficients.

Safety education rather than clinical advice

Medical nuclear procedures follow professional justification, optimization and radiation protection; our lesson explains physical concepts but gives no self-administration, acquisition or handling instructions.

Activity: Bq=s⁻¹; absorbed dose: Gy=J/kg; protection-weighted dose: Sv.

1.A.1.25.35 Radiation interactions with DNA and living tissue

Nuclear decay products interact electromagnetically

Charged particles can ionize and excite molecules along their trajectories; gamma and other photons may create energetic secondary electrons through interactions with matter. Nuclear energy release is connected to biology through these physical interactions with tissues.

Direct and indirect routes

Energy deposition can damage DNA directly or generate reactive chemical species, including radicals from water radiolysis, that can participate in molecular damage. The balance depends on radiation quality and biological environment.

DNA damage is not one uniform outcome

Single-strand and double-strand breaks, base damage and complex lesions can occur. Cellular responses include repair, checkpoint signaling, cell death or survival with alterations, depending on damage and biological context.

LET and dose distribution matter

Linear energy transfer and microscopic energy deposition patterns help distinguish some radiation qualities. Equal absorbed dose can produce different biological consequences across radiation types; a nucleus's B/A alone does not provide this information.

Do not assume every decay causes disease

Biological risk depends on actual tissue dose, radiation quality, temporal and spatial distribution and individual context. An isotope's radioactive property by itself is not a diagnosis or outcome prediction.

1.A.1.25.36 Stable isotope tracing versus radioactive tracing in biochemistry

Stable isotopes are not radioactive by default

Carbon-13, nitrogen-15, deuterium and oxygen-18 are stable nuclides used to label molecules. Their chemical pathways can be investigated through mass or isotope ratio measurements without nuclear decay producing radioactivity.

Radioactive tracers encode a different observable

Certain radioisotopes can label biological compounds and allow measurement of nuclear emissions with appropriate professional detection methods. Their activity is a nuclear transformation rate, while incorporation and biological clearance are separate processes.

A carbon example

A ¹³C label changes the mass of a glucose isotopologue and can be observed in metabolomics. Carbon-14 has an unstable nucleus and may undergo beta-minus decay; its chemical role as carbon and nuclear behavior are distinct.

No direct flux from one endpoint

Whether a tracer is stable or radioactive, one detected label or activity measurement does not automatically reveal metabolic flux. It requires input enrichment or activity, time course, pool size and a validated kinetic/network model.

Why this node belongs in life sciences

Nuclear binding determines which tracer nuclei are stable or radioactive; mass spectrometry and biochemistry determine how labelled molecular species move through biological pathways.

1.A.1.25.37 Nuclear medicine: why technetium-99m is a remarkable isomer

Same nucleon counts, different nuclear energy state

Technetium-99m and technetium-99 share proton number and mass number but differ in nuclear excitation state. The m denotes a metastable isomer with a characteristic transition scheme.

A physical half-life around six hours

The metastable state has a physical half-life of roughly six hours, enabling imaging applications where external detectors observe suitable emitted photons. Decay product and route details require evaluated nuclear data.

The radiopharmaceutical context

The chemical form and biological targeting of the radionuclide determine where the agent travels in the body. Nuclear decay determines the physical emission pattern. These are coupled in imaging but are separate layers of science.

Physical versus biological measurement

Even after an imaging tracer leaves an organ, remaining radionuclide atoms still undergo their characteristic nuclear decay wherever they go. A measured organ time–activity curve reflects both physics and tissue kinetics.

Safety and evidence

Actual radiopharmaceutical uses require licensed professionals, quality controls and patient-specific clinical decisions. The chapter offers scientific context, not application or procedural instructions.

1.A.1.25.38 Radiocarbon, potassium-40 and natural background radioactivity

Carbon-14 exists at trace natural abundance

Carbon-14 is created by nuclear processes in the atmosphere and enters environmental carbon cycles. Its physical half-life of about 5730 years supports chronology models under stated assumptions.

Radiocarbon dating is not one ratio read in isolation

Inferring age requires reference calibration, sample preparation, initial carbon history and contamination assessment. A single raw amount of carbon-14 does not uniquely yield an exact biological specimen age.

Potassium-40 is a minor part of natural potassium

Most potassium atoms in ordinary material are stable potassium-39 and potassium-41; a small fraction are radioactive potassium-40. Potassium ions have major physiological roles, but isotope nuclear decay is distinct from ionic transport.

Natural background is varied

Natural radiation can arise from cosmic radiation, terrestrial radionuclides and other sources. Activity, energy and dose must be described separately, and the presence of a natural radionuclide is not equivalent to an individual clinical finding.

This section connects chemistry and nuclear science

Element identity determines chemical behavior, isotope identity determines nuclear properties, and environmental history determines abundance and exposure context. All three can matter without being interchangeable.

Case study 1. The helium-4 mass deficit on a laboratory whiteboard

A physics student writes the separated constituents of helium-4 as two free protons, two free neutrons and two electrons. Instead of accidentally mixing bare nuclear and neutral atomic mass references, the class uses two neutral hydrogen-1 atoms, two free neutrons and one neutral helium-4 atom. With mH=1.00782503223u, mn=1.00866491595u and mHe=4.00260325413u, the difference is 2mH+2mn−mHe≈0.03037664223u. Worked example 1. Nuclear composition of iron-56

Problem: For ⁵⁶Fe with Z=26, determine neutrons and N/Z.

Full solution: Mass number A=56=Z+N, so N=56−26=30 neutrons. The neutron/proton ratio N/Z=30/26≈1.153846. The result gives nuclear composition; it does not calculate a decay rate or binding energy.

Independent check: 26+30=56 and N/Z>1 as expected for this representative heavier stable nucleus.

Exam pitfall: Do not infer exact stability just from the ratio.

Worked example 2. Nuclear composition of lead-208

Problem: For ²⁰⁸Pb with Z=82, find N and N/Z.

Full solution: N=208−82=126. Thus N/Z=126/82≈1.536585. The values 82 and 126 are classic magic shell closures, a separate structural point from the arithmetic ratio.

Independent check: 82+126=208, and both Z,N are positive integers.

Exam pitfall: Do not say lead-208 has equal neutron and proton numbers.

Worked example 3. Nuclear radius scaling for A=64

Problem: Use R≈1.2A^(1/3)fm for a nuclide with A=64.

Full solution: Since 64^(1/3)=4, R≈1.2×4=4.8fm=4.8×10⁻¹⁵m. This is an empirical scale estimate, not an exact radius or proof of spherical nuclear geometry.

Independent check: 4³=64.

Exam pitfall: Do not convert 1fm into 10⁻¹⁰m; that is an atomic rather than nuclear scale.

Worked example 4. Nuclear radius ratio

Problem: Two nuclei have A=8 and A=64. Find predicted ratio of radii from R∝A^(1/3).

Full solution: R64/R8=(64/8)^(1/3)=8^(1/3)=2. Thus a factor-eight nucleon count leads to a factor-two predicted nuclear radius, assuming the same radius constant.

Independent check: The corresponding spherical volume ratio is 2³=8.

Worked example 5. One atomic mass unit's energy

Problem: Convert a 1u mass-energy equivalent to MeV.

Full solution: Use the CODATA-based conversion m_uc²≈931.49410372MeV. Therefore a mass defect of 1u corresponds to 931.494MeV. The strict statement is 1u×c² in energy, not that u and MeV have the same physical dimension.

Independent check: 0.01u corresponds to about 9.31494MeV by proportionality.

Exam pitfall: Distinguish MeV of energy from MeV/c² of mass.

Worked example 6. A hypothetical mass defect in MeV

Problem: A bound nucleus has mass defect Δm=0.00500u. Calculate binding B.

Full solution: B=Δmc²≈0.00500×931.49410372=4.6574705MeV, or about 4.66MeV at the given significant figures. This is total binding relative to specified free nucleons.

Independent check: 4.6574705/931.49410372=0.00500u.

Exam pitfall: Do not divide by nucleon count unless B/A is requested.

Worked example 7. Convert energy into a mass defect

Problem: A conceptual nucleus has binding B=9.31494MeV. Find Δm in u.

Full solution: Δm=B/(931.49410372MeV/u)≈0.0100000u. The relationship compares nuclear energy with the mass of a specified initial/final state; it is not an atomic mass number.

Independent check: Multiply 0.0100u by 931.494MeV/u to return about 9.31494MeV.

Exam pitfall: Do not attach u to an energy answer.

Worked example 8. The helium-4 mass defect from neutral atomic masses

Problem: Use mH=1.00782503223u,mn=1.00866491595u and m(⁴He)=4.00260325413u. Find Δm.

Full solution: For helium-4, Z=2 and N=2. The atomic-mass formula gives Δm≈2mH+2mn−mHe=2.01565006446+2.01732983190−4.00260325413=0.03037664223u. Atomic electron terms nearly cancel.

Independent check: The separated-constituent reference mass is about 4.03297989636u, greater than bound helium mass.

Exam pitfall: Do not use free proton mass with a neutral helium atomic mass without electron accounting.

Worked example 9. The helium-4 binding energy

Problem: Given Δm(⁴He)=0.03037664223u, find total B in MeV.

Full solution: B≈0.03037664223×931.49410372≈28.295663MeV, about 28.30MeV. This is energy to separate the nuclear constituents in the specified model, not energy of an ordinary helium chemical bond.

Independent check: Dividing by the conversion constant returns Δm≈0.03037664u.

Exam pitfall: Do not call the total energy an energy per nucleon.

Worked example 10. The helium-4 binding per nucleon

Problem: Given B(⁴He)≈28.295663MeV and A=4, find B/A.

Full solution: B/A≈28.295663/4≈7.073916MeV/nucleon. A is the integer number of nucleons, not the decimal helium neutral atomic mass 4.002603u.

Independent check: 7.073916×4≈28.295664MeV.

Exam pitfall: Do not divide by atomic mass in u instead of nucleon count A.

Worked example 11. The deuteron mass defect

Problem: Use mH=1.00782503223u,mn=1.00866491595u,mD=2.01410177812u. Calculate Δm.

Full solution: For deuterium Z=N=1, Δm≈1.00782503223+1.00866491595−2.01410177812=0.00238817006u. Deuterium remains stable despite its smaller binding compared with many heavier light nuclei.

Independent check: The separated reference 2.01648994818u exceeds bound deuterium mass.

Exam pitfall: The neutral hydrogen-1 atomic mass is not equal to free proton mass.

Worked example 12. The deuteron binding per nucleon

Problem: Given Δm=0.00238817006u, find B and B/A for A=2.

Full solution: B≈0.00238817006×931.49410372≈2.224566MeV total. B/A≈2.224566/2≈1.112283MeV per nucleon. This relatively low B/A does not make stable deuterium radioactive.

Independent check: Multiplying 1.112283×2 recovers total≈2.224566MeV.

Exam pitfall: Do not use B/A directly to predict a radioactive half-life.

Worked example 13. Carbon-12 mass defect despite its exact 12u atomic mass

Problem: Using mH,mn values above, find Δm for ¹²C with Z=N=6 and M_atom=12u.

Full solution: Δm≈6(1.00782503223)+6(1.00866491595)−12=0.09893968908u. Carbon-12's exact 12u mass is a definition of the unit, not a statement that the separated nucleons sum to 12u.

Independent check: 6mH+6mn≈12.09893968908u, so Δm positive.

Exam pitfall: Do not assert zero binding energy because carbon-12 defines u.

Worked example 14. Carbon-12 total binding and per nucleon

Problem: Using Δm(¹²C)=0.09893968908u, find B and B/A.

Full solution: B≈0.09893968908×931.49410372≈92.161737MeV. With A=12, B/A≈7.680145MeV per nucleon. This is nuclear cohesion, not ordinary carbon chemical bonding.

Independent check: 12×7.680145≈92.16174MeV.

Exam pitfall: Do not confuse atom count in glucose with nuclear nucleon count.

Worked example 15. Iron-56 nuclear mass defect

Problem: Use Z=26,N=30,mFe=55.93493633u,mH=1.00782503223u,mn=1.00866491595u.

Full solution: Δm≈26mH+30mn−mFe≈0.52846198648u. Use the neutral-atom form because the supplied iron mass includes electrons. The large total defect reflects a 56-nucleon system.

Independent check: The defect is positive and smaller than the mass of a neutron.

Exam pitfall: Do not mix the bare proton formula with a neutral iron atom mass.

Worked example 16. Iron-56 binding per nucleon

Problem: Given Δm≈0.52846198648u and A=56, find B/A.

Full solution: Total B≈0.52846198648×931.49410372≈492.259224MeV. Divide by 56 to obtain B/A≈8.790343MeV/nucleon. Iron-56 lies near the broad B/A maximum.

Independent check: 8.790343×56≈492.2592MeV.

Exam pitfall: The broad maximum is not a claim that iron-56 alone has the highest known B/A.

Worked example 17. Oxygen-16 binding per nucleon

Problem: Use Z=N=8,m(¹⁶O)=15.99491461957u, the same mH,mn and conversion. Find B/A.

Full solution: Δm≈8mH+8mn−15.99491461957≈0.13700496587u. Total B≈127.619318MeV. Dividing by A=16 gives B/A≈7.976207MeV per nucleon.

Independent check: 16×7.976207≈127.6193MeV.

Exam pitfall: Measured oxygen atomic mass is not exactly its mass number 16u.

Worked example 18. Lithium-7 binding per nucleon

Problem: Use Z=3,N=4,m(⁷Li)≈7.0160034366u. Find total B and B/A with neutral hydrogen and neutron masses.

Full solution: Δm≈3(1.00782503223)+4(1.00866491595)−7.0160034366≈0.04213132389u. Binding B≈39.24508MeV and B/A≈39.24508/7≈5.60644MeV/nucleon.

Independent check: 3+4=7 and 5.60644×7≈39.24508MeV.

Exam pitfall: Do not judge all seven-nucleon nuclides by this one isotope's binding.

Worked example 19. Convert helium binding energy to joules

Problem: Helium-4 binding is approximately 28.295663MeV. Convert to J.

Full solution: 1MeV=1.602176634×10⁻¹³J exactly under SI eV conversion. Thus E≈28.295663×1.602176634×10⁻¹³≈4.533×10⁻¹²J per helium-4 nucleus. This is much larger than typical eV chemical bond scales.

Independent check: 28.3MeV times 1.6×10⁻¹³J/MeV is about 4.53×10⁻¹²J.

Exam pitfall: Do not use eV conversion factor 1.602×10⁻¹⁹J without multiplying by 10⁶ for MeV.

Worked example 20. An arbitrary nuclear Q-value from mass defect

Problem: A hypothetical complete reaction has Σm_initial−Σm_final=+0.00200u. Find Q and interpret.

Full solution: Q≈0.00200×931.49410372=1.862988MeV>0. The stated reaction is exoergic under the complete reaction mass accounting. Its rate, branching or physical feasibility still needs conservation laws and transition dynamics.

Independent check: Q would change sign if initial and final masses were reversed.

Exam pitfall: Positive Q is not itself proof of a fast reaction.

Part II — Twenty advanced binding, nuclear transition, decay and biophysical numericals

Worked example 21. Carbon-14 beta-minus Q using neutral atomic masses

Problem: Use M(¹⁴C)≈14.0032419884u and M(¹⁴N)≈14.00307400443u to calculate β⁻ Q in MeV.

Full solution: Using neutral atomic masses for beta-minus, Q≈[M_parent−M_daughter]c². Difference≈0.00016798397u. Multiply by 931.49410372MeV/u to get Q≈0.15648MeV. The electron and antineutrino share decay energy; this is not a monoenergetic electron line.

Independent check: The difference is positive, so energy is available, consistent with observed carbon-14 beta decay.

Exam pitfall: Do not subtract an extra electron mass for β⁻ when using neutral atom masses in this approximation.

Worked example 22. Why positron emission needs a mass threshold

Problem: A hypothetical beta-plus candidate has neutral parent–daughter mass difference 0.00100u. Is spontaneous β⁺ energetically allowed?

Full solution: For neutral atomic masses, β⁺ Q≈(ΔM−2m_e)c². With 2m_e≈2×0.00054857991=0.00109715982u, Q≈−0.00009715982×931.494≈−0.0905MeV. Spontaneous ground-state beta-plus is not energetically allowed by these inputs.

Independent check: The mass difference 0.00100u is smaller than the required 2m_e term.

Exam pitfall: Do not use the β⁻ atomic-mass formula for β⁺.

Worked example 23. An energetically open beta-plus toy case

Problem: A hypothetical β⁺ nuclide has neutral parent–daughter difference 0.00300u. Find Q ignoring excitation and small electron binding corrections.

Full solution: Q≈(0.00300−0.00109715982)×931.494≈0.00190284018×931.494≈1.7724MeV. This is energetically permitted but does not predict whether this or a competing decay channel dominates.

Independent check: Mass difference exceeds 2m_e by a positive amount.

Exam pitfall: Do not treat the positive Q as a half-life or as a guarantee of one branching fraction.

Worked example 24. Alpha decay nuclide bookkeeping

Problem: A conceptual nucleus ²³⁸U with Z=92 emits one alpha. Give daughter A and Z.

Full solution: An alpha carries A=4 and Z=2. Therefore daughter A=238−4=234 and Z=92−2=90, corresponding to thorium-234. Neutron count is 234−90=144. These are conservation consequences, not a calculation of decay rate.

Independent check: Mass numbers 234+4=238 and charges 90+2=92.

Exam pitfall: Do not change A by two rather than four.

Worked example 25. An alpha decay Q from complete masses

Problem: A specified alpha decay has parent atomic mass 238.05078826u, daughter atomic mass 234.043601u, and neutral helium-4 mass 4.002603254u. Estimate Q.

Full solution: The neutral electrons cancel when all product atomic masses are included consistently: Δm≈238.05078826−234.043601−4.002603254=0.004584006u. Q≈0.004584006×931.494≈4.2696MeV. Small differences in evaluated masses affect final digits.

Independent check: Q is positive and of order a few MeV, consistent with an exoergic alpha decay example.

Exam pitfall: Do not equate the alpha kinetic energy alone to all Q without allowing daughter recoil.

Worked example 26. Separation energy of last neutron in carbon-13

Problem: Given neutral masses m(¹²C)=12u, mn=1.00866491595u, m(¹³C)=13.003354835u, find S_n(¹³C).

Full solution: For removal of a neutron, the daughter is carbon-12 plus a free neutron. S_n≈[12+1.00866491595−13.003354835]×931.494≈0.00531008095×931.494≈4.9464MeV. Electron counts match for carbon atoms.

Independent check: The result is positive, indicating energy is required to remove that neutron from the ground state.

Exam pitfall: Do not replace this S_n by carbon-13's B/A.

Worked example 27. N/Z comparison of carbon, iron and lead

Problem: Find N/Z for ¹²C, ⁵⁶Fe and ²⁰⁸Pb.

Full solution: Carbon: N=6,Z=6 gives ratio 1. Iron: 30/26≈1.154. Lead: 126/82≈1.537. The tendency to greater neutron excess at higher proton count is compatible with rising Coulomb cost, but individual nuclide stability needs fuller nuclear information.

Independent check: All listed nuclei have integer N,Z, and each A=N+Z.

Exam pitfall: Do not assert any nucleus with N/Z exactly one is necessarily stable.

Worked example 28. Classify even-even versus odd-odd

Problem: Classify ¹⁴N (Z=7,N=7) and ¹⁶O (Z=8,N=8).

Full solution: ¹⁴N has odd Z and odd N and is odd-odd, whereas ¹⁶O has even Z and N and is even-even. Both have even A, demonstrating why the mass-number parity alone does not identify pairing class.

Independent check: 7+7=14, 8+8=16.

Exam pitfall: Do not classify an even-A nuclide automatically as even-even.

Worked example 29. Use the nuclear radius scale for lead-208

Problem: Estimate R≈1.2A^(1/3)fm for A=208.

Full solution: The cube root of 208 is about 5.925, so R≈1.2×5.925≈7.11fm. The estimate captures mass-number scaling, not exact measured nuclear charge radius or deformation.

Independent check: (7.11/1.2)³≈208 at this precision.

Exam pitfall: Do not use 208fm just because there are 208 nucleons.

Worked example 30. Half-life as an exponential law

Problem: A pure nuclide has physical half-life 6h. What fraction remains after 18h?

Full solution: Number of elapsed half-lives=18/6=3. Remaining expected fraction=2⁻³=1/8=0.125=12.5%. This refers to undecayed parent nuclei in a no-production model, not their clearance from a tissue.

Independent check: Three successive halvings: 100%,50%,25%,12.5%.

Exam pitfall: Do not treat half-life as a deterministic decay date for each nucleus.

Worked example 31. Four half-lives of parent nuclei

Problem: A pure radioactive sample has 1,600,000 parent nuclei and t½=2h. Expected number after 8h?

Full solution: Elapsed intervals=8/2=4. Remaining expected number=1,600,000×(1/2)⁴=100,000 parent nuclei. Small actual samples fluctuate; this is an ensemble expectation.

Independent check: Four halvings: 1,600,000→800,000→400,000→200,000→100,000.

Exam pitfall: Do not mistake the expected remaining count for activity Bq.

Worked example 32. Derive decay constant from a measured half-life

Problem: A nuclide has t½=10days. Find λ in day⁻¹.

Full solution: For exponential decay λ=ln2/t½. Thus λ≈0.693147/10=0.0693147day⁻¹. To express it in s⁻¹, divide by seconds per day; time units must match the time variable used later.

Independent check: λ×10days≈0.693147, so e^(−λt)=0.5 at ten days.

Exam pitfall: Do not report λ in day units rather than inverse days.

Worked example 33. Compute activity from number of undecayed nuclei

Problem: A pure source has N=1.00×10⁶ parent nuclei and λ=0.00100s⁻¹. Calculate A_act.

Full solution: Activity A_act=λN=0.00100×1.00×10⁶=1.00×10³s⁻¹=1000Bq. This means approximately 1000 transformations per second, not 1000 emitted photons necessarily and not 1000Gy.

Independent check: Activity divided by parent count gives the supplied λ in s⁻¹.

Exam pitfall: Do not confuse becquerel with deposited energy or clinical dose.

Worked example 34. Infer parent count from activity

Problem: A sample has A_act=1000Bq for a pure radionuclide whose λ=0.001s⁻¹. Infer N.

Full solution: Rearrange N=A_act/λ=1000s⁻¹/(0.001s⁻¹)=1.0×10⁶ nuclei. The result is a number of parent nuclei under the one-component model.

Independent check: 0.001×10⁶=1000Bq.

Exam pitfall: Do not attach the unit seconds to a particle count.

Worked example 35. Physical and biological half-lives combine as rates

Problem: Physical t½=6h, biological t½=3h, both independent first-order. Find effective t½.

Full solution: For the specified compartment, 1/t_eff=1/6+1/3=1/2 h⁻¹, so t_eff=2h. Physical decay still has its own six-hour half-life, unaffected by biological clearance.

Independent check: Combined effective half-life is shorter than either individual half-life.

Exam pitfall: Do not add 6+3 or assume this one-compartment model universally fits patient kinetics.

Worked example 36. Two equal removal half-lives

Problem: Physical half-life is 8days and biological half-life is 8days in an independent first-order compartment model. Find effective half-life.

Full solution: 1/t_eff=1/8+1/8=1/4 days⁻¹. Therefore t_eff=4days. Equal independent removal rate constants double the rate and halve the effective half-life.

Independent check: 4days is less than 8days.

Exam pitfall: Do not assume the radionuclide's physical half-life itself changes to 4days.

Worked example 37. Find biological half-life from effective and physical values

Problem: A radionuclide has t_phys=6h and measured effective half-life t_eff=2h, assuming independent first-order losses. Find t_bio.

Full solution: 1/t_bio=1/t_eff−1/t_phys=1/2−1/6=1/3 h⁻¹, so t_bio=3h. This depends on the simplified independent, exponential compartment model.

Independent check: 1/6+1/3=1/2 h⁻¹, matching the given t_eff=2h.

Exam pitfall: Do not subtract 6−2=4h to obtain biological half-life.

Worked example 38. Carbon-14 survival fraction across two half-lives

Problem: Taking carbon-14 t½≈5730years, what expected undecayed fraction remains after 11,460years in a closed idealized parent pool?

Full solution: Elapsed number of half-lives=11460/5730=2. Remaining fraction=2⁻²=1/4=25%. A real radiocarbon dating model needs calibration, initial conditions and contamination control; this calculation is only the physical decay law.

Independent check: After one 5730-year interval 50%; after two 25%.

Exam pitfall: Do not directly equate this fraction with age of an arbitrary contaminated object.

Worked example 39. Absorbed dose from deposited energy

Problem: A tissue sample of mass 0.50kg absorbs 0.0020J of radiation energy uniformly in the toy model. Find absorbed dose.

Full solution: D=E_abs/m=0.0020J/0.50kg=0.0040J/kg=0.0040Gy. This calculation describes absorbed energy only, not effective dose in Sv or expected biological outcome.

Independent check: 0.004Gy×0.50kg=0.002J.

Exam pitfall: Do not use an activity in Bq as though it were an energy in joules.

Worked example 40. A half-life and activity graph check

Problem: A pure source initially has activity 800Bq and physical t½=5h. Expected activity after 15h?

Full solution: After 15/5=3 half-lives, A=800×2⁻³=100Bq. Activity follows the parent decay exponential because λ is unchanged. A biological compartment measurement may not obey this simple form if tracer transport is present.

Independent check: 800→400→200→100Bq across three equal half-lives.

Exam pitfall: Do not conflate detected count rate, which needs detector efficiency/background correction, with true source activity.
