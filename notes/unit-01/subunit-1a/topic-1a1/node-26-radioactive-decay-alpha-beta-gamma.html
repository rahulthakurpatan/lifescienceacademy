1.A.1.26.1 A photographic plate reveals spontaneous nuclear change

The unexpected beginning

In 1896 Henri Becquerel studied uranium-containing compounds and noticed effects on photographic plates even without the expected sunlight illumination. The emission was not ordinary sunlight-driven fluorescence, because something in the material itself was releasing detectable radiation.

A scientific rather than mystical explanation

An unstable nucleus can change into a lower-energy nuclear configuration or another nuclide and release energetic particles or photons. Its behavior is unlike normal electronic fluorescence or chemical bond formation.

Why the distinction matters

One nuclide can be radioactive even while the chemistry of its element resembles stable isotopes. Carbon-14 behaves chemically as carbon in many biological pathways but its nucleus undergoes beta-minus decay. Carbon-13 is stable.

Three forms of evidence

Experiments revealed radiation types differing in ionization, charge, deflection and penetration. They eventually became known as alpha, beta and gamma, three classes whose physical nature and effects cannot be understood with one common particle picture.

Our route through the mystery

We will track who identified the emissions, construct complete nuclear equations, understand energies and neutrinos, then connect radioactive measurements to biochemistry, nuclear medicine, radiobiology and responsible scientific conclusions.

Learning outcomes: Identify α/β⁻/β⁺/γ and electron capture; balance daughter A and Z with lepton and charge conservation; distinguish discrete/continuous energies, Q-values, activity, half-life, instruments, dose and biological tracer kinetics; solve original exam problems with independent checks.

1.A.1.26.2 Chronology from Becquerel and Curie to modern decay theory

Becquerel's observation

Uranium salts affected plates without the required light excitation, motivating work on self-emission from matter. The photographic observation preceded a full explanation of atomic nuclei.

Marie and Pierre Curie's systematic work

Ionization measurement and investigation of radioactive substances showed that radioactivity followed specific material constituents even while chemical forms changed. Their careful experiments made radioactivity a quantitative science.

Rutherford, Villard and radiation classification

Alpha and beta were distinguished experimentally by their different penetration and deflection properties. Paul Villard's work revealed gamma radiation; later understanding identified these as helium nuclei, electrons and nuclear-origin photons.

The nucleus and neutron

Rutherford's nuclear model and Chadwick's 1932 neutron discovery gave meaning to nuclide numbers A=Z+N, enabling balanced nuclear transformations to be described precisely.

Beta spectrum and the neutrino

The continuous beta electron energy spectrum was a major puzzle. Pauli's neutrino hypothesis and Fermi's weak-interaction theory explained why an additional nearly invisible particle participates in beta decay.

Modern life-science applications

Evaluated decay databases give half-lives, energies and branching fractions; biological interpretation combines nuclear emissions with molecular labeling, tissue distribution, detector calibration and time-dependent kinetics.

Historical concept timeline; events are schematic, not evenly spaced years.

1.A.1.26.3 The language of nuclear identity: A, Z, N, parent and daughter

A specific element has a specific Z

Proton number determines element identity, while different neutron numbers identify isotopes. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are carbon, but carbon-14 has extra neutrons and different nuclear stability.

Nuclear symbol as a map

In ᴬ_ZX, A counts protons plus neutrons and Z counts protons; N=A−Z. The atomic electron count is separate and may change after nuclear decay as the daughter atom acquires or loses electrons.

Parent and daughter refer to nuclear configurations

A parent nucleus undergoes a spontaneous nuclear transformation and produces a daughter nucleus, possibly in an excited state. A radioactive daughter may transform again as part of a chain.

The state matters

An m suffix indicates a metastable nuclear level, as in technetium-99m. It does not change A or Z. This becomes important when gamma emission leaves the element unchanged.

A stable isotope is not an inert atom

Carbon-13 can undergo extensive biological chemistry but has no ordinary observed radioactive decay; carbon-14 is radioactive despite chemically participating as carbon. Chemical reactivity and nuclear stability are different variables.

ᴬ_ZX: proton number Z, neutron number N=A−Z, mass number A.

1.A.1.26.4 Balancing nuclear equations by conservation laws

Why A must balance

Alpha carries four nucleons and beta/gamma particles have A=0 in customary nuclear notation. The parent nucleon count is reproduced among the daughter and emitted nuclear particles in the relevant processes.

Why charge must balance

An alpha has charge +2, beta-minus electron −1, positron +1, and gamma 0. Daughter Z changes correspondingly to preserve total electric charge.

Leptons have their own accounting

Beta-minus emits an electron antineutrino with the electron, whereas beta-plus emits an electron neutrino with the positron. Including them conserves lepton number and allows consistent energy/momentum sharing.

Other conservation laws

Energy, momentum and angular momentum apply too. Beta decay transitions obey quantum selection rules; a balanced A and Z equation by itself is not proof that a process is energetically permitted or rapid.

Reliable paper method

Write the emitted particle's A and charge, subtract from parent to find daughter, use daughter's Z to identify the element, then include neutrinos, nuclear excitation and relevant energy information.

α: (A,Z)→(A−4,Z−2)+⁴₂He; β⁻: (A,Z)→(A,Z+1)+e⁻+ν̄ₑ; β⁺: (A,Z)→(A,Z−1)+e⁺+νₑ; γ: (A,Z)*→(A,Z)+γ.

1.A.1.26.5 Alpha, beta and gamma are not three versions of the same object

Alpha is a helium nucleus

An alpha has two protons and two neutrons, charge +2e, and rest mass around four u. Its mass and charge cause many ionizing interactions along a typically short track in matter.

Beta minus is an emitted electron

A beta-minus particle is an electron produced in a weak nuclear transformation, not the ejection of an electron already orbiting the atom. An electron antineutrino participates in the same elementary process.

Beta plus is a positron

A positron has the same rest mass as an electron but opposite charge. It is created in an appropriate nuclear beta-plus transition and can subsequently annihilate with a material electron.

Gamma is a photon

Gamma radiation is high-energy electromagnetic radiation associated with a nuclear transition. Its photon has no electric charge or rest mass; it can transfer energy through interactions creating or energizing charged particles.

Penetration does not specify exact danger

The common classification of alpha as relatively short-ranged and gamma as more penetrating is useful, but energy, medium, geometry, external versus internal source location and radiation quality determine real exposure.

1.A.1.26.6 Alpha decay: the simplest nuclear daughter bookkeeping

An alpha is a bound cluster

Because helium-4 is tightly bound, it can appear as an emitted cluster in particular nuclear transformations. A heavy nuclide can have a lower-mass daughter-plus-alpha final state.

General nuclear form

Parent ᴬ_ZX becomes daughter ᴬ⁻⁴_(Z−2)Y plus ⁴₂He. Proton number decreases by two and mass number by four. This changes element identity.

Uranium-238 example

²³⁸₉₂U→²³⁴₉₀Th+⁴₂He. Check A:238=234+4, check Z:92=90+2. The daughter thorium-234 is itself radioactive, so this is one stage of a longer chain.

Energy and recoil

The daughter and alpha share kinetic energy by momentum conservation. An excited daughter may subsequently emit gamma. Hence alpha kinetic energy need not equal all of the decay Q.

Why rate needs quantum physics

An emitted alpha cluster confronts an electrostatic potential barrier, and tunneling contributes strongly to the probability of escape. Positive Q allows an energy-releasing channel but cannot alone calculate half-life.

²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He; A:238=234+4, Z:92=90+2.

1.A.1.26.7 Two-body alpha decay: energy is shared with recoil

Initially a parent nucleus may be taken at rest

For the ideal two-body process parent→daughter+alpha with daughter in a specified state, conservation of momentum gives equal and opposite final momenta.

The kinetic partition

In the nonrelativistic approximation K=p²/(2m), the lighter alpha receives the larger kinetic share while the heavy daughter recoils with a smaller fraction.

Derive a practical formula

If Q appears solely as two-body kinetic energy, K_alpha≈Q M_d/(M_d+m_alpha); K_d≈Q m_alpha/(M_d+m_alpha). For a daughter near 234u, the alpha typically carries most but not all of Q.

Why excitation complicates it

If an excited daughter absorbs energy E*, kinetic shares apply to the reduced energy Q−E*. The daughter may emit gamma later, so a complete decay scheme can involve both alpha and photons.

Do not claim monoenergetic alpha without branch information

Different daughter levels can yield different alpha energies, broadened by measurement effects. A source can have discrete branches rather than one universal energy.

For nonrelativistic two-body final state: Kα≈(Q−E*) M_d/(M_d+mα).

1.A.1.26.8 Alpha tunneling: a decay can be allowed but extraordinarily slow

The barrier puzzle

A positive alpha decay Q says the final rest-mass state is energetically favored. The alpha still encounters a Coulomb barrier created by the positively charged residual nucleus.

Tunneling is a quantum probability

Alpha emission can occur through barrier penetration even when classical motion at the same energy would be forbidden. The probability depends on barrier geometry, alpha energy, and nuclear structure.

An empirical lesson

For related alpha-emitting isotope families, larger alpha energies often correlate with shorter half-lives through Geiger–Nuttall-like relations. But the rule is not universal and does not replace measured nuclear data.

The frequent student mistake

Binding energy and Q are energy differences; half-life is a probability/time quantity. Multiplying Q by an arbitrary time conversion cannot give an alpha half-life.

Why the explanation remains conceptual

A drawn potential barrier illustrates energy geography, not a literal wall. Quantitative tunneling calculations require wavefunctions and nuclear potentials.

Conceptual alpha tunneling across a Coulomb barrier; not a measured potential.

1.A.1.26.9 Alpha interactions and why source location matters biologically

A doubly charged massive particle ionizes densely

An alpha transfers energy through frequent interactions with surrounding atomic electrons. Its range in matter tends to be short for typical alpha energies, but the exact range depends on initial energy and material.

External exposure is not identical to internal exposure

The intact outer skin often prevents an external alpha particle from reaching deep tissue, yet alpha-emitting atoms incorporated inside tissue can deposit energy intensely in a small region.

Radiobiology depends on more than radiation name

Energy, LET, affected tissue, dose, spatial distribution and biochemical repair influence potential effects. Nuclear decay of a source and actual absorbed energy in a target are different quantities.

The limit of textbook shielding mnemonic

‘Paper stops alpha’ can help classify typical relative penetration but cannot provide a reliable protection specification for a real radioactive material. Trained radiation-safety personnel use source-specific assessment.

1.A.1.26.10 Beta minus: neutron-to-proton conversion and the antineutrino

The nuclear process

In beta-minus decay a neutron becomes a proton inside a nucleus, emitting an electron and electron antineutrino. The mass number A stays fixed, while the proton number rises by one.

Balance the elementary transformation

n→p+e⁻+ν̄_e. The proton adds charge +1, the emitted electron −1, and the antineutrino is uncharged. Baryon and lepton numbers balance when the full particles are included.

Carbon-14 example

¹⁴₆C→¹⁴₇N+e⁻+ν̄_e. The daughter nitrogen-14 has one more proton and one fewer neutron than carbon-14, preserving the fourteen nucleons.

Not an orbital electron

The beta electron is produced during the weak process; it is not simply ejected from an existing atomic orbital. Electron reorganization after the daughter element forms is a separate atomic process.

The energy and lifetime are independent observables

Carbon-14 beta Q≈156.5keV and physical half-life≈5730years. A positive energy release does not determine decay probability; transition strength matters.

n→p+e⁻+ν̄ₑ; ¹⁴₆C→¹⁴₇N+e⁻+ν̄ₑ.

1.A.1.26.11 Why beta electrons have continuous energy: the missing particle

A predicted line that was not observed

A parent at rest decaying into only a daughter nucleus and an electron would strongly constrain the electron kinetic energy for each final nuclear state.

The observed beta spectrum has a continuous distribution

Electrons emerge with varied energies up to an endpoint, because another particle can carry a variable portion of available energy and momentum.

The antineutrino solves the energy-accounting puzzle

In beta-minus, the electron antineutrino takes a variable fraction of energy and momentum. Daughter recoil also participates. The full final state preserves conservation laws.

The endpoint and the mean differ

For carbon-14, an endpoint around 156keV is near the maximum available kinetic energy for the electron in the relevant transition; typical electrons carry less, and the antineutrino obtains variable energy.

Other factors shape the spectrum

Weak-interaction matrix elements, phase space and Coulomb effects affect beta-spectrum shape. We must not assume electron and antineutrino always divide Q equally.

Schematic continuous beta-minus electron energy spectrum rather than a fixed line.

1.A.1.26.12 Beta-minus Q from neutral atomic masses

Correct mass bookkeeping

The common neutral-atomic mass approximation Q_β−≈[M_atom(parent)−M_atom(daughter)]c² includes the emitted beta electron consistently with the electron difference between neutral parent and daughter atoms.

Numerical carbon-14 case

Using ¹⁴C mass≈14.0032419884u and ¹⁴N mass≈14.00307400443u gives Δm≈0.00016798397u. Multiply by 931.494MeV/u to obtain Q≈0.15648MeV.

The emitted electron is not massless

The beta electron's customary nuclide notation writes A=0 since A counts nucleons. It still has finite rest mass around 0.511MeV/c² and charge −1.

Why Q is an upper budget

The electron, antineutrino, recoil and possible excited states share the available energy. A single electron does not necessarily have kinetic energy equal to Q.

Half-life requires weak transition theory

The beta Q tells energetic accessibility, while decay rate depends on nuclear structure, spin/parity selection rules and phase space.

Q(β⁻)≈[M_atom(P)−M_atom(D)]×931.494 MeV/u.

1.A.1.26.13 Beta plus: changing a proton into a neutron

The weak transformation

A proton inside a suitable nucleus changes to a neutron with emission of a positron e⁺ and electron neutrino ν_e. Daughter Z decreases by one and mass number stays constant.

The free proton cannot do this spontaneously

A free proton has insufficient rest mass to become a free neutron plus positron and neutrino. Nuclear binding energy differences can make the channel energetically possible in an appropriate parent nuclide.

An oxygen example

¹⁵₈O→¹⁵₇N+e⁺+ν_e. A=15 both before and after; charge balance includes the emitted positive positron.

The neutral-atom threshold

Q_β+≈[M_atom(parent)−M_atom(daughter)−2m_e]c². It is not enough that parent atom mass exceed daughter; their mass gap must exceed twice the electron rest mass in this convention.

Secondary annihilation

After slowing, a positron can annihilate with an electron. In approximately zero net momentum conditions, two photons of approximately 511keV travel in opposite directions. This is distinct from the original nuclear beta transition.

p→n+e⁺+νₑ; Q(β⁺)≈[M_P−M_D−2mₑ]c².

1.A.1.26.14 Electron capture: a competing proton-to-neutron route

Capture of an atomic electron

In electron capture a nuclear proton captures an orbital electron and converts to a neutron while emitting an electron neutrino. Daughter Z decreases by one and A stays constant.

The schematic equation

p+e⁻→n+ν_e, with an electron taken from the atom's electronic structure. An atomic vacancy can subsequently produce characteristic x-rays or Auger electrons.

No 2m_e positron threshold

Using neutral parent and daughter atomic masses, Q_EC≈(M_parent−M_daughter)c² before shell binding and excitation corrections. Thus capture can be possible when beta-plus is closed.

Beryllium-7 is a helpful example

⁷Be undergoes electron capture to ⁷Li; its detailed decay properties depend on atomic/electronic configuration in special environments, showing that electron availability may matter.

Medical and environmental qualification

Electron-capture nuclides can emit secondary radiation from atomic rearrangement and nuclear de-excitation. The dose or detector signature cannot be inferred from daughter Z alone.

ᴬ_ZX+e⁻→ᴬ_(Z−1)Y+νₑ; Q_EC≈[M_P−M_D]c².

1.A.1.26.15 Gamma rays: a nucleus changes internal energy, not its nuclide

Excited nuclear states

Nuclei can occupy discrete quantum states. If a nucleus moves from an excited state to a lower-energy nuclear state, it may emit a high-energy photon called gamma radiation.

What remains unchanged

For a pure gamma transition, nuclear proton number Z and mass number A remain the same. The emitting nuclear state's excitation energy changes, not element identity.

Energy and recoil

Photon energy is related to the level difference Eγ≈E_initial−E_final, with a small fraction sometimes appearing as nuclear recoil. Gamma photons have no rest mass and zero electric charge.

Many emissions can follow one decay

Alpha or beta decay may populate an excited daughter that emits a gamma photon or cascade of photons. Thus a gamma accompanying beta does not necessarily imply a distinct radioactive parent transformation.

The nuclear-equation check

Write ᴬ_ZX*→ᴬ_ZX+γ, where the star indicates excitation. When comparing nuclides, both Z and A are retained.

ᴬ_ZX* → ᴬ_ZX + γ; Eγ=hν=hc/λ.

1.A.1.26.16 Gamma photons versus X-rays: origin is often the distinction

Both are photons

X-rays and gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation, moving at speed c in vacuum and interacting through photon-electron and photon-nuclear processes. Either can be ionizing at sufficient energy.

Nuclear versus electronic origin

Gamma is conventionally associated with nuclear transitions. Characteristic x-rays arise from atomic electron rearrangements; bremsstrahlung x-rays can arise from charged particles slowing in matter.

Energy ranges can overlap

An x-ray may have higher photon energy than some gamma radiation, so classifying radiation by a single energy threshold is not universally correct.

Electron capture provides an example

Capturing an inner-shell electron leaves an atomic vacancy; an electron filling it may emit characteristic x-rays or Auger electrons. These emissions differ in physical origin from nuclear gamma de-excitation.

For biology, interactions matter

When interpreting absorbed dose or tissue response, photon energy and interaction mechanisms are usually more directly relevant than a gamma-versus-x-ray label.

1.A.1.26.17 Technetium-99m: an excited nucleus central to imaging

A metastable nuclear state

⁹⁹ᵐTc has the same Z=43 and A=99 as technetium-99 in its ground state. The m identifies a long-lived nuclear excited state rather than another isotope mass number.

A physical six-hour clock

The metastable level has physical half-life around 6.0 hours. A principal photon line used in imaging is about 140.5keV, according to evaluated clinical/nuclear data.

The photon yield is not simply one per decay

Some nuclear transition energy can pass through internal conversion, with atomic rearrangement emissions. A detected gamma line has a characteristic emission probability, not necessarily 100% of all transformations.

Chemistry determines body location

A radiopharmaceutical's chemical ligand, preparation and biological targeting affect where activity accumulates. Nuclear half-life controls decay statistics but does not identify an organ or pathology alone.

The observation is instrument-dependent

Gamma-camera or spectroscopy counts depend on emissions, scatter, attenuation, efficiency and reconstruction; they are not direct, uncorrected concentrations of technetium molecules.

⁹⁹ᵐTc → ⁹⁹Tc + γ (a schematic isomeric gamma branch), with possible internal conversion alternatives.

1.A.1.26.18 Internal conversion and atomic relaxation: an emitted electron that is not beta

An excited nucleus can transfer energy directly to an orbital electron

Internal conversion ejects a bound electron instead of releasing a gamma photon. It is an electromagnetic nuclear de-excitation mechanism.

An electron's origin identifies the process

A beta-minus electron is formed through a weak nuclear neutron-to-proton transformation, usually changing Z; a conversion electron originates from an atomic electron shell and leaves the original nuclide's Z and A unchanged.

Vacancies lead to follow-up signals

After a conversion electron leaves, the atomic shell may relax through characteristic x-rays or Auger electrons, providing several different emitted particles from a single nuclear state transition.

Why a gamma peak is not activity by itself

If a nuclear transition converts internally with significant probability, the number of gamma photons of a specified energy can be less than the number of nuclear transitions. Calibration and emission probability matter.

Practical interpretation

An isotope detection scheme may include gamma, conversion electron and atomic x-ray signatures; assigning every electron to beta decay would be scientifically wrong.

1.A.1.26.19 Gamma interactions in matter: three major mechanisms

Photoelectric absorption

A photon can be absorbed by an atom while ejecting a bound electron. The ejected electron then transfers its kinetic energy to surrounding matter through ionization or excitation.

Compton scattering

A photon scatters from an electron, changes direction and leaves with reduced energy. The recoiling electron carries kinetic energy and may create a charged-particle track.

Pair production threshold

A photon with energy above 2m_ec²≈1.022MeV can create an electron-positron pair in an appropriate momentum-balancing field such as a nucleus.

Different media, different relative probabilities

The relative importance of these processes depends on photon energy, atomic number and absorber properties. One schematic ranking is not valid at all energies.

Biological mechanism

Gamma photons often damage tissue indirectly through energetic secondary electrons and the reactive molecular species that can follow their ionization tracks.

1.A.1.26.20 Gamma energy, wavelength and photon spectrum

Photon energies are quantized by nuclear levels

In many gamma transitions, photons appear near characteristic energy lines. Nuclear recoil, Doppler effects and instrument resolution can shift or broaden observed peaks.

Relate energy to wave properties

E=hν=hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant and c the vacuum light speed. Greater photon energy means higher frequency and shorter wavelength.

A 140keV example

140keV=140,000eV, which corresponds to about 2.243×10⁻¹⁴J per photon. That is energy per emission, not total source activity or absorbed dose.

Contrast beta electron spectra

Beta-minus electron energy is usually continuous because of variable sharing with the antineutrino and recoil. Gamma transitions typically follow nuclear level differences and can yield resolved lines.

A critical detector limitation

Counting a line's photons requires its emission probability and detector efficiency; identifying line energy is not sufficient to calculate the number of parent atoms.

E=hν=hc/λ; E(J)=E(eV)×1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹.

1.A.1.26.21 Attenuation, penetration and the danger of universal shielding claims

Different radiation types move differently through matter

Charged alpha and beta particles experience frequent energy-loss and scattering interactions. Gamma photons are electrically neutral and may travel between stochastic absorption or scattering events.

An idealized narrow-beam photon model

For monoenergetic unscattered photon intensity in a simple homogeneous material, I(x)=I₀e^(−μx), where μ is the energy- and material-dependent linear attenuation coefficient.

Half-value layer is a distance, not a time

HVL=ln2/μ in the ideal photon model. It measures thickness needed to halve uncollided intensity and must not be confused with the radionuclide half-life.

A beta range needs more physics

Alpha and beta range is described by stopping power and particle transport, not the same idealized photon exponential attenuation formula.

Radiation protection needs professionals

Actual shielding, source handling and dose assessment require qualified radiation-safety work. Paper/aluminium/lead mnemonics provide a qualitative introduction, not engineering specifications.

Ideal narrow-beam photon attenuation: I/I₀=e^(−μx); HVL=ln2/μ.

1.A.1.26.22 Activity in becquerels: the parent transformation rate

A radioactive population

For N parent nuclei with constant decay constant λ under one-nuclide exponential decay, activity A_act=λN describes expected transformations per unit time.

One becquerel is one transformation per second

If A_act=1000Bq, the source has an expected 1000 radioactive transformations per second. The number of gamma photons may differ because different decay pathways have different yields.

The same clock governs parent activity

For a pure parent species with constant λ and no new production, A_act(t)=A₀e^(−λt); half-life is the same for the parent number and its activity.

Detector counts are not activity directly

A measured count rate depends on detector efficiency, geometry, energy response, attenuation, dead time, and background. A gamma camera can register far fewer pulses than the source's total transformations.

Biological uptake introduces another variable

When activity in an organ changes with time, the curve reflects physical decay and chemical/biological transport. One count rate does not uniquely establish radionuclide amount or tissue function.

A_act=λN; 1 Bq=1 nuclear transformation s⁻¹.

1.A.1.26.23 Why the law of decay is statistical

Individual events are unpredictable

Even when a nuclide has a well-defined decay constant, the exact spontaneous event time of a particular nucleus is not generally predictable. Large ensembles have reproducible average behavior.

The differential equation

Under constant λ for a closed one-parent population, dN/dt=−λN. Solving gives N(t)=N₀e^(−λt).

Half-life derivation

Set N(t½)/N₀=1/2; then t½=ln2/λ. This is not the mean lifetime 1/λ, nor a promise that exactly half of a very small group must decay at a given instant.

An accessible example

After three physical half-lives, expected remaining parent fraction=2⁻³=12.5%. After five half-lives, 1/32 remains in expectation.

Natural bridge to the next node

We introduce decay statistics here to interpret alpha, beta and gamma processes; Node 27 has the dedicated syllabus focus 'Half-life: Concepts and Simple Numericals'.

N(t)=N₀e^(−λt)=N₀2^(−t/t½); t½=ln2/λ.

1.A.1.26.24 Branching ratios: one radionuclide may have several decay outcomes

One parent, multiple mutually exclusive modes

A parent radionuclide may transform through more than one channel, or populate multiple daughter energy levels. Each branch has a measured probability.

Partial and total decay constants

If possible decay channels have rates λ_i, total λ=Σλ_i, and branch probability b_i=λ_i/λ. The branching fractions sum to one across a complete accounting.

A simple example

A hypothetical source with 80% branch A and 20% branch B yields 800 and 200 expected channel events per 1000 parent transformations, not necessarily exactly that many in a finite count.

Emission yields differ from branch fractions

One decay may produce a cascade of multiple photons or an internal conversion electron. The yield of a specific gamma line per decay need not equal the branching fraction of an entire parent mode.

A real analytical need

Spectral counts become activities only after matching line identity, emission probability, instrument efficiency and corrections.

λ_total=Σλ_i; branch b_i=λ_i/λ_total, Σb_i=1.

1.A.1.26.25 Decay chains: daughter activity can grow while parent activity falls

The daughter may be radioactive too

In a parent→daughter→granddaughter chain, the initial parent transformation produces a daughter which may then decay independently.

Two competing processes

dN_P/dt=−λ_PN_P; dN_D/dt=λ_PN_P−λ_DN_D, assuming a closed one-step chain with no other source terms.

The surprising phenomenon of ingrowth

Starting with parents and very few daughter atoms, daughter activity can rise as daughters are produced even while parent activity decreases. Later behavior depends on both decay constants.

The Bateman equation idea

Solving coupled parent-daughter equations yields time-dependent daughter populations and activities. Applying a single exponential to every detected daughter signal is generally incorrect.

Chemical transport adds more equations

In biological tracers, different nuclear species and compounds may move among tissues. Their measured curves require both nuclear decay and transport models.

dN_P/dt=−λ_PN_P; dN_D/dt=λ_PN_P−λ_DN_D.

1.A.1.26.26 Physical half-life and biological clearance are not the same clock

Intrinsic nuclear half-life

The physical half-life describes radioactive transformation of a nuclide, under its appropriate physical environment, and does not depend on which organ the nuclide is in for ordinary cases.

Compartment clearance

Biological half-life describes loss from a specified biological pool under a defined kinetic model. Material can leave an organ without nuclear decay.

A restricted combined model

For independent first-order decay and biological clearance, 1/t_eff=1/t_phys+1/t_bio. Physical 6h and biological 3h then give effective 2h.

Biological complexity

Multiple compartments, recycling and physiological change can make an effective half-life time dependent or multi-exponential. A one-number half-life may be insufficient for actual radiopharmaceutical dosimetry.

Stable isotope studies provide a contrast

Deuterium and carbon-13 traces disappear from sampled compartments because of mixing and metabolism, not because those stable nuclei undergo radioactive decay.

Independent first-order only: 1/t_eff=1/t_phys+1/t_bio.

1.A.1.26.27 How radiation detectors turn interactions into numbers

A Geiger–Müller counter

Ionization in a gas causes a pulse. Standard GM instruments are useful for count-based monitoring but do not typically provide the high-resolution photon-energy spectrum needed to distinguish overlapping nuclides.

Scintillation detectors

A scintillating material gives light after radiation energy deposition. Photosensors turn the light into electronic signals; calibration and detector type determine energy information.

Semiconductor detectors

Ionizing interactions create electron–hole pairs in a semiconductor. High-purity germanium detectors provide high-resolution gamma spectroscopy under suitable instrumentation.

Liquid scintillation in biochemical work

Beta emitters such as tritium and carbon-14 can be measured with appropriate liquid scintillation procedures, with background, quenching and detection efficiency treated as important quality-control variables.

Which instrument answers which question?

A count rate estimates detected events; a spectrum offers energy clues; quantitative activity requires correction for geometry, source strength, emission yields, dead time and efficiency.

1.A.1.26.28 Counting noise: a statistical signal is not an exact decay tally

For independent rare counts, Poisson models are useful

In a simple model, event counts in a time interval follow a Poisson distribution with variance equal to expected count. For sufficiently large N, observed standard deviation is approximately √N.

Relative precision improves slowly

With 100 counts, random fractional counting uncertainty is about 10%; with 10,000 counts, about 1%. Reducing random uncertainty tenfold usually needs about one hundred times as many ideal counts.

Background subtraction must include both uncertainties

Subtracting an independently measured background adds the variance of sample and background counts after appropriate exposure-time scaling; the standard deviation is not generally just √(net counts).

A systematic bias remains even with many counts

An incorrectly calibrated detector can give repeatable but inaccurate results. Energy calibration, dead time, geometry and quench correction can matter more than counting noise.

Scientific conclusion

Always distinguish observed counts, count duration, efficiency-corrected activity and uncertainty; none automatically equals dose.

Poisson approximation: σ_N≈√N, relative uncertainty≈1/√N.

1.A.1.26.29 From radiation to DNA: activity, dose and biological outcome

Physical emissions interact with matter

Charged alpha and beta particles deposit energy through their charged interactions, while gamma photons often produce secondary energetic electrons. Chemical changes follow these energy transfers.

Water radiolysis and direct lesions

Ionization can damage DNA directly or produce reactive species through water radiolysis, affecting bases, sugar–phosphate backbones or both. Double-strand breaks are one possible complex lesion.

Activity and absorbed dose have distinct units

Activity Bq measures transformations per second. Absorbed dose Gy is joules deposited per kilogram. Effective or equivalent dose Sv uses radiation and sometimes tissue weighting conventions.

Source location and energy matter

Internally incorporated alpha emitters and external alpha exposure differ dramatically. Beta/gamma outcomes likewise depend on spectrum, geometric distribution, dose, time and affected tissue.

No deterministic health prediction from isotope identity

A decay mode alone does not diagnose a disease or specify biological harm. Clinical and radiation-protection conclusions require actual dose assessment and professional evaluation.

D=E_deposited/m (Gy=J/kg); 1Bq=1 transformation/s; Sv is a protection-weighted dose unit.

1.A.1.26.30 Magnetic-field deflection: an experiment distinguishing charged particles and photons

A charged particle feels a force in a magnetic field

For velocity v in magnetic field B, magnetic force is q(v×B). Alpha has positive charge +2e and beta minus electron has negative charge −e, so suitable trajectories curve in opposite senses under the same velocity orientation.

The beta-plus positron curves with positive charge

A positron is positively charged but much lighter than alpha; curvature depends on momentum as well as charge. Thus a photograph of trajectories must not be interpreted by particle charge alone.

Gamma rays have zero charge

Photons are not deflected by ordinary laboratory magnetic/electric fields through the same Lorentz-force mechanism as charged particles. Their photon direction can change through scattering in matter.

The radius formula belongs to ideal uniform fields

For a relativistic charged particle moving transversely in a uniform field, momentum p=|q|Br. A high-momentum beta can curve less than a lower-momentum beta, so there is no fixed radius for 'beta radiation'.

Historical insight

Deflection and ionization evidence helped identify radiation types before detailed nuclear models. The class should connect measured paths to charge and momentum rather than memorize ‘alpha left beta right’ without specifying the field geometry.

Uniform transverse magnetic field: p=|q|Br; gamma photons have q=0.

1.A.1.26.31 Alpha, beta and gamma penetration: an evidence-based comparison

What the simplest classroom mnemonic gets right

Many alpha particles are stopped by a relatively small amount of matter, beta particles have a different penetration profile, and high-energy gamma rays may require significant attenuation materials. These are qualitative tendencies, not universal distance measurements.

The more precise physical picture

Particle energy, absorber composition, charge, scattering and geometry determine attenuation. A low-energy beta may have a shorter range than some energetic alphas under particular conditions; gamma attenuation is probabilistic.

Internal versus external exposure

An alpha source outside intact skin and an internalized alpha emitter can have very different local biological consequences. A beta emitter's superficial interaction and a penetrating gamma emitter's dispersed interactions also depend on source location.

Secondary processes matter

Beta interactions can generate bremsstrahlung photons, and gamma photons can create energetic electrons. Therefore actual energy deposited in tissue is not completely described by the original radiation label.

Professional safety boundary

No textbook ranking is a substitute for qualified radiation-protection assessment. Detailed shielding, dose rates and source-specific controls depend on validated measurements and procedures.

1.A.1.26.32 Natural carbon-14 and potassium-40: background nuclear processes

Carbon-14 in the environment

Cosmic-ray-related nuclear reactions create carbon-14, which enters carbon-cycle reservoirs and can appear in living matter. It undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14 with physical half-life around 5730years.

Radiocarbon measurements require calibration

A radiocarbon ratio or activity can contribute age information only under a validated carbon-history, calibration and contamination model. Carbon-14's existence does not make ordinary biological carbon metabolism a nuclear reaction.

Potassium-40 as a small fraction of natural potassium

Potassium contains mostly stable potassium-39 and potassium-41 with a small natural radioactive potassium-40 component. Potassium ions are vital in living cells, but ion-channel motion does not cause K-40 decay.

Different source histories

Some radionuclides are cosmogenic, some primordial and some radiogenic daughters. Their environmental activities reflect production, transport, decay and sometimes human sources.

One count cannot identify a source

A gamma or beta measurement from a field sample requires a nuclide-specific spectrum, background subtraction and environmental context. A broad radiation count does not identify the source uniquely.

1.A.1.26.33 Tritium, carbon-14 and phosphorus-32 in biological tracer thinking

Different tracer nuclei have different emissions

Tritium (hydrogen-3) is a low-energy beta-minus radionuclide with physical half-life about 12.3years; carbon-14 also emits beta minus at low energy; phosphorus-32 is a beta-minus emitter with a physical half-life about 14.3days. These nuclear properties differ.

Chemical incorporation provides biological specificity

A radioactive label can be part of a metabolite, nucleic acid or biological probe. Its chemistry determines where the labelled molecule travels, while its nuclear decay enables detection through emitted particles.

The detector is part of the scientific model

Low-energy beta emissions may require appropriate liquid-scintillation or specialized methods with quench and efficiency correction. Raw counts per minute are not automatically disintegrations per minute.

A tracer rate is not a radioactive decay rate

Label appearing in a protein or DNA sample can reflect incorporation, turnover and sample preparation. To infer biosynthetic rate, the experiment needs precursor specific activity, pool size, time course and mathematical assumptions.

Ethical laboratory boundary

Radioactive tracer experiments require institutional approval, professional radiation safety and validated methods; this chapter explains why they work, not how to obtain or handle radioactive materials.

1.A.1.26.34 PET: beta-plus decay leads to annihilation photons

The nuclear event comes first

A positron-emitting radionuclide undergoes beta-plus transformation with daughter Z one lower and a neutrino emitted. A common medical imaging radionuclide is fluorine-18.

Positron motion follows

After emission, the positron transfers kinetic energy to tissue. Eventually it may encounter an electron and annihilate, commonly producing two photons of approximately 511keV each in nearly opposite directions when net momentum is small.

Why PET detects coincidences

Coincident detection of these annihilation photons helps constrain the line along which an annihilation occurred. Reconstruction of distributions involves geometry, attenuation, scattering, random coincidences and corrections.

Nuclear transition is not the same as annihilation

The annihilation photons are created when the positron interacts with an electron in matter; they are not necessarily gamma photons directly produced by nuclear de-excitation of the beta daughter.

Biochemical interpretation has another layer

The PET tracer's compound determines tissue uptake and metabolic relevance. Signal intensity does not equal enzyme activity or pathology without radiopharmaceutical modeling and clinical interpretation.

e⁺+e⁻→γ+γ; at near-rest annihilation, each photon energy≈511keV.

1.A.1.26.35 SPECT and technetium-99m: detecting nuclear gamma emissions

A nuclear isomer provides a photon signal

Technetium-99m is a metastable nuclide with near-six-hour physical half-life and strong clinically useful gamma emissions near 140keV.

The gamma source is nuclear excitation

In an isomeric transition, Z and A remain 43 and 99, respectively. The emitted gamma photon reflects energy released between nuclear states; internal conversion pathways may also occur.

Collimation and projection

SPECT cameras detect photons from multiple angles to reconstruct a distribution of radiopharmaceutical activity, influenced by detector response and attenuation corrections.

Why SPECT differs from PET

SPECT commonly detects directly emitted photons in nuclear transitions; PET relies on paired annihilation photons following positron emission. Both require tracer chemistry and computational reconstruction.

Clinical caution

No single image brightness or physical half-life proves a diagnosis. Radiopharmaceutical kinetics, protocol choices, instruments and clinical context are necessary.

1.A.1.26.36 Iodine-131: why emission type matters in thyroid-related medicine

Beta and gamma can coexist

Iodine-131 decays predominantly by beta-minus transformation to xenon-131 and also produces gamma emissions through excited daughter states. Its physical half-life is about eight days.

Chemistry and nuclear properties have different roles

Iodide handling by thyroid tissue is a biological/chemical process; nuclear beta radiation and gamma photons have different physical roles in possible tissue energy deposition and detection.

Therapeutic and diagnostic contexts differ

Nuclear medicine uses radionuclides according to emissions, biokinetics, targeting and clinical goals. The same element's isotope choice can change radiation physics dramatically.

No dose from isotope name alone

A patient-specific absorbed dose cannot be calculated solely from I-131 half-life or administered activity. Tissue residence, decay energies, transport and dosimetry models matter.

A stable iodine comparison

The common stable iodine isotope iodine-127 participates in thyroid physiology without spontaneous radioactive decay. Elemental identity does not determine whether a particular isotope is stable.

1.A.1.26.37 Alpha-targeted applications and high local ionization

Alpha energy tracks differ from photons

An alpha has +2 charge and high mass, causing dense ionization along comparatively short trajectories for typical energies. This high-LET character can be relevant when an alpha-emitting radionuclide is spatially localized within target tissue.

Targeting is a chemical/biological problem

The molecule or carrier determines where the radionuclide travels; the nuclide determines physical decay properties. Accurate targeting and control of non-target tissue exposure require specific clinical evaluation.

Microscopic distribution matters

Equal macroscopic activity values may yield different microscopic dose patterns depending on localization, internalization and emissions. The mean activity alone cannot describe cellular dose heterogeneity.

Biological repair depends on context

Complex clustered damage can be associated with densely ionizing tracks, but clinical effect cannot be predicted solely from radiation label or binding energy.

Professional boundary

These are high-level scientific principles; medical administration, preparation, source selection and protection require specialized licensed practice.

1.A.1.26.38 Scientific inference from a radioactive time-activity curve

What a time series actually measures

A detector reports events over time in a selected channel. After calibration, one may infer activity in a compartment; that compartment's activity can change due to radionuclide decay, movement of labelled compounds and daughter ingrowth.

Basic physical correction

If only physical decay affects a closed pure source, corrected activity follows A(t)=A₀e^(−λt). If material enters or leaves the measurement region, additional transport terms are required.

A compartment with biological clearance

An independent first-order clearance model gives λ_eff=λ_phys+λ_bio. Multicompartmental biological transport may not obey a single exponential, and radiolabeled metabolites can behave differently.

Branching and detector response

A gamma-line count rate depends on nuclide activity, photon emission probability, detector efficiency, attenuation, background and timing. Counting a detected signal is not equivalent to measuring biochemical flux.

What makes a strong published conclusion

State the nuclide, decay data, instrument calibration, biological compartment, time points, uncertainty and model assumptions. Without them, claims of exact uptake, half-life or reaction rate exceed the data.
