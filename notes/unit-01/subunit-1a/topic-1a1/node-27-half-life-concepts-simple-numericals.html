1.A.1.27.1 A disappearing tracer: the scientific question behind half-life

A small laboratory mystery

Imagine two identical sealed samples of the same radioactive tracer. A counter measures each at the same starting time; the second measurement on the following day shows a lower net signal. Did the isotope evaporate? Did its atoms simply stop being radioactive? Or did individual nuclei transform? The central explanation is nuclear transformation: each parent nucleus has some probability of decaying during an interval.

Now imagine a student says: 'Half of my sample disappeared, so half-life must be one day.' That conclusion requires care: the detector response must track the parent activity, geometry must remain comparable, background must be corrected, and the decay must be approximated by one exponential.

Where the count goes

The nucleus may transform while the substance, molecular label and detector setup remain present. A second signal can fall for non-nuclear reasons such as sample leakage, changing detector position or transfer of labelled material into another compartment. That is why a time-activity curve needs a clearly defined measurement context.

We will use three voices throughout the chapter: the microscopic voice of probability, the mathematical voice of exponentials, and the practical voice of biology. They agree only when the variables and assumptions are kept explicit.

Exam checkpoint: A decrease in a tracer's measured signal is evidence to investigate, not automatic proof that nuclear half-life alone caused the decrease.

1.A.1.27.2 A chronological discovery: from measurements to a universal law

Before a nucleus had a clear identity

Henri Becquerel observed spontaneous emissions from uranium salts in 1896. Marie and Pierre Curie's investigations made radioactivity measurable and showed that its presence followed specific substances even under changing chemical circumstances. These discoveries preceded a detailed nuclear picture.

What the experiments actually established

The transformation idea replaced the older expectation that elements are necessarily immutable: radioactive parents can become different daughter nuclides. Measured rates across many radioactive materials led to the exponential law, with a different decay constant for each nuclear state.

Quantitative half-lives now come from evaluated experimental measurements rather than a formula inferred solely from mass number, atomic number or radiation type. Alpha emitters, beta emitters and gamma-emitting isomers can all have very different half-lives.

Exam checkpoint: Discovery chronology explains why an empirical rate law can be trustworthy before its microscopic mechanism is fully known.

1.A.1.27.3 Parent nuclei, daughter nuclei and what is actually counted

Follow one species at a time

Write N(t) for the number of undecayed parent nuclei at time t. If an atom of the parent radionuclide decays, it leaves that parent population. Its decay products may remain inside the container, but the particular parent is gone as a parent.

From one nucleus to one population

Consider 400 radioactive parent atoms in a sealed teaching model. After an elapsed interval with survival probability 0.25, the expectation is 100 surviving parents and 300 parent transformations. A real experiment may observe a different integer, especially if only hundreds of atoms are present.

The daughters produced are not necessarily 300 stable atoms of one species: a transformation can make a radioactive daughter that undergoes further decay, and branching may produce more than one possible product. Track one explicitly specified parent before summing the chain.

Exam checkpoint: A parent fraction is not a mass fraction for a whole complex molecule.

1.A.1.27.4 Radioactive randomness at the nuclear scale

Constant probability does not mean predictable destiny

Over a very short interval Δt, an undecayed nucleus in a constant nuclear environment has probability approximately λΔt of transforming. This is a conditional probability: a nucleus that has already survived many intervals is not 'due' to decay. Its remaining lifetime has the same statistical distribution as that of an equivalent nucleus freshly considered.

A probability statement, not a prediction of identity

For an unstable parent with decay constant λ, the probability of survival across a finite interval t is e−λt, and the probability of decay during that interval is 1−e−λt. Approximation λΔt is only valid for very short intervals, not a substitute for exact probability over long times.

A population of N initially undecayed independent nuclei has expected survivors Ne−λt. Individual survival events make the observed count fluctuate around this expectation; for a large population relative fluctuations are comparatively small.

Exam checkpoint: Constant per-time hazard is memoryless; it does not mean every nucleus waits exactly T before decaying.

1.A.1.27.5 Building the differential equation dN/dt = −λN

Why the remaining number determines the decay rate

If each surviving parent has the same small probability λΔt of transformation, the expected number decaying during Δt is approximately λNΔt. The parent population therefore changes by ΔN ≈ −λNΔt. Divide by Δt and let the interval shrink.

Why the negative sign belongs there

The differential equation uses N for the number not yet transformed. If λ were negative, the simple law would predict growth of the parent population even when no new parent is produced. A positive λ and explicit negative sign represent decay.

An integration constant is fixed by N(0)=N₀, producing ln[N(t)/N₀]=−λt. The result also tells us that an equal fractional drop occurs over equal intervals regardless of the number of parents then surviving.

dN/N = −λdt   →   ln[N/N₀]=−λt   →   N=N₀e−λt

1.A.1.27.6 Understanding e and the logarithm in a biology problem

Why an unfamiliar number appears

The constant e ≈ 2.71828 is the natural base for continuous proportional change. A differential equation saying 'the speed of loss is proportional to what remains' produces e−λt. The exponential does not mean radioactivity only changes after integer half-lives; transformation continues throughout time.

Why logarithms are worth learning

A logarithm asks what exponent produces a ratio. For example, ln(0.5) ≈ −0.693, ln(0.25) ≈ −1.386 and ln(0.125) ≈ −2.079. Their equal steps encode equal successive half-lives.

If a calculator provides 'log' without saying the base, identify its convention before substituting. The time formula can also use log₁₀ consistently: t=(T/log₁₀2)log₁₀(N₀/N). Mixing ln in the numerator with log₁₀ in the denominator is wrong.

Exam checkpoint: For f<1, ln(f)<0. A physically elapsed time must come out positive.

1.A.1.27.7 Half-life as a statistical population property

Definition without shortcuts

The physical half-life T1/2 is the elapsed time at which the expected number of undecayed nuclei of a given parent radionuclide is half its starting number under the standard single-exponential model. The same time also halves that parent's activity, provided λ remains unchanged.

A simple fraction table

At 0T, 1T, 2T, 3T and 4T, ideal expected parent fractions are 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16. The cumulative transformed fractions are 0, 1/2, 3/4, 7/8 and 15/16 respectively.

Notice the contrast: each new interval removes fewer atoms in absolute number than the previous interval, yet the same 50 percent of the remaining parent population is transformed on average. This is exactly what a decreasing exponential means.

1.A.1.27.8 Deriving the exact connection between λ and half-life

Start with the exponential law

At the moment t = T1/2, N/N₀ = 1/2. Substitute that defining fraction into N/N₀ = e−λt. Take the natural logarithm on both sides; ln(1/2) = −ln2.

A unit-level example

If T = 8 min, then λ = 0.693147/8 ≈ 0.08664 min−1. To express in s−1, divide by 60: λ ≈ 0.001444 s−1. Those two values represent the same physical decay constant.

If λ = 0.25 h−1, then T = 0.693147/0.25 ≈ 2.773 hours. Do not claim that λ is 25 percent 'per hour' without clarifying the exponential probability: the exact survival fraction over one hour is e−0.25 ≈ 0.7788.

Exam checkpoint: The exact decay probability during time Δt is 1−e−λΔt, not λΔt except as a short-time approximation.

1.A.1.27.9 Mean lifetime is not the same as half-life

Two different questions

Half-life asks when the surviving population falls to 50 percent of its initial size. Mean lifetime τ asks for the expected time to decay of a nucleus selected from the exponential distribution. The survival probability is e−t/τ.

A distribution viewpoint

The exponential lifetime distribution has probability density p(t)=λe−λt for t≥0. Half-life is the median of this distribution because half the original ensemble survives to T. Mean lifetime is the average ∫₀∞t p(t)dt=1/λ.

For T=6 h, mean lifetime is approximately 8.66 h. The most probable lifetime in the continuous exponential density is near zero, another reason that 'average', 'median' and 'exact decay time' should not be used as synonyms.

1.A.1.27.10 The equation in half-life form: the easiest starting point

Eliminate λ from the expression

Insert λ = ln2/T into N = N₀e−λt. Since eln2 = 2, we obtain the familiar factor 2−t/T. This formula is easier for quick whole and fractional half-life calculations.

A convenient change of variable

Let n=t/T. It represents the number of half-lives, whether integer or fractional. Then f=2−n. This separates the arithmetic from the choice of seconds, hours, days or years.

For a nuclide with T=14 days and t=35 days, n=2.5. The parent fraction is about 17.68 percent and the transformed fraction is about 82.32 percent. Both percentages must add to 100 within rounding.

n=t/T;   f=2−n;   transformed fraction=1−f.

1.A.1.27.11 Remaining fraction, percentage and decayed fraction

Three closely related but distinct results

For a closed initial parent population, remaining fraction f = N/N₀. Percentage remaining = 100f. Fraction that has decayed by time t is d = 1 − f. Percentage decayed = 100(1 − f). Do not confuse fraction with percentage.

Translate questions into one mathematical symbol first

Words such as 'survives', 'remains', 'undecayed' and 'residual parent' point to f. Words such as 'has decayed', 'transformed' and 'lost from parent population' point to 1−f.

If 2/3 of the original parents have decayed, then the remaining fraction is 1/3, and n=log₂3≈1.585 half-lives. A hurried student may substitute 2/3 directly as the remaining fraction and obtain the wrong time.

Exam checkpoint: Read whether the fraction is remaining or decayed before applying logarithms.

1.A.1.27.12 Counting complete half-lives without a calculator

Create a sequence, not a subtraction table

If 640 parent units have T = 2 hours, then at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 hours the expected remaining amounts are 640, 320, 160, 80, 40. Each step halves the previous amount.

Back-calculation example

In a textbook sample with T=5 min, 50 parent units remain after 20 min. Four half-lives have elapsed, so N₀=50×16=800 units. Checking forwards: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50.

For an amount like 7.5 parent units, treat the result as an expectation or a macroscopic proportional quantity, not as 7.5 literal intact nuclei. Individual nuclei occur in integer counts.

1.A.1.27.13 Non-integer half-lives and fractional times

Decay is continuous, not stepwise

After half of one half-life, the remaining fraction is 2−0.5 = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071; it is not 0.75. Linear interpolation between the 100 percent and 50 percent points is inaccurate because the curve is exponential.

A half-interval has a square-root effect

Across half of T, survival is √(1/2)≈0.7071; across a quarter of T, it is the fourth root of 1/2≈0.8409. Those are exact exponential consequences, not arbitrary interpolation rules.

A quick bound helps prevent calculator mistakes. For 1.5T, f must be between 1/4 and 1/2. A calculated f of 0.60 would be impossible under the model, even before detailed arithmetic.

1.A.1.27.14 Solving for elapsed time using logarithms

The inverse problem

Sometimes a problem gives the original and present populations and asks how long the decay took. Starting from f = 2−t/T, take ln both sides, then rearrange.

Inverse calculation and interpretation

When an observed parent fraction is 0.20, the elapsed count of half-lives is log₂5≈2.322. For T=3 hours, the elapsed time is ≈6.966 hours. This is a theoretical age or elapsed time relative to the stated starting activity.

Do not infer time from current activity alone without a starting reference: 20 Bq could be the beginning of one experiment or the end of another. Two measurements or an initial calibration can supply the necessary ratio.

1.A.1.27.15 Finding an unknown half-life from two measurements

Use a ratio instead of guessing the starting amount

Suppose net activity of a pure radionuclide is A₁ at time t₁ and A₂ at time t₂, measured in comparable conditions with no source production. The interval Δt = t₂ − t₁ is what matters. The ratio A₂/A₁ = 2−Δt/T.

A ratio from two arbitrary time points

Suppose the measured parent activity is 900 Bq at t₁ and 300 Bq at t₂ exactly 12 hours later. The activity ratio is 3, so T=12ln2/ln3≈7.57 hours. The initial activity at t=0 is unnecessary for this ratio method.

In real measurements, use net background-corrected rates or calibrated activities under comparable counting conditions. If a source has physical removal or new production between time points, the inferred 'half-life' may describe a combination of processes, not the nuclear property.

1.A.1.27.16 A strict unit-conversion protocol

The exponent must not carry units

In e−λt, λ and t must cancel units; λ in s−1 requires t in seconds. In 2−t/T, express t and T using the same time unit.

Why inverse time is an unusual unit

If T is expressed in days then λ comes out in day−1. This is not activity. Only λ multiplied by the number of parent nuclei, with units converted to seconds if Bq is required, produces activity.

For T=1 day, λ=0.693147 day−1≈8.022×10⁻⁶ s−1. This is a useful benchmark when checking a calculator result.

1.A.1.27.17 Activity: transformations per second are not atoms per second of exposure

What the becquerel measures

Activity A is the expected number of nuclear transformations per second: 1 Bq = 1 decay/s. It is not the number of photons measured, not a radiation dose and not the number of daughter atoms visible in a detector.

Counts per minute are not Bq

A detector may record 600 net counts/min. That is 10 net counts/s at the detector, but it is not necessarily a 10-Bq source. If the probability of a detected event per parent transformation were an independently known 0.20 under a simple one-count-per-decay model, the inferred source activity would be about 50 Bq.

Such conversion requires a justified response probability including emission abundance and detection efficiency. A mixed emission scheme can produce zero, one or multiple detector pulses per nuclear transformation.

1.A.1.27.18 Turning measured mass into number of radioactive nuclei

The mole connects chemistry with a nuclear population

For a pure sample of a single nuclide with molar mass M, m grams corresponds to m/M moles. Multiply by Avogadro's constant NA ≈ 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol−1 to obtain the approximate number of nuclei.

Exact mole and approximate mass

The Avogadro constant is exactly 6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹ in SI. In typical elementary numerical problems, using 6.022×10²³ is sufficient, but the nuclide's molar mass and isotope fraction are still approximations or measured values.

For m=2×10⁻⁶ g and M=200 g/mol, N≈6.022×10¹⁵ nuclei if the material consists entirely of the specified radionuclide. The exercise should clearly distinguish 'mass of pure radionuclide' from 'mass of solution or labelled compound'.

1.A.1.27.19 Specific activity and the danger of a long-half-life shortcut

Derive the relation

Specific activity is activity per mass for a particular radionuclide or sample specification. For a pure radionuclide at a fixed composition, A/m = λNA/M. Its units may be Bq/g.

Relating activity and half-life for two nuclides

At equal parent atom number N, activity ratios satisfy A₁/A₂=λ₁/λ₂=T₂/T₁. At equal activity A, the population ratio is N₁/N₂=T₁/T₂. The direction of the ratio reverses depending on what is held fixed.

For example, if T₁=2 h and T₂=8 h with the same parent count, A₁ is four times A₂. With equal starting activities instead, nuclide 2 requires four times as many nuclei.

1.A.1.27.20 Decay graphs: reading curves without falling into visual traps

Use both axes before calculating

Determine whether the vertical axis displays parent number, parent activity, percentage remaining, log activity or daughter accumulation. Read the horizontal units and whether the origin is a true reference time.

Check a curve against the fraction table

On linear axes, the 50 percent mark should occur at T and the 25 percent mark at 2T. On log axes the same exponential is a straight line. In either display, the horizontal distance from 80 to 40 must equal that from 40 to 20 for ideal parent behavior.

A flattened trace at late times may be caused by background rather than a physically infinite parent half-life. Reanalyze net counts before drawing a nuclear conclusion.

1.A.1.27.21 The logarithmic graph: turning a curve into a line

A useful research transformation

Start from ln N = ln N₀ − λt. When plotting natural logarithm of parent activity versus time for a single constant-λ parent, the result is linear, with slope −λ. The intercept estimates ln A₀.

A numerical slope example

Suppose ln(A/A₀) is −0.7 at 2 h and −1.4 at 4 h. The slope is (−1.4+0.7)/(4−2)=−0.35 h⁻¹, so λ=0.35 h⁻¹ and T≈1.98 h. The fact that the relationship passes close to 0 at t=0 supports the chosen reference.

If instead log₁₀(A) falls by 0.30103 every 6 hours, it halves every 6 hours, because log₁₀(2)=0.30103. Remember the base used by the plotted logarithm.

Original semilog schematic: the logarithm of a single exponential decreases linearly with time.

1.A.1.27.22 Counting experiments: background is not radioactive parent activity

Counts are observations, not transformations

A detector sees a subset of emitted interactions. If a sample registers 4500 counts in 5 minutes and the background contributes 300 counts in an equal interval, net sample counts are 4200 per 5 minutes, or 14 counts/s.

A rate correction with unequal durations

A sample measured for 2 min registers 800 counts total, while a background run of 5 min registers 100 counts. The sample's observed rate is 400 counts/min and background rate is 20 counts/min. Net sample rate is 380 counts/min, not 700 counts in any meaningful equal interval.

The expected background in the 2-minute sample window is 40 counts. For independent Poisson observations, the variance of the net 2-minute count estimate is approximately 800+(2/5)²×100=816 counts², so the standard deviation is about 28.6 counts.

1.A.1.27.23 Statistical uncertainty: why measurements fluctuate

Randomness enters twice

Nuclear decays occur at random, and detected events are a random sample. In a simple independent counting experiment, the number of detected events often follows Poisson statistics approximately: standard deviation √C for an observed count C.

Use uncertainty to decide what a graph can show

If successive corrected rates differ by only a few counts while each measurement has uncertainty of tens of counts, apparent fluctuations cannot establish a new decay mode. Longer counting and better calibration may be required.

Counting times themselves matter: for a fixed rate, four times the time yields roughly four times as many counts and about half the relative Poisson uncertainty. This improvement does not eliminate systematic detector effects.

1.A.1.27.24 Daughter products do not always follow the parent curve

One decay creates a new species

In a single parent-to-stable-daughter reaction, each parent transformation produces one daughter if no losses or other production intervene. The expected stable daughter amount rises as D(t) = D₀ + N₀(1−e−λt). The parent amount falls; the daughter increases.

The daughter balance in the simplest closed system

Suppose 100000 parent nuclei begin with no daughter and the daughter is stable. After two half-lives, expected parent N=25000 and expected newly formed daughter D=75000. Their sum stays 100000 in the model.

A detector may observe radiation from parent transformation and from excited daughter relaxation. The number of detected gamma photons is not generally equal to D or to N₀−N because photon yields and branch structures vary.

1.A.1.27.25 Physical, biological and effective half-lives

Three clocks answer three questions

Physical half-life Tp describes loss by nuclear transformation. Biological half-life Tb describes loss from a defined body compartment by biological elimination or redistribution in a specified model. Effective half-life Te describes loss of radioactive material from that compartment when both independent first-order effects occur.

Why 'effective' needs a defined compartment

For physical T=6 h and a biological compartment half-time of 3 h, the effective T is (6×3)/(6+3)=2 h. Two independent first-order removal rates produce a faster decline than either process alone.

If material is redistributed rather than fully excreted from the organism, a local organ concentration can halve while total body burden has not. Describe the compartment before attributing a 'biological half-life' to an organism.

1.A.1.27.26 Multi-compartment biology is not one textbook exponential

Why a tracer can appear fast and then slow

A labelled molecule may move from blood into tissues, be metabolized and excreted at different rates. A measured tissue time-activity curve can contain a rapid distribution phase and a slower retention phase. Fitting one straight log plot across all times can conceal these processes.

Why a fitted fast phase is not necessarily a new nuclear half-life

A labelled biological sample might show a rapid drop while excess tracer clears from blood, followed by slower decline associated with tissue retention. The nucleus retains its physical decay constant throughout; redistribution changes where detectable material is found.

For a two-exponential fit, fractions and amplitudes depend on the observed compartment and detection method. The effective half-time of the sum is not simply an average of its two component half-times.

1.A.1.27.27 Nuclear medicine timing: physical decay versus biodistribution

A familiar example

Technetium-99m has a physical half-life of about 6 hours. If a preparation begins with reference activity A₀, its nuclear decay correction over time t is A = A₀2−t/Tp. This is useful for understanding why an activity value needs a reference time.

Separate three components in an imaging problem

Nuclear activity is determined by the number of radioactive parents and λ. Radioactive signal in an organ depends on biodistribution, clearance, decay and detection. Image quality and absorbed dose depend on additional physical and biological factors.

In a basic exam question a stated physical half-life can calculate a decay correction between reference times. Do not infer how much material a patient received or which shielding is appropriate from that isolated number.

1.A.1.27.28 Radiocarbon dating: a half-life becomes a historical clock

Where the original reference comes from

Carbon-14 forms in nature and becomes part of the living carbon cycle. When exchange with the environment stops, the sample's carbon-14 inventory evolves by nuclear decay. A first approximation to elapsed time follows from the remaining fraction of radiocarbon relative to an appropriate baseline.

The reference must describe the sample

Radiocarbon methods estimate ratios relative to a historical reference, corrected as required by scientific conventions. The fraction of modern carbon from an instrument is not necessarily an unmodified N/N₀ of a single closed vessel.

In classroom calculation, one uses a simplified fixed half-life and starting abundance. In authentic radiocarbon interpretation, contamination, calibration curves, reservoir offsets and fractionation must be handled before making a calendar-date claim.

1.A.1.27.29 Radiometric timekeeping is not limited to carbon-14

The parent–daughter approach

Other radiometric dating methods use known nuclear decay constants and parent–daughter isotope measurements in minerals or rocks. Calculations require geochemical assumptions, especially whether the system remained effectively closed to the parent and relevant daughter isotopes after a defined event.

A closed-system condition is a scientific hypothesis

If external water or other geochemical processes move parent or daughter isotopes into or out of a rock after formation, a simple closed-system age formula can fail. It is the interpretation of measured ratios, not the nuclear decay constant itself, that becomes invalid.

This is useful beyond geology: any 'chemical age' inferred from a remaining tracer needs a justified baseline, a specified compartment and a model that accounts for gain and loss outside nuclear decay.

1.A.1.27.30 Stable isotopes: no radioactive half-life to calculate

The word isotope does not mean radioactive

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable isotopes; carbon-14 is radioactive. Oxygen-18 is stable and may be used in isotope tracing without nuclear decay. A stable isotope has no finite radioactive half-life in the usual sense.

Stable tracer kinetics are not nuclear kinetics

A student may observe stable oxygen-18 label wash out of tissue after 20 min and incorrectly call that oxygen-18's radioactive half-life. There is no radioactive loss mechanism being measured. The reported half-time belongs to the tracer movement or chemical system.

Conversely, a radioactive isotope in a stable molecule can undergo nuclear decay even if that molecule is unchanged before decay. Its chemical state and physical nuclear half-life represent different layers of explanation.

1.A.1.27.31 Pulse–chase experiments and radioactive label survival

Two independent reasons a label signal may decline

In a pulse–chase experiment, a labelled precursor is provided temporarily and then replaced by unlabelled material. A radioactive label can produce a signal change because of parent nuclear decay, protein turnover, dilution, transport, degradation or changes in detection.

Turnover and physical decay may be disentangled

A pulse–chase with an unstable parent isotope can estimate biological turnover only after modeling physical decay and assay response. If the isotope has T=5 days and the protein disappears biologically with a first-order T=5 days, the combined observed label may have Te=2.5 days in a simple compartment.

This reciprocal formula would be invalid if turnover were saturation-limited, if new labelled molecules entered the pool, or if multiple pools exchanged labelled molecules at different rates.

1.A.1.27.32 Do not confuse half-life with chemical or pharmacological half-life

The same word appears in several sciences

Drug elimination half-life describes an operational decrease in plasma concentration under a pharmacokinetic model; mRNA half-life describes persistence of transcript molecules; protein turnover half-life refers to changes in populations of proteins. These processes are not nuclear transformations.

Analogous rate laws do not imply identical causation

For a first-order chemical process, reaction molecules have a rate constant that may depend strongly on temperature, pH and catalysts. For ordinary physical nuclear decay, changing laboratory temperature does not turn a long-lived nuclide into a short-lived one.

The same ln2/k formula is a mathematical property of a constant fractional-loss model. Students should always name what k measures, rather than assuming every half-life is a radioactive one.

1.A.1.27.33 Plot reading with paired half-value points

How to infer T from a plotted curve

Suppose a graph shows net activity 320 arbitrary units at 1 hour, 160 at 5 hours and 80 at 9 hours. Each halving takes 4 hours; the inferred half-life is 4 hours. One should not use the starting time of the axis as proof that the original activity was 320.

Check repeated x-distances

To find a physical half-life from a graph, inspect the time difference between A=400 and A=200, then between A=200 and A=100. If these are 6 h and 6 h, T=6 h. If they differ substantially, investigate reading error or a nonideal model.

Absolute activity values do not define the half-life by themselves: an activity of 50 Bq can be reached quickly or slowly depending on the isotope and its initial population.

1.A.1.27.34 Decay correction in quantitative assays

Normalize to an explicit reference time

When comparing nuclear activity measurements taken at different times, one can compute the expected value at a common reference time tᵣ. For a measured parent activity at t, A(tᵣ) = A(t)eλ(t−tᵣ). This is a mathematical normalization, not a physical restoration of lost parent nuclei.

Reference-time correction requires care with direction

If A=100 Bq now and T=3 hours, its ideal parent activity 6 hours earlier was 400 Bq. Six hours later it will be 25 Bq. One moves backward in time by multiplying by 2 per T, and forward by dividing.

This operation does not preserve total emitted energy, undo radioactive transformations or reconstruct biological clearance. It is mathematical decay normalization only.

1.A.1.27.35 The slope of a decay-rate curve and time intervals

Two appearances of 'rate'

The population loss rate is dN/dt = −λN; the positive activity A = −dN/dt = λN. Because activity itself declines, its derivative dA/dt = −λA. Confusing A with dA/dt introduces an extra λ and wrong dimensions.

An integrated-count calculation

Suppose A₀=1000 Bq and λ=0.1 s⁻¹. Then N₀=A₀/λ=10000 parent nuclei. In the first 10 s, expected transformations are 10000(1−e⁻¹)≈6321, not A₀×10=10000.

The approximation counts≈initial activity×time is reasonable only for time intervals much shorter than the decay time scale, when activity changes little. For long intervals use the integral.

1.A.1.27.36 Half-value layer is a thickness, not a duration

A similar-looking equation with a different variable

Nuclear half-life measures changes in parent abundance over time. A half-value layer (HVL) describes a material thickness that halves an idealized narrow-beam photon intensity through attenuation: I = I₀e−μx.

A deliberate dimensional test

A shielding problem gives μ=0.2 cm⁻¹. Its narrow-beam HVL=ln2/0.2≈3.47 cm, not 3.47 seconds. A decay problem gives λ=0.2 h⁻¹; its physical T=3.47 h, not cm.

Similar algebra occurs because both are exponential proportional-loss processes, but the independent variables, assumptions and units are different.

1.A.1.27.37 Different radionuclides in one sample

Why one universal half-life cannot describe mixtures

If a sample contains two independent radionuclide species with physical half-lives T₁ and T₂, the total activity is A(t) = A₁₀2−t/T₁+A₂₀2−t/T₂. The total need not fall to one-half after either isotope's half-life.

A second mixture calculation

If two isotopes have initial activities 80 Bq (T=1 h) and 40 Bq (T=4 h), then after 2 h the respective activities are 20 Bq and 40×2⁻⁰⋅⁵≈28.28 Bq. Total≈48.28 Bq; the starting total was 120 Bq.

The total fraction ≈0.402 is not generally expressible as 2⁻²/T for a single constant T over all observation times. A chosen effective overall halving time could vary as the mixture composition changes.

1.A.1.27.38 Why ten half-lives are not the same as complete disappearance

The mathematical limit

For any finite positive t, e−λt is greater than zero. The exponential model approaches zero asymptotically; the expected population never literally reaches zero at a finite time.

A very small remaining fraction can still be a large count

If N₀=10¹⁴ radioactive nuclei, after 10 half-lives the expected remaining number is about 9.77×10¹⁰ nuclei. Calling that 'all gone' is not scientifically defensible; the measurement context and the radiation properties still matter.

There is a subtle contrast between expected fractional survival and the eventual disappearance of a finite ensemble. Exponential expectations are smooth; individual atom counts are discrete.

1.A.1.27.39 How half-life relates to risk—and how it does not

The misleading single-number judgement

A radionuclide's half-life says how rapidly its nuclei transform, not by itself how much energy is deposited in tissue or which organs retain the material. Radiation type and energy, chemical form, exposure route and distribution, duration and shielding all matter.

What nuclear physics can and cannot rank

Two samples with the same activity may differ in emitted particle energy, biological uptake and exposure pathways. Half-life is one necessary descriptor but cannot replace radionuclide identity, actual activity, chemical form and exposure geometry.

In an exam, reject any statement such as 'a long-half-life isotope is always safer' or 'the shorter the half-life, the greater the medical danger' unless all other relevant factors are defined and a valid comparison is constructed.

1.A.1.27.40 Uncertainty, significant figures and model limitations

State what is measured and what is assumed

A half-life inferred from two detector measurements has uncertainty from Poisson counts, background subtraction, instrument stability and elapsed-time accuracy. A calculation using a tabulated physical T has uncertainty associated with the evaluated nuclear data.

A realistic precision exercise

If measured corrected activities are 200±14 and 100±10 Bq separated by 6.0±0.1 h, the nominal result suggests T≈6 h, but reporting exactly '6.0000 h' ignores measurement uncertainty.

For a rough first-order estimate, ratios and logarithms propagate count uncertainties. More careful analysis uses repeated measurements, calibrated background treatment, statistical model fitting and stated confidence/uncertainty intervals.

1.A.1.27.41 A reliable four-step solving algorithm

Step 1: Identify the physical system

Ask whether the problem concerns a single decaying radionuclide, a mixed sample, an observed net detector signal, a biological compartment or a pharmacological half-life. Name the quantity N, A, C or count rate.

A reusable worked-example layout

Given: label the isotope or hypothetical nuclide, the initial and final data, the time reference and desired quantity. Equation: write the model before numbers. Substitute: convert units and form a dimensionless exponent or ratio. Result: give an appropriately rounded numerical value. Meaning: explain parent amount, activity or biological process without overclaiming.

Check using a second route whenever possible: repeated halving versus exponentials; derived λ versus the half-life; computed decayed fraction versus 1−remaining. This produces exam answers that are both fast and scientifically reliable.

1.A.1.27.42 The short formula collection before practice

Seven relationships worth deriving and remembering

The entire elementary numerical course follows from a fixed parent decay constant and the exponential law. Remember the meaning of each expression rather than treating them as unrelated formulas.

Use one vocabulary consistently

The 'survival fraction' f=N/N₀ is not the 'decayed fraction' 1−f. Activity A=λN is transformations per second, not simply N. Half-life T is the median survival time, whereas τ is the mean lifetime.

The activity-to-number conversion requires the correct λ units. Effective biological half-life adds independent loss rates under a model; it cannot be applied to any arbitrary mixed time-activity curve.

Exam checkpoint: Always validate assumptions before applying the neatest-looking formula.
