1.A.2.01.1 A familiar flame, an unfamiliar question

Light from a candle seems unremarkable until we ask what the flame can tell us about the matter inside it. A sodium-containing sample may tint a flame yellow. Hydrogen gas excited in a discharge tube can glow pinkish, yet a closer examination shows that its light is not distributed continuously across all visible colours. Hidden inside an ordinary-looking glow is a striking regularity.

Now imagine separating that glow into wavelengths. If every colour were represented equally, it would be easy to describe the light as a blend. Instead, hydrogen yields a small set of particularly sharp spectral lines. The challenge for nineteenth-century physicists was that an atom seemed to favour certain energies of light and to avoid the energies in between. What property of the atom could produce such selectivity?

That question marks the beginning of electronic structure as an experimental problem. We already know from Topic 1.A.1 that atoms contain nuclei and electrons. In this chapter we shall not begin by assigning electrons to shells or filling boxes. We shall first examine the evidence that forced scientists to propose distinct electronic energy states.

What you will be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to distinguish a continuous spectrum from line and band spectra; reconstruct the sequence of observations leading to the hydrogen spectral formula; recognize the major hydrogen series; relate emitted or absorbed light to differences between allowed atomic energies; and explain why the spectral evidence makes electron configuration a meaningful next question.

The chapter follows the historical question before the modern answer. That ordering matters: a line spectrum was measured long before quantum orbitals were developed.

1.A.2.01.2 What scientists mean by a spectrum

A distribution, not simply a rainbow

A spectrum describes how a measured property of radiation is distributed with wavelength, frequency, or another spectral coordinate. A spectrum may be represented as coloured light, as intensity plotted against wavelength, or as a numerical table. These are different displays of the same underlying measurement when carefully defined.

A prism bends different wavelengths by different amounts; a diffraction grating separates radiation by interference. Both can make an otherwise mixed light source reveal a structured pattern. The device used to separate and observe light is generally called a spectroscope or spectrometer, depending on its purpose and design.

Why the horizontal axis matters

A graph labelled 'Intensity versus wavelength' asks what wavelengths a source emits or what wavelengths reach the detector. A graph labelled 'Absorbance versus wavelength' asks how strongly a sample reduces transmitted light under a specified arrangement. One cannot exchange their vertical axes and keep the same interpretation.

A spectrum is also larger than visible light. Many biological molecules absorb mainly in the ultraviolet; many rotations and vibrations are interrogated in the infrared. The distinctive spectrum of an isolated atom is not restricted to what the human eye can see.

First read the axes. A peak in an emission plot is not automatically a peak in an absorption plot.

Original simplified spectroscope concept. Real instruments have collimating and focusing optics, calibration and finite resolution.

1.A.2.01.3 The light itself: wavelength, frequency and colour

Why coloured radiation can be measured

Electromagnetic radiation is characterized by wavelength λ, the distance between successive corresponding points of a wave, and frequency ν (Greek nu), the number of oscillations per second. In vacuum they are related by the speed of light c. Shorter wavelengths correspond to higher frequencies; the relationship is exact for vacuum propagation and is approximately used for air in many introductory visible-light examples.

Bright light and short wavelength are not the same thing

Two red beams can have different intensities while containing photons of the same frequency. A bright red line does not therefore become a higher-energy photon merely because the detector reports a large signal. The frequency or wavelength identifies a spectral energy coordinate; the intensity describes how much radiation is measured.

The numerical units need care. Nanometres are convenient for visible wavelengths, but in c=λν the speed c is usually expressed in metres per second, so λ must be in metres. One nanometre equals 10⁻⁹ metre; one ångström equals 10⁻¹⁰ metre. A missing conversion can turn an otherwise correct scientific equation into a wildly incorrect result.

c=λν Vacuum relation: c≈2.998×10⁸ m s⁻¹; λ in m and ν in s⁻¹.

1.A.2.01.4 Before atoms were understood: Newton's prism

A question about white light

When a beam of sunlight passes through a prism, its colours spread out. Newton's seventeenth-century experiments helped establish that the prism does not manufacture the colours out of nothing; it separates components already present in white light. This was a crucial change in the way light was studied.

However, an elegant prism rainbow could still conceal a problem. The colours look blended to the eye, yet under better spectral resolution the sunlight spectrum contains specific dark features. Those missing-looking pieces made light a tool for examining matter rather than merely a subject of optics.

Why this is only the beginning

Newton's prism had no need for a modern electronic configuration model. The later idea that atomic electrons occupy allowed states emerged only when observations of particular spectral lines demanded an explanation. We must not credit nineteenth-century observers with knowledge they did not yet possess.

A subtle change in the experimental question

Newton showed why a prism can display components of white light. Later observers asked a different question: when a continuum is spread apart more carefully, is every wavelength still equally present? Narrow deficits in sunlight were evidence that light and matter interact selectively.

The distinction between separating light and explaining selective loss became important when researchers compared sunlight with light emitted by substances in laboratories. Neither step required knowledge of the electron cloud at the time it was performed.

1.A.2.01.5 A continuous spectrum: when many wavelengths are present

What a continuum looks like

A hot, dense source or a suitable broadband lamp can produce radiation over a broad uninterrupted range of wavelengths. After dispersion, the detector sees a smooth distribution rather than a few isolated bright bars. 'Continuous' means the source emits across a range, not that its intensity is exactly identical at every wavelength.

Thermal sources such as incandescent material are often approximated by a broad thermal radiation distribution. The shape depends on temperature and emissivity; a perfect blackbody is a theoretical idealization. A visible filament may glow from red to white as its temperature rises, but 'white' is still an appearance produced by a mixture of wavelengths.

One important qualification

A dense source need not be a perfectly smooth emitter at all wavelengths. Materials can have selective absorption, surface features or molecular transitions. The continuum concept describes a broad baseline, not a promise that every spectrum from a dense object lacks lines.

The shape may vary without losing continuity

A heated solid can emit a broad smooth spectrum whose intensity varies with wavelength; it need not be a rainbow of equal-brightness colours. Its temperature and emissivity influence the distribution. The term continuum describes its broad spectral coverage, not a flat horizontal intensity graph.

Selective absorption by material between the source and detector can superimpose dark features on an otherwise smooth continuum. A complicated real spectrum can therefore contain both continuous background and narrow lines.

1.A.2.01.6 Emission from an excited, low-density gas

A different observation

When a low-density atomic gas is excited, for example in a suitable discharge apparatus, its emitted radiation may be concentrated in narrow features separated by intervals with relatively little emission. On a dark background these appear as bright lines. The pattern is often specific enough to help identify the atomic species.

The conditions matter. Atoms are not guaranteed to show every permitted line with equal strength, and a visible discharge does not imply that only visible transitions occur. Populations of excited states, the nature of excitation, pressure and the instrument all affect which lines appear and how strongly.

Why a line is not a coloured strip painted onto the gas

The line is the spectrometer's image or plot of radiation concentrated around a characteristic wavelength. It is not a thin physical ribbon extending through the gas. In an ideal isolated transition the emitted radiation is associated with a well-defined energy difference, while real measured features have finite widths for several reasons.

1.A.2.01.7 Dark absorption lines: a second kind of selectivity

Let broadband light pass through a gas

An absorption experiment differs from an emission experiment. Light from a broadband source passes through material, and the transmitted intensity is compared with the incident intensity. If certain photon energies can promote the sample into allowed excited states, radiation at corresponding wavelengths may be preferentially removed from the transmitted beam.

Against a continuous background, these losses can appear as dark lines. The word 'dark' means less light reaches the detector at those wavelengths compared with its surroundings. It does not necessarily mean that no photons of that wavelength remain, nor that the gas destroys energy. Some absorbed energy may later be re-emitted in different directions or converted through other processes.

The geometry is part of the result

If absorption is followed by re-emission, a detector placed along the original transmitted beam may still record a dip because photons were redirected or transformed. In simple thermal astronomical situations, cooler gas in front of a hotter continuum source can yield absorption features. The full radiative-transfer situation is more complicated than a universal rule that cool gas always absorbs.

1.A.2.01.8 Fraunhofer's lines: the Sun had gaps in its rainbow

A surprising solar observation

Early observers had already noticed nonuniformities in sunlight, and Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped numerous dark lines in the solar spectrum in the early nineteenth century. These were precise, repeatable wavelength features rather than random marks caused by the glass prism.

At the time, one could record their locations without knowing the complete modern atomic explanation. The dark lines became reference marks in optical measurements, but they also posed a more interesting question: what in sunlight or its passage through matter selects these wavelengths?

A repeatable reference for later investigations

Fraunhofer's maps gave optical investigators reference positions against which their instruments and observations could be compared. The lines were more than irregularities in a colour display: their locations were stable enough to suggest a physical explanation.

Modern interpretations include selective interactions of light with atoms and ions along the solar light path. But Fraunhofer's careful measurements preceded electrons, quantum numbers and wavefunctions; we should distinguish the data he actually acquired from the theory developed afterward.

1.A.2.01.9 Kirchhoff and Bunsen turned colours into chemical evidence

A productive meeting of experiment and interpretation

In the middle of the nineteenth century, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen connected the characteristic emission of chemical elements with absorption at matching wavelengths. This work helped establish spectroscopy as a method of chemical analysis: the same elemental species could produce recognizable features in very different samples.

An emission line from a controlled atomic source could correspond to a dark absorption line at the same spectral position in light that passed through an appropriate absorbing gas. That correspondence suggested that atoms select particular wavelengths rather than producing an arbitrary mixture of light.

Matching the line is the beginning of analysis

A bright emission feature from one prepared substance may correspond to a missing-looking feature in light transmitted through another preparation of the same relevant atomic species. This correspondence showed that spectral positions carry identity-sensitive information.

For reliable identification, the match should be checked against more than one expected feature where possible. A single yellow observation does not replace wavelength measurement; similar perceived colours can be produced by very different spectral distributions.

1.A.2.01.10 Why a spectral fingerprint is useful—and imperfect

Each species has a characteristic set of transitions

An isolated atom of a specified element and ionization state has a characteristic structure of electronic energy levels. The differences between those levels permit particular spectral transitions. Their wavelengths give an identity-sensitive pattern, which is the basis for the familiar 'atomic fingerprint' analogy.

Yet a fingerprint should not be taken too literally. Different ions of the same element have different spectra, line intensities vary with excitation conditions, and some lines overlap those of other species. Reliable identification often compares multiple features against standards or evaluated databases.

Why a fingerprint is not an infallible barcode

An element may have many lines; a neutral atom and its ion can have different sets. Two spectral features from different species can overlap, and a detector with limited resolution may blend closely spaced lines. A responsible assignment may therefore be tentative if only one noisy peak is available.

Concentration is yet another problem. A strong line supports that an emitting transition is active but does not supply a universal conversion to molar concentration without calibration, source conditions and instrument response.

1.A.2.01.11 Atomic lines, molecular bands and broad biological spectra

Atoms and molecules offer different structures

A free atom's sharp transitions are associated with discrete electronic energy-state differences. Molecules also have electronic states, but their nuclear vibrations and rotations introduce many additional transitions. These can create clustered bands and broad envelopes rather than a few visually isolated atomic lines.

Biology usually involves molecules, not free atoms

The protein in a cuvette or a chlorophyll molecule within a pigment extract has electronic states shaped by covalent bonds, molecular geometry and its surroundings. Vibrational and rotational changes also contribute to optical spectral structure. Many molecular electronic absorptions therefore form broad bands rather than hairline features.

The general idea of a photon matching an energy change remains true, but it would be incorrect to predict a molecular absorbance band using the one-electron hydrogen Rydberg equation. Understanding where a model applies is as important as remembering the model.

1.A.2.01.12 Why hydrogen was the right atom to interrogate

One electron changes the difficulty

The neutral hydrogen atom contains one proton and one electron. Its internal structure is far simpler than the structure of atoms with many interacting electrons. That makes hydrogen a natural starting point for a theory that tries to connect spectral energies to electronic states.

Physicists did not choose hydrogen because it emitted every colour or because its spectrum lacked structure. They chose it partly because a relatively simple and highly regular series of lines could be measured precisely. The regularity was an invitation to find a mathematical rule.

Atomic hydrogen is not the same as every hydrogen-containing light source

A discharge involving hydrogen may contain atomic hydrogen, molecular hydrogen and other excited species depending on conditions. Molecular H₂ produces molecular spectral features of its own, while the familiar Balmer lines refer to atomic hydrogen transitions.

The chosen model is simpler because neutral hydrogen has one electron. This permits an unusually regular account of dominant bound electronic energies, making it a good bridge toward the more complicated many-electron configurations to come.

1.A.2.01.13 Balmer's pattern: four visible lines among a family

The historical surprise

The prominent visible lines of atomic hydrogen occur approximately at 656.3 nm (red), 486.1 nm (blue-green), 434.0 nm (blue-violet) and 410.2 nm (violet). They did not occur at arbitrary wavelengths. Johann Jakob Balmer found in 1885 that their positions could be described by a simple relationship involving integers.

Read the four lines as one related family

All four familiar Balmer lines have the same lower level n=2. The red Hα line corresponds to 3→2. Hβ at 486.1 nm comes from 4→2, Hγ at 434.0 nm from 5→2 and Hδ at 410.2 nm from 6→2. The upper level increases as wavelength decreases. This is a pattern in energy differences, not an arbitrary collection of coloured strips.

The exact measured wavelength may vary in its last displayed digits with the air/vacuum convention and with fine-structure assignments. The following table uses approximate familiar visible air wavelengths.

1.A.2.01.14 Balmer's empirical relationship is a clue, not yet a theory

An integer pattern found before the mechanism

Balmer's formula for the visible hydrogen lines can be written using an integer n greater than 2. One form uses a characteristic wavelength factor B in nanometres: λ=B n²/(n²−4), with n=3,4,5,… for the Balmer series. The modern form is more usually expressed through wavenumber, as we shall see next.

λ = Bn2/(n2−4),   n=3,4,5,…Empirical Balmer relation with B approximately 364.6 nm in the corresponding air convention.

Test the empirical pattern

Substitute n=3: λ≈364.6×9/5≈656.3 nm. Substitute n=4: λ≈364.6×16/12≈486.1 nm. The point is that one compact rule organizes more than one independently observed wavelength. Balmer's finding was mathematical evidence of hidden structure before electrons were understood.

This historical distinction should be preserved. A rule that predicts data is a remarkable achievement, but a prediction alone is not proof of what microscopic object moves where. The later quantum theory provided the physical context for the integer pattern.

1.A.2.01.15 The Rydberg relation organizes hydrogen spectra

From one visible series to a general pattern

Johannes Rydberg developed an empirical relation in which wavelengths of hydrogen lines could be expressed in terms of the difference between reciprocal squares of two positive integers. This form accommodates multiple series, including those outside the visible region.

1/λ=RH(1/nlower2−1/nupper2)nupper>nlower≥1, with RH about 1.0968×10⁷ m⁻¹ for hydrogen.

Why this form changes the scientific question

The Rydberg expression makes the difference-of-reciprocal-squares structure explicit. With lower index 2 it reproduces Balmer. With lower index 1 it predicts the Lyman series in the ultraviolet; with lower index 3, the Paschen series in the infrared. A single measured pattern has become a wider hypothesis about permitted atomic energies.

The constant RH is a wavenumber constant. A classroom book may instead use R∞≈1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, a slightly different physical convention. A precise wavelength computation should specify which value is used and distinguish air from vacuum wavelengths.

1.A.2.01.16 Wavenumber is a convenient spectral coordinate

One wavelength can be written in three useful ways

Wavelength λ, frequency ν and wavenumber σ (also commonly written as a frequency ν with a tilde) all identify a spectral position. The spectroscopic wavenumber is simply 1/λ when λ is measured in vacuum; its unit is inverse length. If λ is in centimetres, wavenumber is in cm⁻¹. The Rydberg formula is especially simple in inverse-length form.

σ=1/λ,   ν=c/λσ is wavenumber in inverse length; ν is frequency in hertz.

A worked conversion prevents a unit trap

At λ=500 nm, convert first to 5.00×10⁻⁵ cm. The corresponding wavenumber is σ=1/(5.00×10⁻⁵ cm)=20,000 cm⁻¹, or 2.00×10⁶ m⁻¹. Both identify the same spectral position. Neither number is frequency in Hz.

In spectroscopy, authors often write a tilde over ν to mean wavenumber rather than frequency. The safest habit is to inspect the units: cm⁻¹ signals wavenumber, s⁻¹ usually signals frequency.

1.A.2.01.17 Why the Balmer lines crowd toward a limit

Let the upper integer increase

For the Balmer series the lower index remains n=2. As the upper index grows, 1/nupper² becomes smaller and approaches zero. Consequently, the wavenumber approaches RH/4 and the wavelength approaches a finite lower limit, approximately 364.6 nm in the familiar Balmer context.

Balmer λlimit≈4/RHAs nupper grows without bound, the term 1/nupper² approaches zero.

Where the series seems to end

The first few Balmer lines are well separated. Higher members increasingly cluster toward the short-wavelength limit near 364.6 nm in the familiar air convention. They do not suddenly cease after Hδ; the selected four-line diagram merely emphasizes features easy to recognize in the visible spectrum.

The limiting value corresponds to the threshold of the bound-state ladder in the ideal model. It is not one more ordinary transition between two finite-index bound levels.

Keep the lower index fixed

Spectral series are classified by the lower energy level involved in the transition. For the Lyman series, nlower=1; for Balmer, nlower=2; for Paschen, nlower=3. Brackett and Pfund follow with nlower=4 and 5.

A destination is more informative than a colour

Transitions ending at n=2 belong to Balmer even when higher members are near the ultraviolet. Likewise a transition 5→3 is Paschen though its upper level is five. A 2→1 and 3→2 transition each change n by one, but they release different energies because the hydrogen ladder has unequal steps.

One upper state can contribute to more than one line through different allowed downward pathways. An atom can also cascade through intermediate states and release multiple photons in separate transitions, rather than emitting a photon whose energy equals its upper level index.

1.A.2.01.19 From observed rules to a question about energy

An atom behaves as if it has a restricted menu

An ordinary continuous classical radiator can vary in energy over an effectively continuous range. Hydrogen's sharp spectral pattern suggested that its internal changes were instead constrained by specific allowed energy differences. If every electronic energy were permitted, the observed regularity would be harder to understand.

What the measured lines actually constrain

An observed spectral wavelength tells us about an energy difference. Even a beautifully exact Rydberg pattern does not by itself specify an electron's classical location or trajectory. Multiple theoretical models may initially fit a subset of evidence, and further observations can rule out incomplete pictures.

The more demanding scientific question is whether a model explains atomic stability, the numerical pattern of wavelengths and the behaviour of different atomic species under appropriate experimental conditions. Quantized electronic states emerged as part of that explanation; modern quantum orbitals came later.

1.A.2.01.20 Planck and Einstein supplied the light-energy bridge

1900: a new way of handling thermal radiation

Max Planck introduced quantization into his explanation of thermal radiation near the beginning of the twentieth century. His work did not start as a theory of hydrogen's internal electron arrangement; it was a response to a different experimental difficulty, the spectrum of thermal radiation.

Ephoton=hν=hc/λRelation connecting radiation frequency to photon energy.

Two separate experiments changed the language of physics

Max Planck's 1900 work on thermal radiation and Albert Einstein's 1905 light-quantum explanation of the photoelectric effect were not attempts to fit Balmer's four lines. They supplied a broader idea: light exchanges energy in quantities tied to its frequency. This made it possible to interpret spectral wavelength as an atomic energy-gap measurement.

Bohr's 1913 model then applied quantized-state reasoning to the nuclear hydrogen atom and showed why a spectrum already organized by Rydberg should arise. Explaining a historical discovery using later concepts is useful, provided we do not falsely attribute the later knowledge to the earlier observer.

1.A.2.01.21 Bohr in 1913: allowed electronic energies

From an empirical formula to a physical hypothesis

Balmer and Rydberg described where hydrogen lines appeared. They did not explain why the atom should be restricted to those wavelengths. In 1913 Niels Bohr joined the nuclear model of the atom to a new proposal: an electron bound to hydrogen could occupy only certain stationary energy states, and radiation would be exchanged when the atom changed state.

What mattered most was the idea of allowed energies, not the familiar image of a tiny planet going round the nucleus. The circular path belonged to the historical model. Modern quantum mechanics replaces definite classical electron trajectories with states described by wavefunctions and probability.

A genuine breakthrough, not a finished theory

Classical physics struggled to account for the stability of a planetary nuclear atom, in which an accelerating charge would radiate. Bohr's stationary-state postulate avoided this classical difficulty and reproduced important hydrogen wavelengths. It could not give a complete account of many-electron spectra, transition intensities or fine structure. Those limitations are useful: a good theory gains credibility through what it explains and becomes more precise through what it cannot.

1.A.2.01.22 Reading the hydrogen energy ladder

Negative does not mean impossible

Choose the energy of a free electron and proton far apart as zero. Bound hydrogen states have lower energies and are represented by negative values relative to that reference. In the introductory hydrogen model, En≈−13.6 eV/n². The ground state n=1 is most strongly bound; higher n values move the energy toward zero.

Why this explains convergence

For large n, adjacent levels crowd close to the zero-energy threshold. Transitions ending at the same lower level can therefore have wavelengths that approach a limiting value as the upper index increases. The crowding in the Balmer series is the visible trace of that underlying energy pattern.

1.A.2.01.23 Photon energy measures the difference between states

The energy is not the level number

When an atom emits radiation through a transition from a higher allowed state to a lower one, its internal energy decreases. The photon receives a positive amount equal to that loss. This difference determines the spectral frequency, and hence the wavelength. A transition 3→2 does not produce a photon with “energy 3” or three photons simply because the upper state is numbered three.

Ephoton = Eupper − Elower = hν = hc/λ

In the most useful sign convention, ΔEatom=Efinal−Einitial is negative for emission and positive for absorption. Photon energy itself is positive. The negative sign in an atomic energy change describes the direction of energy transfer, not a negative physical photon.

Where the next chapter begins

The equation has a clear conceptual purpose here. It connects a spectral line to an atomic energy difference. Numerical conversion among joules, electronvolts, frequency and wavelength is the central subject of Node 1.A.2.02 and is not artificially repeated throughout the present chapter.

1.A.2.01.24 Opposite transitions: emission and absorption

The same gap can be crossed in either direction

In emission, an atom makes a transition to a lower allowed state and releases radiation. In absorption, light of a suitable energy can promote an atom from a populated lower state to an allowed higher state. The energy difference and corresponding spectral position can match, though measured emission and absorption intensities will generally depend on different conditions.

A crucial qualification concerns the population of the initial state. In a cool hydrogen sample, atoms are frequently in the ground state n=1. Visible Balmer absorption requires a lower level n=2 for the simple corresponding bound-bound transitions; it is not guaranteed merely by the existence of Balmer emission lines.

The most reliable scientific statement is that matching absorption depends on an available, populated initial state and an allowed transition—not merely on an element having a recognisable colour.

1.A.2.01.25 Hydrogen series as destinations rather than colours

Follow the lower level

Series names are best understood as a classification rule. A transition ending at n=1 belongs to Lyman; one ending at n=2 belongs to Balmer; one ending at n=3 belongs to Paschen. Brackett and Pfund terminate at n=4 and n=5. Their names do not say how many photons are emitted, and the classification does not depend solely on visible colour.

For example, 5→2 is Balmer, while 5→3 is Paschen. The same starting level can contribute to different series because the lower endpoint is different. An electron that later falls through several intermediate levels may produce multiple successive photons through separate transitions.

Why wavelength regions differ

The Lyman series lies in the ultraviolet and many Paschen, Brackett and Pfund lines lie in the infrared. Balmer contains familiar visible lines and further members near the ultraviolet. The differences arise from the atomic energy gaps, not from a special property of the human eye. Recognition of the lower level is more dependable than memorising an approximate colour.

1.A.2.01.26 Connecting Bohr's energy expression to Rydberg's data

The same reciprocal squares appear twice

From the elementary hydrogen model Eₙ∝−1/n², the difference between an upper and lower bound state is proportional to (1/nlower²−1/nupper²). Because photon energy is inversely related to wavelength, the emitted wavenumber has that same mathematical structure.

1/λ ≈ RH(1/nlower2 − 1/nupper2)

This matters historically. Balmer and Rydberg measured and fitted a regularity before an adequate microscopic explanation existed. Bohr supplied an energy-state model that accounted for the main pattern. Agreement with spectral data supported the energy-level hypothesis; it did not demonstrate that electrons were physically travelling on the exact classical circular paths pictured in early textbooks.

The sign must be sensible

For an emission line, nupper exceeds nlower. The bracket is therefore positive. A negative reciprocal wavelength reveals a reversed subtraction or mistaken use of emission/absorption directions. It is not evidence for a negative wavelength.

1.A.2.01.27 Why peak heights cannot be used as photon energies

Two axes, two physical meanings

In an emission intensity plot, the horizontal position tells us wavelength or frequency. The vertical height tells us the measured radiative strength under the instrument's conditions. A bright red hydrogen line can be stronger than a violet line even though a single violet photon has greater energy.

The number of atoms in excited states, the probability of each transition, gas conditions, collection optics and detector sensitivity all affect intensity. If a discharge tube's brightness changes, the heights of its spectral features may change without a corresponding transformation into another element.

How to reason in an exam

If asked to rank photon energies, compare wavelengths using E∝1/λ rather than plotted peak heights. If asked to rank line intensities, do not pretend that the Rydberg formula alone predicts them. Such a question requires knowledge of populations and transition strengths.

1.A.2.01.28 Why not every conceivable state pair gives a bright line

Energy matching is not the only condition

An atom may possess two electronic energies separated by a particular value yet have little or no observable radiation from the corresponding transition under the chosen conditions. Quantum mechanical selection rules restrict transition probabilities, while population of the starting state controls whether emission or absorption can occur.

For many ordinary atomic electric-dipole transitions, the orbital quantum number l changes by ±1, alongside other conditions. We mention this only to explain why spectra cannot be reduced to a list of all mathematical energy differences. The quantum numbers and orbital symmetries underlying this rule will be developed later in Nodes 06–10.

Students should distinguish between “a pair of levels with an energy gap” and “an experimentally prominent line.” Real spectra carry information about both structure and the physical conditions producing radiation.

1.A.2.01.29 A high-precision hydrogen line has extra structure

The limits of a simple textbook equation

The approximate energy expression for hydrogen works remarkably well, but accurate measurement reveals finite proton mass effects, fine structure and hyperfine structure. These can produce small changes or separations beyond the simple Bohr picture. A calculation using R≈1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ should not be reported with many unjustified digits.

Air versus vacuum wavelength

The frequency of light is the same on passage across a stationary air/vacuum boundary, whereas its wavelength changes slightly with propagation speed. Tabulated visible Balmer wavelengths are often given in standard air; straightforward Rydberg calculations produce vacuum-model wavelengths. Near 656.3 nm in air and a slightly different vacuum value need not be contradictory.

Modern spectroscopic practice therefore identifies the nuclide, transition, measurement conditions and wavelength convention. Even a familiar red line has a richer physical meaning than a colored stroke on a classroom diagram.

1.A.2.01.30 Why real spectral lines have width

A textbook stroke is not a measured profile

We often draw one atomic spectral line as a thin coloured bar. On a real instrument it occupies a finite wavelength interval. This may reflect the instrument's limited resolving power and physical broadening of the transition itself. The distinction matters because two close lines may appear merged until a sufficiently resolving spectrometer separates them.

Different sources of width have different physical meanings. Thermal motion produces Doppler broadening; interactions during collisions produce pressure-related broadening; finite excited-state lifetimes contribute natural linewidth. A real line profile can contain several such effects simultaneously, and the observed shape is modified by instrument response.

What the broadening does not imply

A wider line does not mean that the atom has lost every restriction on its energies. Nor does it automatically mean a new element has formed. A good analysis asks whether width arose from atomic physics, source conditions or the measuring instrument.

1.A.2.01.31 Resolution: when a doublet looks like one line

A powerful example from sodium

The strong yellow feature associated with neutral sodium near 589 nm contains closely spaced components. A low-resolution spectroscope may show them as one bright yellow line, while a higher-resolution instrument can distinguish them. The additional detail was present in the light before the instrument resolved it.

Resolving power is commonly related to wavelength divided by the smallest discernible separation Δλ, but whether two peaks can be separated depends on the shape and signal-to-noise ratio as well as nominal resolution. The precise spectral splitting has atomic origins that go beyond the present introductory hydrogen model.

Resolving power (indicative) R = λ/Δλ

Why this is scientifically useful

Suppose two compounds emit or absorb close features. Without enough resolution, a single measured peak could be wrongly attributed to one source. Spectral interpretation must start with a realistic view of what the instrument can distinguish.

1.A.2.01.32 Temperature and excitation decide what an observer actually sees

Structure versus population

An atom has possible electronic states even when its sample does not show a bright line from every state. The population of excited states depends on how the gas is energized. Temperature, radiation fields, collisions and electrical excitation can change how frequently atoms occupy the states that produce particular features.

A hydrogen discharge, a cooler absorbing gas and a stellar atmosphere can show very different-looking spectra without contradicting the underlying existence of hydrogen's atomic energy structure. A spectral line may weaken because few atoms occupy its upper emitting state, not because its energy gap has ceased to exist.

The science behind a useful qualification

Statements like “hot gas produces emission lines and cool gas absorption lines” can help beginners picture common experimental configurations, but they are not general laws. Whether a line appears in emission or absorption depends on the radiation source, optical depth, excitation and geometry. Reliable answers describe a specific arrangement rather than a temperature slogan.

1.A.2.01.33 How spectroscopists identify an element

Wavelength gives a clue; a pattern is stronger evidence

Imagine a spectrum with a peak close to a known sodium line. Is sodium certainly present? Not yet. An instrument can be miscalibrated, another species may contribute a nearby line, and the emitting atom may be an ion rather than a neutral atom. A laboratory checks several expected features, their relative positions and independent standards when possible.

Reference data compiled by organizations such as NIST report carefully evaluated wavelengths, energy levels and transition information. Such a database is not merely a list of colours: it links observed spectral positions to identified atomic or ionic species and records measurement conventions.

Identity and concentration are separate questions

A matching line can support elemental identity. Estimating how much of the element is present normally requires response calibration, control of sample matrix effects and appropriate standards. A tall peak does not have a universal conversion into grams or moles.

1.A.2.01.34 Biological pigments: the same quantum idea in molecules

Why a leaf absorbs selectively

The electronic structure of molecules also restricts which light energies can be absorbed. Chlorophyll molecules contain extended electronic systems that interact strongly with particular regions of visible light. Chlorophylls a and b have prominent absorption in blue and red regions, while absorption is comparatively weaker over much of the green range. Many leaves therefore appear green in reflected or transmitted light.

This is related in principle to atomic spectroscopy but not identical in detail. A free hydrogen atom has discrete atomic lines. Chlorophyll has molecular electronic states influenced by bonding, vibrations and its surrounding environment, yielding broader spectral bands. Its absorptions cannot be predicted by placing the numbers of a hydrogen Balmer equation into a molecule.

Absorption and biological action are not identical

A pigment absorption spectrum describes which wavelengths it takes up. An action spectrum describes how effective illumination at different wavelengths is at driving a chosen biological process. Several pigments and downstream reactions can affect that biological response, so the two plots need not have the same shape.

1.A.2.01.35 Protein UV absorbance: a tool that requires chemical judgement

Where the 280-nm measurement comes from

Tryptophan and tyrosine side chains contribute substantially to the near-UV absorption of many proteins around 280 nm; phenylalanine also absorbs, with different strength and spectral characteristics. Laboratories exploit this behaviour to estimate protein content under suitable assumptions.

It is a mistake to say that every protein absorbs equally at 280 nm. Proteins differ in amino-acid composition, environment and extinction coefficient. Nucleic acids and other contaminants may interfere with the measurement. A concentration estimate needs an appropriate calibration or molecular extinction coefficient.

Why this belongs after hydrogen rather than before it

The underlying principle is selective excitation of electronic states. In proteins, the states belong to molecular chromophores and are influenced by chemical bonding. The spectrum is usually a broad molecular band, not an isolated Rydberg series. Understanding the difference prevents a common failure of transfer: treating every UV-visible graph as though it came from a one-electron atom.

1.A.2.01.36 Haemoglobin and the meaning of colour in biology

A pigment that changes with chemical state

Haemoglobin contains heme groups whose absorption depends on the molecular environment, oxidation state and bound ligands. Oxygenated and deoxygenated forms show different visible absorption patterns. Their differences are useful for studying blood oxygenation with appropriately designed optical methods.

The effect is molecular rather than an emission spectrum of free hydrogen or iron atoms. It reflects electronic states of the heme-containing protein system. The wavelengths of its absorption bands cannot be calculated from the hydrogen Rydberg formula.

What one measurement cannot tell us

A broad absorption peak alone is insufficient to identify a patient's oxygenation status. Instruments and physiological interpretations require multiple wavelengths, calibration and light-transport assumptions as relevant. An introductory physics relationship does not become a clinical rule merely because the sample is biological.

1.A.2.01.37 Fluorescence: absorption need not end in the same colour

After a molecule is excited

Absorbing a photon can place a molecule in an electronically excited state. The system may then transfer energy to surrounding molecules, undergo internal relaxation or emit radiation. Fluorescence is one possible radiative outcome, not the inevitable result of every absorption event.

Many familiar fluorophores emit at longer wavelengths than the light used to excite them because some of the absorbed energy has been dissipated before emission. The separation between excitation and emission bands is commonly called a Stokes shift. Different relaxation routes and more complicated photophysical processes can modify this simple pattern.

Atomic versus molecular contrast

The simple hydrogen level model helps explain why an energy change is associated with a photon. It does not by itself describe the broad excitation and emission bands, quantum yields or environmental sensitivity of fluorescent proteins and dyes. Those require molecular photophysics.

1.A.2.01.38 Transmittance and absorbance: the language of a UV-visible instrument

Two intensities, one useful ratio

A spectrophotometer may compare incident intensity I₀ with intensity I transmitted through a sample. Transmittance T=I/I₀ describes the fraction that passes through. Absorbance is defined as A=−log₁₀T=log₁₀(I₀/I); it is dimensionless. If 10% is transmitted, A=1. If 50% is transmitted, A≈0.3010.

T = I/I₀   ;   A = log10(I₀/I)

In a homogeneous solution under appropriate conditions, Beer–Lambert law writes A=εlc, where ε is the molar absorptivity, l is optical path length and c is concentration. This is an introductory application, not a universal guarantee that every biological suspension behaves linearly.

A = εlcWith ε in L mol−1 cm−1, l in cm and c in mol L−1, absorbance is dimensionless.

When the simple law becomes unreliable

Scattering, stray light, high concentration, aggregation and chemical equilibria can change a measured signal. A loss of transmitted light may arise partly from scattering rather than true molecular absorption. Even the letter A changes meaning across sciences: absorbance is not radioactive activity in becquerels.

1.A.2.01.39 Spectral evidence does not reveal an electron's exact position

What the data establish

Line positions reflect permitted energy differences. Their regularity was important evidence that electrons in atoms do not behave as unrestricted classical orbiting particles. But recording a wavelength does not give an electron's unique point in three-dimensional space.

The modern quantum description assigns states with spatial probability distributions. An orbital is not a rigid container or painted circular track. These ideas arise from matter-wave behaviour, uncertainty and quantum numbers, which will follow in the planned nodes.

A useful boundary for this lesson

We have learned why electronic states must be taken seriously. We have not yet learned to list all allowed orbitals or decide how several electrons occupy them. Those are new questions, requiring further scientific concepts rather than memorizing an electron-filling mnemonic prematurely.

1.A.2.01.40 The first step toward electronic configuration

Return to the hydrogen mystery

At the beginning we asked why excited hydrogen does not produce every possible visible wavelength. Its measured lines clustered at special positions. Balmer and Rydberg organized those positions mathematically. Quantum ideas about radiation and Bohr's allowed atomic energies then gave the pattern a physical explanation. Modern theory refined that explanation without erasing the original evidence.

A hydrogen atom has one electron. Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and iron have many. Their electronic behaviour raises new questions: which states exist, how many electrons can occupy each state, how states differ in energy and why some configurations are more stable than others. The notation we call an electronic configuration is a compact answer to those questions.

The next chapter's opening question

Before learning the occupancy rules, we must be comfortable translating a wavelength into frequency and photon energy. That translation will be the central task of Node 1.A.2.02. In this chapter, the important achievement is not a memorized Balmer table but the ability to explain why measurements forced scientists to ask about permitted electronic energies.

1.A.2.01.41 Twelve original investigations: interpreting spectra without guessing

Each investigation begins with a measured result, then asks what can safely be inferred. This mirrors how spectroscopy actually became a scientific method: a spectrum earns its meaning through experimental conditions, not through a decorative list of colours.

Case 01: A prism shows a rainbow, but the spectrometer shows missing light

Observed situation: A student observes sunlight through a prism and initially reports a continuous series of colours. A higher-resolution arrangement reveals narrow dark features embedded within the broader solar continuum.

Analysis: The colour separation reveals that sunlight contains many wavelengths. The dark features mean less light reaches the observer at selected wavelengths, consistent with absorption and the solar-atmosphere radiative transfer picture. The dark features are not proof that sunlight was originally made of separate dark rays.

Question to carry forward: Distinguish the observed pattern from the later physical explanation. Ask what would happen if the same instrument viewed a different continuum lamp.

Case 02: A discharge tube has a pink glow and four sharp hydrogen lines

Observed situation: The tube appears pinkish to the unaided eye. A spectrometer resolves prominent features near 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm, along with possible other visible or nonvisible emissions.

Analysis: The four listed lines are members of the atomic hydrogen Balmer series, interpreted by transitions ending at n=2. The visible colour of the full tube is a mixture and may also depend on source conditions or molecular emission. Hydrogen's line positions do not directly tell how many atoms are present without calibration.

Question to carry forward: A colour impression and a resolved spectrum are not equivalent datasets.

Case 03: The same atomic species: bright lines in one setup, dark in another

Observed situation: One experiment observes light produced by an excited atomic gas; another passes a continuum through material containing the same relevant atomic species and measures the transmitted radiation.

Analysis: An energy difference can correspond to emission or absorption at the same spectral position. But absorption needs a populated lower state and incident photons; emission needs a populated upper state and a sufficiently probable transition.

Question to carry forward: A bright emission line is not guaranteed to produce an equally strong absorption line under every source condition.

Case 04: Kirchhoff and Bunsen identify a line without knowing orbitals

Observed situation: A laboratory observes a yellow feature from sodium-rich material and matches it with characteristic neutral sodium lines. The experiment takes place before the complete quantum orbital framework exists.

Analysis: The line assignment is an empirical chemical identification based on spectral correspondence. Later quantum theory explains electronic energy differences, but the historical identification was not made by first deriving a modern sodium electronic configuration.

Question to carry forward: The order of scientific discovery matters: observation can be reliable before a final microscopic model exists.

Case 05: A student applies the Balmer formula to the wrong series

Observed situation: A calculation inserts nlower=2 and nupper=5 and obtains a near-434-nm line. The student then labels it 'Lyman beta' because the upper integer is five.

Analysis: The calculation belongs to Balmer because its lower index is 2. The corresponding observed prominent feature is Hγ around 434 nm. In a standard spectral-series convention, Lyman has lower index 1 and the series label is based on the lower destination, not on the numerical value of the upper level.

Question to carry forward: Use both endpoint numbers when assigning transitions; the series name alone is insufficient to identify an exact wavelength.

Case 06: Two spectral peaks merge on a simple instrument

Observed situation: An observer reports one broad yellow feature from a sodium source. A higher-resolution spectrometer reveals two nearby components.

Analysis: The instrument's response had previously blended two features. Changing instruments improves resolved detail; it does not change the element's internal allowed energies. Detection limits, line broadening and calibration still matter when claiming the doublet is present.

Question to carry forward: An apparently smooth or single peak can conceal unresolved components.

Case 07: A red line is brighter than a violet line

Observed situation: Two atomic emission lines appear at 656 nm and 410 nm. The red line has the larger peak height on the intensity plot.

Analysis: The violet photon has greater energy because its wavelength is shorter, while peak height reports source intensity and detector response. More emitted low-energy photons can yield a higher integrated signal than fewer high-energy photons.

Question to carry forward: Never use intensity height as a direct ranking of energy per photon.

Case 08: Why a cool hydrogen cloud does not absorb every Balmer line strongly

Observed situation: A bright continuum shines through hydrogen gas whose atoms are overwhelmingly in the n=1 ground state.

Analysis: Most simple Balmer absorption transitions start in n=2, not n=1. The absence of strong Balmer absorption in a particular cool sample can therefore be consistent with hydrogen being present. The Lyman absorption region is relevant to ground-state hydrogen, subject to optical conditions.

Question to carry forward: An element's identity and a particular line's visibility are separate statements.

Case 09: A botanical experiment compares absorption and photosynthetic action

Observed situation: A student measures visible absorption of chlorophyll extracts and compares the curve with a whole-leaf photosynthesis response under monochromatic illumination.

Analysis: Absorption peaks provide molecular evidence about pigment excitation. The action spectrum is influenced by multiple pigments, energy transfer, light availability and downstream photosynthetic processes. It need not match the absorption of one isolated purified pigment exactly.

Question to carry forward: A molecular absorption band is not a free-atomic hydrogen Balmer line.

Case 10: A laboratory estimates protein using absorbance at 280 nm

Observed situation: Two purified proteins at the same molar concentration produce different UV absorbances around 280 nm.

Analysis: Different amino-acid compositions, especially aromatic residues, and extinction coefficients can explain the difference. The instrument reads an optical property, not a universal protein mass counter. A known sequence or standard can help with calibration.

Question to carry forward: Do not use one universal A280-to-concentration conversion for every protein.

Case 11: Haemoglobin provides an optical clue, not a diagnosis by itself

Observed situation: A biomedical instrument records different absorption in oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin-containing samples.

Analysis: Heme electronic structure and ligand-related molecular changes affect broad visible absorption bands. Quantitative physiological interpretation requires multiple wavelengths, calibration and suitable handling of tissue scattering and path length.

Question to carry forward: Molecular heme bands cannot be predicted by inserting iron's atomic number into the hydrogen Rydberg equation.

Case 12: Fluorescence reveals a second step after absorption

Observed situation: A biological fluorophore is excited by a shorter-wavelength source and emits over a broader band at longer wavelengths.

Analysis: Photon absorption can raise molecular electronic energy; relaxation can dissipate part of that energy before radiative emission. The difference is often described using a Stokes shift. Its magnitude and spectrum depend on molecular photophysics and environment.

Question to carry forward: Fluorescence and line emission both involve radiation, but they are not identical processes or necessarily the same spectral profile.

1.A.2.01.42 Forty worked investigations and calculations

These exercises use a declared hydrogen Rydberg constant and mark wavelengths from the idealized model as vacuum values. Where a common observed Balmer line is quoted in air, the distinction is stated explicitly. This avoids the false precision that often creeps into spectroscopy notes.

Worked example 01: Convert 656.3 nm to metres

Problem: A visible red hydrogen line has wavelength 656.3 nm. Write it in metres.

Working: 1 nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metre. Multiply 656.3 by 10⁻⁹.

Answer: 6.563 × 10⁻⁷ m.

Interpretation: Before using c=λν in SI, the wavelength must be in metres.

Worked example 02: Convert a wavelength to ångströms

Problem: A line is reported at 486.1 nm. Express it in Å.

Working: 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m, so 1 nm = 10 Å.

Answer: 4861 Å.

Interpretation: Spectroscopic handbooks often quote wavelengths in ångströms; the same line can have different numerical representations.

Worked example 03: Wavenumber of a 500 nm line

Problem: Calculate σ=1/λ in cm⁻¹ for 500 nm vacuum wavelength.

Working: 500 nm = 5×10⁻⁵ cm; reciprocal gives 1/(5×10⁻⁵).

Answer: 20,000 cm⁻¹.

Interpretation: Always retain the inverse-length unit; 20,000 is not the frequency in Hz.

Worked example 04: Convert 15000 cm⁻¹ to wavelength

Problem: An optical line has vacuum wavenumber 15000 cm⁻¹. Find λ in nm.

Working: λ=1/15000 cm = 6.6667×10⁻⁵ cm = 666.67 nm.

Answer: 666.667 nm.

Interpretation: This corresponds to red-region light; the calculation alone does not identify the element.

Worked example 05: Compare red and blue frequencies

Problem: Two lines have wavelengths 656.3 and 486.1 nm. What is νblue/νred?

Working: ν=c/λ, so νblue/νred=λred/λblue.

Answer: 1.3501.

Interpretation: The blue-green photon has higher frequency, regardless of which line looks brighter.

Worked example 06: Frequency of the red hydrogen line

Problem: Find frequency of light at 656.3 nm using vacuum c for this introductory approximation.

Working: ν=c/λ; substitute 656.3×10⁻⁹ m.

Answer: 4.5679e+14 Hz.

Interpretation: Hz means cycles per second, not decays per second.

Problem: Use Rh=1.0967758×10⁷ m⁻¹ for hydrogen and the n=3→2 transition.

Working: 1/λ=Rh(1/4−1/9)=Rh(5/36); λ is the reciprocal.

Answer: 656.47 nm (idealized vacuum calculation).

Interpretation: This is close to the commonly quoted air wavelength near 656.3 nm; the slight difference is a convention and precision issue.

Worked example 15: Brackett alpha

Answer: 4.05228 µm.

Interpretation: A Brackett transition begins at n=5 and ends at n=4; it lies in the infrared.

Worked example 16: Lyman series limit

Working: 1/nupper²→0, so λlimit=1/Rh.

Worked example 17: Balmer series limit

Problem: Estimate Balmer lower wavelength limit in the simple hydrogen formula.

Working: With nlower=2 and nupper→∞, 1/λ=Rh/4; λ=4/Rh.

Answer: 4.

Worked example 20: Identify the series and order two lines

Working: Lower indices 2 and 3 mean Balmer and Paschen. The 5→2 energy gap is larger, so its photon has shorter wavelength.

Answer: 5→2 is Balmer and has the shorter wavelength; 5→3 is Paschen.

Interpretation: The number of level steps alone does not define an intensity or a photon count.

Problem: An introductory spectrometer reports 400 nm in air; estimate the frequency using vacuum c as an approximation.

Reasoning: λ≈4.00×10⁻⁷ m; ν≈c/λ.

Problem: How many times greater is photon energy at 410 nm than at 656 nm?

Worked example 38: Predict absorbance after a twofold concentration change

Problem: A solution has A=0.20 at a chosen wavelength and path length. If only c doubles and linear Beer–Lambert conditions remain, find A.

Answer: A=0.40.

1.A.2.01.44 Thirty misconceptions that distort spectral evidence

These are not tricks about terminology. Each mistaken statement would lead to a wrong interpretation of a real spectrum. A student should be able to explain the correction in ordinary scientific language before trying to memorize any additional formula.

Misconception 01

The tempting but incorrect claim: A prism creates the colours of sunlight.

Better scientific explanation: A prism disperses wavelengths already present in incident light. It does not manufacture different atomic transitions.

Misconception 02

The tempting but incorrect claim: Continuous spectrum means equal intensity at every wavelength.

Better scientific explanation: A continuum contains a broad uninterrupted range; intensity can vary strongly with wavelength.

Misconception 03

The tempting but incorrect claim: An emission line is a coloured thread physically inside the gas.

Better scientific explanation: A line is a narrow wavelength feature recorded by an optical system, not a literal ribbon running through a sample.

Misconception 04

The tempting but incorrect claim: Every excited atom emits every wavelength of visible light.

Better scientific explanation: Emission is restricted by allowed energy differences, transition probabilities and state populations.

Misconception 05

The tempting but incorrect claim: Dark absorption lines mean atoms destroy photons without conserving energy.

Better scientific explanation: Absorption transfers energy into matter; subsequent processes may redistribute or re-emit energy.

Misconception 06

The tempting but incorrect claim: Fraunhofer explained the solar lines using modern electron orbitals.

Better scientific explanation: Fraunhofer measured and charted lines long before electron wavefunctions and quantum orbitals were developed.

Misconception 07

The tempting but incorrect claim: Kirchhoff and Bunsen measured full electron configurations.

Better scientific explanation: They demonstrated characteristic elemental spectral analysis and emission-absorption relationships before the modern configuration framework.

Misconception 08

The tempting but incorrect claim: An element has one single characteristic wavelength.

Better scientific explanation: Most atoms and ions have many transitions. Identifications are strengthened by a pattern of lines.

Misconception 09

The tempting but incorrect claim: Two samples that look the same colour must contain the same element.

Better scientific explanation: Different spectral distributions can produce similar perceived colours, and a spectrometer can distinguish them.

Misconception 10

The tempting but incorrect claim: Atomic hydrogen and molecular hydrogen give identical spectra.

Better scientific explanation: H₂ has molecular energy structure and bands; atomic hydrogen transitions are a different spectroscopic system.

Misconception 11

The tempting but incorrect claim: Balmer's formula was a direct derivation from Schrödinger's equation.

Better scientific explanation: Balmer gave an empirical relationship in 1885; Schrödinger's wave mechanics was developed decades later.

Misconception 12

The tempting but incorrect claim: The four familiar visible Balmer lines are all possible hydrogen lines.

Better scientific explanation: Hydrogen has many additional transitions, including higher Balmer members and ultraviolet/infrared series.

Misconception 13

The tempting but incorrect claim: Hydrogen Hα occurs near 410 nm.

Better scientific explanation: The prominent Hα air wavelength is near 656.3 nm; the near-410-nm Balmer line is Hδ.

Misconception 14

The tempting but incorrect claim: A spectral series is named from its starting level.

Better scientific explanation: The lower level identifies the Lyman, Balmer, Paschen and other standard hydrogen series.

Misconception 15

The tempting but incorrect claim: Rydberg's relation implies all atoms have exactly the same one-electron spectral structure.

Better scientific explanation: The simple hydrogen relation applies to hydrogenic systems under defined conditions; multi-electron systems have more complex structures.

Misconception 16

The tempting but incorrect claim: The Balmer limit is a fifth ordinary finite upper-index transition.

Better scientific explanation: It is the limiting wavelength approached as the upper bound-state index grows without bound, associated with the bound-to-continuum threshold.

Misconception 17

The tempting but incorrect claim: The photon frequency and a line's intensity are the same quantity.

Better scientific explanation: Frequency locates radiation in the spectrum; intensity measures the amount of radiation under an experimental definition.

Misconception 18

The tempting but incorrect claim: Red photons always carry more energy than violet photons.

Better scientific explanation: For light in vacuum, E=hc/λ, so shorter-wavelength violet photons have greater individual energy.

Misconception 19

The tempting but incorrect claim: A negative bound-state energy gives a negative-energy photon.

Better scientific explanation: Negative atomic levels reflect a reference convention; emitted photon energy is positive and equals the atom's energy loss.

Misconception 20

The tempting but incorrect claim: A 4→2 transition means four photons are emitted.

Better scientific explanation: It denotes one electronic change from upper index 4 to lower index 2 in the simplest radiative picture.

Misconception 21

The tempting but incorrect claim: Bohr's planetary circles are literal modern electron trajectories.

Better scientific explanation: The modern description uses quantum states and probability distributions rather than definite classical electron tracks.

Misconception 22

The tempting but incorrect claim: Every pair of energy levels must give a strong optical line.

Better scientific explanation: Transition selection rules and populations influence the probability and strength of radiation.

Misconception 23

The tempting but incorrect claim: Air and vacuum wavelengths of the same line are exactly identical.

Better scientific explanation: Frequency remains the same across a stationary boundary, but wavelength differs slightly with propagation speed; spectral tables may use either convention.

Misconception 24

The tempting but incorrect claim: A measured spectrum must have perfectly thin zero-width lines.

Better scientific explanation: Physical processes and finite instrument resolution give observed line profiles nonzero widths.

Misconception 25

The tempting but incorrect claim: A taller spectral peak always means a larger photon energy.

Better scientific explanation: Peak height concerns measured intensity; photon energy is determined by frequency or wavelength.

Misconception 26

The tempting but incorrect claim: A broad chlorophyll absorption band is a hydrogen Balmer line.

Better scientific explanation: Molecular chromophores have electronic and vibrational structure unlike a free hydrogen atom.

Misconception 27

The tempting but incorrect claim: Every protein has the same absorptivity at 280 nm.

Better scientific explanation: Aromatic residue content, molecular environment and extinction coefficient vary among proteins.

Misconception 28

The tempting but incorrect claim: An absorption spectrum is identical to an action spectrum.

Better scientific explanation: Absorption measures photon uptake; an action spectrum measures a chosen biological response involving additional processes.

Misconception 29

The tempting but incorrect claim: Every absorbed photon must later be emitted as fluorescence.

Better scientific explanation: Excited molecules may relax nonradiatively, transfer energy, undergo chemistry or fluoresce.

Misconception 30

The tempting but incorrect claim: Absorbance measured in A is radioactive activity measured in becquerels.

Better scientific explanation: Optical absorbance is dimensionless; nuclear activity is transformations per second, a physically different quantity.

1.A.2.01.45 Ten higher-order investigations connecting experiment and theory

These ten problems combine the physics of wavelengths with experimental judgement. They ask for an argument, not merely an answer option. Each worked discussion identifies the evidence, the appropriate formula where needed, and the limits of the conclusion.

Integrated challenge 01: An unexpected pair of solar and laboratory lines

Problem: A student observes a dark feature near 656.3 nm in transmitted sunlight and a bright feature at a matching wavelength in emission from an excited hydrogen source. Explain why the match is meaningful and why equal intensities are not expected.

Worked reasoning: The close wavelength agreement is consistent with a shared hydrogen transition energy. In the simple Balmer description this is Hα, a transition involving n=3 and n=2. A solar absorption signal requires appropriate populations and radiative-transfer conditions; a laboratory emission line requires suitable excited atoms. Line strengths depend on source conditions, illumination and detection. The shared position supports an energy-gap interpretation, not identical observed intensities.

Integrated challenge 02: Reconstruct the Balmer sequence from data

Problem: A spectrum has narrow lines near 656.3, 486.1, 434.0 and 410.2 nm. Assign the four familiar hydrogen Balmer labels and associated upper-level indices, then explain the trend.

Worked reasoning: Hα: 3→2, red 656.3 nm; Hβ: 4→2, blue-green 486.1 nm; Hγ: 5→2, blue-violet 434.0 nm; Hδ: 6→2, violet 410.2 nm. The common lower level is n=2. As nupper increases, Rydberg's reciprocal-square term 1/nupper² shrinks, so wavenumber increases toward a limit and wavelengths become shorter and closer together. This is a measured spectral regularity, not evidence that every peak has the same strength.

Integrated challenge 03: Calculate an unknown Balmer upper level

Problem: A hydrogen line is assigned to Balmer and has model vacuum wavelength about 434.17 nm. With Rh=1.0967758×10⁷ m⁻¹, infer the upper level.

Worked reasoning: Convert λ≈434.17 nm≈4.3417×10⁻⁷ m. Then 1/(Rhλ)≈0.210, so 1/nupper²≈1/4−0.210≈0.0400=1/25. Therefore nupper=5 and the transition is 5→2, Hγ. Rounding and the air/vacuum distinction should be stated if matching a precise laboratory line table.

Integrated challenge 04: Understand why one level step is not one colour

Problem: Hydrogen makes a 2→1 transition and separately a 3→2 transition. Both go down one index. Compare their approximate vacuum wavelengths with the hydrogen Rydberg relation.

Worked reasoning: For 2→1, 1/λ=Rh(1−1/4)=3Rh/4, giving λ≈121.6 nm (UV). For 3→2, 1/λ=Rh(1/4−1/9)=5Rh/36, giving λ≈656.5 nm (red-region). The gaps are unequal because bound hydrogen energy levels are not equally spaced. Counting level steps is not a valid method for determining photon colour.

Integrated challenge 05: Distinguish spectral intensity from photon energy

Problem: A red 656-nm line is twice as high as a violet 410-nm line on one instrument's intensity plot. Which photons have greater energy, and what extra data are needed to compare total radiant energy in the lines?

Worked reasoning: For a single photon E∝1/λ, so E410/E656≈656/410≈1.60; the violet photon has higher energy. Comparing total energy requires defining the plotted intensity, the line widths, calibration and integration across each feature as appropriate. A simple peak-height ratio does not reveal the number of photons emitted or their total radiant power.

Integrated challenge 06: Absorption in a mostly ground-state sample

Problem: A research student predicts strong Balmer absorption at 656 nm from any cool hydrogen cloud simply because an excited hydrogen discharge emits Hα. Critically assess the prediction.

Worked reasoning: The corresponding Hα absorption transition requires atoms in the lower Balmer state n=2. If most atoms occupy n=1, the population available for that absorption may be small. Ground-state hydrogen has important Lyman absorption transitions in the ultraviolet. Detectability also depends on optical depth, incident continuum and the observation geometry. Emission and absorption line positions may correspond, but the actual observed strengths are not automatically equal.

Integrated challenge 07: Separate two nearby spectral lines

Problem: A hypothetical instrument observes lines near 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm. Estimate minimum nominal resolving power R=λ/Δλ needed at that wavelength and explain the limits of this estimate.

Worked reasoning: Using λ≈589.3 nm and Δλ≈0.6 nm gives R≈589.3/0.6≈982.2. This is a nominal resolving-power estimate; actual separation also depends on instrument line shape, physical broadening, noise and detection criteria. Two lines might merge on a low-resolution device even though the source has distinct atomic transitions.

Integrated challenge 08: Use an optical band without confusing it with an atomic line

Problem: A leaf-pigment extract absorbs strongly in blue and red regions, while the intact leaf shows a related photosynthetic action spectrum. Would applying hydrogen's Rydberg equation to the extract's absorption peaks be justified?

Worked reasoning: No. The extract contains molecular chromophores with electronic states influenced by bonding, vibrations and environment. The action spectrum measures a biological output involving multiple pigments and processes. The common principle of selective electronic excitation remains valid, but the simple isolated-hydrogen reciprocal-square formula is not the correct quantitative description of chlorophyll's molecular absorption bands.

Integrated challenge 09: Two proteins, identical concentration, different A280

Problem: Protein X and protein Y are each 1 mg/mL in matched 1-cm cells but produce different absorbance at 280 nm. Explain a plausible chemical cause and give a justified measurement strategy.

Worked reasoning: Tryptophan and tyrosine content, protein folding or environment, and co-absorbing impurities can change optical extinction. Equal mass concentration does not guarantee equal absorbance. Use an appropriate protein-specific extinction coefficient or calibration, confirm sample purity and instrument baseline, and avoid assuming that a universal A280 reading uniquely determines protein amount.

Integrated challenge 10: From spectrum to the next chapter's question

Problem: A student has mastered continuous spectra, hydrogen Balmer series, and the concept that photon energy matches allowed state-energy differences. What important step remains before discussing how carbon's six electrons occupy orbitals?

Worked reasoning: First translate spectral wavelength, frequency and photon energy quantitatively so the measured gaps are meaningful. Next understand electron wave behaviour, uncertainty and orbital states; then learn quantum numbers, orbital capacities and the rules governing many-electron occupancy. Spectra motivate this structure but do not themselves list every carbon electron's permitted quantum numbers. The logical sequence prevents electronic configuration from becoming mere memorization.

1.A.2.01.46 Master revision: the experiment-to-energy-state argument

Read the history as an argument, not a list of names

The chapter began with a simple visual fact: a glowing source may contain more information than its apparent colour reveals. A prism displays a broad mixture. Careful observations of sunlight reveal narrow dark features. Controlled atomic sources produce bright line patterns; comparing them with absorption features allows scientists to recognize chemical species. Hydrogen's spectrum is unusually orderly, and Balmer and Rydberg captured its regularities before the internal quantum mechanism was known.

Planck's and Einstein's work made a quantitative language of light-energy exchanges possible. Bohr's proposal that the hydrogen atom has allowed electronic energies then accounted for the main form of the observed pattern. Modern quantum mechanics preserved quantized states while replacing definite classical electron orbits with wave-based descriptions. This is the chain of evidence that makes later discussion of electronic configuration meaningful.

The conceptual map in eight questions

  1. What is measured? A distribution of radiation as a function of wavelength, frequency or wavenumber.
  2. Which appearance? A broad continuum, narrow atomic emission features, dark absorption features, or molecular bands.
  3. Whose spectrum? The element, ionization state, molecule and experimental conditions must be established.
  4. Where is the line? Its horizontal position identifies the spectral energy coordinate, while peak height reports a separate intensity quantity.
  5. What pattern appears in hydrogen? The Balmer and Rydberg relationships organize line positions by a pair of positive integer level indices.
  6. What does the pattern imply? Specific differences between permitted bound electronic energies.
  7. How does theory explain it? Quantum radiation and allowed electronic states connect photon energy to atomic transitions.
  8. What does this not yet establish? The exact occupancy, quantum numbers and many-electron electronic configurations of arbitrary atoms.

How to study this chapter without simply memorizing it

Before revising the formulas, try to explain why a hot dense source may appear continuous while an excited low-density atomic source gives narrow emission features. Then draw the two-state diagram for absorption and emission and state the experimental conditions required in each. Build Balmer lines from the common lower state n=2 rather than memorizing wavelengths alone. After that, practice converting nm to m, recognizing wavenumber, and using the Rydberg relation with clearly declared assumptions. End with a comparison: why does chlorophyll show broad bands instead of a hydrogen-like set of sharp atomic lines?

A student who can answer those questions has reached the intended outcome. Electronic configuration is now a natural next question about the permitted states of a multi-electron atom—not an arbitrary sequence of s, p and d letters learned without an experimental reason.
