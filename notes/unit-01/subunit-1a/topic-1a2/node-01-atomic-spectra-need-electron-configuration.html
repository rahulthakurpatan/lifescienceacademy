Atomic Spectra and the Need for Electron Configuration

A scientific detective story told through real observations: how missing colours in sunlight and bright lines in hydrogen led to the idea of allowed electronic energies.

Learning outcomes: Read and classify continuous, emission, absorption and molecular spectra; explain the actual history from Fraunhofer to Balmer and Rydberg; identify hydrogen spectral series, line positions and convergence; interpret elementary wavelength and wavenumber relations; distinguish measurements from models; and explain why atomic spectra motivated discrete electron energy states and, later, electronic configurations.

1.A.2.01.1 A familiar flame, an unfamiliar question

Light from a candle seems unremarkable until we ask what the flame can tell us about the matter inside it. A sodium-containing sample may tint a flame yellow. Hydrogen gas excited in a discharge tube can glow pinkish, yet a closer examination shows that its light is not distributed continuously across all visible colours. Hidden inside an ordinary-looking glow is a striking regularity.

Now imagine separating that glow into wavelengths. If every colour were represented equally, it would be easy to describe the light as a blend. Instead, hydrogen yields a small set of particularly sharp spectral lines. The challenge for nineteenth-century physicists was that an atom seemed to favour certain energies of light and to avoid the energies in between. What property of the atom could produce such selectivity?

That question marks the beginning of electronic structure as an experimental problem. We already know from Topic 1.A.1 that atoms contain nuclei and electrons. In this chapter we shall not begin by assigning electrons to shells or filling boxes. We shall first examine the evidence that forced scientists to propose distinct electronic energy states.

What you will be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to distinguish a continuous spectrum from line and band spectra; reconstruct the sequence of observations leading to the hydrogen spectral formula; recognize the major hydrogen series; relate emitted or absorbed light to differences between allowed atomic energies; and explain why the spectral evidence makes electron configuration a meaningful next question.

The chapter follows the historical question before the modern answer. That ordering matters: a line spectrum was measured long before quantum orbitals were developed.

1.A.2.01.2 What scientists mean by a spectrum

A distribution, not simply a rainbow

A spectrum describes how a measured property of radiation is distributed with wavelength, frequency, or another spectral coordinate. A spectrum may be represented as coloured light, as intensity plotted against wavelength, or as a numerical table. These are different displays of the same underlying measurement when carefully defined.

A prism bends different wavelengths by different amounts; a diffraction grating separates radiation by interference. Both can make an otherwise mixed light source reveal a structured pattern. The device used to separate and observe light is generally called a spectroscope or spectrometer, depending on its purpose and design.

Why the horizontal axis matters

A graph labelled 'Intensity versus wavelength' asks what wavelengths a source emits or what wavelengths reach the detector. A graph labelled 'Absorbance versus wavelength' asks how strongly a sample reduces transmitted light under a specified arrangement. One cannot exchange their vertical axes and keep the same interpretation.

A spectrum is also larger than visible light. Many biological molecules absorb mainly in the ultraviolet; many rotations and vibrations are interrogated in the infrared. The distinctive spectrum of an isolated atom is not restricted to what the human eye can see.

First read the axes. A peak in an emission plot is not automatically a peak in an absorption plot.

1.A.2.01.3 The light itself: wavelength, frequency and colour

Why coloured radiation can be measured

Electromagnetic radiation is characterized by wavelength λ, the distance between successive corresponding points of a wave, and frequency ν (Greek nu), the number of oscillations per second. In vacuum they are related by the speed of light c. Shorter wavelengths correspond to higher frequencies; the relationship is exact for vacuum propagation and is approximately used for air in many introductory visible-light examples.

1.A.2.01.4 Before atoms were understood: Newton's prism

A question about white light

When a beam of sunlight passes through a prism, its colours spread out. Newton's seventeenth-century experiments helped establish that the prism does not manufacture the colours out of nothing; it separates components already present in white light. This was a crucial change in the way light was studied.

However, an elegant prism rainbow could still conceal a problem. The colours look blended to the eye, yet under better spectral resolution the sunlight spectrum contains specific dark features. Those missing-looking pieces made light a tool for examining matter rather than merely a subject of optics.

Why this is only the beginning

Newton's prism had no need for a modern electronic configuration model. The later idea that atomic electrons occupy allowed states emerged only when observations of particular spectral lines demanded an explanation. We must not credit nineteenth-century observers with knowledge they did not yet possess.

1.A.2.01.5 A continuous spectrum: when many wavelengths are present

What a continuum looks like

A hot, dense source or a suitable broadband lamp can produce radiation over a broad uninterrupted range of wavelengths. After dispersion, the detector sees a smooth distribution rather than a few isolated bright bars. 'Continuous' means the source emits across a range, not that its intensity is exactly identical at every wavelength.

Thermal sources such as incandescent material are often approximated by a broad thermal radiation distribution. The shape depends on temperature and emissivity; a perfect blackbody is a theoretical idealization. A visible filament may glow from red to white as its temperature rises, but 'white' is still an appearance produced by a mixture of wavelengths.

One important qualification

A dense source need not be a perfectly smooth emitter at all wavelengths. Materials can have selective absorption, surface features or molecular transitions. The continuum concept describes a broad baseline, not a promise that every spectrum from a dense object lacks lines.

1.A.2.01.6 Emission from an excited, low-density gas

A different observation

When a low-density atomic gas is excited, for example in a suitable discharge apparatus, its emitted radiation may be concentrated in narrow features separated by intervals with relatively little emission. On a dark background these appear as bright lines. The pattern is often specific enough to help identify the atomic species.

The conditions matter. Atoms are not guaranteed to show every permitted line with equal strength, and a visible discharge does not imply that only visible transitions occur. Populations of excited states, the nature of excitation, pressure and the instrument all affect which lines appear and how strongly.

Why a line is not a coloured strip painted onto the gas

The line is the spectrometer's image or plot of radiation concentrated around a characteristic wavelength. It is not a thin physical ribbon extending through the gas. In an ideal isolated transition the emitted radiation is associated with a well-defined energy difference, while real measured features have finite widths for several reasons.

1.A.2.01.7 Dark absorption lines: a second kind of selectivity

Let broadband light pass through a gas

An absorption experiment differs from an emission experiment. Light from a broadband source passes through material, and the transmitted intensity is compared with the incident intensity. If certain photon energies can promote the sample into allowed excited states, radiation at corresponding wavelengths may be preferentially removed from the transmitted beam.

Against a continuous background, these losses can appear as dark lines. The word 'dark' means less light reaches the detector at those wavelengths compared with its surroundings. It does not necessarily mean that no photons of that wavelength remain, nor that the gas destroys energy. Some absorbed energy may later be re-emitted in different directions or converted through other processes.

The geometry is part of the result

If absorption is followed by re-emission, a detector placed along the original transmitted beam may still record a dip because photons were redirected or transformed. In simple thermal astronomical situations, cooler gas in front of a hotter continuum source can yield absorption features. The full radiative-transfer situation is more complicated than a universal rule that cool gas always absorbs.

1.A.2.01.8 Fraunhofer's lines: the Sun had gaps in its rainbow

A surprising solar observation

Early observers had already noticed nonuniformities in sunlight, and Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped numerous dark lines in the solar spectrum in the early nineteenth century. These were precise, repeatable wavelength features rather than random marks caused by the glass prism.

At the time, one could record their locations without knowing the complete modern atomic explanation. The dark lines became reference marks in optical measurements, but they also posed a more interesting question: what in sunlight or its passage through matter selects these wavelengths?

1.A.2.01.9 Kirchhoff and Bunsen turned colours into chemical evidence

A productive meeting of experiment and interpretation

In the middle of the nineteenth century, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen connected the characteristic emission of chemical elements with absorption at matching wavelengths. This work helped establish spectroscopy as a method of chemical analysis: the same elemental species could produce recognizable features in very different samples.

An emission line from a controlled atomic source could correspond to a dark absorption line at the same spectral position in light that passed through an appropriate absorbing gas. That correspondence suggested that atoms select particular wavelengths rather than producing an arbitrary mixture of light.

1.A.2.01.10 Why a spectral fingerprint is useful—and imperfect

Each species has a characteristic set of transitions

An isolated atom of a specified element and ionization state has a characteristic structure of electronic energy levels. The differences between those levels permit particular spectral transitions. Their wavelengths give an identity-sensitive pattern, which is the basis for the familiar 'atomic fingerprint' analogy.

Yet a fingerprint should not be taken too literally. Different ions of the same element have different spectra, line intensities vary with excitation conditions, and some lines overlap those of other species. Reliable identification often compares multiple features against standards or evaluated databases.

1.A.2.01.11 Atomic lines, molecular bands and broad biological spectra

Atoms and molecules offer different structures

A free atom's sharp transitions are associated with discrete electronic energy-state differences. Molecules also have electronic states, but their nuclear vibrations and rotations introduce many additional transitions. These can create clustered bands and broad envelopes rather than a few visually isolated atomic lines.

1.A.2.01.12 Why hydrogen was the right atom to interrogate

One electron changes the difficulty

The neutral hydrogen atom contains one proton and one electron. Its internal structure is far simpler than the structure of atoms with many interacting electrons. That makes hydrogen a natural starting point for a theory that tries to connect spectral energies to electronic states.

Physicists did not choose hydrogen because it emitted every colour or because its spectrum lacked structure. They chose it partly because a relatively simple and highly regular series of lines could be measured precisely. The regularity was an invitation to find a mathematical rule.

1.A.2.01.13 Balmer's pattern: four visible lines among a family

The historical surprise

The prominent visible lines of atomic hydrogen occur approximately at 656.3 nm (red), 486.1 nm (blue-green), 434.0 nm (blue-violet) and 410.2 nm (violet). They did not occur at arbitrary wavelengths. Johann Jakob Balmer found in 1885 that their positions could be described by a simple relationship involving integers.

1.A.2.01.14 Balmer's empirical relationship is a clue, not yet a theory

An integer pattern found before the mechanism

Balmer's formula for the visible hydrogen lines can be written using an integer n greater than 2. One form uses a characteristic wavelength factor B in nanometres: λ=B n²/(n²−4), with n=3,4,5,… for the Balmer series. The modern form is more usually expressed through wavenumber, as we shall see next.

1.A.2.01.15 The Rydberg relation organizes hydrogen spectra

From one visible series to a general pattern

Johannes Rydberg developed an empirical relation in which wavelengths of hydrogen lines could be expressed in terms of the difference between reciprocal squares of two positive integers. This form accommodates multiple series, including those outside the visible region.

1.A.2.01.16 Wavenumber is a convenient spectral coordinate

One wavelength can be written in three useful ways

Wavelength λ, frequency ν and wavenumber ṽ (often written σ or ᵥ) all identify a spectral position. The spectroscopic wavenumber is simply 1/λ when λ is measured in vacuum; its unit is inverse length. If λ is in centimetres, wavenumber is in cm⁻¹. The Rydberg formula is especially simple in inverse-length form.

1.A.2.01.17 Why the Balmer lines crowd toward a limit

Let the upper integer increase

For the Balmer series the lower index remains n=2. As the upper index grows, 1/nupper² becomes smaller and approaches zero. Consequently, the wavenumber approaches RH/4 and the wavelength approaches a finite lower limit, approximately 364.6 nm in the familiar Balmer context.

1.A.2.01.18 The other hydrogen series extend beyond visible light

Keep the lower index fixed

Spectral series are classified by the lower energy level involved in the transition. For the Lyman series, nlower=1; for Balmer, nlower=2; for Paschen, nlower=3. Brackett and Pfund follow with nlower=4 and 5.

1.A.2.01.19 From observed rules to a question about energy

An atom behaves as if it has a restricted menu

An ordinary continuous classical radiator can vary in energy over an effectively continuous range. Hydrogen's sharp spectral pattern suggested that its internal changes were instead constrained by specific allowed energy differences. If every electronic energy were permitted, the observed regularity would be harder to understand.

1.A.2.01.20 Planck and Einstein supplied the light-energy bridge

1900: a new way of handling thermal radiation

Max Planck introduced quantization into his explanation of thermal radiation near the beginning of the twentieth century. His work did not start as a theory of hydrogen's internal electron arrangement; it was a response to a different experimental difficulty, the spectrum of thermal radiation.
