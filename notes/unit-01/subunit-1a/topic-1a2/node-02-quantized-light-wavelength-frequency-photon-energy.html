1.A.2.02.1 A red line poses a quantitative question

From recognition to measurement

Last chapter, a discharge tube gave us a striking clue: the red hydrogen Hα line appears at a particular wavelength. Its position suggests a difference between allowed atomic energies, but recognizing a line is not the same as calculating the energy of the radiation. How much energy is delivered by one photon whose wavelength is about 656 nm? This is the question that leads our new chapter.

The answer has consequences far beyond hydrogen. A biologist studying a chlorophyll absorption band, a fluorescence microscope or a photosynthetic photon flux is asking different versions of the same question: what energy does radiation provide, and how many photons are involved? A line position, a light intensity and a photon count are three different measurements.

We shall first examine the wave description that gives wavelength and frequency, then follow the historical evidence that made quantized energy necessary. Only after the reasoning is clear will we treat the equations as tools for numerical science. The outcome should be more durable than memorizing one conversion formula.

The guiding distinction: photon energy depends on frequency; the energy carried by a beam also depends on how many photons are present.

1.A.2.02.2 Wavelength describes a distance, not an orbit

One repeating pattern

The wavelength λ is the distance from one point of a periodic wave to the next corresponding point in the same phase, such as crest to crest. It can be expressed in metres, nanometres or micrometres. A sketch of a wave represents variation in an electromagnetic field; it does not show the literal path followed by a photon through space.

If two different wavelengths are displayed on the same horizontal distance, the shorter-wavelength pattern completes more cycles. This visual comparison will soon explain why frequency rises as wavelength falls, provided propagation speed stays the same.

Why wavelength matters to an instrument

An optical spectrometer separates radiation according to its spectral characteristics. A line labelled 500 nm refers to radiation with a characteristic wavelength, not to a 500-nm electron orbit. The difference between the measured wavelength and a possible electronic state change must be established through an energy relation, not assumed from a diagram.

Original schematic of a field oscillation. The curve is a representation of a wave component, not an electron trajectory.

1.A.2.02.3 Frequency counts cycles per second

Watch a single location as a wave passes

At a fixed point in space, the electric field of a periodic wave oscillates in time. Its frequency ν is the number of complete oscillations per second. One hertz means one cycle per second, and 5×10¹⁴ Hz is an entirely reasonable visible-light frequency. Frequency is an inverse-time quantity.

The period T is the time taken for one cycle. If T is small, more cycles occur each second and frequency is high. Thus ν=1/T. The Greek letter ν is read 'nu'; textbooks may also use f. Neither of these is the number of photons arriving at the detector each second.

A thought experiment with two red lamps

Two sources can emit radiation at nearly the same frequency yet differ greatly in brightness. One source may send far more photons each second. That changes the total radiative output, not the intrinsic frequency or energy of each red photon. This distinction will be central to the photoelectric effect.

ν = 1/Tν in hertz (s⁻¹); T in seconds.

1.A.2.02.4 Derive the wave relation instead of memorizing it

How fast does a repeating wave pattern move?

A periodic travelling wave advances by one wavelength in one period. Speed is distance divided by time, so v=λ/T. Since ν=1/T, it follows that v=λν. In vacuum the propagation speed of electromagnetic radiation is c, giving c=λν. The equation is an account of the wave's geometry and timing, not yet a quantum postulate.

Its units provide a useful test. Wavelength has units of metres and frequency has units of inverse seconds. Multiplying them gives metres per second. If the final unit is metres squared per second, the derivation or substitution went wrong.

c = λν   ;   ν = c/λ   ;   λ = c/νVacuum speed c=299 792 458 m s⁻¹, exact in SI.

The meaning of inverse proportionality

In vacuum, c is fixed. Halving wavelength therefore doubles frequency. A violet photon has higher frequency than a red photon because its vacuum wavelength is shorter. But the higher frequency does not by itself tell us which beam is brighter.

A wavelength comparison is an energy-per-photon comparison only after we introduce the photon relation. A brightness comparison requires a separate photon-count or power measurement.

1.A.2.02.5 When light enters a medium, keep the frequency straight

Air and vacuum are almost—but not exactly—the same

The vacuum relation c=λν uses vacuum wavelength. For a monochromatic component entering a transparent stationary medium, frequency remains continuous across the boundary while phase velocity and wavelength may change. If the phase refractive index is n, vphase=c/n and λmedium=λvacuum/n for the usual simple description.

This is why an atomic wavelength tabulated in standard air can differ slightly from its vacuum counterpart even though the transition frequency is the same. Spectral analysis should not treat an air-vacuum convention as a different atomic energy gap.

A subtle distinction in real materials

A pulse of light can have group and energy-transport velocities that differ from the phase velocity, especially in dispersive media. At this stage we need only know that using an in-medium wavelength in hc/λ while silently retaining the vacuum c can yield an incorrect photon energy. E=hν avoids that particular ambiguity.

Refractive index can depend on wavelength and material conditions. For introductory visible-light calculations, a problem may explicitly say 'assume vacuum' or permit the approximation that air and vacuum values are nearly the same. Precision work must state the convention.

λmedium = λvacuum/nFor a phase refractive index n at the given frequency; this is not a general formula for every pulse or dispersive phenomenon.

1.A.2.02.6 Unit conversions must come before arithmetic

The nanometre is not the SI metre

One nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m; one micrometre is 10⁻⁶ m; one ångström is 10⁻¹⁰ m. Therefore 500 nm = 5.00×10⁻⁷ m = 0.500 µm = 5000 Å. The numbers are different only because the unit changes. The physical wavelength has not been altered.

A calculation with c in metres per second and a wavelength typed as '500' without its 10⁻⁹ conversion can miss the correct frequency by nine orders of magnitude. This is a scientific error, not merely a cosmetic formatting problem.

The practical way to avoid it

Write the given value with its unit, convert into the units required by the constants, substitute into the equation, cancel the units and only then round the answer. If you use a special eV·nm shortcut later, check that your input is actually in nanometres.

1.A.2.02.7 The photon relation gives energy to one quantum

What a wavelength can tell us

Planck's constant h provides the scale relating electromagnetic frequency to energy per photon. A photon of frequency ν has energy E=hν. Combining this with ν=c/λvacuum yields E=hc/λvacuum. At last, a measurement of wavelength becomes a measurement of the energy associated with one quantum of light.

This energy is tiny in ordinary joules but significant on atomic and molecular scales. Many biological absorptions correspond to electronic energy changes of a few electronvolts. The number of photons in a beam determines its total energy; the individual photon frequency determines its energy per quantum.

Ephoton = hν = hc/λ₀λ₀ is the vacuum wavelength; h=6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J s.

A conceptual comparison

A 400-nm photon has nearly twice the energy of an 800-nm photon. But a powerful 800-nm source can deliver more total energy in one second than a weak 400-nm source because the photon arrival rates can differ enormously. The equation cannot be used to rank source powers from colours alone.

Never replace photon energy with beam intensity. The equation E=hν describes a single photon; total radiation energy requires the number of photons or an appropriate power measurement.

1.A.2.02.8 Check the photon equation with physical dimensions

Why the units cancel

Planck's constant h has the units joule second, while frequency has inverse seconds. Multiplying gives joules. The product hc has units joule metres; dividing by wavelength in metres again gives joules. The dimensional result is exactly what we need for an energy.

This is a powerful protection against errors. If a student combines h in J s with a frequency in s⁻¹ and obtains an answer whose units are J s², the algebra cannot be correct even if the numerical digits look familiar.

(J s)(s−1) = J   ;   (J m)/m = JDimensional validation of E=hν and E=hc/λ₀.

Rearranging the formula without guessing

If energy is given, ν=E/h and λ₀=hc/E. If wavelength is given, convert it to a vacuum-equivalent wavelength and compute frequency first, then energy. If frequency is given, energy is a direct multiplication. Each route describes the same radiation as long as all units and assumptions are consistent.

1.A.2.02.9 Planck's constant connects enormous frequencies to tiny energies

The scale of the microscopic world

Planck's constant has the exact SI value 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J s. It is universal; one constant is used for hydrogen, a silicon detector and a biological pigment. The very small numerical value reflects the energy and time scales of quantum phenomena when expressed in everyday SI units.

Multiplying h by a visible-light frequency near 5×10¹⁴ Hz gives a photon energy of order 3×10⁻¹⁹ J. That is an extremely small quantity in joules, but atomic and molecular energy changes occur on just such scales. Millions of photons are not required to make an elementary electronic transition possible; a single correctly absorbed photon can excite a suitable molecular system.

A constant versus an experimental input

The exact defining value of h is not a fresh empirical parameter adjusted separately to make every textbook numerical work. The wavelength or frequency is the measured input and can have uncertainty. When an input wavelength has only three reliable significant digits, reporting a photon energy to ten digits is not justified simply because h is now exact by SI definition.

h = 6.62607015 × 10−34 J sPlanck constant; exact in the SI.

1.A.2.02.10 Planck's blackbody puzzle preceded modern photons

Why a smooth thermal spectrum created a theoretical crisis

A hot object emits radiation over a broad range of wavelengths. The blackbody is an ideal thermal emitter whose spectral distribution depends on temperature. Classical approaches did not account correctly for its observed full frequency distribution. Around 1900, Max Planck found a successful law by treating energy exchange of idealized oscillators in quantized units tied to frequency.

This history is often presented too quickly: Planck did not simply look at hydrogen's red line and announce the modern photon. Thermal radiation was a different experimental problem. The quantitative result introduced the scale hν into physics and helped open the way to a new account of how light and matter exchange energy.

Why thermal curves and atomic lines are different

A blackbody-like distribution is broad and varies smoothly with wavelength. A low-density atomic source can emit characteristic narrow lines. Both can be explained within modern quantum physics, yet it is incorrect to use the hydrogen Rydberg formula to predict the shape of a thermal radiator. A model is useful only within the physical system it describes.

1.A.2.02.11 Einstein asks why a metal emits electrons only under certain light

The experimental threshold

In the photoelectric effect, light incident on a suitable material can eject electrons. The key observation is not merely that light can release electrons. Under ordinary single-photon photoemission, there is a minimum incident frequency below which increasing the light intensity does not cause the expected emission.

There is also a distinction between how many electrons are produced and the maximum kinetic energy of those electrons. Above threshold, increasing intensity can raise the number emitted per second under suitable conditions; changing the frequency changes the energy available to each electron. These observations gave the photon idea clear experimental work to do.

Einstein's 1905 proposal

Einstein treated light as transferring energy in localized quanta of size hν, providing an explanation of the photoelectric observations. A single absorbed photon supplies energy to an electron. Some of that energy is needed to leave the surface; the remainder can appear as kinetic energy. This was not a denial that light exhibits wave interference and diffraction, but a demonstration that wave behaviour alone does not describe every interaction.

In the ordinary single-photon photoelectric model, more photons below threshold do not compensate for insufficient energy in each photon. Intensity and frequency must not be confused.

1.A.2.02.12 The work function is a specific energy requirement

What must an electron overcome?

A metal surface retains electrons through its electronic structure. The work function φ is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the relevant surface to vacuum under the chosen conditions. It is often reported in eV and depends on the surface material, chemical contamination and other conditions.

If the incident photon has energy hν≥φ, the elementary Einstein balance gives Kmax=hν−φ. That is the maximum kinetic energy of an ejected electron in the model, not a guarantee that every escaping electron has exactly that energy. Some may lose energy before reaching the detector.

Kmax = hν − φ   (if hν ≥ φ)Energy balance for idealized single-photon photoemission.

Negative kinetic energy is not an answer

If hν is smaller than φ, inserting the numbers gives a negative formal expression for Kmax. Electron kinetic energy is not physically negative. The correct conclusion is that the surface cannot emit an electron by the assumed single-photon mechanism at that frequency. Multiphoton effects under unusually intense illumination are a separate advanced phenomenon, not a contradiction of the stated model.

1.A.2.02.13 Threshold frequency and the longest effective wavelength

Derive the boundary

At the ideal emission threshold, the ejected electron can have vanishing maximum kinetic energy. Therefore hν₀=φ, and ν₀=φ/h. Light at higher frequency contains more energy per photon and may eject electrons; lower frequency does not meet the ordinary single-photon threshold.

The wavelength statement is reversed because frequency and vacuum wavelength are inversely related. The threshold wavelength λ₀=hc/φ is the longest vacuum wavelength capable of photoemission in the simple model. Wavelengths longer than this give photons with insufficient energy.

ν₀ = φ/h   ;   λthreshold = hc/φThese are ideal threshold relations; convert φ to joules for SI computation.

A quick conceptual test

If the work function increases while everything else remains the same, the threshold frequency increases and the threshold wavelength decreases. A surface that is harder to eject an electron from requires more energetic photons, not a brighter low-frequency beam. The relation between frequency and wavelength is what makes the two threshold statements consistent.

Minimum frequency and maximum wavelength refer to the same threshold. Reversing one but not the other is a frequent examination error.

1.A.2.02.14 Stopping potential turns a photon question into voltage

How the maximum electron energy is measured

In a simple photoelectric setup a retarding electric potential can prevent the most energetic emitted electrons from reaching the collecting electrode. Its stopping magnitude Vstop satisfies eVstop=Kmax. We use e as the positive magnitude of the elementary charge; the signed applied potential depends on the arrangement of electrodes.

Combining this with Einstein's equation gives eVstop=hν−φ. A graph of Kmax against ν has slope h, while a graph of Vstop against ν has slope h/e when the same ideal energy balance applies. This provides an experimental way of connecting frequency, voltage and the quantum constant.

Vstop = (h/e)ν − φ/eStopping-potential magnitude for a fixed surface in the ideal model.

What intensity does and does not change

Increasing light intensity above threshold can change the photocurrent—the rate of collected photoelectrons—under suitable conditions. It does not increase the maximum kinetic energy merely by increasing the number of arriving photons. To change the maximum electron energy, change the photon frequency or the surface conditions.

Original idealized graph of maximum electron energy versus frequency. This is a theoretical relation, not invented measured data.

1.A.2.02.15 Intensity and frequency are different experimental controls

Two ways to change the illumination

Imagine a monochromatic source with fixed photon frequency. Making it brighter generally increases the rate at which photons arrive at a surface, while the energy of each photon stays hν. In an ordinary photoelectric experiment above threshold, the resulting photocurrent may increase, but the maximum electron kinetic energy need not change.

Now imagine increasing frequency while holding the surface's work function fixed. Each photon carries more energy, so the maximum kinetic energy of an emitted electron increases once the threshold has been exceeded. The electron emission rate still depends on photon arrival and the probability of successful emission. More energy per photon does not necessarily mean more electrons per second.

Read the experimental graph

A photocurrent-versus-intensity graph concerns electron count rate, whereas a stopping-voltage-versus-frequency graph concerns maximum electron kinetic energy. They are not interchangeable. Identify which variable is held fixed before interpreting a change in the signal.

1.A.2.02.16 The electronvolt gives a useful energy scale

Why not always write joules?

A visible-light photon has energy around 10⁻¹⁹ J, so comparing atomic energies in joules can be cumbersome. An electronvolt (eV) is the amount of energy equal to the elementary charge magnitude multiplied by one volt. In the modern SI, one electronvolt is exactly 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ joules.

Despite its name, eV measures energy rather than electrical potential. A photon has no electric charge but can still carry energy of 2 eV. An electron moving through an electrical potential difference also changes energy, yet those are distinct physical contexts sharing the same energy unit.

1 eV = 1.602176634 × 10−19 JExact relation in SI units.

A conversion that should be automatic only after understanding

If photon energy is 3.20×10⁻¹⁹ J, divide by 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV to obtain approximately 2.00 eV. Conversely, multiply an eV value by the same conversion factor to express it in joules. The energy itself has not changed during the conversion.

Do not confuse an eV with a volt. Electronvolts are energy units; volts describe potential differences. They are related by the charge involved, but a photon-energy calculation does not turn photons into charged particles.

1.A.2.02.17 The 1239.84 shortcut comes from exact physical constants

Derive the number rather than memorizing it

Start with E=hc/λ₀, where λ₀ is wavelength in vacuum. Multiplying h=6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J s by c=299792458 m/s gives hc in joule metres. Convert joules to electronvolts using the elementary charge relation, and metres to nanometres. The numerical product becomes about 1239.841984 eV·nm.

This unit combination is the source of the familiar photon conversion E(eV)=1239.841984/λ₀(nm). The constant is not a separate law, and it is not dimensionless: the numerator carries an energy-length unit, which cancels the nanometre in the denominator.

E (eV) ≈ 1239.841984 / λ₀ (nm)Use vacuum-equivalent wavelength in nanometres.

One example checks the physical trend

At 400 nm, E≈3.100 eV; at 800 nm, E≈1.550 eV. The shorter wavelength gives twice the energy per photon. A powerful 800-nm beam can nevertheless deliver greater total power if its photon rate is much larger, which is why photon energy and radiative intensity must not be confused.

If wavelength is supplied in micrometres or ångströms, first convert it to nm or change the conversion factor accordingly. Using 1239.84 with a micrometre number causes a thousand-fold error.

1.A.2.02.18 The electromagnetic spectrum viewed in photon energies

One rule describes every electromagnetic region

The relation E=hν applies to radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma radiation. These named regions cover enormous ranges of photon energies. Their boundaries are approximate conventions, and radiation can be classified both by energy and by how it was generated or detected.

For a useful scale, wavelengths around 1000 nm carry about 1.24 eV per photon; 550 nm about 2.25 eV; 250 nm about 4.96 eV. The energy rises smoothly as wavelength shortens, even though the names of spectral regions change.

Why a biological response is not determined by region name

Ultraviolet photons often have more energy than visible photons, yet they do not all produce the same chemical or biological outcome. Absorption probability, the molecular excited state and what happens after excitation matter. Meanwhile lower-energy visible photons can cause highly specific biological effects when suitable pigments absorb them. A photon-energy calculation describes available energy, not an automatic chemical reaction.

1.A.2.02.19 Energy conservation during photon absorption and emission

Trace the atom's energy, not just the light

When an atom absorbs a photon and moves to an allowed higher state, its energy gain is positive. In emission the atom moves to a lower state and loses energy, while the emitted photon carries a positive energy equal to the loss. Thus ΔEmatter=+hν for absorption and −hν for emission under the ideal one-photon model.

The sign convention is not optional bookkeeping. An expression Efinal−Einitial can be negative while the energy of the emitted photon is positive. In more complicated molecules, energy can flow to vibrations, heat, molecular rearrangements or other channels, and a later emitted photon may carry less energy than the one absorbed.

These distinctions will reappear in fluorescence and photosynthesis. One cannot declare a violation of energy conservation merely because emission occurs at a longer wavelength: the rest of the energy may have moved into other degrees of freedom.

ΔEmatter = +hν (absorption)   ;   ΔEmatter = −hν (emission)

1.A.2.02.20 Hydrogen transitions now become numbers in electronvolts

Revisit the Balmer evidence

For the red Hα photon at about 656.3 nm, the conversion gives E≈1239.84/656.3≈1.89 eV. For Hβ near 486.1 nm, E≈2.55 eV. The blue-green photon is higher in energy per photon, regardless of whether its line is less intense on a particular detector.

The elementary hydrogen bound-state formula Eₙ≈−13.6/n² eV gives E3≈−1.51 eV and E2≈−3.40 eV, whose difference is about 1.89 eV. The numerical match links the observed spectral wavelength to an atomic energy difference. It does not tell us the electron's literal path through space.

Ground-state hydrogen has a binding energy near 13.6 eV in the introductory model. That ionization threshold is not the same as the energy of every visible Balmer photon. A bound-to-bound transition and a bound-to-continuum transition are distinct phenomena.

En≈−13.6/n2 eV   ;   Eemit=Eupper−Elower

1.A.2.02.21 Do not mistake the photon wavelength for orbital size

Two physically different kinds of descriptions

The wavelength of a hydrogen emission photon describes electromagnetic radiation. It is not an electron's orbit radius, orbital diameter or distance travelled in one transition. In modern quantum physics, the electron is described by a state with spatial probability structure, not by a trajectory inferred directly from a colour.

A photon wavelength can constrain a difference between electronic energies. It cannot, from one spectral line alone, supply the complete occupancy pattern of all electrons in a multi-electron atom. Those patterns will require quantum numbers, orbital symmetries, electron interactions and filling principles.

The next node of this course introduces electron matter waves and the de Broglie relation. Although both topics use wavelength, the wavelength of a photon and the wavelength associated with an electron's momentum are not interchangeable without understanding the separate physical systems.

The measured light belongs to the electromagnetic field. The electron's quantum state belongs to the material system. Spectroscopy connects their energies; it does not turn one into the other.

1.A.2.02.22 From one photon to beam power

Total energy needs a photon count

If a beam contains N photons, all at frequency ν, its energy is N hν. If Ndot photons arrive each second, the associated radiant power is P=Ndot hν. Power is measured in watts, where one watt means one joule per second. These relations are simple but conditional on a known photon energy or spectral distribution.

At the same radiant power, longer-wavelength light contains more photons per second because each photon carries less energy. At the same photon arrival rate, shorter-wavelength light carries more radiant power. A red and blue beam described as equally powerful are therefore not necessarily delivering equal numbers of photons.

For broadband illumination, wavelengths vary across the source. A single wavelength cannot represent all photons unless a suitable spectral approximation is justified. This is one reason modern plant-light measurements distinguish radiometric power from photon-based measurements.

Etotal=N hν   ;   P=(N/t)hν

1.A.2.02.23 Photon arrival rate converts power into a count

A monochromatic source delivering one milliwatt of 500-nm radiation carries an enormous number of photons each second. The energy per photon is hc/λ, while the source power is joules per second. Divide the power by the energy per photon to obtain photons per second. This is a count-rate estimate, not a measure of the photon frequency, which is already fixed by the wavelength.

For a broadband source there is no single characteristic photon energy unless a reasonable spectral approximation is stated. If both red and blue photons contribute to a measured power, their numbers must be related to their different energies. An arbitrary average wavelength cannot be substituted without justification.

A detector also does not necessarily count every incident photon. Collection efficiency, transmission losses and quantum efficiency affect what is recorded. A spectrometer or microscope signal must therefore be calibrated before converting detected events into a true photon arrival rate.

Photon rate = P/(hν) = Pλ₀/(hc), for monochromatic radiation.

1.A.2.02.24 The energy contained in one mole of photons

One mole of entities corresponds to Avogadro's constant, 6.02214076×10²³. If those entities are all photons at the same wavelength, their total energy is simply the energy of one photon multiplied by that enormous number. This makes the unit kJ per mole of photons useful in biophysics and photochemistry.

At 500 nm, an individual photon carries about 3.97×10⁻¹⁹ J. A mole of such photons therefore carries approximately 239 kJ. The calculation refers to incident photons; it does not prove that all energy is absorbed by a pigment or stored as chemical energy. Losses, reflection and reaction pathways require separate measurements.

The older term einstein has been used for a mole of photons, but 'mol photons' is clearer. Photon amount is not interchangeable with moles of glucose or oxygen produced. A biological quantum yield is needed to connect the number of absorbed photons to a measured molecular event.

Energy per mole of photons = N_A hν = N_A hc/λ₀.

1.A.2.02.25 Irradiance and exposure add area and time

Radiant power is the rate at which energy is delivered: watts are joules per second. Irradiance is radiant power incident per unit surface area under a defined geometrical arrangement, commonly W m⁻². Radiant exposure integrates irradiance through time, yielding J m⁻² for a constant or appropriately integrated exposure.

Photon irradiance counts photons incident per unit area and time, instead of counting joules. For a monochromatic beam normal to a surface, dividing irradiance by the energy of one photon gives photon count per square metre per second. The relationship can be extended to chemical amount units by dividing by Avogadro's constant.

Geometry should never be omitted. Tilting a leaf changes its projected receiving area; scattered light may arrive from multiple directions. Planar photon irradiance and photon fluence rate are defined differently when the directional distribution matters. A number without units and geometry is insufficient to characterize the light environment.

Irradiance = P/A; photon irradiance = (P/A)/(hν) for a normally incident monochromatic beam.

1.A.2.02.26 PAR and PPFD: why plant science counts photons

Photosynthetically active radiation commonly refers to wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm. Photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) measures incident photons in this range per square metre per second and is commonly expressed in µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Unlike W m⁻², this unit counts quanta rather than their combined radiant energy.

Pigments absorb discrete photons, but not every incident photon causes photochemistry. Reflection, transmission, spectral absorption, saturation and metabolism influence the eventual biological result. PPFD is therefore a controlled description of illumination, not a direct rate of photosynthesis.

A narrowband 500-nm source makes the conversion between energy-based irradiance and PPFD simple. For a broadband plant lamp, each wavelength has a different energy per photon. Accurate conversion must use spectral irradiance or a suitably calibrated photon sensor; one arbitrary representative wavelength is not generally enough.

PPFD = (photons m⁻² s⁻¹)/N_A, reported in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ after multiplying by 10⁶.

1.A.2.02.27 Equal photon flux is not equal power

Suppose a red source at 650 nm and a blue source at 450 nm each deliver the same number of photons per second. Because energy per photon is inversely related to wavelength, the blue source supplies more radiant power. Its energy per photon exceeds that of red by a factor of 650/450, about 1.44.

Reverse the comparison: if both sources have equal radiant power, the 650-nm source delivers more photons per second. This is not because red light is more 'effective' in all biological experiments; it is simply a consequence of photon energy accounting. The wavelength-dependent biological absorption of pigments is a further and separate question.

Researchers must therefore distinguish radiometric energy flux from photon flux and from photosynthetic response. Equal PPFD does not imply equal carbon fixation, and equal W m⁻² does not imply equal numbers of incident photons. Good experimental descriptions specify the measured quantity, spectral range and units.

At equal photon count, P_blue/P_red = E_blue/E_red = λ_red/λ_blue.

1.A.2.02.28 How many incident photons are actually absorbed?

An incident beam may contain a known photon count, but a specimen can reflect, transmit, scatter and absorb the radiation. The number of incident photons is therefore not the same as the number absorbed. If the absorbed fraction is a at a specified wavelength, the simple count relation is Nabsorbed=aNincident when a refers to the appropriate photon number fraction.

In a controlled optical measurement with negligible reflection and scattering, absorbance A=-log10 T may be connected to transmittance T=I/I0. A=1 corresponds to 10% transmission, so one might infer 90% loss from the transmitted beam. That is not always 90% true molecular absorption; scattering and reflection can also reduce transmitted light.

When connecting absorbed photons to chemical events, specify the absorbed photon count, sample geometry and detection model. An absorption reading is not automatically a photon flux, nor is it automatically a reaction yield. These distinctions will become important in the next chapter's quantum chemistry and in later photobiology.

Transmittance T=I/I0; A=-log10(T). Photon absorption fraction requires a valid optical loss model.

1.A.2.02.29 A chlorophyll molecule responds to photon energy

In photosynthesis, light-harvesting pigments contain electronic states that can absorb photons of appropriate energies. Absorption promotes an electronic excitation, and the energy can then be transferred within a pigment-protein complex toward a reaction centre. These processes depend on both photon energy and the molecular energy structure, not on a general rule that every visible photon is equally absorbed.

Chlorophylls a and b have strong absorption bands in parts of the blue and red visible regions. The absorption bands are broad molecular features shaped by bonding, vibrations and the environment. A 660-nm red photon has less energy than a 450-nm blue photon, yet both can participate in biological light harvesting when suitable transitions are available.

The photon equation specifies energy entering through one absorption event. It does not reveal the whole subsequent pathway to chemical energy, ATP, NADPH or fixed carbon. Those require an account of electron transfer, proton gradients and biochemical reactions, which belong to more advanced photosynthesis chapters.

For one absorbed photon at frequency ν, the primary energy input is hν. Biological efficiency is a separate measured quantity.

1.A.2.02.30 Absorption spectrum versus biological action spectrum

A pigment absorption spectrum tells how strongly the pigment absorbs light at each wavelength under specified conditions. An action spectrum measures how effectively different wavelengths drive a chosen biological response. The two are related but need not be identical, because an organism contains several pigments and downstream biochemical steps.

For example, a leaf can absorb blue and red light through chlorophyll and associated pigments, yet the rate of oxygen evolution or carbon assimilation under those wavelengths also depends on light intensity, acclimation and metabolic state. A light wavelength with high photon energy does not automatically produce the strongest photosynthesis.

To compare light sources fairly, a researcher might specify equal photon flux rather than equal radiant power, depending on the question. But equal incident photons still do not guarantee equal absorbed photons. This chain—from incident to absorbed to productive quanta—prevents the common shortcut of identifying light intensity with biological activity.

Incident photons → absorbed photons → excitation events → measured biological response: each step may have different efficiency.

1.A.2.02.31 Fluorescence begins after absorption, not before it

A fluorescent molecule first absorbs light into an excited state when a permitted interaction occurs. It may undergo internal relaxation, energy transfer, nonradiative decay or fluorescence. Fluorescence is radiative emission from an excited electronic state, but not every absorbed photon leads to a fluorescent photon.

In a familiar fluorophore, excitation can occur with blue light and emission may be observed at a longer wavelength such as green. This is not energy creation. The emitted photon can carry less energy because some of the original excitation energy was dissipated or transferred before emission.

The photon relation lets us calculate the energy of each incident and emitted photon separately. To compare total optical power one must also know photon counts and detection efficiencies. A bright fluorescence image is therefore not a direct readout of the energy of an individual emitted photon.

E_excitation=hc/λ_excitation and E_emission=hc/λ_emission, using vacuum wavelengths.

1.A.2.02.32 Stokes shift: a wavelength gap between excitation and emission

Many fluorescent molecules emit at a longer wavelength than that used to excite them. The difference is called a Stokes shift, commonly expressed as a wavelength separation or, more fundamentally in energy terms, a difference between excitation and emission energies. Since E is inversely related to λ, a longer emission wavelength means a lower emitted photon energy.

Take a hypothetical fluorophore excited at 450 nm and emitting at 520 nm. The excitation photon has energy about 2.76 eV and the emission photon about 2.38 eV, a difference near 0.37 eV. That energy difference may have been transferred through nonradiative processes; the exact path depends on molecular photophysics.

Not every fluorescence system follows one simple wavelength shift, and some cases involve anti-Stokes emission or other advanced processes. The standard beginner example should therefore be identified as common behaviour rather than an inviolable rule. The physics of fluorescence is richer than a two-colour arithmetic exercise.

ΔE = hc(1/λ_exc − 1/λ_em), positive for ordinary longer-wavelength emission.

1.A.2.02.33 Quantum yield connects absorbed photons with counted events

The word quantum yield is sometimes used casually as if it were another name for photon energy. In photochemistry, a quantum yield relates the number of a specified events to the number of photons absorbed under suitable definitions. For fluorescence, a common quantum yield is the number of emitted fluorescence photons divided by the number of absorbed excitation photons.

A fluorescence quantum yield of 0.60 means that, under the stated conditions, an average of 0.60 fluorescent photons are emitted per absorbed photon in the defined channel. It does not mean that each photon has sixty percent of its original energy. An emitted photon has its own energy hνem, and nonradiative pathways can account for missing energy.

Some photochemical quantum yields can exceed one when chain processes or other mechanisms yield multiple counted events per absorbed photon, so a blanket statement that every quantum yield must lie between zero and one would be false. The particular event being counted and the mechanism have to be specified.

Fluorescence quantum yield Φ_F = emitted fluorescence photon count / absorbed excitation photon count.

1.A.2.02.34 Ultraviolet photons and biologically relevant energy

Shorter-wavelength ultraviolet photons generally carry more energy per photon than visible photons. That increased energy can make additional molecular excitations accessible, including transitions in nucleic-acid bases and proteins. However, the effect of a UV source on DNA depends on absorption, wavelength, dose, exposure geometry and repair processes, not solely on an eV number.

At a typical 260-nm ultraviolet wavelength, one photon carries about 4.77 eV. This energy can be discussed quantitatively without claiming every such photon causes a fixed lesion. In real cells, absorbed energy can be dissipated or can lead to photochemical damage depending on molecular pathways and environmental context.

Photobiology therefore needs at least two descriptions of light: photon energies determined by the spectrum, and quantities of incident or absorbed radiation determined by photon flux, power and exposure. Neither description on its own predicts a medical outcome or a guaranteed molecular reaction.

Photon energy at 260 nm ≈ 1239.84/260 = 4.77 eV (vacuum approximation).

1.A.2.02.35 Protein absorbance and excitation energy

Aromatic amino-acid side chains, particularly tryptophan and tyrosine, can absorb ultraviolet light near 280 nm in many proteins. That wavelength corresponds to approximately 4.43 eV per photon. The measurement reflects molecular electronic transitions influenced by the surrounding chemical environment, not a universal one-electron hydrogen spectrum.

Two proteins with equal mass concentration may show different 280-nm absorbance because they contain different numbers of absorbing residues or have different molar extinction coefficients. A spectrophotometric concentration estimate therefore requires an appropriate standard or molecular coefficient and attention to interfering substances.

Once light is absorbed, protein fluorescence can provide information about some local environments or conformational changes, but a measured spectrum is not proof of one unique molecular structure. Photon energy defines an excitation scale; interpretation of optical signals requires chemical specificity and measurement controls.

E_photon for 280 nm ≈ 4.43 eV; absorbance and concentration need independent optical calibration.

1.A.2.02.36 Photoactive proteins and the danger of one-colour rules

Some biological proteins contain chromophores that absorb particular visible or ultraviolet wavelengths and trigger conformational or signalling changes. Plant photoreceptors, animal visual pigments and microbial light-sensitive proteins illustrate that even a modest-energy visible photon can initiate an important sequence of biological events if the molecular system is tuned to that wavelength.

A photon must be absorbed by the relevant chromophore for the primary light response to begin. The photon energy alone cannot identify the subsequent signalling pathway; protein structure, excited-state dynamics and molecular partners determine what follows. This is why an action spectrum is an experimental property of the system rather than a table obtained directly from hc/λ.

These examples connect electronic energy to life science without prematurely replacing the later chapters on molecular orbitals, phototransduction or photosynthetic electron transport. Our aim here is to establish the quantitative light input and its scientifically justified interpretation.

A photoreceptor's response requires both a suitable absorption transition and a downstream molecular mechanism.

1.A.2.02.37 A detector measures a signal, not every photon that exists

Optical sensors convert incident radiation into a measured electrical or digital signal. The relationship between the number of photons incident and the number of registered detection events depends on detector sensitivity, optical transmission, geometry and, where applicable, quantum efficiency. A detector that responds strongly at green wavelengths may respond weakly at an infrared wavelength despite equal incident photon counts.

Suppose 1000 photons are incident on a sensor with an effective probability of detection of 0.40 under defined conditions. The expected registered count is about 400, not 1000. The detected signal need not be exactly 400 in every trial because counting is statistical, and real systems may have background, saturation and nonideal response.

A biologically useful measurement distinguishes the sample's emitted or transmitted photons from the detected subset. Instrument calibration, filters, collection angles and background subtraction affect interpretation. Converting raw signal directly into molecular concentration or reaction rate without those controls is not scientifically justified.

Expected detected photons = effective detection fraction × incident photons (simple counting model).

1.A.2.02.38 Measurement uncertainty and meaningful significant digits

The exact SI values of h, c and elementary charge do not make all photon calculations exact. A wavelength measurement may be rounded, a source may have a finite spectral bandwidth, and a detector may be affected by calibration uncertainty. Calculated energy inherits uncertainty from the measured wavelength or frequency.

For E=hc/λ, small relative changes have opposite signs: a slightly larger wavelength gives a slightly lower calculated energy. The magnitude of the relative fractional energy uncertainty approximately matches that of wavelength in the simple single-variable case, provided uncertainties are small and other contributions negligible.

A wavelength stated as 500 nm to three significant digits justifies a photon energy such as 2.48 eV rather than 2.479683968... eV. Reporting a large number of decimals is not evidence of scientific accuracy; it can hide uncertainty in the inputs and in assumptions such as air-versus-vacuum wavelength.

For small uncertainty: ΔE/E ≈ Δλ/λ in magnitude, when E=hc/λ and other constants are exact.

Original conceptual inverse-energy relation, not measured experimental data.

1.A.2.02.39 Common photon-calculation traps and how to catch them

Most numerical errors in this topic arise before the final arithmetic. A common failure is using a wavelength in nanometres with c in metres per second without converting. Another is treating a photon frequency as the number of photons arriving every second; the first measures cycles of an electromagnetic field, while the second measures arrivals or flux.

The conversion E(eV)=1239.84/λ(nm) is powerful but depends on a vacuum-equivalent wavelength in nanometres. A photoelectric work function expressed in eV must be converted consistently if h is used in J s. If a calculated photoelectron Kmax is negative, the correct answer is not a negative kinetic energy but failure of the ordinary single-photon threshold.

A final trap is biological: PPFD, radiant irradiance, absorbed photons and photosynthetic yield describe different stages of an experiment. Treating one as another without a model can be a larger error than misplacing a decimal. Always name the physical quantity before calculating it.

Check the sequence: known quantity → correct formula → conversion → units → magnitude → physical interpretation.

1.A.2.02.40 The next question belongs to the electron, not the photon

We began with an atomic spectral line whose wavelength had been measured. Frequency linked that spatial pattern to a time-based oscillation, and Planck's relation gave the energy of a single photon. Einstein's photoelectric explanation made the energy-packet idea experimentally compelling, while biological examples showed why both photon energy and photon number matter.

None of these results has yet explained why electrons occupy particular spatial states. The wavelength we have computed belongs to electromagnetic radiation. A different quantum problem asks whether an electron with momentum also possesses a wavelength and how that affects the possibility of classical orbits. This was the conceptual opening for de Broglie's matter-wave hypothesis.

Node 1.A.2.03 will therefore move from photons to electrons. It will preserve the distinctions established here: a frequency is not a photon count, a photon wavelength is not an orbital radius, and a formula only makes a scientific prediction when its assumptions and units are satisfied.
