1.A.2.05.1 An electron has a state, not a pre-drawn route

The problem the last four nodes have brought us to

We have reached a productive difficulty. Hydrogen emits light at selected frequencies, photons have quantized energies, electrons show diffraction, and attempts to picture an electron moving along a perfectly specified atomic track collide with the position–momentum uncertainty relation. A planetary sketch can still help us remember the early Bohr model, but it cannot tell us the probability of finding an electron in a particular part of an atom.

Suppose a student asks where an electron is in hydrogen before a position measurement. A classical answer gives a point on an orbit and a velocity tangent to that circle. A quantum answer instead begins with the state in which the atom has been prepared. The state determines the possible measurement outcomes and their probabilities. Its spatial representation is a wavefunction, usually written ψ.

Why this chapter belongs before orbital quantum numbers

It would be easy to introduce 1s, 2p and 3d as names of boxes and immediately memorize filling rules. That reverses the learning order. Before learning orbital labels, we should understand what an orbital represents, why probability density matters, how normalization works and why certain quantum-state patterns are physically acceptable. The later quantum numbers will then describe allowed structures rather than arbitrary labels.

Guiding question: What mathematical object describes the electronic state, and how does a measurement turn that description into a position outcome?

1.A.2.05.2 1926: Schrödinger searched for a wave equation of matter

From de Broglie waves to atomic states

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that a material particle with momentum has an associated wavelength. Erwin Schrödinger developed wave mechanics in 1926, seeking equations whose solutions would describe the possible states of a quantum system. His celebrated work on quantization as an eigenvalue problem turned the idea of a matter wave into a route to calculating stationary atomic states and their energies.

The equation was not a claim that electrons are made of a vibrating fluid. It connected quantum wave amplitudes to kinetic and potential energies. With the right boundary conditions, the solutions need not allow every conceivable energy. A system can admit discrete bound states, and changes between states can account for the line spectra that began our atomic-structure story.

What a wave equation changes

The old approach had placed restrictions on an electron's path by hand. Wave mechanics instead asks which wavefunctions are compatible with the system's Hamiltonian and physical boundary conditions. The allowed patterns emerge from the mathematical problem. Later we shall learn how quantum numbers identify particular allowed families of solutions. For now the crucial point is that the equation predicts states, not miniature tracks.

This is the link to Heisenberg uncertainty: a spatially distributed wavefunction does not generally assign simultaneously sharp position and momentum. A quantum state can be stationary in its density while still possessing nonzero spreads of both observables.

1.A.2.05.3 Born's statistical interpretation gave the wavefunction physical meaning

A challenge Schrödinger's mathematics did not settle by itself

If the theory assigns a wavefunction ψ(x,t), what should a detector observe? A wavefunction can be negative or complex, so it cannot simply be interpreted as an ordinary probability. Max Born's 1926 statistical interpretation showed how amplitudes relate to the probabilities of quantum measurement outcomes, originally in the context of collision and scattering problems.

For a one-dimensional position description, |ψ(x,t)|² is the probability density. The chance of observing the particle in a sufficiently small interval from x to x+dx is |ψ(x,t)|² dx. Over a finite interval, the probability is the area under the density curve. In three dimensions we use |ψ(r,t)|² times a small volume element, integrated over the specified region.

ρ(x,t) = ψ*(x,t) ψ(x,t) = |ψ(x,t)|²   ;   P(a≤x≤b)=∫ab|ψ|² dxBorn rule for the position representation of a normalized single-particle state.

A local probability, not a material cloud

Calling |ψ|² an 'electron cloud' can be a helpful first visualization if we remember what the cloud represents. It is not an electron physically shredded into mist. Repeating an appropriate position measurement on identically prepared atoms builds a distribution; one idealized position detection remains a localized event. The cloud represents the pattern of possible outcomes.

1.A.2.05.4 A wavefunction is an amplitude, not an ordinary probability

Why squaring matters

A probability must be real and nonnegative. A quantum amplitude ψ can be a complex number. Its squared magnitude ψ*ψ is nonnegative, which makes it suitable for a probability density after normalization. A position amplitude of −0.2 and one of +0.2 have the same squared magnitude, but their signs can matter when they interfere with other amplitudes.

For a general complex amplitude a+ib, its complex conjugate is a−ib and the product is a²+b². This explains why |ψ|² is real and nonnegative. It does not imply that ψ is simply the square root of a probability density with an arbitrary sign that can always be ignored.

Distinguish two meanings of 'wave'

In a water wave, height may be measured directly. In quantum mechanics, the complex phase of an amplitude is generally not a direct meter reading. Its consequences can nevertheless be observed through interference. A spatial probability density alone does not reveal every phase relationship that the complete wavefunction carries, so two states with the same immediate |ψ|² profile may differ in momentum or future evolution.

ψ=a+ib   ;   ψ*=a−ib   ;   |ψ|²=a²+b² ≥ 0a and b are real-valued amplitude components. In one dimension, |ψ|² has units of inverse length for a normalized state.

1.A.2.05.5 Probability density is not the probability of a single exact point

A continuous variable needs an interval

Imagine a normalized electron wavefunction along the x axis. The quantity |ψ(x)|² is a density, typically measured in m⁻¹, so it is not itself a dimensionless probability. Integrating over a finite interval gives a number between zero and one. A density can have a numerical value larger than one in chosen units without violating probability rules, because the integrated area—not its peak height—is the probability.

For a continuous position distribution, the probability of measuring one mathematically exact coordinate is zero in the ideal probability model. This does not stop a laboratory detector from recording a result with finite spatial resolution. An observed dot represents a finite detection region and an outcome of the instrument, not a claim that the distribution has a finite probability mass at one continuum point.

The measurement question sets the region

Asking 'what is the probability the electron lies between 0 and L/2?' has a meaningful answer once ψ and the state are specified. Asking 'what is ψ at x=L/2?' is a different question: it asks for an amplitude at one coordinate. Only |ψ|² dx in an infinitesimal interval or an integral over a finite region produces a position probability.

P(x∈[a,b]) = ∫ab ρ(x) dx;   ρ=|ψ|²The integral, not the density at one point, is a dimensionless probability.

This distinction will matter in both molecular orbital maps and solved numerical exercises. A high probability density near one region does not mean all measurements occur there, and a low density does not always mean a measurement is absolutely impossible.

1.A.2.05.6 Normalization makes probabilities add up to one

A quantum state must describe some outcome somewhere

For a particle whose position could lie anywhere on a one-dimensional line, the probability of finding it somewhere is one. Therefore ∫|ψ(x,t)|²dx=1 over the full allowed domain. This is the normalization condition. In three dimensions the corresponding integral is taken over volume: ∫|ψ(r,t)|²d³r=1.

A raw mathematical solution of a wave equation may contain an arbitrary multiplicative amplitude. For example, ψ(x)=A f(x) often has a constant A that the differential equation does not determine. Normalization fixes the magnitude |A| so that the total probability is one. Multiplying by an overall constant complex phase of magnitude one does not change normalization.

A test before interpreting a graph

If ∫|ψ|²dx=4, the amplitude has not been normalized; probabilities obtained from that uncorrected function will not have the right total. Divide the amplitude by the square root of four, not by four. Probabilities depend on the square of amplitude, so normalization uses square roots.

∫all x|ψ(x,t)|² dx=1   ;   ∫all space|ψ(r,t)|² d³r=1For a normalized one-particle position wavefunction.

1.A.2.05.7 What changes when we study a three-dimensional atom?

From one coordinate to a location in space

An atomic electron is not restricted to one horizontal line. We ordinarily need coordinates (x,y,z), or alternatively spherical coordinates (r,θ,φ). Its wavefunction ψ(r,t) and probability density |ψ(r,t)|² describe a three-dimensional distribution. The probability of detection inside a chosen spatial volume is the volume integral of density.

Here a subtlety begins: probability per unit volume and probability per unit radial distance are not the same. A spherical shell at distance r has greater volume than a very thin shell nearer the centre, even if the density at each point were equal. To calculate a radial distribution in a spherically symmetric case, a factor 4πr² appears. The later atomic-orbital chapters will use this distinction to interpret hydrogen's radial shapes.

Why a density plot may appear like a cloud

An image can shade regions of high |ψ|² and leave low-density regions faint. This makes the word 'orbital cloud' intuitive. But the shading threshold is a choice, often enclosing a specified fraction of total probability. The atom does not have a hard solid outer surface merely because the picture has a drawn boundary.

P(V)=∫V|ψ(r,t)|² d³r;   P(r to r+dr)=4πr²ρ(r)drThe radial-shell expression assumes a spherically symmetric position density ρ(r).

1.A.2.05.8 An orbital is a permitted state, not a container for a ball

How chemistry vocabulary changes meaning

An atomic orbital in an appropriate one-electron description is a spatial wavefunction belonging to an allowed electronic state. It may be used as a building block in multi-electron atomic and molecular calculations. It is not a plastic shell through which an electron travels on a known course, nor is it a fixed region with a perfectly impenetrable edge.

The familiar names 1s, 2p and 3d combine quantum-number information that later nodes will explain systematically. Their shapes and nodes arise from wave equations and angular dependence. We should not memorize their allowed orientations before seeing why wavefunctions can have different spatial patterns.

What the orbital can predict

Once the wavefunction and state are defined, |ψ|² predicts position probabilities. Other observables, including energy and momentum, require their respective operators or basis representations. An orbital drawing by itself does not determine every observable; it is a visualization of part of the state information.

In a many-electron atom, a single orbital picture is also an approximation or component of a many-particle description. Electron correlation, antisymmetry and electron–electron repulsion complicate the exact state. Recognizing this boundary protects us from promising more than a single-electron wavefunction can deliver.

1.A.2.05.9 Relative phase is meaningful, global phase is not

Concept and physical meaning

Multiplying an entire normalized wavefunction by the same phase factor e^(iφ) does not change its probabilities, because that factor has squared magnitude one. Such a global phase is physically redundant in the ordinary quantum-state description. This is an important reason ψ itself cannot be interpreted as a directly observed physical density.

Work through the consequences

By contrast, imagine two coherent alternatives contributing amplitudes ψ1 and ψ2 to an event. Their total amplitude is ψ1+ψ2, so the squared magnitude contains an interference term depending on relative phase. Two contributions of +0.30 reinforce to give squared magnitude 0.36, whereas +0.30 and −0.30 cancel at that event location. Adding 0.09 and 0.09 as independent probabilities would lose the coherent interference.

Scientific interpretation

Relative phase later becomes important in bonding and electronic transitions: constructive and destructive interference can change the distribution of density. It is not correct to erase the sign of an orbital wavefunction before forming a coherent combination. Only the overall global phase of one whole state is freely unobservable; relative relationships between components can affect experiments.

1.A.2.05.10 Why an orbital may contain lobes and regions of zero amplitude

Concept and physical meaning

Wavefunctions can reinforce in some spatial regions and cancel in others. When an allowed atomic state has a complicated angular or radial structure, its amplitude can change sign or phase across space. An orbital visualization captures part of that pattern, and density |ψ|² indicates where a position measurement is more or less likely.

Work through the consequences

The lobe of an orbital is not a physical capsule containing a fragment of an electron. A wavefunction can have amplitude on both sides of a nodal surface while the detector produces one localized outcome in a suitable measurement. The different colours often used for opposite lobes usually identify opposite mathematical phase or sign, not two different electron charges.

Scientific interpretation

Orbital patterns arise from the potential and the allowed solutions of a quantum-state equation. Simply drawing a two-lobed shape does not prove that an electron occupies a p state; actual orbital labels require quantum numbers and the underlying spatial wavefunction. We establish the meaning of nodes now, while their detailed counting and angular structures belong to later curriculum nodes.

1.A.2.05.11 A node means an exact zero, not merely a faint region

Concept and physical meaning

A nodal point, line or surface is a location where a specified wavefunction has exactly zero amplitude in the mathematical state. Since probability density equals its squared magnitude, that idealized state has zero position probability density at the node. A pale region in an image need not be a true node; it may simply have a small nonzero density or be suppressed by display settings.

Work through the consequences

Consider the simple standing-wave illustrations sin(πx/L) and sin(2πx/L) on 0

Scientific interpretation

The sign change across a nodal surface can matter when wavefunctions interfere or overlap with another state. Yet density alone cannot distinguish positive from negative real amplitude, since both yield positive squares. We should therefore never confuse an electron orbital's nodal plane with a wall, trajectory or partition of electric charge.

1.A.2.05.12 Candidate wavefunctions need more than attractive drawings

Concept and physical meaning

A formula proposed for a bound electronic state must satisfy the relevant Schrödinger equation, physical domain and boundary conditions. It must allow probabilities to be normalized and give suitable expectation values for the observables being discussed. A symmetric or smooth-looking graph is not sufficient evidence that the function represents an allowed state.

Work through the consequences

Square-integrability over the allowed spatial region is central to ordinary normalized bound states. Generalized plane-wave momentum states used for scattering are a different mathematical idealization and cannot be normalized by the same integral over an infinite line in the straightforward way. A student must identify whether the question concerns a bound state, an ideal free wave or a localized packet.

Scientific interpretation

Regular finite potentials normally impose appropriate continuity requirements, whereas infinite barriers and singular interactions need their own boundary conditions. Never state without qualification that all first derivatives of all physical wavefunctions are continuous across every possible potential discontinuity. The Hamiltonian and its domain decide which mathematical functions are acceptable.

1.A.2.05.13 The time-dependent Schrödinger equation governs state evolution

Concept and physical meaning

Schrödinger's time-dependent equation can be written compactly as iℏ ∂Ψ/∂t=ĤΨ. The left side describes how the complex state amplitude evolves in time. The Hamiltonian on the right represents the energy structure of the modeled system. This is a law for quantum-state dynamics, not a measurement of where the electron sits at one instant.

Work through the consequences

For a single nonrelativistic particle in a scalar potential, the familiar Hamiltonian contains a kinetic term −(ℏ²/2m)∇² and a potential-energy function V(r,t). The Laplacian is a spatial second-derivative operator. Notice that V denotes potential energy in this formula; it is not automatically an accelerating voltage measured in volts. The physical model must specify which interaction energy is intended.

Scientific interpretation

The equation can be applied to free or confined particles and is an important basis for atomic wave mechanics. More complete models incorporate spin, electromagnetic vector potentials, relativity and multiple interacting electrons. This introductory chapter explains what the equation says and why states depend on a potential, but does not claim to solve its full differential equation.

1.A.2.05.14 The time-independent equation identifies allowed energy states

Concept and physical meaning

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, one can seek stationary states whose spatial wavefunctions satisfy Ĥψ=Eψ. Such an equation is called an eigenvalue problem. It asks which wavefunctions retain their proportional relationship under the energy operator and which energy values E are compatible with the potential and boundary conditions.

Work through the consequences

Not every arbitrary function with an attractive shape is an eigenfunction. Nor can one select an energy E at random without solving or otherwise constraining the physical problem. Hydrogen's Coulomb potential, an ideal one-dimensional box and a complicated molecule present very different Hamiltonians, so their state patterns and allowed energies differ.

Scientific interpretation

The time-dependent and time-independent equations are not rival theories. The latter gives stationary energy patterns in a special setting; the former governs state evolution generally. Multi-electron chemistry uses spatial orbitals as useful components, but the exact many-electron state typically depends on the coordinates and spins of all particles, not only one ordinary ψ(r).

1.A.2.05.15 Why a stationary state still has quantum dynamics

Concept and physical meaning

A stationary energy eigenstate of a time-independent Hamiltonian can be expressed as Ψ(r,t)=ψ(r)exp(−iEt/ℏ). Its complex phase changes with time, but exp(−iEt/ℏ) has magnitude one. Therefore |Ψ(r,t)|²=|ψ(r)|²: the position probability density is time independent even though the state is not literally a marble frozen at a coordinate.

Work through the consequences

This is consistent with Heisenberg uncertainty. The electron may have a distribution of positions and momenta while the density remains stationary. The phrase 'stationary' describes the state probability pattern, not zero momentum, zero kinetic energy or the absence of quantum evolution. The first hydrogen orbital will later make this distinction particularly clear.

Scientific interpretation

A coherent combination of two different energy eigenstates can have a changing relative phase and time-dependent interference. Thus not every solution of a time-independent Hamiltonian has constant local density. Stationarity is a property of particular energy states, while a general superposition can produce changing spatial probabilities.

1.A.2.05.16 Boundaries and the physical potential select quantum patterns

Concept and physical meaning

An energy eigenvalue is not predicted by writing the symbol E on the page. Its value follows from the Hamiltonian, the spatial domain and appropriate boundary conditions. A particle whose wavefunction must vanish at two idealized walls faces different constraints from an electron attracted to a proton by an approximately Coulomb potential.

Work through the consequences

A one-dimensional box is a useful analogy because only wave patterns fitting the boundaries are accepted; different patterns can have different energies and nodes. We can calculate probabilities with simple given box wavefunctions without solving the full differential equation in this node. That preserves the syllabus boundary while giving students meaningful mathematics to practise.

Scientific interpretation

In real chemistry, molecular electronic states depend on several charged nuclei, multiple electron interactions and an environment. Orbital shapes and energy differences cannot be deduced merely by memorizing a generic standing-wave formula. The correct habit is to ask: what potential, what boundaries, what preparation and which observable are being calculated?

1.A.2.05.17 How to normalize a simple constant amplitude

Build the concept

Suppose a one-dimensional illustration assigns ψ(x)=A for 0≤x≤L and zero outside, with the understanding that we are discussing a normalized piecewise probability state rather than solving a particular Hamiltonian. The probability density in the interval is |A|², a uniform constant. Probability must integrate to one, so L|A|²=1, giving |A|=1/√L.

Work through the mathematics

Notice that the amplitude has units L^(−1/2), not dimensionless units. That is required because |ψ|²dx must be dimensionless. If the length L is specified in metres, A is measured in m^(−1/2). A uniform position density means equal-length subintervals have equal probabilities; it does not assert the electron has no momentum spread.

Interpretation and limitations

A student may incorrectly set A=1/L because total probability is one. But squaring A would then give 1/L², and integrating over L would give 1/L rather than one. The square root is not an algebraic decoration. It is the central consequence of using amplitude rather than probability directly. A discontinuity at a hard boundary also needs care if one later asks whether this function is a solution for a particular potential.

1.A.2.05.18 Normalization of a sine-shaped packet on a finite interval

Build the concept

A frequently supplied teaching function is ψ(x)=A sin(nπx/L) for 0

Work through the mathematics

Requiring ∫|ψ|²dx=1 therefore gives |A|²L/2=1 and A=√(2/L) if we choose a positive real representative. Any constant overall phase factor of magnitude one would leave the density unchanged. The density becomes (2/L)sin²(nπx/L), and the integer n changes its internal nodal structure without changing the overall normalization formula.

Interpretation and limitations

For n=1, the density is symmetric and has no interior node. For n=2, the wavefunction changes sign and vanishes at x=L/2, so the density has two lobes separated by an internal node. These are one-dimensional illustrations, not a shortcut for assigning an atomic orbital's quantum numbers or its three-dimensional density.

Normalization also fixes the units of the coefficient: if x and L are measured in metres, √(2/L) has units m^(−1/2). Multiplying its squared magnitude by dx then produces a dimensionless probability. A common mistake is to treat the normalization factor as a bare number independent of the chosen length unit; changing from metres to nanometres changes its numerical representation while leaving physical predictions unchanged.

1.A.2.05.19 Integrating density to find an interval probability

Build the concept

A normalized wavefunction allows quantitative questions beyond 'where is the density largest?'. For the ideal n=1 sine state on 0

Work through the mathematics

Compute P(0,L/4)=(2/L)∫₀^(L/4)sin²(πx/L)dx. Use sin²u=(1−cos2u)/2 and integrate. The result is 1/4−1/(2π), approximately 0.0908, much smaller than 0.25. This is a useful calculation because it forces students to use the area under density rather than infer probability from the fraction of distance.

Interpretation and limitations

A probability calculation must report a dimensionless number between zero and one, and intervals that partition the whole domain must add to one. A correct result can be checked by symmetry: the rightmost quarter has the same probability as the leftmost quarter in this n=1 state. If a calculation gives a negative result or a probability larger than one, inspect normalization and integration limits first.

1.A.2.05.20 Expectation values describe averages, not deterministic coordinates

Build the concept

For a normalized one-dimensional position state, the expectation value of x is ⟨x⟩=∫x|ψ(x)|²dx over the allowed domain. It is the mean position outcome across an appropriate ensemble of identically prepared particles. It does not say that every individual electron is located exactly at the mean or that the mean is always the point of greatest probability density.

Work through the mathematics

For a state with density symmetric about L/2, such as an ideal sine-shaped box state, the average is ⟨x⟩=L/2. But the state also has a nonzero spread. The variance is (Δx)²=⟨x²⟩−⟨x⟩². These definitions connect naturally to the Heisenberg relation from Node 04: the Δ there is the quantum-state standard deviation, not the visible width of a drawn electron cloud.

Interpretation and limitations

Distinguishing mean from probability maximum will matter for three-dimensional atomic states. A radial probability curve may have its maximum at a certain radius even when the mean radial distance is different. Neither number produces a literal electron orbit of one permanent radius. An orbital is a distribution with several useful descriptors, not a unique classical track.

1.A.2.05.21 Three-dimensional normalization and volume elements

Build the concept

An electron bound to an atom can be detected anywhere in three-dimensional space according to its state distribution. The full normalization condition becomes ∫|ψ(x,y,z)|²dx dy dz=1, integrated over the allowed volume. A three-dimensional position density has dimensions m^(−3), while ψ has dimensions m^(−3/2) for a one-particle normalized state.

Work through the mathematics

The coordinate system is a choice for doing calculations. In spherical polar coordinates, the volume element is r²sinθ dr dθ dφ. That geometric factor cannot simply be dropped because it accounts for how much volume is present at different radii and angles. A density that is high near the nucleus and a radial probability that peaks farther out are therefore not contradictory claims.

Interpretation and limitations

Many popular orbital pictures use coloured surfaces enclosing a chosen fraction of integrated density. That means a contour for probability content, not a physical solid wall. If the plotted surface encloses ninety percent of probability, ten percent of position outcomes remain outside it in the ideal mathematical state. One must state which quantity, coordinate convention and density threshold a visualization represents.

1.A.2.05.22 Radial distribution and why r squared appears

Build the concept

For a spherically symmetric density ρ(r), the probability of finding a particle between r and r+dr is the density multiplied by the thin spherical-shell volume, approximately 4πr²dr. Thus the radial distribution per unit radius is 4πr²ρ(r). A density measured per unit volume and a probability density measured per unit radial distance are different functions with different units.

Work through the mathematics

At r=0, the shell-volume factor r² vanishes. Even if the volume density is finite and maximal at the centre, the probability in an infinitesimal spherical shell at exactly r=0 tends to zero. The radial distribution can peak at a positive distance. This is one of the clearest examples of why a wavefunction does not turn into a classical orbit when a probability plot has a maximum.

Interpretation and limitations

Applied later to the hydrogen 1s state, the volume density decreases outward from the nucleus, whereas the radial distribution has a maximum at a nonzero radius due to the r² shell factor. We will use that as a carefully labelled illustration, not claim that the electron moves on the spherical shell where the radial distribution is largest.

1.A.2.05.23 Hydrogen 1s as a grounded illustration, not a full derivation

Build the concept

A familiar normalized nonrelativistic hydrogen 1s spatial wavefunction is ψ₁s(r)=e^(−r/a₀)/√(πa₀³), where a₀ is the Bohr radius, approximately 0.0529 nm. This formula results from solving the Coulomb Schrödinger problem, but we present it as a supplied known example rather than deriving the differential equation here. It provides a precise contrast between amplitude, volume density and radial probability.

Work through the mathematics

The volume density is |ψ₁s|²=e^(−2r/a₀)/(πa₀³), highest at r=0 and decreasing with r. The radial probability density is 4r²e^(−2r/a₀)/a₀³. Its maximum occurs at r=a₀ because the expanding spherical-shell volume initially outweighs the falling density. These two different maxima do not contradict one another.

Interpretation and limitations

Do not conclude that a 1s electron 'orbits at radius a₀'. The radial maximum is a statistical feature of an allowed state. The electron can be detected at many radii, including distances larger than a₀. Electron spin, finite nuclear mass and more advanced effects can refine the complete physical description, and a multielectron atom does not generally inherit this exact hydrogen wavefunction.

1.A.2.05.24 Radial nodes, angular nodes and future orbital labels

Build the concept

A nodal structure may involve surfaces on which a wavefunction vanishes because of radial or angular dependence. A radial node occurs at a particular radius for the relevant state and is often represented by a spherical nodal surface. An angular node occurs at a specified angle and can appear as a plane or cone in an idealized orbital description.

Work through the mathematics

These words are introduced here to explain the images that students encounter, not to rush into formulas for all atomic quantum numbers. Counting the angular and radial nodes of hydrogenic states requires the permitted n and l values and the corresponding wavefunctions. Later nodes are specifically reserved for the principal, azimuthal and magnetic quantum numbers and detailed orbital shapes.

Interpretation and limitations

A diagram with two lobes and opposite signs should be read as a phase-labelled representation of a quantum amplitude. The electron does not carry positive charge in one lobe and negative charge in the other. Squaring the wavefunction gives nonnegative density on both sides, while the node between them has exactly zero amplitude in that idealized state.

1.A.2.05.25 Constructive combinations can redistribute molecular electron density

Physical connection

Consider two atomic wavefunctions centred on neighbouring atoms. When their amplitudes combine coherently, the resulting molecular-state pattern can show greater amplitude between the nuclei or a node in that region, depending on their relative phase and spatial symmetry. This is a conceptual introduction to why bonding and antibonding orbital combinations have different density distributions.

Follow the evidence and model

The simplified expressions ψplus∝φA+φB and ψminus∝φA−φB are mathematical combinations, not instructions to add already-squared probability densities. Because |φA±φB|² contains overlap cross terms, the relative signs affect the electron density. In a suitable simple two-centre model, enhanced density between nuclei can contribute to bonding, but actual energies also depend on kinetic energy, nuclei and electron interactions.

Scientific boundary

We should avoid saying that every positive sum is always bonding and every negative sum always antibonding without considering orbital symmetry and the Hamiltonian. The correct physical claim is narrower: coherent combinations of amplitudes can reinforce or cancel in space, producing different nodal and density patterns. Later molecular-orbital lessons will add proper energy ordering and electron occupancy.

1.A.2.05.26 Antibonding combinations and nodal regions

Physical connection

An antibonding molecular orbital in a common two-centre model can have a node between nuclei because contributions from the two atomic amplitudes cancel there. With |ψ|² close to zero in the internuclear region, the electron density differs substantially from a constructive combination. This shows why the phase of a wavefunction cannot be thrown away before combining amplitudes.

Follow the evidence and model

A nodal region does not mean the electron is physically cut into two half-particles. A detector records complete localized interactions, while the spatial state describes outcome probabilities. Opposite signs used to colour an orbital diagram represent amplitude phase, not alternating positive and negative electron charge.

Scientific boundary

Antibonding occupancy can reduce bond order in familiar qualitative orbital models, but predicting an actual bond's stability still requires comparing total electronic energies, nuclear repulsion and the full many-electron state. This node uses the nodal concept to connect quantum amplitude and chemical meaning without prematurely replacing molecular orbital theory with a cartoon.

1.A.2.05.27 Why pigments absorb some colours and not others

Physical connection

A chlorophyll or other biological chromophore absorbs radiation only when an appropriate electronic transition is allowed and the incident photon energy fits the transition and its molecular environment. The wavefunction description gives a language for distinct electronic states; spectroscopy probes energy gaps and transition properties between such states.

Follow the evidence and model

The amount of absorption cannot be determined merely from two drawn electron clouds. Transition strengths depend on a transition matrix element, symmetry and coupling to vibrations and surroundings. The photon energy hν sets the possible energy scale, but not every photon with that energy is automatically absorbed. This is why chlorophyll has a structured absorption spectrum rather than equally efficient absorption at every visible wavelength.

Scientific boundary

Photosynthetic light capture then involves further energy transfer and biochemical pathways. The initial electronic excitation is only the first stage. It would be inaccurate to conclude from a wavefunction figure alone how much oxygen, ATP or reduced cofactor a leaf must produce. Quantum states and biological action spectra are connected through additional physical and biochemical processes.

Original conceptual energy-level scheme. Actual molecular bands, transition strengths and fluorescence yields depend on wavefunctions, vibrational levels and environment.

1.A.2.05.28 Protein ultraviolet absorption needs chemical specificity

Physical connection

Proteins often show ultraviolet absorption near 280 nm because aromatic amino-acid side chains, especially tryptophan and tyrosine, have suitable electronic transitions. Their wavefunctions and molecular environments help explain why photons can be absorbed in that spectral range. A photon around 280 nm carries roughly 4.43 eV, but this value alone does not specify which residue absorbed it.

Follow the evidence and model

Two proteins at the same mass concentration can have different 280-nm absorbance because the number and environment of absorbing chromophores differ. Molar extinction coefficients, path length and sample contaminants matter. The Born interpretation tells us how electronic states relate to probabilities, but it is not a direct formula converting any arbitrary UV peak into protein concentration.

Scientific boundary

Protein fluorescence can also depend on the local environment of tryptophan, but an observed fluorescence shift cannot uniquely identify a complete three-dimensional protein structure. A scientifically valid conclusion requires controls and a separate spectroscopic model, not only a claim that an electron has moved to another 'orbital shell'.

1.A.2.05.29 Fluorescence: electronic states, radiation and relaxation

Physical connection

A fluorescent chromophore can absorb light into an excited electronic state and later emit light when a suitable transition occurs. The associated electronic wavefunctions help define the states and possible transitions. Ordinary longer-wavelength fluorescence often produces photons of lower energy than the absorbed excitation photons because some excitation energy leaves through nonradiative pathways.

Follow the evidence and model

A two-state sketch may show a ground state and an excited state connected by arrows, but it is not a complete kinetic model. Vibrational relaxation, quenching, energy transfer and the chemical environment determine whether and when a photon is emitted. The wavefunction's phase and density information do not by themselves reveal a fluorescence quantum yield or lifetime.

Scientific boundary

Microscopy uses many such emission events to form molecular images. The observed spot and its estimated location depend on optical optics and detected photon statistics; they do not map the internal wavefunction of each electron in the chromophore. Distinguishing electronic state transitions from fluorescent molecule localization is essential for interpreting experiments.

1.A.2.05.30 Why an electron-density map is not a probability cloud for one electron

Physical connection

X-ray crystallography, electron microscopy and quantum-chemical calculations all display quantities that may be called 'density', but they are not identical. An X-ray-derived electron-density map reflects scattering and a structural model for a whole molecular system. Electron microscopy records transmitted or scattered beam information and may reconstruct potential or structural features. A single-particle |ψ|² is a position probability density of a specified normalized quantum state.

Follow the evidence and model

A protein model may show atom centres and density regions without revealing an exact internal electron path. A cryo-EM reconstruction does not measure every protein electron's x and px simultaneously. Similarly, a computed orbital is a model-dependent quantum object, not a direct photograph of an electron sitting inside a hard-edged coloured sphere.

Scientific boundary

Good interpretation starts by asking what the instrument measured and what processing or theoretical assumptions converted those measurements into a map. The shared word 'density' does not authorize substituting a scattering-potential map for an orbital probability density. These distinctions prevent both exaggerated microscopy claims and misleading AI-style scientific illustrations.

1.A.2.05.31 Probability flow and why density can move

Physical connection

The Born density |Ψ|² can change in time when a quantum state is not stationary. For a simple nonrelativistic particle with a real scalar potential, the Schrödinger equation also implies a probability continuity relation. It connects the local change of density with the flow of probability through space, conserving total probability under appropriate boundary conditions.

Follow the evidence and model

In one dimension, a standard probability-current density is j=(ℏ/m)Im(Ψ*∂Ψ/∂x). The continuity equation is ∂ρ/∂t+∂j/∂x=0. These equations are not new claims that charge is physically smeared along a tube. They are tools for describing how the distribution of possible position outcomes changes and how probability is conserved as a quantum state evolves.

Scientific boundary

A bound stationary state can have time-independent density; general coherent superpositions and propagating packets can have evolving densities and currents. The current is especially useful for interpreting scattering and transport problems later. Here we introduce it as a conceptual extension, not ask students to derive it from the full differential equation.

1.A.2.05.32 Orthogonality and why different allowed states can be distinct

Physical connection

Two normalized wavefunctions may represent different possible states of a system. Their inner product is ⟨φ|ψ⟩=∫φ*ψ over the appropriate domain. If it equals zero, the states are orthogonal. Orthogonality is a mathematical distinction between state amplitudes; it is not the assertion that their position probability densities never overlap in space.

Follow the evidence and model

For a Hermitian Hamiltonian under appropriate conditions, energy eigenstates with different energy eigenvalues are orthogonal. Degenerate energy eigenspaces may require a suitable orthogonal choice of basis. One-dimensional sine standing waves on a common interval provide a familiar demonstration: different positive integer indices give zero cross integral even though their probability densities occupy the same overall interval.

Scientific boundary

Orbital orthogonality becomes important when constructing electron configurations and multi-electron wavefunctions, but occupancy and spin introduce further rules. It would be premature to derive the Pauli principle only from a picture of nonoverlapping electron clouds. We need the proper quantum-state framework and later quantum-number lessons to make those connections precise.

1.A.2.05.33 A wavefunction is a representation of a state, not the whole universe in one plot

Follow the state description

Quantum states may be described in different mathematical representations. A position-space wavefunction ψ(x) expresses amplitudes for position, while its Fourier transform describes momentum-space amplitudes. Both refer to the same prepared quantum state in different bases. It would be a mistake to assume that because a position plot is broad, the electron has no momentum information.

Test the implications

The relative phase of position amplitudes helps determine the momentum distribution. Two wavefunctions can have identical |ψ(x)|² at one time yet differ in their spatially varying phases, giving different currents, momentum distributions or later evolution. A position probability cloud is therefore an incomplete picture of the state, even when it is a very helpful one for introductory atomic chemistry.

Scientific interpretation

Later orbital labels and quantum numbers will identify particular structures of permitted state functions. They do not change the central distinction between a state and one graphical representation. If an experiment measures a different observable, probabilities are calculated in the corresponding representation or with the correct observable operator, not by reading every answer directly from a static density plot.

1.A.2.05.34 Symmetry can simplify probability without solving a differential equation

Follow the state description

A normalized probability density symmetric about the midpoint x=L/2 assigns equal probabilities to the two halves of a domain. Therefore P(0≤x≤L/2)=1/2, provided the boundaries and state genuinely possess the symmetry. One can reach that result from integration and symmetry without knowing every detail of the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem.

Test the implications

Symmetry is not a licence to claim every quarter or every equal-length interval has equal probability. A sine-squared density places more weight near its peaks. In the n=1 standing-wave illustration the left half has probability 1/2, while the leftmost quarter has 1/4−1/(2π), around 0.0908. Same length does not mean same probability unless the density is uniform.

Scientific interpretation

Atomic orbitals also have symmetry properties, but in three dimensions symmetry must be interpreted with the volume element. An orbital's position density may be symmetric about the nucleus and still have a nontrivial radial distribution because shells have different volumes. This is why meaningful diagrams distinguish coordinate, volume density and integrated probability.

1.A.2.05.35 A coherent superposition is not an incoherent mixture

Follow the state description

Consider two allowed amplitudes ψ1 and ψ2. A coherent superposition has a wavefunction ψ=c1ψ1+c2ψ2, and the density includes interference terms depending on relative phase. An incoherent statistical mixture instead assigns classical preparation probabilities to alternatives and combines the individual densities with weights. These two situations can produce different predictions even if the state labels appear identical.

Test the implications

Suppose two alternatives have amplitudes +a and −a at a certain position. In a coherent equal-weight combination those contributions may cancel, forming a node. In an incoherent mixture one does not add amplitudes before squaring, so those probability contributions do not cancel in the same way. This demonstrates why summing two electron-cloud pictures is not a general recipe for an orbital or wavefunction.

Scientific interpretation

In molecules, coherent electronic-state combinations underlie interference and molecular orbital concepts; in spectroscopy, coherence can affect time-dependent signals. The level of coherence must be specified. Simply seeing two bright lobes does not reveal whether a preparation is a pure state, a superposition or a mixed state; experimental context and a suitable theory are required.

Original schematic distinguishing amplitude addition from probabilistic mixing. Shapes are illustrative, not exact normalized distributions.

1.A.2.05.36 What measurement changes—and what repeated preparation reveals

Follow the state description

When a position-sensitive detector registers an electron, it produces a localized event. The collection of many comparable preparations tests the probabilities predicted by the state. The Born rule relates these outcomes to the squared amplitude, but it does not say that the experimental image directly displays the complex wavefunction's phase at every coordinate.

Test the implications

Measurement can affect subsequent state preparation and outcomes, and the detailed interpretation of collapse or state update depends on the chosen quantum framework. For this introductory chapter, it is enough to distinguish the probability distribution for a prepared state from an individual detector result. The phrase 'electron cloud' belongs to the first; a dot on the screen belongs to the second.

Scientific interpretation

The measurement problem is not solved by saying 'the electron was everywhere' in a literal classical sense, nor by assuming it followed an unseen precise circular orbit. Standard quantum mechanics makes operational predictions about possible outcomes. More interpretational debate exists, but it does not change how students correctly normalize a state and calculate probabilities for textbook problems.

1.A.2.05.37 Counting an orbital's nodes is a later consequence of allowed labels

Follow the state description

Allowed orbital states can possess radial and angular nodes. Their detailed number and geometry are linked to quantum numbers that arise from solving the appropriate wave equation for a hydrogen-like potential. Since the next curriculum nodes introduce principal and angular quantum numbers, this chapter confines itself to understanding what a node means and why phase can change across it.

Test the implications

A student may have seen the familiar rule about the number of total nodes in hydrogen-like states. It can be useful once n and l have been defined and the hydrogenic assumptions are stated. Using it before introducing those values would produce memorization without explanation, and applying hydrogenic formulas indiscriminately to multi-electron orbitals would overstate their exactness.

Scientific interpretation

The essential concept is simpler and more durable: an allowed wavefunction can vanish at certain coordinates. There is zero density on the ideal node; neighboring regions can have nonzero amplitude, possibly with opposite phase. These features are properties of a quantum state, not walls separating fragments of an electron.

1.A.2.05.38 A graph must label amplitude, density, units and domain

Follow the state description

An unlabelled curve can be dangerous in quantum mechanics. A wavefunction ψ(x) can be real, signed or complex; a density |ψ|² is real and nonnegative. A graph of radial probability 4πr²ρ(r) has a different vertical-axis quantity from a graph of volume density ρ(r). The position coordinate, domain and normalization should be supplied before drawing physical conclusions.

Test the implications

Suppose an orbital visualization uses one colour for positive amplitude and another for negative amplitude. Those colours are not positive and negative electric charges. If a picture instead shades |ψ|², the amplitude's sign is not visible. Contour surfaces often enclose a chosen probability fraction such as ninety percent, not all of the electron's possible positions.

Scientific interpretation

For quantitative practice, a density integrates to one over its correct measure and domain. If the vertical axis is measured in nm⁻¹ and x in nm, the area is dimensionless. In three dimensions a volume density may be measured in nm⁻³, and the volume element restores a dimensionless probability. Ignoring units can create a graph that looks plausible while reporting impossible probabilities.

1.A.2.05.39 The limits of one-electron orbital language in real biology

Follow the state description

Hydrogen's one-electron orbital problem provides a clean starting point. In a protein or DNA molecule, many electrons interact with many nuclei. The exact many-electron wavefunction depends on more coordinates and spins than a single ψ(r), and practical methods often use approximations such as molecular orbitals, electronic structure calculations and effective Hamiltonians.

Test the implications

An enzyme's reaction rate or a pigment's absorption spectrum cannot be read directly from the height of one pictured probability cloud. Transition probabilities, energy differences, nuclear motion, solvent effects and molecular geometry all matter. Quantum electronic states establish the physical framework, while detailed biological predictions require the relevant chemical model and experimental validation.

Scientific interpretation

The value of the orbital concept is therefore substantial but not unlimited. It replaces the fixed classical trajectory with a state description that can support calculations and chemical reasoning. It does not make every complex biological phenomenon solvable by one line of wavefunction notation. That boundary is particularly important in high-stakes scientific education.

1.A.2.05.40 The next node can teach quantum numbers without arbitrary boxes

Follow the state description

We began this chapter with the electron's apparent conflict between localized detection and wave-like diffraction. A wavefunction supplies a coherent language for those phenomena. Born's interpretation gives probabilities, normalization makes those probabilities add to one, and the Schrödinger framework identifies allowed spatial state patterns under specified potentials and boundary conditions.

Test the implications

Quantum orbital shapes, nodes, density and phase are now meaningful rather than mysterious. The next chapter can introduce the principal quantum number n as a label associated with allowed shells and hydrogen-like energy structure. Later azimuthal, magnetic and spin quantum numbers will refine the picture, and only then should students consolidate electronic configuration and occupancy rules.

Scientific interpretation

Carry three questions into every new orbital lesson: Which physical system and state are being modeled? Which observable or probability density is plotted? What assumptions make the result valid? These questions preserve the study decorum of a rigorous textbook and keep the learning sequence free from unsupported claims about microscopic electron paths.

1.A.2.05.41 Twelve evidence-led quantum-state investigations

Each case starts with a real scientific interpretation problem and ends by stating exactly what the evidence does—and does not—support. The emphasis is on probability, state preparation and appropriate measurement models.

Case 01: A hydrogen electron density picture seems highest at the nucleus

Observation: A student sees the ideal 1s volume density e^(−2r/a₀)/(πa₀³) and then a radial graph peaking at r=a₀. The student assumes the two graphs cannot represent the same wavefunction.

Analysis: Both graphs use the same 1s state but describe different densities. The first is probability per unit volume at a spatial point. The second includes the 4πr² volume of a thin spherical shell, giving radial probability per unit radius. The increasing shell area shifts the radial maximum away from the origin even while volume density decreases outward.

Scientific limit: The radial maximum is not a circular orbit or a radius at which the electron must always be detected.

Case 02: A wavefunction plot crosses below zero

Observation: A normalized one-dimensional real wavefunction becomes negative in part of its domain, and an examiner argues that negative amplitude means negative position probability.

Analysis: Probability density is |ψ|², which is nonnegative. A negative real amplitude is compatible with a physical state and can be important for relative phase and interference. For a real ψ, both +0.2 and −0.2 yield local squared magnitude 0.04 in the corresponding units.

Scientific limit: The amplitude itself is not probability; the integrated squared magnitude across a region supplies the dimensionless probability.

Case 03: Two coherent routes interfere although both are individually probable

Observation: At one detector coordinate, two coherent alternatives contribute amplitudes a and −a. The observer expects a positive detection rate because each path alone has squared amplitude |a|².

Analysis: For a coherent superposition, amplitudes add first. Their sum is zero, so the probability density at that coordinate vanishes in the ideal model. If the alternatives become distinguishable and incoherent, their probabilities may instead add. Relative phase and the experimental coherence condition determine which reasoning applies.

Scientific limit: The cancellation is local to an event channel; it is not the destruction of electric charge or a claim that the full state has zero probability everywhere.

Case 04: A laboratory report prints a probability density above one

Observation: A student reports ρ(x)=2 nm⁻¹ for a part of a normalized position state and concludes that the wavefunction is impossible because probability exceeds one.

Analysis: Density has inverse-length units. The probability over a short interval Δx=0.1 nm with roughly constant density is about 2 nm⁻¹×0.1 nm=0.2, which is permitted. The relevant requirement is that the nonnegative density integrate to one over the whole allowed domain.

Scientific limit: One must check the total normalization and the length unit; density height is not a standalone dimensionless probability.

Case 05: A simple sine wave has a dark central point

Observation: A one-dimensional ψ∝sin(2πx/L) has an internal zero at x=L/2. A student describes a physical wall dividing a particle into two halves.

Analysis: The central position is a node where the wavefunction vanishes and local position density is zero. Its neighbouring amplitudes can have opposite sign, while a position measurement still yields a complete particle outcome. The node follows the chosen standing-wave state, not an inserted solid barrier.

Scientific limit: The illustration is not a literal 2p orbital; atomic nodal geometry depends on three-dimensional solutions and quantum numbers.

Case 06: A stationary state is mistaken for an electron that has stopped

Observation: For Ψ(x,t)=ψ(x)e^(−iEt/ℏ), a detector ensemble gives the same position distribution at different times. The student concludes the electron has zero kinetic energy and known fixed coordinates.

Analysis: The time phase has unit magnitude, so |Ψ|²=|ψ|² and density is stationary. This does not imply vanishing quantum momentum or kinetic-energy distributions. Stationary means the probability density does not evolve under that energy eigenstate and time-independent Hamiltonian.

Scientific limit: An eigenstate can possess nonzero momentum uncertainty; static density is not a classical orbit or a stopped particle.

Case 07: Two energy states are coherently combined

Observation: An atom is prepared in a coherent superposition c₁ψ₁e^(−iE₁t/ℏ)+c₂ψ₂e^(−iE₂t/ℏ), with E₁ different from E₂. Someone assumes a time-independent Hamiltonian requires a time-independent position density.

Analysis: Different energy factors accumulate a changing relative phase. Interference cross terms can therefore make local |Ψ|² evolve even if the Hamiltonian is constant. A single energy eigenstate is stationary; a general coherent superposition need not be.

Scientific limit: A detector observing time evolution depends on state preparation and measurement, and not every superposition yields an easily resolvable time signal.

Case 08: An orbital contour encloses ninety percent of density

Observation: A molecular-orbital software image uses a surface enclosing 90% of position probability. The student announces the electron can never be found outside the surface.

Analysis: By definition a 90% probability region leaves about 10% of position outcomes outside that chosen region in the ideal normalized state. It is a plotting convention, not a hard atomic boundary. Changing contour threshold changes the pictured volume without changing the underlying normalized wavefunction.

Scientific limit: A contour can also depict equal amplitude rather than enclosed probability; the legend and software convention must be read.

Case 09: An X-ray protein map is misidentified as one electron's ψ

Observation: A student points to a crystallographic electron-density map and says it is the Born probability density of a specific bound electron in the protein.

Analysis: A crystallographic map is reconstructed from diffraction data under structural and scattering models and reflects a many-electron molecular system. It is not generally the normalized position probability density of one chosen electron state. The quantities share a word but arise from different measurements and interpretations.

Scientific limit: Scattering maps, molecular orbital densities and single-particle probabilities must be distinguished before performing calculations or claims.

Case 10: A pigment absorbs blue light but not all nearby wavelengths equally

Observation: A chlorophyll-like chromophore has structured absorption bands. A learner assumes that because a photon has adequate energy, it must be absorbed and initiate photosynthesis.

Analysis: Permitted electronic transitions depend on wavefunction structure, symmetry and coupling to molecular vibrations and environments. Photon energy sets a candidate gap but not the transition strength or subsequent biological yield. Absorption can be wavelength-dependent and downstream photochemistry has separate efficiencies.

Scientific limit: A complete photosynthetic action spectrum requires experimental and biochemical information beyond a single ground-state orbital picture.

Case 11: One-electron orbital theory is applied directly to an enzyme

Observation: An enzyme with many electrons is described as having one exact ψ(r), and its reaction rate is claimed to follow from the spatial maximum of that function.

Analysis: A multi-electron electronic state generally depends on all relevant electronic coordinates and spin, and may require many-body approximations. A single orbital is a useful component in some models but not the exact complete state of an enzyme. Reaction rates involve energy barriers, nuclear motion, solvent and other effects.

Scientific limit: Quantum probability provides the foundation for bonding but does not independently yield a quantitative enzyme mechanism or rate constant.

Case 12: A student tries to normalize a function with infinite total probability

Observation: A candidate bound-state function is a nonzero constant over the entire real x-axis. Its density is constant and its integral from negative to positive infinity diverges.

Analysis: No finite nonzero constant can make this particular function square-integrable on the whole real line. It is therefore not an ordinary normalizable bound-state position wavefunction. Ideal plane-wave momentum eigenstates are handled as generalized scattering states with different normalization conventions.

Scientific limit: Reject the unnormalizable function as an ordinary bound state without concluding that all useful ideal scattering plane waves are mathematically forbidden.

1.A.2.05.42 Forty worked probability, normalization and orbital-state exercises

The forty exercises are original and deliberately ordered: normalization and density first, then wavefunction phase, three-dimensional radial probability and scientific interpretations. SI units and model assumptions accompany the arithmetic.

Worked example 01: Normalize a uniform 1D amplitude on a 2-nm interval

Given: A constant ψ=A for 0≤x≤L, L=2.00 nm, is given as a probability-state illustration.

Method and substitution: Set ∫₀ᴸ|A|²dx=|A|²L=1; choose the real positive phase A=1/√L.

Answer: A=22360.68 m^(−1/2), or 0.707107 nm^(−1/2).

Physical interpretation: The normalization amplitude carries inverse-square-root length units. This is not a claim the discontinuous profile solves a specified Hamiltonian.

Worked example 02: Find interval probability for uniform density

Given: A normalized uniform state spans 0–5.00 nm; find the probability from x=1.00 to 3.00 nm.

Method and substitution: Density ρ=1/L=1/5 nm⁻¹. Integrate over interval width 2 nm.

Answer: P=(3−1)/5=0.4 (40%).

Physical interpretation: The fraction of length equals the probability only because the density is uniform in this example.

Worked example 03: Compute position standard deviation of a uniform state

Given: Position is uniformly distributed between 0 and L=2 nm.

Method and substitution: For uniform interval, ⟨x⟩=L/2 and Var(x)=L²/12, so σx=L/√12.

Answer: σx=0.57735 nm.

Physical interpretation: A distribution may have a sharply defined interval but not a single exact position.

Worked example 04: Normalize a n=1 sine amplitude on 0 to 1 nm

Given: ψ=A sin(πx/L), L=1 nm, for 0

Method and substitution: The identity ∫₀ᴸsin²(πx/L)dx=L/2 gives A²L/2=1.

Answer: A=√(2/L)=44721.36 m^(−1/2).

Physical interpretation: The sine's shape does not by itself establish that it is an atomic orbital.

Worked example 05: Locate interior nodes of an n=3 standing-wave illustration

Given: ψ∝sin(3πx/L) on 0

Method and substitution: Set sin(3πx/L)=0; interior integers give 3πx/L=π,2π.

Answer: Interior nodes x=1 nm and 2 nm.

Physical interpretation: A node is a zero of amplitude and squared density, not a physical wall.

Worked example 06: Probability in left half of n=1 sine density

Given: The normalized state is ψ=√(2/L)sin(πx/L).

Method and substitution: By symmetry P(0,L/2)=(2/L)∫₀^(L/2)sin²(πx/L)dx.

Answer: P=0.500 (50%).

Physical interpretation: This happens by symmetry, not because the density is uniform.

Worked example 07: Leftmost quarter for n=1 sine density

Given: Calculate P(0,L/4) for ψ=√(2/L)sin(πx/L).

Method and substitution: Use integral (2/L)∫sin²(πx/L)dx and sin²u=(1−cos2u)/2.

Answer: P=1/4−1/(2π)≈0.09084506.

Physical interpretation: Equal-length quarters do not have equal probabilities for a peaked density.

Worked example 08: Middle-half probability for the n=1 sine density

Given: Find P(L/4,3L/4) for the same normalized n=1 state.

Method and substitution: By mirror symmetry the outer quarters each have probability 1/4−1/(2π).

Answer: P=1−2[1/4−1/(2π)]=1/2+1/π≈0.8183099.

Physical interpretation: Most position outcomes lie in the middle half for this state.

Worked example 09: First quarter for n=2 sine density

Given: ψ=√(2/L)sin(2πx/L) on 0

Method and substitution: Integrate density (2/L)sin²(2πx/L) from 0 to L/4; the sine term at the boundary vanishes.

Answer: P=0.250 (25%).

Physical interpretation: Unlike n=1, this selected quarter contains exactly a quarter of total probability in the n=2 illustration.

Worked example 10: Left half for n=2 standing wave

Given: Use the normalized ψ=√(2/L)sin(2πx/L).

Method and substitution: Two identical density lobes occupy the left and right halves; integrate one lobe or use symmetry.

Answer: P(0,L/2)=0.500.

Physical interpretation: The central point is a node but the probability of an entire half-interval remains nonzero.

Worked example 11: Normalize a decaying exponential amplitude on x≥0

Given: ψ=A exp(−x/a), x≥0, with a=2.00 nm.

Method and substitution: Integrate |A|²∫₀∞exp(−2x/a)dx=|A|²(a/2)=1.

Answer: A=√(2/a)≈31622.78 m^(−1/2).

Physical interpretation: The profile is a supplied normalizable example, not asserted to solve any arbitrary potential.

Worked example 12: Probability within one exponential length a

Given: For the normalized exponential density ρ=(2/a)exp(−2x/a), compute P(0,a).

Method and substitution: Integrate ρ from 0 to a: 1−exp(−2).

Answer: P≈0.8646647.

Physical interpretation: The characteristic amplitude length a is not the interval enclosing exactly half of probability.

Worked example 13: Probability beyond x=a in exponential state

Given: Same normalized exponential on x≥0.

Method and substitution: P(x>a)=∫ₐ∞(2/a)exp(−2x/a)dx=exp(−2).

Answer: P≈0.1353353.

Physical interpretation: The tail probability remains finite even though density declines rapidly.

Worked example 14: Mean position of normalized exponential density

Given: Density is (2/a)e^(−2x/a) for x≥0; a=2.0 nm.

Method and substitution: Compute ∫₀∞x(2/a)e^(−2x/a)dx=a/2.

Answer: ⟨x⟩=1.00 nm.

Physical interpretation: The mean differs from the location of maximum density, which is x=0.

Worked example 15: Standard deviation of exponential position density

Given: A normalized exponential density has rate 2/a and a=2 nm.

Method and substitution: For exponential rate k=2/a, standard deviation equals 1/k=a/2.

Answer: σx=1.00 nm.

Physical interpretation: The spread does not equal the full spatial range, which is unbounded.

Worked example 16: Gaussian probability within one standard deviation

Given: Position density is a normal Gaussian with mean μ and standard deviation σ.

Method and substitution: Integrate Gaussian from μ−σ to μ+σ, equivalently standard normal between −1 and 1.

Answer: P≈0.682689 (68.27%).

Physical interpretation: This percentage is specific to Gaussian density; it is not a universal feature of orbitals.

Worked example 17: Gaussian FWHM from σ=0.20 nm

Given: A position probability density is Gaussian and has σx=0.20 nm.

Method and substitution: FWHM=2√(2 ln2)σ, not simply σ.

Answer: FWHM≈0.470964 nm.

Physical interpretation: Peak width definitions must be converted before using a formula that requires standard deviation.

Worked example 18: Normalize a uniform 3D state in a cube

Given: A constant spatial wavefunction fills a cubic region of side L=2.0 nm and is zero outside.

Method and substitution: Volume V=L³=8 nm³. Set |A|²V=1 and choose A=1/√V.

Answer: A=0.3535534 nm^(−3/2); ρ=1/8 nm⁻³.

Physical interpretation: A 3D wavefunction has different units from a 1D one; the probability element is volume.

Worked example 19: Probability in half-width subcube of uniform 3D state

Given: An electron has uniform density throughout a cube of side L; find the central subcube of side L/2.

Method and substitution: Because density is uniform, probability equals volume ratio (L/2)³/L³.

Answer: P=1/8=0.125 (12.5%).

Physical interpretation: Half of each coordinate length encloses only one eighth of volume, not one half.

Worked example 20: Complex amplitude and a small local interval

Given: At a location ψ=(3+4i) nm^(−1/2), assumed approximately constant within a 0.010-nm interval.

Method and substitution: Calculate |ψ|²=3²+4²=25 nm⁻¹; multiply by approximate interval length 0.010 nm.

Answer: P≈0.250 within that narrow interval.

Physical interpretation: This is a local approximation; a complete global wavefunction and normalization must still be specified.
