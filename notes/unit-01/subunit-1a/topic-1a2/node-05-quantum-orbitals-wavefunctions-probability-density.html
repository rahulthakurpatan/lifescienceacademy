Quantum Orbitals: Wavefunctions and Probability Density

From electron diffraction and uncertainty to the mathematical state that predicts where an electron may be detected.

1.A.2.05.1 An electron has a state, not a pre-drawn route

The problem the last four nodes have brought us to

We have reached a productive difficulty. Hydrogen emits light at selected frequencies, photons have quantized energies, electrons show diffraction, and attempts to picture an electron moving along a perfectly specified atomic track collide with the position–momentum uncertainty relation. A planetary sketch can still help us remember the early Bohr model, but it cannot tell us the probability of finding an electron in a particular part of an atom.

Suppose a student asks where an electron is in hydrogen before a position measurement. A classical answer gives a point on an orbit and a velocity tangent to that circle. A quantum answer instead begins with the state in which the atom has been prepared. The state determines the possible measurement outcomes and their probabilities. Its spatial representation is a wavefunction, usually written ψ.

Why this chapter belongs before orbital quantum numbers

It would be easy to introduce 1s, 2p and 3d as names of boxes and immediately memorize filling rules. That reverses the learning order. Before learning orbital labels, we should understand what an orbital represents, why probability density matters, how normalization works and why certain quantum-state patterns are physically acceptable. The later quantum numbers will then describe allowed structures rather than arbitrary labels.

Guiding question: What mathematical object describes the electronic state, and how does a measurement turn that description into a position outcome?

1.A.2.05.2 1926: Schrödinger searched for a wave equation of matter

From de Broglie waves to atomic states

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that a material particle with momentum has an associated wavelength. Erwin Schrödinger developed wave mechanics in 1926, seeking equations whose solutions would describe the possible states of a quantum system. His celebrated work on quantization as an eigenvalue problem turned the idea of a matter wave into a route to calculating stationary atomic states and their energies.

The equation was not a claim that electrons are made of a vibrating fluid. It connected quantum wave amplitudes to kinetic and potential energies. With the right boundary conditions, the solutions need not allow every conceivable energy. A system can admit discrete bound states, and changes between states can account for the line spectra that began our atomic-structure story.

What a wave equation changes

The old approach had placed restrictions on an electron's path by hand. Wave mechanics instead asks which wavefunctions are compatible with the system's Hamiltonian and physical boundary conditions. The allowed patterns emerge from the mathematical problem. Later we shall learn how quantum numbers identify particular allowed families of solutions. For now the crucial point is that the equation predicts states, not miniature tracks.

This is the link to Heisenberg uncertainty: a spatially distributed wavefunction does not generally assign simultaneously sharp position and momentum. A quantum state can be stationary in its density while still possessing nonzero spreads of both observables.

1.A.2.05.3 Born's statistical interpretation gave the wavefunction physical meaning

A challenge Schrödinger's mathematics did not settle by itself

If the theory assigns a wavefunction ψ(x,t), what should a detector observe? A wavefunction can be negative or complex, so it cannot simply be interpreted as an ordinary probability. Max Born's 1926 statistical interpretation showed how amplitudes relate to the probabilities of quantum measurement outcomes, originally in the context of collision and scattering problems.

For a one-dimensional position description, |ψ(x,t)|² is the probability density. The chance of observing the particle in a sufficiently small interval from x to x+dx is |ψ(x,t)|² dx. Over a finite interval, the probability is the area under the density curve. In three dimensions we use |ψ(r,t)|² times a small volume element, integrated over the specified region.

ρ(x,t) = ψ*(x,t) ψ(x,t) = |ψ(x,t)|²   ;   P(a≤x≤b)=∫ab|ψ|² dxBorn rule for the position representation of a normalized single-particle state.

A local probability, not a material cloud

Calling |ψ|² an 'electron cloud' can be a helpful first visualization if we remember what the cloud represents. It is not an electron physically shredded into mist. Repeating an appropriate position measurement on identically prepared atoms builds a distribution; one idealized position detection remains a localized event. The cloud represents the pattern of possible outcomes.

1.A.2.05.4 A wavefunction is an amplitude, not an ordinary probability

Why squaring matters

A probability must be real and nonnegative. A quantum amplitude ψ can be a complex number. Its squared magnitude ψ*ψ is nonnegative, which makes it suitable for a probability density after normalization. A position amplitude of −0.2 and one of +0.2 have the same squared magnitude, but their signs can matter when they interfere with other amplitudes.

For a general complex amplitude a+ib, its complex conjugate is a−ib and the product is a²+b². This explains why |ψ|² is real and nonnegative. It does not imply that ψ is simply the square root of a probability density with an arbitrary sign that can always be ignored.

Distinguish two meanings of 'wave'

In a water wave, height may be measured directly. In quantum mechanics, the complex phase of an amplitude is generally not a direct meter reading. Its consequences can nevertheless be observed through interference. A spatial probability density alone does not reveal every phase relationship that the complete wavefunction carries, so two states with the same immediate |ψ|² profile may differ in momentum or future evolution.

ψ=a+ib   ;   ψ*=a−ib   ;   |ψ|²=a²+b² ≥ 0a and b are real-valued amplitude components. In one dimension, |ψ|² has units of inverse length for a normalized state.

1.A.2.05.5 Probability density is not the probability of a single exact point

A continuous variable needs an interval

Imagine a normalized electron wavefunction along the x axis. The quantity |ψ(x)|² is a density, typically measured in m⁻¹, so it is not itself a dimensionless probability. Integrating over a finite interval gives a number between zero and one. A density can have a numerical value larger than one in chosen units without violating probability rules, because the integrated area—not its peak height—is the probability.

For a continuous position distribution, the probability of measuring one mathematically exact coordinate is zero in the ideal probability model. This does not stop a laboratory detector from recording a result with finite spatial resolution. An observed dot represents a finite detection region and an outcome of the instrument, not a claim that the distribution has a finite probability mass at one continuum point.

The measurement question sets the region

Asking 'what is the probability the electron lies between 0 and L/2?' has a meaningful answer once ψ and the state are specified. Asking 'what is ψ at x=L/2?' is a different question: it asks for an amplitude at one coordinate. Only |ψ|² dx in an infinitesimal interval or an integral over a finite region produces a position probability.

P(x∈[a,b]) = ∫ab ρ(x) dx;   ρ=|ψ|²The integral, not the density at one point, is a dimensionless probability.

This distinction will matter in both molecular orbital maps and solved numerical exercises. A high probability density near one region does not mean all measurements occur there, and a low density does not always mean a measurement is absolutely impossible.

1.A.2.05.6 Normalization makes probabilities add up to one

A quantum state must describe some outcome somewhere

For a particle whose position could lie anywhere on a one-dimensional line, the probability of finding it somewhere is one. Therefore ∫|ψ(x,t)|²dx=1 over the full allowed domain. This is the normalization condition. In three dimensions the corresponding integral is taken over volume: ∫|ψ(r,t)|²d³r=1.

A raw mathematical solution of a wave equation may contain an arbitrary multiplicative amplitude. For example, ψ(x)=A f(x) often has a constant A that the differential equation does not determine. Normalization fixes the magnitude |A| so that the total probability is one. Multiplying by an overall constant complex phase of magnitude one does not change normalization.

A test before interpreting a graph

If ∫|ψ|²dx=4, the amplitude has not been normalized; probabilities obtained from that uncorrected function will not have the right total. Divide the amplitude by the square root of four, not by four. Probabilities depend on the square of amplitude, so normalization uses square roots.

∫all x|ψ(x,t)|² dx=1   ;   ∫all space|ψ(r,t)|² d³r=1For a normalized one-particle position wavefunction.

1.A.2.05.7 What changes when we study a three-dimensional atom?

From one coordinate to a location in space

An atomic electron is not restricted to one horizontal line. We ordinarily need coordinates (x,y,z), or alternatively spherical coordinates (r,θ,φ). Its wavefunction ψ(r,t) and probability density |ψ(r,t)|² describe a three-dimensional distribution. The probability of detection inside a chosen spatial volume is the volume integral of density.

Here a subtlety begins: probability per unit volume and probability per unit radial distance are not the same. A spherical shell at distance r has greater volume than a very thin shell nearer the centre, even if the density at each point were equal. To calculate a radial distribution in a spherically symmetric case, a factor 4πr² appears. The later atomic-orbital chapters will use this distinction to interpret hydrogen's radial shapes.

Why a density plot may appear like a cloud

An image can shade regions of high |ψ|² and leave low-density regions faint. This makes the word 'orbital cloud' intuitive. But the shading threshold is a choice, often enclosing a specified fraction of total probability. The atom does not have a hard solid outer surface merely because the picture has a drawn boundary.

P(V)=∫V|ψ(r,t)|² d³r;   P(r to r+dr)=4πr²ρ(r)drThe radial-shell expression assumes a spherically symmetric position density ρ(r).

1.A.2.05.8 An orbital is a permitted state, not a container for a ball

How chemistry vocabulary changes meaning

An atomic orbital in an appropriate one-electron description is a spatial wavefunction belonging to an allowed electronic state. It may be used as a building block in multi-electron atomic and molecular calculations. It is not a plastic shell through which an electron travels on a known course, nor is it a fixed region with a perfectly impenetrable edge.

The familiar names 1s, 2p and 3d combine quantum-number information that later nodes will explain systematically. Their shapes and nodes arise from wave equations and angular dependence. We should not memorize their allowed orientations before seeing why wavefunctions can have different spatial patterns.

What the orbital can predict

Once the wavefunction and state are defined, |ψ|² predicts position probabilities. Other observables, including energy and momentum, require their respective operators or basis representations. An orbital drawing by itself does not determine every observable; it is a visualization of part of the state information.

In a many-electron atom, a single orbital picture is also an approximation or component of a many-particle description. Electron correlation, antisymmetry and electron–electron repulsion complicate the exact state. Recognizing this boundary protects us from promising more than a single-electron wavefunction can deliver.
