Principal Quantum Number n: Shells, Scale and Energy

From the observation of discrete hydrogen lines to modern shell quantum labels, binding energies, radial scale and careful biological interpretation.

Learning outcomes: Interpret the positive integer n and K–Q shell names; distinguish a quantum shell from a classical orbit; derive and use the hydrogenic energy pattern under stated assumptions; solve emission, absorption and ionization problems; compare hydrogen-like ions and radial scales; interpret radial probability versus point density; and recognize the limits of the one-electron approximation in real biological atoms and molecules.

1.A.2.06.1 The unresolved question after wavefunctions

We ended the previous lesson with an electron described by a normalized wavefunction rather than a tiny object travelling along a drawn circle. That change solves the problem of how an atomic state can predict position measurements, but it introduces a new question. If a wavefunction could have any arbitrary pattern and any energy, why would hydrogen repeatedly emit only particular wavelengths of light?

A stable nucleus attracts a negatively charged electron, yet attraction alone is not enough to predict a line spectrum. The state must satisfy a quantum wave equation and the physical conditions for a bound state. Only certain standing-wave-like solutions are permitted. The principal quantum number, written n, helps name their families. Understanding why n is restricted, what it predicts, and what it does not predict is the purpose of this chapter.

At first, think of n as an integer label on families of permitted bound states. By the end we will associate it with hydrogenic energy, typical spatial scale, spectroscopy and ionization. We will keep the angular-momentum and spin labels for subsequent chapters, because n never specifies an entire electronic state by itself.

1.A.2.06.2 The laboratory came first: hydrogen's line spectrum

In the nineteenth century spectroscopists separated the light from excited gases into individual bright lines. Hydrogen gave repeatable patterns. These were not continuous bands created by electrons wandering through arbitrary energies. In 1885 Johann Balmer recognized a striking numerical pattern among visible hydrogen lines; later Johannes Rydberg developed a broader reciprocal-wavelength description.

The useful observation is not the colour alone. If the emitted photon has frequency ν, its energy is hν. A line at one wavelength therefore reveals an energy difference between two atomic states. An entire family of lines constrains the allowed energy ladder. But the experiment cannot show a literal electron route inside the atom, and an energy-level diagram is not a photograph.

That distinction matters for biology as well. A spectrophotometer may detect discrete electronic transitions without directly mapping where an electron travelled. Measurement supplies wavelength and intensity; an atomic or molecular model is required to interpret the underlying states.

1.A.2.06.3 Balmer, Rydberg, Bohr and the wave-mechanical correction

Bohr's 1913 model proposed discrete permitted hydrogen energies and accounted for gross spectral regularities. It described a circular orbit with a quantization rule, useful historically but incompatible with the modern orbital probability picture. De Broglie's matter-wave proposal, electron diffraction and Schrödinger's 1926 wave mechanics replaced an orbit prescription with a wave equation. Born supplied the probability interpretation of the state amplitude.

Bohr's n survived this scientific change, but its meaning became more precise: it labels bound-state solutions rather than a path radius. An older successful formula may remain useful inside a new theory even when the original mental image is no longer correct. This is a valuable lesson in scientific reasoning: preserve a model's empirically supported predictions while revising an unsupported mechanism.

Conceptual history, not a claim that all discoveries occurred in isolated steps; the dates refer to landmark work.

1.A.2.06.4 The allowed values of n and their meaning

For a bound electronic state in the idealized one-electron Coulomb atom, the principal quantum number can take the values n = 1, 2, 3, .... The lowest allowed bound state has n = 1. The labels n = 0, −1 and 2.5 are not permitted principal quantum numbers for these states. n has no SI unit: it is a dimensionless positive integer.

Why not zero? The mathematical wavefunctions must remain normalizable and satisfy the boundary conditions of the bound-state Schrödinger equation. Those conditions yield an integer indexing scheme that begins at one. Saying that nature 'does not like zero' is not an explanation; the restrictions come from the mathematical and physical requirements on the state.

Although n helps organize the solutions, it is not an electron count. Nor does it by itself specify an orbital's angular structure, spatial orientation or electron spin. Those require other quantum numbers, introduced in the next lessons.

1.A.2.06.5 K, L, M and N: the language of shells

Historically the innermost principal shell was named K, followed by L, M, N, O, P and Q. Thus K corresponds to n=1, L to n=2, M to n=3 and N to n=4. A shell is the collection of atomic orbitals sharing the same principal quantum number. It is not a thin wall with a fixed radius, and not a set of planet-like concentric tracks.

The first four shell names are common in introductory chemistry, biological elemental analysis and X-ray spectroscopy. Some X-ray emission labels also use K and L to identify the shells involved in transitions. These are naming conventions, not proof of a unique circular orbit.

1.A.2.06.6 What makes a hydrogen-like atom special?

Hydrogen has one proton and one electron. A hydrogen-like or hydrogenic ion has one bound electron but may have a larger nuclear charge +Ze, for example He⁺ (Z=2) or Li²⁺ (Z=3). A neutral helium atom, with two electrons, is not hydrogenic even though each electron feels its nucleus's positive charge.

The simple model treats the nucleus as a point charge and the system as nonrelativistic. For electron–nucleus separation r, its potential energy is V(r) = −Ze²/(4πε₀r). Here e is the magnitude of the elementary charge (C), ε₀ is the vacuum permittivity, Z is nuclear charge number and r is measured in metres. The negative sign signifies attractive electric potential relative to separated particles at infinity.

When precision matters, nucleus and electron both move around their common centre of mass. Replace the electron mass mₑ by the reduced mass μ=mₑM/(mₑ+M), where M is the nuclear mass. Other refinements include relativistic effects, fine and hyperfine structure and quantum electrodynamics. Keeping the assumptions visible prevents an elementary formula being mistaken for every atom's exact energy.

1.A.2.06.7 Where the hydrogenic energy equation comes from

The stationary Schrödinger equation is Ĥψ = Eψ. For the relative coordinate in the one-electron Coulomb problem, Ĥ = −(ħ²/2μ)∇² − Ze²/(4πε₀r). The first term is the kinetic-energy operator; the second is the attractive potential. A bound solution must be finite, single-valued and square-integrable over space.

Separation in spherical coordinates gives a radial equation and an angular equation. Without reproducing the complete differential-equation solution, the important logical step is that acceptable radial solutions require a terminating polynomial-like factor; this creates integer quantum labels and an energy spectrum. For the ideal nonrelativistic point-Coulomb system, the result is:

Eₙ = − μ Z² e⁴ / [2(4πε₀)² ħ² n²] ≈ −(13.6057 eV)Z²/n² when μ≈mₑ.

Here ħ=h/(2π); eV is an energy unit (1 eV ≈ 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J). The numerical coefficient is appropriate to the infinite-nuclear-mass approximation and elementary precision, not a statement that measured levels are exactly identical to that expression. The 1/n² dependence is the key pattern that spectroscopy probes.

1.A.2.06.8 Negative total energy is not negative motion

We choose the electron–nucleus energy at infinite separation to be zero. A bound state has a total energy below that threshold, so Eₙ is negative. Hydrogen n=1 is about −13.61 eV; n=2 is about −3.40 eV. The second number is higher on an energy axis, because −3.40 is larger than −13.61. It is also less tightly bound.

Do not infer that kinetic energy is negative. The electron's kinetic-energy expectation is positive, while attractive potential energy is negative enough that the sum is negative. For the ideal Coulomb bound state, the virial theorem gives ⟨T⟩=−E and ⟨V⟩=2E, making E=⟨T⟩+⟨V⟩ consistent. These are state expectations, not measurements of an electron flying around a fixed orbit.

The choice of zero is important whenever we talk about ionization. Adding 13.61 eV to hydrogen's lowest state brings the electron to the threshold at E=0, not to an imaginary 'n=0 shell'.

1.A.2.06.9 A level diagram must respect the energy axis

As n increases, Eₙ approaches zero from below. The separation between neighbouring levels decreases: n=1 and n=2 differ much more in energy than n=4 and n=5. This is why a correct energy-level picture does not draw every adjacent line with equal vertical spacing.

Level labels carry numerical energies. The transition directions, positive photon energies and zero continuum are the physical information—not electron trajectories.

The display is schematic: the large n=1 to n=2 gap makes accurately scaled small high-n gaps hard to resolve in a small graphic. Always use the numeric energies, rather than pixel distances, to calculate line wavelengths.

1.A.2.06.10 Comparing hydrogen levels with exact ratios

With Z fixed, Eₙ is proportional to −1/n². Consequently E₂/E₁=1/4 and E₄/E₂=1/4; ratios of negative energies are positive. But the amount of energy required for a transition is a difference, not a ratio. For hydrogen, ΔE₁→₂ = E₂−E₁ ≈10.2043 eV.

The progression n=1,2,3,4 produces approximate energies −13.6057, −3.4014, −1.5117, −0.8504 eV. An excited atom with n=4 can fall to n=3 or n=2 or n=1, subject to transition selection rules and available pathways. The permitted electromagnetic transitions are governed by more than n alone; the orbital angular momentum rules come later. A calculation based only on energy difference predicts a possible photon energy, not guaranteed transition intensity.

1.A.2.06.11 Absorption and emission: get the signs right

Suppose an atom is initially in state nᵢ and ends in n𝒇. The atom's energy change is ΔE_atom=E𝒇−Eᵢ. During absorption, ΔE_atom is positive and an incoming photon supplies this positive energy. During emission, ΔE_atom is negative while the outgoing photon energy is −ΔE_atom and therefore positive. Confusing an atom's signed energy change with the photon's nonnegative energy is a common examination error.

For a photon in vacuum, Eγ=hν=hc/λ. The convenient conversion hc≈1239.841984 eV·nm lets you find λ in nanometres if Eγ is in electronvolts. A wavelength calculated this way is the vacuum wavelength under the chosen model. Air-versus-vacuum conventions and small physical corrections can shift tabulated observed values.

As an illustration, hydrogen n=3→2 emits about 1.88968 eV, corresponding to about 656.1 nm with the simplified coefficient. This lies in the red Balmer region. The photon corresponds to the difference between the two levels; neither n=2 nor n=3 alone is the wavelength.

1.A.2.06.12 Where Balmer, Lyman and Paschen series fit

When transitions terminate at n=1, the hydrogen lines belong to the Lyman series, predominantly ultraviolet. Transitions terminating at n=2 form the Balmer series, including familiar visible lines. Transitions to n=3 form the Paschen series in the infrared. Those are historical series names for families of energy differences, not names for extra kinds of orbitals.

We can express the line pattern as 1/λ = R_Z(1/n𝒇²−1/nᵢ²) for emission with nᵢ>n𝒇, where R_Z is the appropriate reduced-mass-corrected Rydberg wavenumber for a one-electron system and any additional corrections are ignored. Because each term is inverse-square, higher series members crowd toward a finite series limit. An experiment measuring these wavelength patterns supported a discrete hydrogenic energy ladder.

It would be misleading to transfer the same formula to absorption bands from chlorophyll or protein aromatic residues. Molecular states include different physics, and those spectra cannot be interpreted as isolated hydrogen transitions.

1.A.2.06.13 The ionization boundary and the meaning of n→∞

The energies Eₙ rise toward zero as n becomes large. In this limiting sense a series of bound levels approaches the ionization threshold. The notation n→∞ describes a mathematical limit. It does not announce an additional ordinary bound orbital labelled infinity; positive-energy continuum states require a different treatment.

From the idealized hydrogen ground state, the threshold energy is about 13.6057 eV. From its excited n=2 level, it is about 3.4014 eV. The general threshold formula for the bound level n of a one-electron ion is Iₙ = −Eₙ ≈13.6057 Z²/n² eV, subject to the same reduced-mass and ideal-model assumptions. A photon carrying more than Iₙ can produce an outgoing electron with excess kinetic energy if other conservation conditions are satisfied.

Ionization potential in volts is numerically equal to the corresponding single-electron ionization energy in eV when divided by the elementary charge, but energy and electric potential are not identical physical quantities. Always specify which quantity an exam question asks for.

1.A.2.06.14 Why n also relates to the spatial scale

Two trends emerge from the hydrogenic wave equation: higher n generally corresponds to a more extended bound-state probability distribution, and greater nuclear charge Z draws the one-electron distribution inward. A natural length scale is the Bohr radius a₀≈0.529177 Å for the infinite-nuclear-mass Z=1 model. One ångström is 10⁻¹⁰ m; equivalently a₀≈0.0529177 nm.

For an electron–nucleus system with reduced mass μ, let a_μ=4πε₀ħ²/(μe²). The characteristic hydrogenic scale becomes a_μ/Z. The simplified Bohr quantity n²a_μ/Z is a useful scale for a principal shell, but it is not an exact thin-ring radius and not every orbital with the same n has the same mean radial position.

A wavefunction can have nonzero probability at many radii. A measurement of an electron in an n=3 state is not guaranteed to place it beyond every possible position in n=2. Greater typical extent means a statistical change in the distribution, not a hard wall separating shells.

1.A.2.06.15 Mean radius depends on more than n

For normalized bound states in the ideal nonrelativistic Coulomb problem, the expected electron–nucleus separation is ⟨r⟩ₙₗ = (a_μ/2Z)[3n²−l(l+1)]. The angular momentum label l is a nonnegative integer whose allowed values will be derived carefully in the next Node. For now, the equation simply demonstrates that an n label alone does not fix a unique mean radius.

For hydrogen, ⟨r⟩ for 1s is 1.5a₀≈0.7938 Å, whereas its most probable radial distance is a₀≈0.5292 Å. The n=2 examples differ too: 2s has mean distance 6a₀≈3.175 Å, while 2p has mean distance 5a₀≈2.646 Å. Both are n=2 states in the ideal hydrogen model and have the same gross energy, but not the same mean radius.

An expectation value averages many identically prepared measurements. It does not assign a permanent point at exactly that distance. When describing shell size, specify whether you mean a characteristic scale, a radial probability maximum, a mean value or a chosen probability contour.

1.A.2.06.16 The radical difference between density and radial probability

The previous lesson explained that |ψ(r,θ,φ)|² is a position probability density per unit volume. For a normalized hydrogenic state with separated radial and angular factors, ψ=Rₙₗ(r)Yₗₘ(θ,φ), the probability of finding the electron in a thin spherical shell from r to r+dr is Pₙₗ(r)dr = r²|Rₙₗ(r)|²dr, after integrating over angular directions. The factor r² appears because spherical shells at larger radii contain more volume.

In the hydrogen 1s case, the three-dimensional density is greatest at the nucleus, while radial-shell probability is zero at r=0 and reaches a maximum at r=a₀ (approximately, for ordinary hydrogen). These facts do not conflict: a mathematical point has zero volume and a thin spherical shell has volume proportional to r². Never read the maximum of a density per unit volume as the maximum of the radial-shell distribution.

To obtain an actual probability between distances r₁ and r₂, integrate ∫[r₁,r₂]Pₙₗ(r)dr. The radial probability density has units of inverse length, so its integral is dimensionless. This distinction will become important when later diagrams compare orbital shapes and nodes.

1.A.2.06.17 An original radial illustration: 1s and 2s

Using x=r/a₀ for hydrogen with the simplified mass approximation, the normalized dimensionless radial probability curves satisfy a₀P₁ₛ(r)=4x²e⁻²ˣ and a₀P₂ₛ(r)=⅛x²(2−x)²e⁻ˣ. Their areas from x=0 to infinity each equal one. The 2s state has a radial node at x=2, where the radial probability becomes zero, but we defer the systematic counting of nodes and shapes to the later orbital-geometry lesson.

Both curves use the same horizontal and vertical units, allowing a meaningful shape comparison. Exact curve shapes use the stated elementary hydrogenic equations; the labels are explanatory rather than raw experimental recordings.

Notice that a larger-n state can still have some probability near the nucleus, and a smaller-n state has a tail extending outward. Probability distributions overlap. A shell never acts as a perfect cage.

1.A.2.06.18 Hydrogen and He⁺: energy scales as Z²

The hydrogenic energy expression predicts that at fixed n and nearly the same reduced mass, the magnitude of binding energy increases as Z². Thus He⁺ at n=1 has approximately −54.42 eV, four times the hydrogen magnitude; Li²⁺ at n=1 has about −122.45 eV, nine times the hydrogen magnitude. At the same n, the characteristic size falls as 1/Z.

Be careful about an exam trap: a helium atom is not He⁺. Neutral helium contains two electrons and electron–electron repulsion; it does not obey the one-electron Eₙ=−13.6Z²/n² expression exactly. Likewise the observed energy levels of neutral lithium cannot be computed by placing Z=3 into a hydrogenic one-electron formula.

These scaling rules are powerful for comparing idealized H, He⁺ and Li²⁺, provided you identify the actual charge state and acknowledge reduced-mass and fine-structure corrections.

1.A.2.06.19 Why the ideal degeneracy breaks in real atoms

In the ideal nonrelativistic point-Coulomb problem, states with the same n have the same gross energy even if their l values differ. The mathematical symmetry that produces this degeneracy is special. For multi-electron atoms, mutual electron repulsion, shielding, penetration and electron correlation make orbital energies depend on additional quantum structure. Relativistic and radiative corrections can further split levels even in hydrogen.

It is therefore not correct to say that n=3 must have exactly one energy in iron, oxygen or a protein metal centre. Nor is it valid to assert that the energy always increases strictly with n across different multielectron subshells. The eventual filling order is more subtle and will be taught in the later shielding and Aufbau nodes.

The useful transfer is conceptual: n describes a principal electronic shell, and an atomic state may be expanded in orbitals with such labels. The exact one-electron energy formula cannot be moved unchanged to all atoms or molecules.

1.A.2.06.20 Shell size does not mean shell capacity

A larger n provides a wider family of allowed spatial states, but n itself is not 'the number of electrons in that shell'. Electron capacity depends on the number of allowed orbital quantum states and on the Pauli exclusion principle. Later we will derive the familiar 2n² maximum capacity for the conventional nonrelativistic orbital-shell counting scheme, not merely announce it as a magical property of a Bohr circle.

At this stage, remember only the distinctions: shell label comes from n; subshell labels require l; each spatial orbital has further quantum labels; including spin permits electron occupancy rules. The table of K, L, M, N describes shell identity regardless of how many electrons a particular real atom places in them.

1.A.2.06.21 A shell is a family of functions, not a spherical slab

When an illustration draws several rings around a nucleus, it compresses a difficult mathematical result into an attractive visual metaphor. Each actual bound-state wavefunction extends through a range of space; different states often overlap and have characteristic probability distributions. The angular probability distribution may be directional rather than spherical, even for the same n. We will study those patterns in the later orbital-shape node.

The n=2 manifold includes states with different angular structure. In the ideal hydrogenic model their gross energy is the same, but their spatial patterns and radial means differ. That single counterexample defeats the claim that a shell must be one geometric surface. Think of 'shell' as an organizing quantum label, not an impenetrable layer.

1.A.2.06.22 Two ways of describing the size-energy relationship

One route is historical. Bohr's circular-orbit calculation suggests a characteristic radius proportional to n²/Z and binding energy proportional to −Z²/n². A second route is modern. The hydrogenic Schrödinger equation gives bound stationary states that reproduce the corresponding gross energy dependence, but provides probability distributions rather than exact orbits. It is the second explanation that correctly predicts multiple shapes and radial expectation values.

A useful dimensional intuition is that squeezing a wavefunction into a very small region raises the kinetic-energy scale associated with rapid variation; attraction to the nucleus favours smaller distances. The stable bound-state pattern balances these effects subject to quantization. This intuition does not replace a solution of the wave equation, but helps explain why energy and spatial extent are linked.

For advanced learners, the Coulomb virial theorem relates the expected kinetic energy, potential energy and total energy. This is a statement about stationary-state averages, not a classical model of instantaneous circular motion.
