1.A.2.07.1 A missing label within the principal shell

In the previous node, hydrogenic energies were labelled by the positive integer n, but a puzzle remained: the states called 2s and 2p share the same n=2 and, in the simplest Coulomb model, the same gross energy, yet they have different probability distributions. A single shell cannot mean just one rigid spherical surface.

The missing idea is angular structure. An orbital wavefunction may vary as we move outward from the nucleus and as we turn around it. The azimuthal or orbital angular-momentum quantum number, written as lowercase l (pronounced “ell”), distinguishes permitted subshell families within one principal shell. Our goal is not to memorize shapes: it is to understand why states with different l exist, what can be measured about them and how the names s, p, d and f arise.

The sequence here follows the scientific problem from observed spectra through wave mechanics to quantum-state restrictions, before asking which conclusions carry into chemistry and biology.

1.A.2.07.2 Spectroscopy provided the older language

Atomic spectroscopists classified groups of lines before wave mechanics existed. Words such as sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental described recognizable spectral features; their initial letters survived as s, p, d and f. The history of that notation is not a record of people seeing four kinds of tiny orbiting paths.

Bohr explained gross hydrogen spectral regularities using quantized states; the old quantum theory proposed further restrictions but retained problematic classical-orbit pictures. Schrödinger’s 1926 wave mechanics replaced these paths with acceptable stationary wavefunctions. The quantum number l now identifies an eigenvalue of orbital angular-momentum squared, providing a mathematically defensible reason for subshell distinctions.

Historical labels can be retained even when a theory changes their interpretation. This is common in science, and an important warning against treating a convenient orbital illustration as a photograph.

1.A.2.07.3 A point near an atom needs three coordinates

We can locate a position relative to a nucleus using Cartesian coordinates (x,y,z), but a central potential is especially convenient in spherical coordinates: radial distance r, polar angle θ and azimuthal angle φ. The potential energy V(r)=−Ze²/(4πε₀r) of an ideal point nucleus depends only on distance. At one fixed r it has the same value in every direction.

Such rotational symmetry allows the Schrödinger equation to separate into radial and angular parts. A bound hydrogenic orbital can be written ψₙₗₘ(r,θ,φ)=Rₙₗ(r)Yₗᵐ(θ,φ). The radial factor R depends on n and l; the angular factor Y is a spherical harmonic and depends on l and an additional magnetic quantum label m, treated next time.

This expression predicts that different orbital wavefunctions may share n but differ in angular structure. It should not be transferred unchanged to an arbitrary molecule, which usually lacks the same spherically symmetric one-electron potential.

1.A.2.07.4 Central potentials and exact versus approximate l

For an electron in an ideal central potential, the Hamiltonian is invariant under rotations around the chosen nucleus. Its stationary functions can therefore be selected with definite squared orbital angular momentum. This is why l is an exact quantum label for the hydrogenic Coulomb problem. The physical role is deeper than naming a shell letter.

In a many-electron atom, electron–electron interactions are present and atomic orbitals are often constructed using central-field approximations. The individual-orbital l can still be a very useful label, but a genuine interacting atom may require total many-electron angular-momentum labels instead. In a molecule, an orbital can contain contributions from several atomic s-, p- and d-type functions without possessing one exact atomic l.

When using subshell notation in biological chemistry, state whether it is an exact isolated one-electron solution, an approximate atomic orbital or a component of a molecular basis.

1.A.2.07.5 The allowed values of l

For a bound state with principal quantum number n in the hydrogenic Coulomb model, the secondary quantum number must satisfy l = 0, 1, 2, …, n−1. Thus it is a nonnegative integer, always smaller than n. Unlike n, l can be zero. Neither negative values nor fractions are valid in this ordinary orbital classification. Both quantum numbers are dimensionless.

For n=1 the only allowed l is 0; for n=2 the allowed values are 0 and 1; for n=3 they are 0, 1 and 2; for n=4 they are 0, 1, 2 and 3. The range does not describe how many electrons a shell contains. It lists which subshell states exist as mathematical possibilities.

The key examination test is simple: first check n≥1 is an integer, then confirm l≥0 is an integer, and finally check l≤n−1. Do not guess from the familiar appearance of an orbital.

1.A.2.07.6 From integer l to s, p, d, f and beyond

The conventional mapping is l=0 → s, l=1 → p, l=2 → d, l=3 → f, l=4 → g. Consequently 3p means n=3,l=1, while 4f means n=4,l=3. The first number is a principal-shell label; the letter is the subshell family. A 3f label is forbidden because l=3 is not smaller than n=3. Likewise 2d and 1p do not exist as bound Coulomb labels.

The letter s is not an abbreviation for electron spin. The actual spin and its projection require different quantum labels. Nor is 3p a fully specified single spatial orbital: it identifies a subshell, which includes states distinguished by additional angular information.

For unfamiliar higher subshells, translate the letter to l before deciding legality. A 5g designation is valid, since n=5 and l=4 satisfy the inequality; 4g is not valid in this bound-state sequence.

1.A.2.07.8 Shell, subshell, orbital and electron state are different

A shell fixes n; a subshell fixes n and l. A particular central-potential spatial orbital additionally requires a magnetic quantum label ml; a fully specified single-electron state includes its spin degree of freedom. These are different levels of description. Chemistry teaching often calls 3p a “p orbital,” but rigorously it is the 3p subshell designation unless an individual angular function is specified.

For n=3, we have the permitted subshells 3s, 3p and 3d. That does not establish the number of electrons that an actual atom puts into any of them. Allowed describes the available quantum-state family; occupied describes a physical electronic configuration after energy ordering, exclusion and atomic conditions are considered.

Preserving the distinction helps us avoid the later error of claiming that calcium necessarily contains a 4f electron just because 4f is a mathematically permitted family.

1.A.2.07.9 Orbital angular momentum: the physical quantity

Classically, angular momentum about an origin is the vector L = r × p, where r is position and p is linear momentum. Its SI units are kg·m²·s⁻¹, which is equivalent to joule-second. The cross product encodes both magnitude and direction, but this classical formula must not be interpreted as evidence that the quantum electron follows a precisely specified circular track.

In quantum mechanics, momentum becomes a differential operator p̂=−iħ∇ and orbital angular momentum becomes L̂ = r × p̂. The operator L̂² has well-defined eigenfunctions in a central potential. For a state with orbital quantum number l, L̂²ψ = l(l+1)ħ²ψ. Here ħ=h/(2π), the reduced Planck constant.

This is a statement about possible outcomes of angular-momentum-squared measurements on suitably prepared quantum states. It is not a claim that an electron's classical position and tangent velocity can be reconstructed from l.

1.A.2.07.10 Deriving the magnitude from the L² eigenvalue

Since a definite-l state has L² eigenvalue l(l+1)ħ², its orbital angular-momentum magnitude is |L| = ħ√[l(l+1)]. The expression is not simply lħ. For l=0 the magnitude is zero; for l=1 it is √2ħ; for l=2 it is √6ħ; for l=3 it is √12ħ. The square root is essential.

1.A.2.07.11 The l=0 state is not an electron at rest

The s family has l=0 and so zero orbital angular-momentum magnitude. That does not mean a stationary classical particle sitting at the nucleus, a zero kinetic-energy expectation, or an electron without intrinsic spin. Hydrogen 1s is a quantum bound state with an extended spatial wavefunction; it has positive kinetic energy in expectation while L²=0.

For an ideal central-potential s orbital, the angular wavefunction is constant over directions, giving spherical symmetry of the position probability density at a fixed radius. Its radial distribution still varies with r. When we distinguish spherical point density from spherical-shell probability, we see that spherical symmetry is compatible with nontrivial quantum dynamics and measurement statistics.

Biological molecules built partly from atomic s-like basis functions generally do not inherit the spherical symmetry of a free isolated atomic s state: their total molecular environment matters.

1.A.2.07.12 Same n, different l: control the comparison

Compare 3s, 3p and 3d in ideal hydrogen. Their principal number n=3 is the same and therefore their gross Coulomb energies are degenerate, yet their l values are 0, 1 and 2. Their orbital angular-momentum magnitudes are correspondingly 0, √2ħ and √6ħ. Their angular and radial wavefunctions differ despite the energy coincidence.

Now compare 2p and 3p. Their principal numbers and radial functions differ, but both have l=1 and thus the same |L|=√2ħ. The experiments and calculations answer different questions: the principal energy differs with n in a pure Coulomb system, while angular-momentum magnitude depends on l.

By holding one variable constant while changing the other, we avoid suggesting that increasing n automatically increases angular momentum, or that subshell type alone determines energy in every real atom.

1.A.2.07.13 The mathematical reason l is an integer

The angular part of the central-potential Schrödinger equation is solved using spherical harmonics, functions that must be well behaved and single-valued over the sphere. Their orbital-angular-momentum eigenvalues are labelled by nonnegative integers l. An arbitrary fractional l would not be an allowed label for these ordinary single-valued orbital wavefunctions.

For the Coulomb bound-state radial equation, acceptable square-integrable solutions impose an additional relation n=nᵣ+l+1, where nᵣ is a nonnegative integer radial index. Therefore n≥l+1 and l≤n−1. This explains why angular regularity and radial normalizability together give the familiar n–l range. The derivation is for a bound Coulomb problem; it should not be asserted for every possible central potential without checking its radial spectrum.

Later we will study how nᵣ relates to radial nodes and why angular nodal patterns differ among subshells. For now, the bound-state inequality is already a complete reason to reject 1p and 2d.

1.A.2.07.14 The angular eigenvalue equation

When the potential depends only on r, the angular part of a stationary orbital obeys L̂²Yₗᵐ = l(l+1)ħ²Yₗᵐ. In spherical coordinates, L̂² = −ħ²[(1/sinθ)∂θ(sinθ ∂θ) + (1/sin²θ)∂²φ]. The θ and φ symbols are angles; ∂ denotes a partial derivative with respect to one of them. This operator describes how the wavefunction changes upon angular motion through coordinate space.

The spherical harmonics Yₗᵐ can be normalized over the solid angle: ∫|Yₗᵐ|²dΩ=1, where dΩ=sinθ dθ dφ. This is why simply writing ∫|Y|²dθ dφ=1 without the spherical factor would be the wrong normalization in general.

Solving the entire associated-Legendre differential equation is beyond this lesson's scope, but understanding the eigenvalue equation and regularity conditions makes the angular-momentum formula scientifically meaningful instead of a detached rule.

1.A.2.07.15 Angular patterns: phase, density and the meaning of a shape

The angular factor for l=0 is direction independent. When l>0, spherical harmonics can depend strongly on direction and may have nodal surfaces where the wavefunction vanishes. In a commonly used real basis, a p-like function can be drawn as two lobes separated by a nodal plane; the contrasting colours often mark opposite signs of wavefunction phase, not opposite electric charge.

Simple shape charts are only representations: a surface may indicate a selected probability density, a signed amplitude or a mathematical isosurface. Complex Yₗᵐ functions and real linear combinations do not all have the same familiar lobe picture, even though each has the same L² eigenvalue for the chosen l. Therefore a p subshell is not a single rigid dumbbell that an electron follows.

Node 10 will systematically discuss shapes and node counts. For this chapter, use shapes only to understand why angular structure is new information beyond the principal shell.

1.A.2.07.16 Triangular n–l selection: an original illustrated decision rule

Allowed n–l combinations form a triangle because each new principal shell permits one additional angular momentum family. The s family begins at n=1, p first appears at n=2, d first appears at n=3, f first appears at n=4, and g first appears at n=5. This is the same inequality l≤n−1 shown geometrically.

Notice the difference between impossibility and vacancy. The blank 3f cell is mathematically forbidden as a bound Coulomb n–l label; an allowed but unoccupied 4f cell in an actual atom is a different situation.

1.A.2.07.17 An eigenvalue plot: angular momentum is not a continuous dial

The allowed |L| values for l=0,1,2,3,4 are 0,√2ħ,√6ħ,√12ħ,√20ħ. They are discrete, not an arbitrary set of decimal values between successive points. The familiar expression lħ would produce the wrong magnitude at every positive l.

These exact stem heights correspond to √[l(l+1)]. Axes and values are labelled; no connecting line suggests continuously allowed l.

An eigenvalue plot displays the outcome of a measurement of a well-defined quantum observable. It does not describe how a particle would physically climb a staircase of classical orbits.

1.A.2.07.18 Why l determines a magnitude, not a direction

The operator L̂² specifies squared orbital angular momentum. It says nothing by itself about the component measured along an arbitrarily chosen laboratory axis. Angular momentum is a vector quantity in classical mechanics, but the quantum components cannot in general all be simultaneously sharp. The commutation relations of its components show why additional projection information is needed.

It is tempting to think of a p orbital labelled l=1 as a single dumbbell that must point along x, y or z before a measurement. That is not a satisfactory quantum description. A complete angular state requires another label; rotating coordinates can change which linear combination of angular functions is used while leaving l unchanged. The magnetic quantum number ml and the meaning of orbital orientation will be developed in Node 08.

Keep the precise statement: l fixes the eigenvalue l(l+1)ħ² of L², and does not identify the direction of a measured angular-momentum projection.

1.A.2.07.19 Spherical harmonics, relative phases and real orbital drawings

For a central potential, the angular part Yₗᵐ(θ,φ) is a spherical harmonic. Spherical harmonics may be complex-valued. Many chemistry diagrams instead use real combinations of states within the same l multiplet, producing familiar Cartesian forms such as px, py and pz. The visible lobes of these real functions mark phase and spatial dependence.

A phase sign on one lobe does not make that lobe positively charged or require an electron to occupy it half of the time. Observable position probability is proportional to |ψ|², and the overall normalization must be handled correctly. A point at which ψ=0 is a node, while a colour change between signs can indicate a nodal separation.

For l=0, Y is constant in angle, but the radial function R can still have zeros and a complex radial dependence. Conversely, angular functions for l>0 can have directional nodes. A later node will examine shape and node counting systematically, rather than treating a cartoon as a complete solution.

1.A.2.07.20 A mathematically meaningful normalization over angles

The angular part of an orbital is normalized using the solid-angle measure dΩ=sinθ dθ dφ. Thus a normalized spherical harmonic satisfies ∫₀²π∫₀π|Yₗᵐ(θ,φ)|²sinθ dθ dφ = 1. The sine factor reflects the changing area of an element on a sphere and is essential; omitting it would improperly weight directions near the poles.

For the s family, the normalized angular factor is Y₀⁰=1/√(4π), constant in every direction. Verify normalization directly: |Y₀⁰|²=1/(4π), and the total solid angle of a sphere is 4π steradians, so its integral is exactly one.

This elementary calculation explains why probability density can be spherically symmetric without being constant in radial distance. The complete orbital probability includes the radial factor and the three-dimensional volume element r²dr dΩ, as introduced in Node 05.

1.A.2.07.21 Why 2d is forbidden but 4f is permitted

Consider a proposed 2d state. The number 2 means n=2; the letter d means l=2. But the highest allowed l for n=2 is n−1=1. Hence 2d is not a legal Coulomb bound-state label. The fact that d orbitals occur in the periodic table does not override this mathematical restriction.

For 4f, n=4 and l=3. Because 0≤3≤4−1, the label is valid. The same reasoning permits 5g (n=5,l=4), 6g (n=6,l=4) and 6f (n=6,l=3). An uncommon label can still be legal; its occupancy in any real atom is a separate physical question.

When solving examination questions, translate letters to integers before comparing them. That single method handles familiar and unfamiliar labels without needing exceptions or guesswork.

1.A.2.07.22 Orbital parity gives an additional symmetry clue

A pure spherical harmonic with orbital angular number l acquires the factor (−1)l under inversion of the position vector. Consequently s and d angular functions (l even) have even parity, while p and f angular functions (l odd) have odd parity. This follows from the mathematical angular functions, not from the shape of a hand-drawn cloud.

Parity can help interpret quantum selection rules, but it is only one ingredient. Whether a radiative transition is allowed and intense depends on transition operators, quantum-state labels and other physical conditions. The inversion property also requires appropriate symmetry; a biological molecule without a centre of inversion does not inherit a simple global atomic angular parity label.

We introduce parity as a glimpse of why l has mathematical content beyond subshell names, not as a substitute for the later spectroscopy and molecular symmetry chapters.

1.A.2.07.23 Hydrogenic degeneracy and the multielectron correction

In the ideal nonrelativistic one-electron Coulomb Hamiltonian, energy depends on n and not on l, leading to degeneracy among 3s, 3p and 3d. In real atoms, interactions can lift this simple degeneracy. Even isolated hydrogen has small fine-structure and radiative effects beyond the elementary model.

For many-electron atoms, shielding, penetration and electron–electron repulsion affect orbital energies and often distinguish subshells at equal n. Saying “3p energy is always identical to 3s” is therefore as mistaken as saying “3p must always have higher energy in every conceivable atom or molecule.” Energy ordering and the Aufbau approximation require more careful modelling than l alone.

This topic is developed later in the roadmap. Here the point is to separate a quantum-state label from a universal energy prediction.

1.A.2.07.24 A quantum-state classification is not an occupancy statement

The mathematical permission for a subshell does not tell us whether it is occupied. 4f is allowed because n=4,l=3; it can exist as a basis state or an excited atomic-state family even when a particular neutral atom's ground-state configuration does not include a 4f electron. In the same way, the number of possible l values at n=4 is four, not four electrons.

To determine electron count we eventually need an orbital-projection label and the exclusion principle; to determine ground-state occupancy we also need relative orbital energies and filling rules. Those lessons follow this one in the deliberately ordered curriculum.

For a biological metal ion, the existence of a d subshell is only the beginning. Oxidation state, ligands, geometry and electron interactions shape the actual states. Resist the temptation to turn the letter d into an entire explanation of colour or magnetism.

1.A.2.07.25 Why orbital angular momentum and electron spin must remain separate

The word “angular momentum” also appears in discussions of electron spin. However, spin is an intrinsic quantum degree of freedom and not a tiny particle literally spinning around an internal mechanical axis. Orbital angular momentum is associated with spatial wavefunction structure relative to a chosen origin.

An electron in an l=0 s state has zero orbital angular-momentum magnitude yet still possesses its intrinsic spin. The total angular momentum of actual atomic states may involve coupling between orbital and spin contributions; this is why careful physical descriptions distinguish L, S and, where appropriate, total J, rather than treating them as synonyms.

Node 08 develops the magnetic quantum number and spin projection. We do not prematurely assign a full single-electron state from n and l alone.

1.A.2.07.26 Biology case: why oxygen's p-type electrons matter

An isolated oxygen atom is commonly discussed using s- and p-type valence orbitals. In that language p means l=1, a family with angular properties different from s. The distinction prepares us for later discussion of bonding directionality, molecular orbital mixing and oxygen-containing functional groups, but it is not by itself a complete prediction of molecular oxygen's ground-state magnetism or bond order.

Molecular oxygen requires a molecular orbital description, and biological oxygen reactions often involve electronic states influenced by molecular environment. A simplistic picture that combines two isolated atomic p blobs cannot replace the quantum explanation of O₂'s unpaired electrons and reactivity.

The scientifically useful lesson is that identifying l is necessary vocabulary, but never sufficient to infer a biological mechanism without studying the actual molecular electronic structure.

1.A.2.07.27 Biology case: transition-metal d contributions in proteins

Proteins containing iron, copper and other transition metals may have electronic states with substantial d-type atomic-orbital contributions. The d designation corresponds to l=2 in an atomic basis. The label helps chemists discuss angular functions that can interact differently with surrounding ligands, but it is not an exact, standalone angular-momentum quantum number for an electron delocalized across the complete protein.

Heme and other coordination environments can change the energies, symmetries, oxidation states and spin states of metal-centred electronic configurations. Consequently two proteins containing the same metal can exhibit different absorption bands or magnetic responses even though the atomic d label is unchanged.

This is a useful boundary test: a correct learning chapter introduces s/p/d/f as physical quantum notation, but refrains from predicting metalloprotein spectra from l alone.

1.A.2.07.28 Bridge from subshells toward electron configurations

We can now explain the notation 3d without memorization: n=3 places the state in the third principal shell and d means l=2. The physical orbital angular momentum magnitude is √6ħ. We can distinguish a permitted subshell from a filled subshell, and a subshell from an individual orbital.

The remaining question is why a single subshell contains several distinguishable orbital functions, and how electron spin enters. That question requires the magnetic projection quantum number ml and spin projection ms, the subjects of Node 1.A.2.08. After those labels are understood, Node 09 will derive orbital counts and electron capacity.

Try these self-tests before continuing: Is 1p legal? What is the angular momentum of 3s? Why can a 2p and a 4p state share the same |L|? Can we determine a heme iron's electronic absorption wavelength from the value l=2? Accurate answers demonstrate conceptual readiness.

1.A.2.07.29 Twelve evidence and life-science cases

These cases deliberately test scientific reasoning across exact quantum models, spectroscopy and biological contexts. They are original explanatory applications rather than reproduced observations from a particular dataset; their boundaries are stated to prevent overextension.

Case 1: Early line-series names

An older spectroscopy paper describes sharp and diffuse line families without showing orbitals. The names capture patterns in measured lines. The s and d terminology survived into wave mechanics, but the modern l quantum number comes from angular-momentum eigenfunctions, not visible paths.

Model boundary. Historical nomenclature is not proof of a classical trajectory.

Case 2: Hydrogen 3s, 3p and 3d

In the ideal nonrelativistic point-Coulomb hydrogen model, three subshell families share n=3 and the same gross energy. They nevertheless have l=0,1,2, different angular wavefunctions and orbital angular momentum magnitudes 0,√2ħ,√6ħ.

Model boundary. A shared gross energy does not make the underlying wavefunctions identical.

Case 3: Forbidden 2d label

A candidate orbital is labelled 2d. Since d represents l=2 but the allowed bound Coulomb range for n=2 is l=0,1, the label is invalid. This is not a statement that a laboratory lacks sensitive enough instruments to observe it.

Model boundary. Validity is a mathematical boundary condition, not a technological limitation.

Case 4: Legal but unoccupied 4f

A drawing includes an empty 4f subshell in the scheme for an atom. Its label is legal because n=4,l=3, but it may be unoccupied in that ground-state configuration. A mathematical basis state and an experimentally realized occupancy describe different questions.

Model boundary. Allowed and occupied must be distinguished.

Case 5: One electron in an s state

A question argues that l=0 proves an electron is motionless. This fails because angular-momentum-squared can vanish in a nontrivial stationary spatial state whose kinetic-energy expectation is positive. Electron spin persists separately.

Model boundary. Orbital angular momentum is not the same as kinetic energy or intrinsic spin.

Case 6: A signed p drawing

A teaching figure shows two p lobes in different colours. In a real-valued wavefunction picture, these colours can indicate phase sign; they are not positive and negative electric charge. Squared amplitude gives position probability.

Model boundary. The graphical legend determines the physical meaning.

Case 7: Molecular oxygen

Oxygen atoms have p-like valence contributions, but predicting O₂'s ground-state magnetism requires molecular orbital theory. Atomic l=1 alone cannot determine unpaired-electron arrangements in the whole molecule.

Model boundary. The atomic label is a prerequisite, not a molecular answer.

Case 8: Photosynthetic pigment absorption

A pigment contains a conjugated molecular framework with electronic transitions. Spectra involve molecular orbital energies, transitions and surroundings. Assigning its absorption directly to a hydrogen-like n,l pair without evidence is invalid.

Model boundary. Molecular electronic structure cannot be inferred from an isolated atomic subshell label.

Case 9: Heme iron ligands

An iron-containing protein has d-type basis contributions, but ligand geometry, covalency, spin and oxidation state alter the electronic response. Different heme environments may yield different spectra despite the same formal d designation.

Model boundary. Biological spectroscopy demands the molecular environment.

Case 10: Angular normalization in a simulation

An angular function Y₀⁰=1/√(4π) yields ∫|Y|²dΩ=1 because total solid angle is 4π. A numerical grid that omits the sinθ integration weight will produce incorrect angular probabilities.

Model boundary. Geometry of the coordinate measure is part of probability.

Case 11: An external magnetic field

Applying a magnetic field may separate states that would be degenerate in an ideal spherically symmetric model. To describe the observed components, one needs the angular-momentum projection label m_l and interaction terms, not merely the magnitude l.

Model boundary. A field probes additional labels beyond l.

Case 12: A student reading orbital images

Two diagrams both labelled 3p appear different: one is a real Cartesian combination and another represents complex angular behaviour. Different basis choices can portray different patterns while retaining l=1. Compare what the figures actually plot before deciding they contradict each other.

Model boundary. A picture is incomplete without its mathematical basis and legend.

1.A.2.07.30 Forty worked n–l and angular-momentum problems

Every solution explicitly states the rule or model. Numerical examples use ħ≈1.054571817×10⁻³⁴ J·s and standard dimensionless l labels. Simpler exercises establish prerequisite logic; later problems test operators, SI conversions, angular parity and validity conditions.

Worked example 1: List the l values for n=1

Problem. What are the legal orbital angular momenta in the first principal shell?

Reasoned solution. Bound-state condition 0≤l≤n−1 gives 0≤l≤0. Only l=0, an s family, is permitted; 1p is excluded.

Worked example 2: Find all subshells for n=2

Problem. Translate each allowed l to a letter.

Reasoned solution. n−1=1, so l=0,1. These are 2s and 2p; 2d would require l=2.

Worked example 3: Find all subshells for n=3

Problem. How many subshell families appear?

Reasoned solution. Allowed l values are 0,1,2: hence 3s,3p,3d. There are three families, not three electrons.

Worked example 4: Find all subshells for n=4

Problem. Write the ordered list.

Reasoned solution. l=0,1,2,3. The families are 4s,4p,4d,4f. The mathematical list says nothing about occupancy.

Worked example 5: Five principal shells

Problem. What is the highest l for n=5?

Reasoned solution. l_max=n−1=4, corresponding to g. Thus 5g is valid, but 5h would require l=5 and is invalid.

Worked example 6: Check 1p

Problem. Is the state n=1,l=1 permitted?

Reasoned solution. No. The maximum l in n=1 is zero; 1p is an impossible bound Coulomb designation.

Worked example 7: Check 2d

Problem. Is n=2,l=2 legal?

Reasoned solution. No. The highest allowed l is one. The letter d means l=2 and fails l

Worked example 8: Check 3d

Problem. Is 3d a legal designation?

Reasoned solution. Yes. n=3,l=2 satisfies 0≤2≤2.

Worked example 9: Check 3f

Problem. Is 3f legal?

Reasoned solution. No. f means l=3 but n=3 requires l≤2.

Worked example 10: Check 4f

Problem. Is n=4,l=3 legal?

Reasoned solution. Yes, since l_max=3. Allowed does not imply occupied in a particular atom.

Worked example 11: Check 4g

Problem. Can a 4g Coulomb bound state be labelled?

Reasoned solution. No. g corresponds to l=4 while the n=4 limit is l=3.

Worked example 12: Check 5g

Problem. What is the first principal shell allowing g?

Reasoned solution. g means l=4, so n≥l+1=5. The first possible one is 5g.

Worked example 13: Identify l from 6f

Problem. What is l in the label 6f?

Reasoned solution. The letter f represents l=3. It is allowed because 3≤6−1.

Worked example 14: Find the smallest n for p

Problem. What is the least n that admits l=1?

Reasoned solution. From n≥l+1, the minimum is n=2. There is no 1p state.

Worked example 15: Find the smallest n for d

Problem. What is the earliest d subshell?

Reasoned solution. d has l=2, so n_min=3 and 3d is the earliest permitted designation.

Worked example 16: Find the smallest n for f

Problem. What is the earliest f subshell?

Reasoned solution. f has l=3, so n_min=4: the first is 4f.

Worked example 17: Find l maximum for n=7

Problem. What is the largest allowed l?

Reasoned solution. l_max=7−1=6. A higher spectroscopic letter is possible, but the rule is the useful result.

Worked example 18: Count l families for n=6

Problem. How many distinct subshell l values are allowed?

Reasoned solution. Integers 0,1,2,3,4,5 give six distinct l values. This is a subshell-family count, not orbital or electron capacity.

Worked example 19: Identify 3p

Problem. Which two quantum labels are fixed by 3p?

Reasoned solution. n=3 and l=1. An additional angular projection label is required to identify a particular spatial orbital.

Worked example 20: Name a pair

Problem. Convert n=5,l=2 into subshell notation.

Reasoned solution. l=2 means d; combining with n=5 gives 5d. The inequality 2≤4 confirms it is valid.

Worked example 21: Angular momentum of s

Problem. Calculate |L| when l=0.

Reasoned solution. |L|=ħ√[0(1)]=0. This does not imply zero kinetic energy or zero electron spin.

Worked example 22: Angular momentum of p

Problem. Calculate |L| in units of ħ for l=1.

Reasoned solution. √[1(2)]=√2≈1.4142, so |L|≈1.4142ħ.

Worked example 23: Angular momentum of d

Problem. Calculate |L| for l=2.

Reasoned solution. |L|=ħ√[2(3)]=√6ħ≈2.4495ħ.

Worked example 24: Angular momentum of f

Problem. Calculate |L| for l=3.

Reasoned solution. |L|=ħ√[3(4)]=√12ħ≈3.4641ħ.

Worked example 25: Angular momentum of g

Problem. Calculate |L| for l=4.

Reasoned solution. |L|=ħ√[4(5)]=√20ħ≈4.4721ħ.

Worked example 26: SI angular momentum of p

Problem. Convert the p-subshell magnitude to J·s.

Reasoned solution. Multiply √2 by ħ≈1.054571817×10⁻³⁴ J·s. The result is ≈1.491×10⁻³⁴ J·s; units follow from ħ.

Worked example 27: SI angular momentum of d

Problem. Convert the d magnitude to SI units.

Reasoned solution. √6×1.054571817×10⁻³⁴≈2.583×10⁻³⁴ J·s. The √6 factor is dimensionless.

Worked example 28: Recover l from L²

Problem. A state has L²=6ħ². Determine l.

Reasoned solution. Solve l(l+1)=6. Nonnegative integer l=2 works; therefore the family is d, not p.

Worked example 29: Recover l from |L|

Problem. The magnitude equals √12 ħ. Find l.

Reasoned solution. Square the dimensionless factor to obtain l(l+1)=12. l=3 gives 3×4=12, so the family is f.

Worked example 30: Reject an unphysical angular eigenvalue

Problem. Can l=1.5 label an ordinary orbital state?

Reasoned solution. No. Although one could insert 1.5 into a numerical formula, regular single-valued orbital angular functions use nonnegative integer l. Formula substitution alone does not validate a state.

Worked example 31: Two states with one l

Problem. Compare orbital L of 2p and 5p.

Reasoned solution. Both have l=1 and hence |L|=√2ħ, although their principal n and radial functions differ.

Worked example 32: Two states with one n

Problem. Compare orbital L of 3s and 3d.

Reasoned solution. Both have n=3, but l=0 gives 0 whereas l=2 gives √6ħ. Their gross Coulomb energies coincide in the simplest hydrogen model.

Worked example 33: Radial index of 3p

Problem. For n=3,l=1 compute n_r.

Reasoned solution. Use n_r=n−l−1=3−1−1=1, a nonnegative integer. This confirms consistency of the labels in the Coulomb bound-state classification.

Worked example 34: Radial index of 4f

Problem. For n=4,l=3 compute n_r.

Reasoned solution. n_r=4−3−1=0. The nonnegative result is compatible with a bound Coulomb state.

Worked example 35: Radial index of 2d

Problem. Why would this give an impossibility?

Reasoned solution. n_r=2−2−1=−1, which violates the nonnegative radial index required by a normalizable bound Coulomb solution.

Worked example 36: How many subshell families at n=9?

Problem. Count without naming every letter.

Reasoned solution. l runs from 0 through 8 inclusive, giving 9 values. This is not an electron-count formula.

Worked example 37: Parity of a p angular function

Problem. Find inversion parity for l=1.

Reasoned solution. (−1)^1=−1. The angular factor changes sign under spatial inversion in the central-potential spherical harmonic description.

Worked example 38: Parity of a d angular function

Problem. Find inversion parity for l=2.

Reasoned solution. (−1)^2=+1. The angular factor is even under inversion; this does not predict an arbitrary molecular spectrum.

Worked example 39: Normalize the angular s factor

Problem. Check Y₀⁰=1/√(4π).

Reasoned solution. The square modulus is 1/(4π). Multiply by total sphere solid angle ∫dΩ=4π, obtaining integrated angular probability 1.

Worked example 40: Distinguish orbital angular momentum from spin

Problem. Does l=0 imply a spinless electron?

Reasoned solution. No. l characterizes spatial orbital angular momentum. Electron intrinsic spin remains a separate quantum degree of freedom.
