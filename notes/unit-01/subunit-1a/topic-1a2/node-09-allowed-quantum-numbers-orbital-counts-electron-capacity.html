1. The puzzle: why can an atom have many states but only so many electrons?

In the previous three lessons we met four labels: the principal number n, angular-momentum number l, magnetic projection ml, and electron-spin projection ms. Each label answers a different question. Yet a collection of four arbitrary numbers is not necessarily an allowed electron state. The labels must fit together.

Imagine that a spectroscopist identifies an electron in the third principal shell, specifically in a p subshell. How many distinct spatial orbitals are available? How many electron states are possible when spin is included? Why are there exactly six possible one-electron states associated with 3p, while the whole third shell offers eighteen? The answer does not come from memorizing a capacity table. It comes from counting the quantum states that the wave equation allows.

This chapter builds that count carefully, checks proposed quantum-number sets, and explains what the answer can—and cannot—tell us about real chemical atoms. Treat it as a bridge from the meaning of quantum numbers (Nodes 06–08) to orbital shapes, energies and electronic configurations (Nodes 10–22).

Central learning question: Once the permitted values of n, l, ml and ms are known, how do we derive the number of orbitals and the maximum number of electrons without mistaking capacity for filling order?

2. Why counting quantum states became necessary

The early quantum model helped explain the existence of discrete atomic spectra, but spectra alone did not reveal how the electrons of a many-electron atom were organized. The development of wave mechanics in the 1920s supplied allowed one-electron states. Pauli's exclusion principle then linked the idea of distinct states to how many electrons a collection of orbitals could accommodate. This historical progression is important: the orbital counts arise from wave-mechanical quantum numbers; the electron capacity additionally needs a restriction on identical electrons.

A classical picture would allow a continuously adjustable orbit. A quantum state is different: acceptable wavefunctions obey boundary and symmetry conditions, so the quantum labels take restricted values. We do not need to solve the Schrödinger equation afresh here, but we must preserve the distinction between a physical state and a convenient drawing. A box in an orbital diagram represents a permitted spatial state, not a tiny compartment in which a particle sits.

For the basic isolated-atom configuration model, we count one-electron spatial orbitals labelled by (n, l, ml), then attach the two available spin projections. More sophisticated atomic descriptions can use coupled angular-momentum labels such as j and mj; the simple counting remains an extremely useful and well-defined starting point under the stated one-electron-orbital framework.

3. The four restrictions, in the correct order

Every candidate tuple must be checked in a particular order because one quantum number limits the next. The values of n, l and ml are integers. Only ms is a half-integer in the electron-spin part of this familiar notation.

n = 1, 2, 3, …
l = 0, 1, …, n − 1
ml = −l, −l + 1, …, 0, …, l − 1, l
ms = +½ or −½
Check n first; only then can l and mₗ be judged.

Step 1: A principal shell is indexed by a positive integer n; n = 0 and negative n are not permitted for the ordinary bound hydrogenic orbital labels. Step 2: For this n, l must be an integer from zero through n−1. Step 3: For the chosen l, ml must be one of the integers from −l through +l, including zero. Step 4: The electron-spin projection is exactly +½ or −½ along the chosen quantization axis.

Notice a subtle but decisive point: ml is not restricted by n alone. For example, ml = +2 is allowed in n = 3 only when l = 2, not when l = 0 or 1. Similarly, +½ is a permitted electron-spin projection; +1 and 0 are not.

4. Decision diagram: test a proposed quantum-number tuple

A frequent examination mistake is to test the spin projection and stop, overlooking that the orbital label itself may be impossible. Use the following decision chain; every step is necessary, and no later step repairs an earlier failure.

As a demonstration, test (3, 2, −2, +½). The third shell exists; l = 2 is within 0…2; ml = −2 belongs to −2…+2; and the spin is allowed. The tuple is valid. Now test (3, 1, −2, +½). The first two conditions pass, but p orbitals have ml = −1, 0, +1, so the tuple is invalid, despite its perfectly acceptable spin.

5. An orbital is three labels; a spin-orbital is four

It is easy to say “four quantum numbers describe an electron” and forget the hierarchy behind that sentence. In this model (n, l, ml) identifies a spatial orbital. When ms is added, we have a one-electron spin-orbital (a spatial function paired with an electron spin state). Because ms has two options, each spatial orbital generates two distinct spin-orbitals.

For 3p the three spatial orbitals are (3,1,−1), (3,1,0) and (3,1,+1). Each has a +½ and a −½ spin choice. We have not placed six electrons into these orbitals; we have simply identified six available one-electron states. Electron occupation is a separate question.

In multielectron quantum chemistry, orbitals are approximate one-electron functions used to construct antisymmetric many-electron states. The counting is reliable for this instructional model, but electrons are indistinguishable: one should not imagine that electron A permanently owns a numbered box in a many-electron atom.

6. Counting the orbital orientations in one subshell

Fix n and l. The only varying spatial label is ml, which runs through every integer from −l to +l. There are l negative values, one zero and l positive values. Hence the number of spatial orbitals for this subshell is l + 1 + l.

Number of orbitals in subshell l = 2l + 1
A discrete count (dimensionless); independent of n once l is allowed.

If l = 0 (an s subshell), ml = 0 only: one orbital. For l = 1 (p) there are three; l = 2 (d) gives five; l = 3 (f) gives seven. The next letter, g, corresponds to l = 4 and nine orbitals. A g subshell exists in the mathematical n ≥ 5 orbital family even though it is not part of the usual ground-state configurations of common elements.

This rule counts degenerate magnetic projections in the spherically symmetric central-potential approximation. It does not assert that a real environment or applied magnetic field keeps every state at exactly the same energy.

7. From spatial orbitals to possible electron states

Now include spin. For every allowed ml, the electron has two permitted spin projections, +½ and −½. Multiplying choices is justified because we are taking two independent labels in this one-electron basis.

Spin-orbitals in subshell = 2(2l + 1)
Capacity in electrons follows when Pauli exclusion prevents duplicate occupation of one spin-orbital.

For p, l = 1, so 2l + 1 = 3 spatial orbitals, and 2 × 3 = 6 spin-orbitals. This is why a p subshell can accommodate at most six electrons in the standard atomic configuration notation. A filled p subshell is written p6; an incompletely occupied one might be p2 or p5.

We are previewing Pauli exclusion here because without it “six different spin states exist” would not automatically mean “only six electrons can be in these states.” A deeper discussion of identical fermions, antisymmetry and orbital pairing belongs to Node 14.

8. The 3p example: draw every allowed state

Start from (n,l) = (3,1). The three choices of ml are −1, 0 and +1. Each choice is combined with two choices of ms, creating the six ordered four-number tuples: (3,1,−1,+½), (3,1,−1,−½), (3,1,0,+½), (3,1,0,−½), (3,1,+1,+½) and (3,1,+1,−½).

The diagram can be used to check the count without writing a formula: three boxes × two allowable spin labels = six. It cannot by itself tell us whether an atom with two p electrons chooses two separate boxes or pairs them. That energy and spin-ordering problem is reserved for Hund's rule in Node 15.

Counting errors often begin by answering a question about one level with a rule for another. “How many orbitals in 3d?” asks for 2l+1 = 5. “How many electrons in a complete 3d subshell?” asks for 2(2l+1) = 10. “How many orbitals in the n = 3 shell?” requires summing over all allowed l, not looking only at d.

10. How many subshells are permitted in shell n?

Once n is fixed, l runs through the integers 0, 1, 2, …, n−1. That gives n possible subshells in the conventional hydrogenic orbital family. For n = 1 there is 1s only; n = 2 permits 2s and 2p; n = 3 permits 3s, 3p and 3d; and n = 4 permits 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f.

A useful check is the constraint n ≥ l+1. If someone proposes 2d, d means l = 2 but n = 2 allows at most l = 1, so 2d is impossible. For 4f, l = 3 and n = 4 allows l up to 3, so 4f is permitted. Names such as 5g are mathematically allowed because l = 4 is permitted when n = 5.

Do not treat “the third shell includes 3d” as an instruction to fill 3d in a neutral atom before moving to any orbital with n = 4. The ordering of occupied ground-state orbitals in many-electron atoms depends on energies, not simply on these available labels.

11. Deriving n squared: every spatial orbital in a shell

To count the orbitals belonging to one principal shell, sum the number in each of its permitted subshells. As l moves from 0 to n−1, each subshell contributes 2l+1 orbitals.

Norbitals(n) = Σl=0n−1 (2l + 1)

Separate the sum into two familiar pieces. The sum of l from 0 through n−1 is n(n−1)/2, and there are n terms each contributing one. Therefore the orbital count is 2[n(n−1)/2] + n = n(n−1)+n = n². You can also see the pattern directly: the first n odd positive integers sum to n².

1 + 3 + 5 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n²
An exact discrete combinatorial identity, not an empirical approximation.

For n = 3: 1 (3s) + 3 (3p) + 5 (3d) = 9 orbitals. For n = 4: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16. These are counts of distinct spatial orbitals in the idealized one-electron shell classification.

12. Deriving 2n squared: maximum occupancy of a principal shell

Each of the n² spatial orbitals can be paired with two different electron-spin states. Hence the total number of one-electron spin-orbitals carrying principal label n is 2n². In the independent-orbital configuration model, Pauli exclusion makes this the maximum number of electrons that can occupy the shell's states.

Maximum electrons in shell n = 2 n²
A capacity, not a claim about which shells are filled in a real ground-state atom.

For n = 3, the limit is 2×3² = 18. For n = 4, it is 2×4² = 32. No factor of atomic number Z appears because Z changes the energy and radial behaviour of the orbitals, not these elementary allowed angular-momentum quantum-number counts.

Critical limitation: The formula 2n² is often misused as an automatic filling instruction. It says that the set of n-labelled orbitals has room for up to 2n² electrons; it does not say that n = 3 must be filled to eighteen before any electron can occupy n = 4.

14. What degeneracy does—and does not—mean

Two one-electron states are called degenerate if they have the same energy. In a simple nonrelativistic hydrogen-like Coulomb problem, energy is a function of n alone, so the different l and ml states with the same n have equal energy. But our n² counting is a count of allowed spatial states, even when interactions split their energies.

In many-electron atoms, shielding, penetration and electron–electron interactions separate the energies of subshells. A magnetic field can split states with different projections. Spin–orbit coupling further changes the convenient choice of quantum labels. None of these effects turns an otherwise allowed 3p projection into an impossible quantum number; they change energies and the accuracy of a particular basis, not the elementary combinatorial result within the model.

A textbook diagram with three equal-height p boxes is therefore often an assumed degenerate-sublevel illustration, useful for building Hund-type arguments. It is not a guarantee of exact degeneracy in every atom, molecule or external field.

15. Why electron capacity is not electron configuration

Imagine that a library has eighteen places for books on one shelf. That capacity does not tell you which shelf a librarian uses first. Similarly, n = 3 allows eighteen electrons in its s, p and d spatial orbitals combined, but the order of electrons in real multi-electron atoms depends on orbital energies and the many-electron problem.

The familiar chemical pattern that introduces 4s occupation before substantial 3d occupation in neutral potassium and calcium does not violate 2n². At such stages the third shell has not reached its full maximum. Later, when studying transition metals and cations, even the simple 4s/3d ordering will need context rather than a rigid absolute slogan. Those issues belong to Nodes 11–19.

Also, a complete subshell (for example 3p⁶) is not the same as a complete principal shell (which for n = 3 would include 3s², 3p⁶ and 3d¹⁰). The numbers 2, 8, 18 and 32 refer to ideal shell capacities; they are not universally observed valence-electron counts.

16. A complete enumeration of n = 2

The n = 2 shell has l = 0 or 1. For l = 0, ml = 0 and there are two spin labels, giving two 2s spin-orbitals. For l = 1, ml = −1, 0 or +1, each with two spin labels, giving six 2p spin-orbitals. Total = 8 possible one-electron states.

17. Advanced counting with fixed or partially specified labels

Many analytical examination problems hold one or two labels fixed. For example, if n = 4 and ml = 0, every allowed l = 0,1,2,3 permits that magnetic projection: four spatial orbitals. With both spin possibilities there are eight distinct one-electron tuples. This is not the same as asking for every orbital in n = 4, which would yield sixteen spatial orbitals.

For a fixed n and an integer projection ml = k, a compatible l must satisfy |k| ≤ l ≤ n−1. Provided |k| ≤ n−1, the count is n−|k| different spatial orbitals with that projection, or 2(n−|k|) spin-orbitals. If |k| ≥ n there are zero such states. This follows by enumerating l = |k|, |k|+1, …, n−1; no new quantum-mechanical assumption has been made.

Fixed n and fixed mₗ = k: number of orbitals = n − |k|
Only if k is an integer and |k| ≤ n−1. Multiply by 2 for both electron-spin projections.

A different restriction is fixed n and l but not ml: there are 2l+1 orbitals. Keeping the question's specified labels visible prevents accidental use of a formula for the wrong state space.

18. Why the count survives a change of spatial basis

A p subshell is often drawn using three real-valued orbitals called px, py and pz. Earlier we counted magnetic-projection eigenfunctions with ml = −1,0,+1. Are these two different sets of three orbitals? No. They are alternative choices of basis for the same three-dimensional orbital subspace. The common real px and py shapes are linear combinations of the ml = ±1 functions in the usual complex spherical-harmonic basis.

Changing the basis does not increase the dimension of the orbital space. You must not count px, py, pz in addition to ml = −1,0,+1. For the present calculation, “three possible p orbitals” means a three-dimensional spatial state space regardless of which orthonormal set of three functions is chosen.

This is also why a probability-cloud picture can be helpful for understanding the shape of an orbital but is not needed to count how many independent spatial orbital states exist.

19. A case study: why one impossible d label matters

A student is given 2d, 3d and 4d and asked which labels could describe spatial orbitals. The correct approach starts with d → l = 2. For 2d, the highest possible l is n−1 = 1, so 2d does not exist in the familiar hydrogenic labelling. For 3d and 4d, l = 2 is allowed. Each of those subshells has five ml options and ten electron-spin states.

It is tempting to write “d means ten electrons, therefore 2d can hold ten.” That is doubly mistaken. A capacity formula cannot license a nonexistent subshell, and a maximum of ten does not imply the subshell is actually occupied. The existence test always comes before the counting formula.

20. Biological relevance: what counting contributes to chemistry

Living organisms depend on the chemical differences among hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur, sodium, potassium and metal ions. These differences cannot be understood merely by knowing atomic masses. The availability of s, p and d states helps organize the periodic patterns and the possible electronic occupations from which bonding, reactivity and redox chemistry arise.

For example, oxygen's role in water and biomolecules ultimately depends on its electron structure, but the capacity of its 2p subshell alone does not predict the water bond angle or molecular polarity. Those require chemical bonding models. Similarly, understanding Fe-containing proteins benefits from recognizing that d orbitals exist and have five spatial components, but counting them alone does not determine ligand-field splitting, oxidation states or enzyme function.

The life-science link here is therefore structural preparation: knowing the legal state space keeps later explanations of valence, coordination chemistry and spectroscopy logically sound. Real biological observations provide rich applications, but their specific mechanisms are not derivable from 2l+1 or 2n² alone.

21. Scientific case: closed shells and chemical patterns

A completely occupied set of spatial orbitals paired with spin states is called closed in the simple configuration picture. For example, an s subshell has one spatial orbital and can accommodate two electrons; a p subshell has three spatial orbitals and can accommodate six. The presence or absence of open outer subshells helps organize familiar chemical trends.

However, closed-subshell counting does not independently prove noble-gas inertness, nor does it fully explain bonding. Energies, electrostatics, electron correlation and accessible reactions matter. Treat shell counting as a necessary organizational layer, not a complete theory of chemical behaviour.

22. Scientific case: magnetic projections and measured splitting

A measurement in a magnetic field can distinguish different angular-momentum projections, which helped make the idea of quantum labels physically meaningful. The existence of ml values −l to +l leads to the familiar multiplicity 2l+1, but the observed Zeeman pattern of a real spectral line also depends on spin, coupling and selection rules. In other words, counting the allowed projections is not the same calculation as predicting all observed spectral lines.

This distinction protects us against an overconfident inference: “five d orbitals” does not mean every d-related spectrum must show exactly five equally strong lines. That would ignore the particular transition, quantum coupling and experimental conditions.

23. Scientific case: when ideal orbital capacities are enough—and when they are not

In a teaching exercise, consider an isolated atom whose electron configuration is represented using orthonormal one-electron spatial orbitals. Asking the maximum occupation of a 4f subshell is then a clean counting problem: l = 3 gives seven orbitals, each paired with two spin states, for fourteen electrons. We can answer without a periodic table.

If the question instead asks which 4f states are occupied in a particular lanthanide ion, or how its spectrum changes in a protein binding site, the capacity of fourteen is only the opening constraint. The actual state involves electron count, interactions, symmetry, spin–orbit coupling and the surroundings. Good science begins by stating which question our simple model can answer.

24. Algorithmic counting and an independent check

An independent check of 2n² is to enumerate legal tuples with a four-level loop. For n = 4, take l = 0…3. For each l, take ml = −l…+l. For each resulting orbital, append the two spin values. Count the resulting distinct tuples: 2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32. The output agrees exactly with 2 × 4².

function countStates(n) {
  if (!Number.isInteger(n) || n < 1) throw Error("Invalid n");
  let spatialOrbitals = 0, spinOrbitals = 0;
  for (let l = 0; l < n; l++) {
    for (let ml = -l; ml <= l; ml++) {
      spatialOrbitals++;
      for (const ms of [-0.5, 0.5]) spinOrbitals++;
    }
  }
  return {spatialOrbitals, spinOrbitals};
}
// n = 4 → { spatialOrbitals: 16, spinOrbitals: 32 }

This is deliberately a small exact counting algorithm, not a numerical solution of an atomic wave equation. Its agreement with the analytic formula checks the arithmetic and logical enumeration. It does not tell us which states have the lowest energy in a real element.

25. Mathematical extensions: counting across several shells

If you are asked for the maximum number of electron states in all shells from n = 1 through n = N, the answer is the sum of their separate capacities: 2Σn=1Nn². This is a different question from the capacity of the Nth shell alone.

Σn=1N 2n² = N(N + 1)(2N + 1) / 3
Counts allowed spin-orbitals across the first N shells of the ideal classification.

For N = 3 the sum is 2 + 8 + 18 = 28; the formula gives 3×4×7/3 = 28. Do not infer from this cumulative number that a real atom with 28 electrons fills precisely the shells n = 1, 2 and 3 before using any n = 4 states. The combinatorial problem and the energetic filling problem are distinct.

Similarly, a fixed n shell with one restricted spin projection has n² one-electron states instead of 2n². If a test specifies spin-up only, the factor of two must not be introduced again.

26. Conceptual pitfalls before attempting problems

“A shell with n = 3 contains only three orbitals.” No. It contains three subshells, but nine spatial orbitals across them.
“The 3d subshell contains five electrons.” Five is its spatial orbital count; it can hold up to ten electrons.
“ml = 0 always means an s orbital.” No. Any permitted l has a zero projection.
“Two arrows mean one electron with two spins at once.” No. They represent two different permitted single-electron spin states, available for two electrons with different ms values.
“2n² predicts the ground-state order.” No. It counts available states, not orbital energies or occupation sequence.
“If a tuple has ms = +½ it must be valid.” No. n, l and ml must also obey their restrictions.
“The three px, py, pz functions are additional to ml = −1,0,+1.” No. Those are alternative bases of the same three-dimensional subspace.
“A half-integer l should be allowed because electron spin is half-integer.” No. Orbital angular momentum l is an integer for this atomic-orbital scheme; electron spin has its own independent quantum number.
“The entire 3d subshell has just one ml.” No. It has five distinct projection values.
“The capacity of 3p becomes different for different atoms.” In the standard orbital-label model its six spin-orbital states remain the same count, although their energies, occupation and wavefunctions may change.

27. Fully worked examples: from validity checks to advanced counts

Work in the order shown. The first group applies the restrictions; the middle group separates orbital count from electron capacity; the final group changes which labels are held fixed. Each solution states why its counting method is appropriate.

Worked example 1. Decide whether (1,0,0,+½) is allowed

Reasoned solution: For n = 1, the only l is 0; this forces mₗ = 0. The electron spin +½ is permitted. All four tests pass; it is an allowed 1s spin-orbital.

Worked example 2. Why is (1,1,0,−½) impossible?

Reasoned solution: n = 1 permits l = 0 only. The proposed l = 1 fails before we even inspect mₗ or spin. It is not a 1p state.

Worked example 3. Test (3,2,+2,−½)

Reasoned solution: n = 3 permits l = 2. For l = 2, mₗ may be −2 through +2 inclusive. Spin −½ is allowed. This is a legal 3d spin-orbital.

Worked example 4. Test (4,2,−3,+½)

Reasoned solution: A 4d subshell exists because 0 ≤ 2 ≤ 3. Yet its magnetic projections are only −2,−1,0,+1,+2. The −3 choice fails; the tuple is illegal.

Worked example 5. Test (4,3,0,0)

Reasoned solution: n = 4, l = 3 and mₗ = 0 all pass. Electron spin projection mₛ = 0 fails because only ±½ is allowed.

Worked example 6. Can a 3d subshell exist?

Reasoned solution: d means l = 2; n = 3 allows l = 0,1,2. Therefore 3d exists and carries mₗ = −2,−1,0,+1,+2.

Worked example 7. Can a 2d subshell exist?

Reasoned solution: d requires l = 2, whereas n = 2 permits l no greater than 1. The notation 2d is not an allowed orbital label.

Worked example 8. How many orbitals are in 3p?

Reasoned solution: For p, l = 1. Orbital count is 2l+1 = 3: mₗ = −1,0,+1. n = 3 identifies the shell but does not change that subshell count.

Worked example 9. How many electrons fit in 3p?

Reasoned solution: Three spatial orbitals each admit two distinct electron-spin states. Capacity = 2×3 = 6 electrons, not three.

Worked example 10. How many orbitals are in 4d?

Reasoned solution: d implies l = 2. Values −2 through +2 give five orbital projections. The capacity would be ten electrons, but the requested number of orbitals is five.

Worked example 11. How many electrons fit in 4f?

Reasoned solution: f means l = 3; 2l+1 = 7 orbitals. With two spin choices each, the maximum is 14 electrons.

Worked example 12. What about 5g?

Reasoned solution: g means l = 4. n = 5 allows l up to 4, so 5g exists in the orbital basis, with 9 spatial orbitals and 18 possible electrons.

Worked example 13. Count the n = 2 spatial orbitals

Reasoned solution: Allowed subshells: 2s has one orbital and 2p has three. Therefore 1+3 = 4 = n² spatial orbitals.

Worked example 14. Count the n = 2 electron capacity

Reasoned solution: Four spatial orbitals × two spin labels = 8 = 2×2² electrons maximum.

Worked example 15. Count n = 3 spatial orbitals by summation

Reasoned solution: l = 0,1,2 contributes 1+3+5 = 9 spatial orbitals. Check: n² = 3² = 9.

Worked example 16. Count n = 3 electron capacity by subshell

Reasoned solution: 3s can hold 2, 3p 6 and 3d 10. Sum 2+6+10 = 18, agreeing with 2n² = 18.

Worked example 17. Count n = 4 spatial orbitals

Reasoned solution: l = 0,1,2,3 contributes 1+3+5+7 = 16 orbitals. The square rule gives 4² = 16.

Worked example 18. Count n = 4 maximum electrons

Reasoned solution: Four-shell capacity is 2×4² = 32 electrons. The calculation does not say how many are actually present in a particular atom.

Worked example 19. A shell has capacity 50: find n

Reasoned solution: Solve 2n² = 50, so n² = 25 and n = 5 (positive integer). It is the fifth principal shell.

Worked example 20. A shell has capacity 20: can it exist?

Reasoned solution: 2n² = 20 would give n² = 10, not a positive integer square. No entire principal shell has maximum capacity 20 in this model; a subshell or partial occupancy is a different question.

Worked example 21. Count the states with n = 3, l = 2, mₗ = 0

Reasoned solution: All three spatial labels are fixed, so this is one spatial orbital. With mₛ = +½ or −½, there are exactly two one-electron states.

Worked example 22. Count states with n = 4 and mₗ = 0

Reasoned solution: Every allowed l = 0,1,2,3 admits mₗ = 0: four spatial orbitals, each with two spin possibilities. Total eight spin-orbitals.

Worked example 23. Count states with n = 4 and mₗ = +2

Reasoned solution: Only l = 2 and l = 3 permit +2 within n = 4. There are two spatial orbitals and four spin-orbitals.

Worked example 24. Count states with n = 5 and mₗ = −3

Reasoned solution: Compatible l values are 3 or 4 (two choices). Thus two spatial orbitals × two spins = four one-electron states.

Worked example 25. Count states with n = 5 and mₛ = +½

Reasoned solution: Fixing spin leaves one option per spatial orbital. There are n² = 25 spatial orbitals, hence 25 one-electron states with mₛ = +½.

Worked example 26. How many orbitals have n = 3 and l ≤ 1?

Reasoned solution: Keep 3s and 3p but exclude 3d. Their orbital counts are 1 + 3 = 4; capacity is eight electrons.

Worked example 27. How many electron states exist in shells 1 through 3?

Reasoned solution: Add distinct shell capacities: 2 + 8 + 18 = 28. This cumulative total is not the capacity of shell n = 3, which is only 18.

Worked example 28. How many choices of mₗ exist for l = 5?

Reasoned solution: From −5 to +5 inclusive there are 2×5+1 = 11 magnetic projections. Such a subshell requires n ≥ 6.

Worked example 29. Which shell first permits an f orbital?

Reasoned solution: f requires l = 3; since l ≤ n−1, the smallest possible n is 4. Thus 4f exists, while 3f does not.

Worked example 30. Does an empty 4d orbital cease to be allowed?

Reasoned solution: No. ‘Allowed state’ means the orbital belongs to the permitted basis. Occupation zero is possible. Capacity and actual electron population are separate properties.

Worked example 31. What does the formula 2(2l+1) count if l = 0?

Reasoned solution: It gives 2(1) = 2 spin-orbitals in any allowed s subshell. A single spatial s orbital offers two spin projections.

Worked example 32. Fixed n = 6 and mₗ = −2: how many?

Reasoned solution: l may be 2,3,4,5: four spatial orbitals, or eight spin-orbitals. General check: 2[n−|mₗ|] = 2(6−2) = 8.

Independent numerical checks: 1+3+5 = 9; 1+3+5+7 = 16; 2+6+10 = 18; 2+6+10+14 = 32; 2×5² = 50. All are exact integer counts with no physical units.

28. Original CSIR-NET / SET-style questions with explanations

These are original practice questions written for this lesson, not claimed previous-year questions. Attempt the question before reading its answer. The explanations identify the counting rule or invalid restriction, not just the answer letter.

Practice question 1

Which tuple is legal? (A) (2,2,0,+½) (B) (2,1,−1,−½) (C) (0,0,0,+½) (D) (2,1,2,+½)

Correct answer: B. For n=2, only l=0 or 1 is possible; l=1 allows mₗ=−1,0,+1. A violates l; C violates n; D violates mₗ.

Practice question 2

The 4p subshell contains how many spatial orbitals? (A) 4 (B) 6 (C) 3 (D) 8

Correct answer: C. p means l=1, so 2l+1=3, regardless of the particular permitted n.

Practice question 3

Maximum electrons in 4p? (A) 2 (B) 6 (C) 8 (D) 14

Correct answer: B. Three p orbitals times two spin states each gives six.

Practice question 4

How many subshells does n=4 allow? (A) 4 (B) 8 (C) 16 (D) 32

Correct answer: A. l = 0,1,2,3 gives four subshells; 16 is the total number of spatial orbitals.

Practice question 5

How many spatial orbitals belong to n=4? (A) 4 (B) 8 (C) 16 (D) 32

Correct answer: C. The sum 1+3+5+7 = 16 = n².

Practice question 6

Which is impossible? (A) 4f (B) 5g (C) 3d (D) 3f

Correct answer: D. 3f requires l=3, but n=3 permits only l≤2.

Practice question 7

For l=2, mₗ has which set? (A) 0,1,2 (B) −2,−1,0,1,2 (C) ±½ (D) −3 to +3

Correct answer: B. A magnetic projection includes all integers from −l to +l; spin is a separate quantum number.

Practice question 8

If l=3, number of orbital projections? (A) 3 (B) 6 (C) 7 (D) 14

Correct answer: C. 2l+1 = 7; fourteen is the maximum electron capacity of an f subshell.

Practice question 9

In the familiar electron notation, mₛ is (A) any integer (B) 0 or 1 (C) ±½ (D) any real number

Correct answer: C. Electron spin is 1/2, and a chosen-axis spin projection has exactly two eigenvalues.

Practice question 10

Which value of mₗ can occur in any permitted subshell? (A) +4 (B) −3 (C) 0 (D) +½

Correct answer: C. All integer l≥0 allow mₗ=0. The other integers require sufficiently large l; +½ is never mₗ.

Practice question 11

How many electron states share n=3, l=1, mₗ=+1? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 6

Correct answer: B. The spatial orbital is fixed and the two allowed electron-spin projections remain.

Practice question 12

For n=4, mₗ=0, how many spatial orbitals? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 4 (D) 16

Correct answer: C. There is one such orbital in each permitted l=0,1,2,3 subshell.

Practice question 13

For n=4, mₗ=+3, how many spin-orbitals? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 4 (D) 8

Correct answer: B. Only l=3 is compatible. One spatial orbital has two spin states.

Practice question 14

Which mathematical statement is correct? (A) 2l+1 subshells in shell n (B) n² subshells (C) n subshells (D) l² orbitals per subshell

Correct answer: C. There are exactly n allowed l values in shell n. The orbital count is 2l+1 per subshell and n² across the shell.

Practice question 15

The capacity of the n=3 shell is (A) 3 (B) 6 (C) 9 (D) 18

Correct answer: D. 2n² = 2×9 =18. Nine is its spatial orbital count.

Practice question 16

The capacity of 5f is (A) 5 (B) 7 (C) 14 (D) 50

Correct answer: C. f means l=3, yielding seven orbitals and fourteen spin-orbitals. Fifty is the whole n=5 shell capacity.

Practice question 17

Assertion: all n=3 states must be occupied before any n=4 state. Reason: 2n² counts capacity. (A) both true (B) assertion false, reason true (C) assertion true, reason false (D) both false

Correct answer: B. The capacity formula is correct, but actual energetic orbital filling in many-electron atoms does not follow strict shell completion.

Practice question 18

Assertion: (3,1,0,+½) and (3,1,0,−½) share a spatial orbital. Reason: n,l,mₗ are the same. (A) both true with correct explanation (B) both true, unrelated (C) assertion false (D) reason false

Correct answer: A. The three orbital labels coincide, while the electron-spin projection differs.

Practice question 19

Which single change fixes (3,2,+3,−½)? (A) n→2 (B) l→1 (C) mₗ→+2 (D) mₛ→0

Correct answer: C. For l=2, mₗ must lie from −2 through +2. Changing +3 to +2 creates a valid tuple.

Practice question 20

Number of spatial orbitals for l=4 is (A) 4 (B) 5 (C) 9 (D) 18

Correct answer: C. 2l+1=9, provided n≥5; eighteen would count spin-orbitals.

Practice question 21

The first principal shell that permits g is (A) 3 (B) 4 (C) 5 (D) 6

Correct answer: C. g corresponds to l=4 and requires n≥l+1=5.

Practice question 22

If a problem fixes mₛ=+½ and n=2, how many one-electron states remain? (A) 2 (B) 4 (C) 8 (D) 16

Correct answer: B. There are n²=4 spatial orbitals and only one spin projection is allowed by the question.

Practice question 23

If a 3p subshell is empty, the number of allowed spatial orbitals is (A) 0 (B) 1 (C) 3 (D) 6

Correct answer: C. Allowed state count does not depend on occupation; 3p still denotes three spatial orbitals.

Practice question 24

Is pₓ an extra fourth p orbital beyond mₗ=−1,0,+1? (A) yes (B) no (C) only in ions (D) only for n=2

Correct answer: B. Real pₓ, pᵧ, p_z functions are an alternative orbital basis of the same three-dimensional space.

Practice question 25

If 2n² = 32, n equals (A) 2 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 8

Correct answer: C. n²=16, and n is positive, so n=4.

Practice question 26

For fixed n=5 and mₗ=+2, how many spatial orbitals? (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 5

Correct answer: C. Compatible l=2,3,4 gives three spatial orbitals.

Practice question 27

Which formula counts the orbitals in one d subshell? (A) n² (B) 2n² (C) 2l+1 with l=2 (D) n−1

Correct answer: C. Only the angular-momentum l value controls the mₗ multiplicity of a fixed subshell.

Practice question 28

How many electron states exist across n=1 and n=2 shells together? (A) 4 (B) 8 (C) 10 (D) 18

Correct answer: C. 2×1² + 2×2² = 2+8 =10 possible spin-orbitals.

Practice question 29

For n=3, which condition on l is valid? (A) −3≤l≤3 (B) l∈{0,1,2} (C) l∈{1,2,3} (D) l=½

Correct answer: B. l is a nonnegative integer no greater than n−1=2.

Practice question 30

How many electrons can occupy one spatial orbital in the standard Pauli model? (A) 1 only (B) 2 at most (C) 2l+1 (D) n²

Correct answer: B. There are two different electron-spin projections; Pauli excludes duplicate occupation of an identical spin-orbital.

29. Scientific and life-science case studies

The following cases deliberately distinguish a conclusion that state counting supports from a further conclusion requiring energy, bonding or spectroscopic theory.

Case 1: A 3p orbital diagram in a student's notebook

The student draws three boxes but inserts a third arrow into the first box. Counting tells us each spatial orbital has only two electron-spin labels, so that third arrow cannot represent a third electron in the same orbital within the single-determinant configuration model. The correction is to distribute electrons into available distinct spin-orbitals; deciding the preferred distribution among equal-energy boxes additionally requires Hund's rule.

Case 2: An invalid tuple on an admissions mock test

A tuple (4,1,−2,+½) looks plausible because −2 is a familiar magnetic number, but for l=1 the range is −1 through +1. The problem tests ordered dependence, not memory of all magnetic values encountered in chemistry. Checking l before mₗ resolves it.

Case 3: A spectroscopic table lists 3d states

Five magnetic-projection labels exist for 3d, but that does not mean five identical observed peaks in a complex real-atom spectrum. Measured transitions depend on coupled states and selection rules. The orbital count supplies basis dimension, not a complete line-intensity model.

Case 4: A claim about potassium filling

An explanation says that 3d must be full before 4s because the 3rd shell's capacity is eighteen. This confuses state capacity with orbital-energy order. Potassium's neutral ground-state configuration is conventionally written [Ar]4s¹; that fact belongs to the energy and Aufbau lessons, and it does not invalidate n=3's 18-state maximum.

Case 5: Iron at the heart of a biological cofactor

The five possible d orbital spatial states are a crucial starting point for later understanding metal coordination. Yet oxygen binding to haem proteins or redox behaviour cannot be predicted from five alone. Ligand fields, charge, spin states and surrounding protein chemistry alter the actual outcome.

Case 6: Constrained quantum-number enumeration in a computation

A simple loop over n,l,mₗ,mₛ correctly produces sixteen spatial orbitals and thirty-two spin-orbitals for n=4. If a programmer accidentally loops mₗ from 0 to +l, the count is too small. Enumerating both positive and negative projections and counting zero once repairs the error.

Case 7: Comparing two seemingly different pictures of p orbitals

One diagram labels p orbitals by mₗ; another depicts three Cartesian lobed patterns. They seem to show six orbitals in total, but each set is a different basis for the same three-dimensional space. A change of basis does not create extra allowed states.

Case 8: Magnetic field and state energies

An external magnetic field may separate energies among projection-labelled states. Students sometimes conclude that degeneracy breaking changes how many states exist. It changes their energies, not their count in the chosen model. Spectral interpretations require additional information about actual transitions.

30. Integrative challenges: reason before calculating

These problems combine more than one idea. Hide the solutions on a sheet of paper and attempt each question with your own reasoning first; then compare both the number and the scope statement.

Challenge 1: Mixed validity and counting

Task: Consider n=4, l=2. List all mₗ and determine how many four-number tuples exist with this pair.

Worked reasoning: The mₗ values are −2,−1,0,+1,+2: five spatial orbitals. Each has two mₛ values, so there are ten valid tuples. No extra factor of n is needed because n and l are fixed.

Challenge 2: Partial-shell puzzle

Task: Someone says n=3 has three orbitals and nine spin-orbitals. Identify both mistakes.

Worked reasoning: Three is the count of subshells, not orbitals. The orbital count is 1+3+5=9, and each orbital has two spin states, giving eighteen spin-orbitals.

Challenge 3: A stricter magnetic-projection condition

Task: At n=5, count the spin-orbitals with mₗ either +3 or −3.

Worked reasoning: For each chosen mₗ, l may be 3 or 4: two spatial orbitals. Both +3 and −3 contribute two each, giving four spatial orbitals and eight spin-orbitals.

Challenge 4: Fixed spin and bounded l

Task: At n=4, count all spin-orbitals with mₛ=−½ and l ≤ 1.

Worked reasoning: l=0 contributes one orbital; l=1 contributes three; fixed spin gives one state per spatial orbital. Total four.

Challenge 5: Prove the square rule

Task: Without memorizing the result, derive the total number of spatial orbitals in shell n.

Worked reasoning: Sum (2l+1) for l=0 through n−1. This is 2×[n(n−1)/2]+n = n². The proof counts angular projections, not occupancy.

Challenge 6: Explain the Pauli step

Task: Why cannot one conclude electron capacity solely from the formula 2l+1?

Worked reasoning: That formula counts spatial orbitals. Each allows two electron spin states, and the maximum two-electron occupancy per spatial orbital requires Pauli exclusion for identical fermions. Thus capacity is 2(2l+1).

Challenge 7: Cumulative count with an interpretation trap

Task: Find the total capacity of n=1,2,3,4 and explain what the result does not imply.

Worked reasoning: Add 2+8+18+32=60, or use N(N+1)(2N+1)/3 at N=4. The value does not prescribe actual ground-state shell filling of a sixty-electron atom.

Challenge 8: Find the allowed n

Task: A hypothetical subshell has eleven spatial orbitals. Determine l and the lowest possible n.

Worked reasoning: 2l+1=11 means l=5; the earliest permitted principal shell is n=l+1=6. For electron capacity, double eleven to twenty-two.

Challenge 9: A basis-choice trap

Task: A lecturer draws pₓ,pᵧ,p_z and also labels three mₗ eigenfunctions. Are there six independent p spatial orbitals?

Worked reasoning: No. Both are bases for one three-dimensional p subspace. In the usual central-potential model, one p subshell has exactly three spatial orbitals.

Challenge 10: Quantum tuple diagnosis

Task: Assess (5,4,−4,+½), (5,4,+5,−½) and (5,5,0,+½).

Worked reasoning: The first is allowed: l=4 and |mₗ|≤4. The second fails because +5 exceeds l=4. The third fails because n=5 permits l only up to 4.

31. Revision: a method rather than a memory trick

When you face an unfamiliar quantum-number problem, ask in sequence: (1) which labels are already fixed? (2) are those labels allowed? (3) am I counting subshells, spatial orbitals or one-electron spin-orbitals? (4) has the question fixed or excluded a spin projection? (5) is it asking about capacity or actual occupation?

Conceptual consolidation: The allowed quantum numbers define a state space. Orbital counts are its dimensions. Spin doubles the one-electron state count, and Pauli limits the number of electrons occupying those distinct states. None of this alone determines which states are populated in a particular atom.
