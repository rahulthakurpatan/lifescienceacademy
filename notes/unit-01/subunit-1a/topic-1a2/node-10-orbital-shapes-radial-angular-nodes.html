Unit 01 · Sub-unit 1.A · Topic 1.A.2 · Node 10

Orbital Shapes and Nodes: s, p, d and f

A chronological journey from wavefunctions and probability clouds to real orbital geometry, radial nodes, angular nodes and the meaning of scientific orbital diagrams.

Learning outcomes: Explain spatial versus radial probability, visualize real s/p/d/f orbitals without treating them as hard bodies, determine radial and angular nodes, interpret wavefunction phase, read scientifically labelled orbital plots and solve conceptual and numerical orbital problems.

Contents · 1.A.2.10

1. The mystery that an orbital picture must solve

Imagine a chemist asking a beginner to draw an electron in an atom. The beginner draws a dot traveling around the nucleus like a planet. That drawing is attractive because it is familiar, but it introduces an important problem: it promises an exact path for a particle whose quantum state does not give us one. The earlier Academy chapters showed why experiments on spectra, matter waves, uncertainty and quantum numbers forced physicists to abandon that simple picture. Now we face a new question. If an electron does not follow a sharp orbit, what exactly is the familiar round s picture, the two-lobed p picture, or the four-lobed d picture?

These pictures are ways of displaying spatial probability information from a mathematical wavefunction. The picture changes depending on what quantity we plot: the signed wavefunction, its squared magnitude, a slice of three-dimensional density, or a boundary surface chosen to enclose a stated probability. One drawing can be useful without being a literal photograph of an electron. This distinction will guide every section.

The chapter continues logically from Node 09. There we counted one, three, five and seven spatial orbitals in s, p, d and f subshells. Here we ask what the individual orbitals look like, why certain regions have zero probability density, and how orbital pictures help us prepare for chemistry and biology without replacing the complete many-electron theory.

2. What was missing from Bohr's planetary picture?

Bohr's quantized orbits explained selected hydrogen spectral energies, but an orbit does not describe the probability of detecting an electron at different positions around the nucleus. As wave mechanics developed in the 1920s, the relevant mathematical problem shifted: find permissible wavefunctions under the nuclear electric attraction, subject to normalization and suitable boundary conditions. The resulting stationary states give distributions in space rather than a collection of sharply drawn tracks.

This change did not mean that an electron became a mist of ordinary charged matter. The wavefunction is a complex-valued probability amplitude; a position measurement has a distribution given by its modulus squared. A simplified cloud picture represents this statistical distribution. For a single electron in a stationary bound state, its probability distribution does not have to rotate like a rigid object.

Hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions are our clean teaching examples because their one-electron Coulomb problem admits exact analytical orbitals. When we later consider carbon, oxygen, iron or a metal in a protein, the same orbital language remains useful, but electron–electron interactions and chemical surroundings make literal hydrogenic formulas an approximation, not a universal law.

3. How the spatial probability rule answers the new question

Suppose an electron is described by a normalized wavefunction ψ(x,y,z). The probability of detecting it inside a tiny volume dV near a chosen point is |ψ|²dV. The absolute-square operation removes the phase of the amplitude and produces a non-negative density. The wavefunction itself can be positive, negative or complex; the probability density cannot be negative.

Probability in a small volume: dP = |ψ(x,y,z)|² dV
For a normalized single-electron spatial state; |ψ|² has SI units m⁻³.

Normalization requires that the probability over all space be one, not ninety percent or merely the part enclosed in a convenient drawing. If we choose to display only the region containing ninety percent of the total probability, ten percent still lies outside that chosen region. Thus a boundary surface is not a solid wall. It is a communication device for a continuous probability distribution.

A wavefunction can also have a sign or phase difference between neighboring regions. That sign is invisible to |ψ|² but profoundly matters when wavefunctions combine during chemical bonding. We will use two colours in some diagrams to distinguish wavefunction phase; they are not electric charges and they do not mean that probability is negative.

4. The coordinate system that makes atomic orbitals understandable

An isolated Coulomb attraction is centred on the nucleus, so spherical coordinates are more natural than ordinary horizontal and vertical graph paper. Position is specified by distance r from the nucleus, polar angle θ measured from the chosen z axis, and azimuthal angle φ about that axis. One can picture θ as the tilt from the vertical axis and φ as the direction of rotation around it.

The word radial means changing the distance r. The word angular means changing a direction while holding the radius fixed. That division is more than vocabulary; it is reflected in the separable solutions of the hydrogenic Schrödinger equation. It also explains why two different kinds of nodes can appear: a spherical surface at a special radius, or a direction-dependent surface on which the wavefunction vanishes.

dV = r² sinθ dr dθ dφ
Spherical-coordinate volume element; the r² term is essential when calculating radial probabilities.

A thin shell at a larger radius contains much more volume than one of the same thickness near the origin. If a problem asks for the probability of finding an electron between r and r + dr, its answer must account for that shell volume. If it asks for density at one point, we do not insert the same volume factor.

5. How the hydrogenic wavefunction separates into two stories

The hydrogenic bound-state wavefunction can be expressed as a product of a radial function Rnl(r) and an angular function Ylm(θ,φ). The quantum number n influences the radial structure; l affects both radial and angular structure; ml selects an angular basis function. Spin is an additional degree of freedom and is not the cause of the spatial lobes in these diagrams.

ψnlm(r,θ,φ) = Rnl(r) × Ylm(θ,φ)
Standard normalized hydrogenic central-Coulomb eigenfunctions; the position distribution is |R|²|Y|².

This equation provides a useful mental experiment. Keep Y fixed while changing R: the orientation and angular pattern stay related, while the radial distribution may grow or develop additional spherical nodes. Keep R's angular-momentum family fixed while changing the angular basis: the directional structure changes without altering the count of states in that subshell.

The angular factor is described mathematically by spherical harmonics; the radial factor involves polynomials multiplied by an exponentially decaying envelope for bound states. A complete general derivation requires solving a differential equation, which belongs to advanced quantum mechanics, but we can use the solution's structure to make reliable predictions and test numerical examples.

6. Three pictures, three different scientific meanings

A wavefunction or phase picture distinguishes the sign or complex phase of ψ in different regions. It is particularly useful for understanding nodes and how orbitals overlap. A density picture plots |ψ|² and shows relative probability per unit volume. A boundary surface selects an illustrative contour or a volume intended to contain a chosen fraction of probability. These are not equivalent pictures, even when they are printed next to each other in a textbook.

A two-dimensional contour map is a slice through a three-dimensional density. One must know which plane was cut. A nodal plane may appear as a straight line in the slice, while the actual nodal set extends in three dimensions. A 3D representation often uses opacity or an isosurface to make the shape easier to inspect. Details can disappear because of projection, not because the corresponding part of the wavefunction does not exist.

Important picture-reading rule: red and blue lobes usually distinguish opposite wavefunction phases, not positive and negative probabilities. Both phase regions have non-negative |ψ|². Do not call a drawn boundary the limit beyond which an electron is forbidden.

7. Read a p orbital in three visual languages

Before learning any individual orbital shape, compare the three images below. In the first, the two lobes are assigned opposite phases; the white middle line marks a nodal plane in a cross-section. In the second, squaring the amplitude leaves two non-negative density lobes and a central region of zero probability. In the third, a boundary is drawn at a selected illustrative contour.

The central white region represents zero probability density exactly on the nodal plane in the idealized orbital. It should not be imagined as a thick empty slab: an ideal mathematical plane has zero thickness. Most real measurements sample finite regions, and the integrated probability within a narrow slab can be small rather than absolutely zero.

8. What a node actually is

A node is a location or set of locations where ψ = 0, so |ψ|² is also zero. Nodes are features of particular quantum states, not holes torn out of a material cloud. A radial node occurs at a nonzero radius at which Rnl(r) vanishes, typically forming a spherical nodal surface for a hydrogenic orbital. An angular node arises from the angular factor vanishing along particular directions; conventional real orbitals commonly have nodal planes, cones or combinations of such surfaces.

A node is not simply any place the density is extremely small. Hydrogenic bound-state densities decay toward zero at large distance, but the distant tail is not a finite-radius nodal surface merely because it is difficult to draw. Likewise the origin may be a zero of a p or d wavefunction; the textbook formula radial nodes = n−l−1 counts positive-radius radial nodes, not every zero at r = 0.

Nodes are important because they connect mathematical sign changes and angular structure to observable consequences of wavefunctions. They are part of the reason orbital overlap and electronic transitions cannot be understood by treating the orbitals as solid geometric balloons.

9. Why there are radial and angular nodes

Because the wavefunction is the product R × Y, it can vanish when the radial factor is zero or when the angular factor is zero. This provides the conceptual origin of the two kinds of nodal surfaces. The exact positions of the radial nodes depend on the particular radial solution and its length scale; the angular nodes reflect the angular-momentum character and choice of basis.

Radial nodes = n − l − 1
Angular nodes = l
Total = n − 1
Standard hydrogenic orbital-node rule, in the conventional real-orbital/angular-node description.

The total follows by addition: (n−l−1)+l = n−1. This elegant result also helps diagnose impossible labels. A 2d orbital would require l = 2 while n = 2, giving a nonsensical negative radial-node count; the correct approach is to reject the orbital first because l ≤ n−1 must hold.

Advanced caveat: counting angular nodal surfaces is commonly taught using real-valued p, d and f orbital combinations. Complex ml eigenfunctions can display angular zeros with different geometric descriptions, including zeros along axes; one should not naively count visible planes in every plotted complex spherical harmonic. The standard count l is a useful hydrogenic textbook classification under its stated convention.

10. Deriving the total-node pattern from permitted quantum numbers

The first principal shell, n = 1, admits l = 0 only. Thus a 1s orbital has zero radial and zero angular nodes. In the second shell, l can be 0 or 1: 2s has one radial node, whereas 2p has one angular node. Both total one. In the third shell, 3s has two radial nodes, 3p has one radial plus one angular node, and 3d has two angular nodes. Every orbital in a fixed n has the same standard total-node count n−1, but the partition between radial and angular nodes changes with l.

This is why saying “a 3d orbital has two nodes” is incomplete: in the simplest 3d hydrogenic state those two are angular, not radial. Similarly, drawing a 3s orbital as a plain filled sphere hides its two spherical nodal surfaces and the phase changes between successive radial regions.

The simplest table is not merely a memory chart; it follows from restricting l to 0…n−1, splitting the radial and angular factors, and recognizing that l determines the angular-node count in the standard real basis.

11. A first four-orbital comparison

The next illustration uses coloured regions to highlight sign or phase rather than electron charge. A 1s orbital has no nodes. A 2s orbital has one spherical nodal surface; in a two-dimensional cut, a sphere becomes a circle. A 2p real orbital has one plane of zero amplitude through the nucleus; viewed in a suitable cut, it becomes a line. A representative real 3dxy orbital has two orthogonal nodal planes and four lobes.

Notice that the 2s inner and outer phase regions are parts of one orbital. Similarly, the 3d four lobes all belong to one spatial orbital. The number of visible lobes must not be used to recount the number of orbitals in a subshell.

12. The simplest orbital, 1s, begins with spherical symmetry

The 1s state has n = 1 and l = 0, so its angular function is constant with direction: there is no preferred axis. Its electron probability density is spherically symmetric. In hydrogen, the normalized spatial wavefunction has an exponential radial dependence, ψ100 ∝ exp(−r/a0). This is an amplitude, not a graph of an orbit.

ψ1s(r) = (1/√πa₀³) e−r/a₀
Nonrelativistic hydrogen atom, Z = 1; a₀ ≈ 0.0529177 nm is the Bohr radius.

At r = 0, ψ is finite, so the spatial probability density |ψ|² is maximum at the nucleus. As r increases, the density decreases smoothly but never becomes exactly zero at any finite nonnegative radius. The familiar sphere in a picture is thus not the radius of a precise electronic shell: it is a chosen region to display a diffuse, unbounded distribution.

The same general spherical angular symmetry characterizes 2s, 3s and higher s orbitals, but those radial functions have nodes and changes of phase. “All s orbitals are spherical” is correct for their angular symmetry; “all s orbitals have the same probability distribution” is not.

13. A surprising comparison: density at a point versus probability in a shell

Here is a puzzle worth resolving slowly. The 1s density is largest at the nucleus, yet many books say the most probable radius is approximately a0. How can both be true? The two statements concern different quantities. One asks for density per unit volume at a location. The other asks for the probability in a thin spherical shell containing all directions at a given radius. A shell farther out contains more volume.

When the radial and angular functions are normalized independently, the probability between r and r+dr is P(r)dr with P(r)=r²|R(r)|². For 1s hydrogen, P(r)=4r²a0−3exp(−2r/a0). The r² term makes the shell probability density zero at r=0 even though the point probability density is greatest there.

P(r) = r² |Rnl(r)|²
P1s(r) = 4r²/a₀³ · e−2r/a₀
P(r) has units length⁻¹; P(r) dr is a dimensionless probability.

Differentiating the 1s radial distribution, or comparing its increase near the origin with its exponential decrease far away, shows that its maximum occurs at r=a0. There is no contradiction. The nucleus maximizes local density, while the shell near a0 maximizes probability per equal radial thickness.

14. The exact hydrogen 1s probability in a finite sphere

Because electron clouds have no sharp boundary, we can ask a better numerical question: what fraction of the 1s electron probability lies within a sphere of radius R? Integrate the radial distribution from zero to R. For u=R/a0, the result is F(u)=1−e−2u(1+2u+2u²). This is a cumulative probability between zero and one.

F(R) = 1 − e−2R/a₀ [1 + 2R/a₀ + 2(R/a₀)²]
Hydrogen 1s state; R ≥ 0, dimensionless exponential argument.

For R=a0, F=1−5e−2 ≈ 0.3233. So roughly 32.3% of the probability lies inside one Bohr radius, even though that radius is where the radial distribution P(r) reaches its peak. Finding a sphere containing 90% of the probability gives R≈2.66a0 by numerical solution, not because the wavefunction vanishes there. About 10% lies outside. The exact percentage matters because boundary-surface conventions differ and must not be silently mixed with an arbitrary equal-density contour.

A useful scientific habit is to specify whether a diagram means “points of constant density” or “surface enclosing a chosen fraction of total probability.” Those are distinct constructions, even if both happen to look like spheres for an s state.

15. Three real radial distributions instead of three decorative clouds

For hydrogen, the 1s, 2s and 2p radial probability distributions can be compared on the same axes using analytic functions, without pretending that these are all the same kind of orbital shape. The graph below plots probability per radial interval as a function of radius in units of a0. It is not a graph of the value of ψ at a specific angle.

The 1s peak is at a0. The 2p distribution, proportional to r⁴exp(−r/a0), peaks at 4a0. The 2s distribution has two maxima separated by an exact radial node at 2a0. This is a concrete reason why a single spherical cartoon cannot communicate all radial information for excited orbitals.

16. The 2s orbital: discovering a spherical node

Move from 1s to the first excited s state in hydrogen. The label 2s has n=2, l=0. It remains spherically symmetric, yet the radial function is no longer a simple exponential. It contains the factor (2−r/a0). This factor is positive inside r=2a0, zero at that radius and negative outside.

R20(r) ∝ (2 − r/a₀) e−r/(2a₀)
Hydrogen Z=1; the omitted normalization factor does not change nodal locations.

The surface r=2a0 is a radial node. Because it has the same radius in all directions, it is a sphere in three dimensions and a circle in an ordinary planar slice. The inner and outer regions have opposite signs of ψ; both nevertheless contribute positive probability density after squaring. A beginner might see two coloured rings in a section and count two orbitals. There is only one 2s spatial orbital with one radial node.

At the node itself the probability density is zero. A small finite-thickness spherical shell centred on the node still has a nonzero integrated probability except in the infinitesimal limit. This careful wording separates a mathematical zero surface from the physical volume of a measurement bin.

17. Why 2s has two radial probability peaks

Insert R20 into P(r)=r²|R|². Ignoring a positive normalization constant, P2s(r) is proportional to r²(2−r/a0)²exp(−r/a0). At r=2a0 the squared polynomial vanishes; near the nucleus the factor r² makes P(r) small; far from the nucleus the exponential suppresses it again. Two probability peaks must appear between these constraints.

If u=r/a0, differentiating u²(2−u)²e−u gives stationary positive radii at u=3±√5, in addition to the zero at u=2. The two radial maxima are therefore approximately 0.764a0 and 5.236a0. The inner peak is not evidence for an independent inner electron; it is a second region of radial probability for the same orbital state.

This example shows why the location of a radial node and the location of a radial probability maximum are different mathematical features. Memorizing “2s has one node” is useful; explaining the two maxima from a polynomial multiplied by a decaying exponential shows genuine understanding.

18. The 3s orbital: two radial nodes, three phase regions

Once the 2s pattern is understood, 3s becomes a natural next chapter within the same story. With n=3 and l=0, the radial-node rule predicts n−l−1=2 radial nodes. The hydrogenic radial polynomial now has two positive roots, leading to three successive radial phase regions rather than two.

R30(r) ∝ [27 − 18u + 2u²] e−u/3, with u=r/a₀
Hydrogen Z=1; radial node radii u=(9±3√3)/2 ≈ 1.902 and 7.098.

The alternating signs of the radial wavefunction can be represented as nested spherical regions separated by two nodal spheres. The actual density is not uniform within any one region. A figure showing three solid rings is therefore a phase illustration, not a graph of constant probability density. The same reasoning extends to 4s, which has three radial nodes, and in the hydrogenic family to ns with n−1 radial nodes.

A useful check against over-generalization: a 3s orbital in a many-electron atom is not guaranteed to have exactly the same numerical nodal radii as hydrogen. The n−l−1 rule comes from the hydrogenic Coulomb model; in central-field approximations for multielectron atoms, radial functions and nodal patterns depend on the effective potential and on how orbitals are defined.

19. Why all s orbitals are spherical but not all identical

The letter s tells us l=0, so the angular factor has no directional dependence. That is the reason 1s, 2s and 3s are all spherically symmetric. It does not imply that all are uniform balls, identical-sized spheres, or that higher ns orbitals must have all probability outside lower ns orbitals. Their radial functions can overlap substantially, and higher ns states may have inner density regions as well as broad outer regions.

One can compare the distribution of point density and the radial shell probability for each ns state, but it is essential to say which is being compared. An s electron can have nonzero probability density at the nucleus in the nonrelativistic hydrogenic model. Other l values have a wavefunction that vanishes at r=0 because their regular radial functions behave as rl near the origin. This is an important qualitative reason the near-nuclear behaviour of s and non-s orbitals differs.

The chemical idea of orbital penetration and its effects on subshell energies becomes a major topic later in the Academy; this lesson establishes the geometric prerequisite without prematurely turning orbital drawings into a many-electron energy-ordering rule.

20. Why a p orbital has two lobes, not two orbitals

With l=1, the angular wavefunction is directional. Choose the familiar real pz orbital. Its angular factor is proportional to cosθ, so it is positive for directions with z>0, zero in the xy plane (θ=π/2), and negative for z<0. Multiplying this angular function by the radial factor gives two opposing phase regions oriented along the z axis.

Yp_z(θ,φ) ∝ cosθ = z/r
Real pz basis, angular factor; the nodal surface is the xy plane, z=0.

The ideal xy nodal plane passes through the nucleus. The two lobes are not two independent electron compartments; they are parts of one continuous wavefunction with a sign change across its nodal plane. After squaring, both lobes contribute positive electron density. A two-colour picture is therefore a representation of amplitude sign, not an assertion of opposite charges.

There are three independent p orbitals in one p subshell, commonly pictured as px, py and pz. Their orientation labels indicate axes chosen for a convenient real orbital basis. That is consistent with the ml count from Node 09; the Cartesian real orbitals can be formed from appropriate combinations of complex spherical-harmonic eigenfunctions.

21. The three Cartesian p orbitals and their nodal planes

For px, the wavefunction changes sign across the yz plane (x=0), while the high-density lobes are oriented along +x and −x. For py, the nodal plane is xz (y=0) and lobes follow the y axis. For pz, the nodal plane is xy (z=0) and lobes follow the z axis. These are different spatial directions, not different energy levels in the isolated spherically symmetric hydrogenic problem.

The three real orbitals form an orthogonal basis for the same l=1 space. There is no fourth p orbital because one can draw p functions rotated into any arbitrary direction: a rotated orientation can be expressed as a linear combination of the three basis functions. If an illustration shows five differently oriented p-like arrows, it is not displaying five independent orbitals.

In molecules, chemical bonds and external fields can select meaningful axes, and the convenient orbital basis may differ. Avoid treating a specific x/y/z orientation as an immutable property of an isolated atom.

22. The 2p orbital: one angular node and no radial node

Now combine both factors for 2p. The label means n=2, l=1. The radial-node count is 2−1−1=0, while the angular-node count is one. Thus there is a nodal plane in a typical real p orbital, but no additional spherical surface of zero amplitude at a positive radius.

R21(r) ∝ (r/a₀) e−r/(2a₀)
Hydrogen Z=1; positive for r>0, so no radial nodes at positive radius.

The factor r means the wavefunction vanishes at the exact nucleus, which must not be miscounted as a radial node under the n−l−1 rule. The angular zero plane passes through the nucleus, so the two statements are compatible. The radial probability P(r) is proportional to r⁴e−r/a₀, reaching its maximum at 4a0; this is not the same as the radius of a sharp dumbbell boundary.

The 2p lesson is a decisive test of scientific clarity: “one node” must be identified as angular, and “zero at the nucleus” should not be called an extra radial nodal sphere.

23. The 3p orbital: a radial node added to a p-shaped angular pattern

Increase n from 2 to 3 while keeping l=1. The orbital remains of p angular character, so it still has one angular node in a conventional real basis. The radial-node formula becomes 3−1−1=1. The resulting 3p state has one spherical radial node plus one angular node, making two nodes in the standard classification.

R31(r) ∝ u(6 − u)e−u/3, u=r/a₀
Hydrogen Z=1; one positive-radius radial node at r=6a₀.

Think of an ordinary p orbital with two opposing lobes. The radial node at r=6a0 divides each phase region into inner and outer radial parts. As r passes the spherical node, the radial factor changes sign; crossing the angular plane also changes sign. Thus a phase diagram can show several regions even though this remains a single 3p spatial orbital.

If a test asks how many angular and radial nodes belong to 3p, writing “two” alone hides the mechanism. A correct full explanation states one radial spherical surface and one angular plane for a conventional real 3p basis, and notes that neither is a physical wall.

24. A 3p diagram that makes both nodes visible

In the next diagram, the dashed circle is the cross-section of a sphere where R31=0. The horizontal dashed line represents the intersection of an angular nodal plane with the drawing. The colours change across those surfaces because ψ changes sign, not because positive and negative electrons are located there.

As a study exercise, mentally rotate this cross-section in three dimensions. The angular plane extends outward, while the radial nodal circle becomes a spherical surface. Students who confuse a line with a complete nodal plane or a circle with a spherical nodal surface will misinterpret many later figures.

25. Understanding d orbitals without memorizing a single cloverleaf

When l=2, the number of independent spatial orbitals is 2l+1=5. Four familiar real d orbitals are often drawn as four-lobed patterns: dxy, dxz, dyz and dx²−y². A fifth, dz², has two large regions along the z axis and a toroidal belt around its centre. None of these pictures means that one d electron is split into four particles or that the torus is a separate orbital.

The angular-node count for conventional real d orbitals is two. For dxy, two intersecting nodal planes can be identified: x=0 and y=0. For dx²−y², the nodal planes include x=y and x=−y. For dz², the angular factor is proportional to (3cos²θ−1), so its zero set comprises two cones given by cos²θ=1/3, rather than two Cartesian planes.

This last difference matters. “All d orbitals have four lobes and two nodal planes” is not correct. A reliable description says that there are five independent d orbitals in the standard real basis, each with d-type angular character, and that angular-node geometry varies by the chosen orbital.

26. The five common real d orbitals and their axes

dxy lies in the xy plane with lobes between x and y axes, and dx²−y² points mainly along the x and y axes. Their angular factors are related to xy/r² and (x²−y²)/r² respectively. Similarly, dxz and dyz have lobes between the corresponding pairs of axes. For dz², the angular factor behaves like (3z²−r²)/r², giving two axial lobes and a ring-like region of the opposite phase near the equatorial directions.

These familiar names refer to real orbital combinations within a five-dimensional d subspace, not five permanently oriented clouds in every physical environment. An applied field or molecular surroundings can select a natural axis and different linear combinations. In a free hydrogenic central field, the conventional real d orbitals share energy because the Hamiltonian is rotationally symmetric.

When preparing for biochemistry, note only the general significance: directional d-orbital overlap can become important in metal–ligand bonding. The specific orbital splittings and electronic states of metal complexes require a later discussion, not a guess from a d orbital's outline.

27. How the unusual dz squared geometry changes the node story

dz² is a particularly instructive exception to the oversimplified textbook slogan “d is cloverleaf.” Its angular function changes sign between directions close to the z axis and directions near the equator. The boundary between the signs satisfies 3cos²θ−1=0. Solving gives cosθ=±1/√3, which corresponds to two conical nodal surfaces at polar angles of approximately 54.7° and 125.3°.

Those angular zero surfaces exist whether or not the orbital happens to be occupied in a real atom. In hydrogenic 3d, n−l−1=3−2−1=0, so there are no additional positive-radius radial nodes. In hydrogenic 4d, the corresponding count becomes one radial node, layered over the d-type angular structure. This is another demonstration that changing n can add radial structure while preserving the general angular-momentum family.

28. The 3d and 4d comparison: equal angular family, different radial structure

The first allowed d subshell is 3d because l=2 requires n≥3. For hydrogenic 3d, the radial factor is proportional to r² exp(−r/3a0) and has no positive-radius zero. Its nodes are angular only. A hydrogenic 4d orbital has the same l=2 angular family, but a radial polynomial factor that introduces one spherical radial nodal surface.

3d: n=3, l=2 → radial 0, angular 2
4d: n=4, l=2 → radial 1, angular 2
Total standard node counts are 2 for 3d and 3 for 4d.

A viewer focusing only on a qualitative cloverleaf outline might conclude that 3d and 4d are identical. They are not: the angular family resembles itself, but the radial probability distribution, size scale and radial nodes differ. This is a central reason orbital diagrams must be labelled with both n and l, not just the letter d.

Later, many-electron atomic energies can give familiar 3d and 4s occupation patterns; nothing about that follows automatically from this shape comparison. Orbital energy is treated as its own topic next.

29. f orbitals: seven independent spatial functions, not one memorized flower

With l=3, the first allowed f subshell is 4f. Each f subshell contains 2l+1=7 independent spatial orbitals. In a common real basis, several f orbitals have six or eight lobes, and some include ring-like features. Their angular patterns are more complicated than p or d functions, which is why simplistic labels such as “the eight-lobed f shape” are unreliable as general definitions.

A 4f hydrogenic orbital has radial-node count 4−3−1=0 and angular-node count three in the conventional real-orbital classification. A 5f orbital has one radial node, 6f has two, and so on. That simple progression is much easier to reason about than trying to reproduce every f picture from memory.

For advanced learners, the mathematical angular functions are degree-three spherical harmonics. Their nodal geometry depends on which real combination is chosen. For biology learners, the relevant message is that more complex angular functions provide additional directions and phase regions for overlap; actual f-electron chemistry is complicated by energetic and relativistic effects that a shape drawing alone cannot capture.

30. Why higher-l shapes are directional without having hard edges

The angular wavefunction changes sign across nodal sets and distributes amplitude unevenly over direction. Increasing l generally allows more complicated angular patterns. Yet it would be a mistake to imagine a p orbital as a rigid dumbbell, a d orbital as four fixed spheres, or an f orbital as an object with literal coloured petals. Each illustration is a compact depiction of a continuous function in three-dimensional space.

Even in a conventional real orbital, an isodensity contour is created by choosing some numerical threshold of |ψ|². Different thresholds produce different outlines. At a lower threshold, a lobe extends farther into space; at a higher threshold, only the densest central parts remain visible. The mathematical node surfaces are different: the exact wavefunction zero does not depend on an illustrator's contour threshold.

When you read a scientific figure, look for its caption, quantity, colour key, coordinate axes and contour threshold. If these are missing, treat the figure as a qualitative teaching sketch. Such a sketch may still be very useful, but it should never be mistaken for quantitative experimental evidence.
