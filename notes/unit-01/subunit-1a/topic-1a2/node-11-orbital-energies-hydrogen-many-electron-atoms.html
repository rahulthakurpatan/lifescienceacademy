Unit 01 · Sub-unit 1.A · Topic 1.A.2 · Node 11

Orbital Energies in Hydrogen and Many-Electron Atoms

A scientific story from hydrogen spectral lines to bound-state energies, quantum degeneracy, electron–electron repulsion and environment-dependent orbital ordering.

Learning outcomes: Derive and interpret hydrogenic Eₙ, convert photon energy into wavelength, explain degeneracy, contrast 2s/2p in hydrogen and multielectron atoms, distinguish orbital eigenvalues from total energy differences, and evaluate 4s–3d ordering cautiously.

Contents · 1.A.2.11

1. The question left unanswered by the orbital-shapes chapter

Our previous lesson gave electrons a spatial language. A 1s state is spherically symmetric; a real p orbital may have two phase lobes; d and f states carry more complicated angular structure. Yet a new question immediately appears. Why does an electron occupy one of these states rather than another? Why does a hydrogen emission spectrum contain particular colours? Why does the simple hydrogen diagram place 2s and 2p at the same energy, while an introductory diagram for a multi-electron atom separates them? And why does a drawing that places 4s below 3d sometimes cease to describe a transition-metal ion?

This chapter answers those questions in a connected sequence. We begin with what spectroscopy measured, introduce the physically meaningful zero of atomic energy, derive the pattern of bound hydrogenic levels, and then examine what changes when the atom contains more than one electron. Each new mathematical step is tied to an observation. We will deliberately keep shielding calculations, systematic filling rules and configurations of specific transition metals at the level needed for comprehension, because the following Academy nodes teach those subjects thoroughly.

Our final goal is not to memorize an arrow chart. It is to distinguish an exact result for a one-electron ideal model from an approximation to a many-electron system, to read energy diagrams correctly, and to explain why an orbital's energy depends on its physical surroundings.

2. How coloured atomic lines became a question about energy

In the nineteenth century, light from a heated gas or discharge tube did not always form a continuous rainbow when dispersed through a spectroscope. Hydrogen showed distinct lines at reproducible wavelengths. Different gases showed different line patterns. This meant that an atom could not radiate arbitrary amounts of energy as a featureless classical oscillator might suggest; something about its internal behaviour permitted selected changes.

Balmer found a numerical relationship among the visible hydrogen lines in 1885. Rydberg later expressed spectral regularities using reciprocal wavelengths. The formula was an excellent description of measured patterns, but it was not yet a full mechanical explanation of why the pattern existed. Quantum theory transformed a numerical rule into a statement about discrete differences between stationary-state energies.

To read a spectrum intelligently, remember that a detected photon measures an energy difference between an initial and final quantum state. The photon does not directly measure the absolute energy of an electron in the manner of a height ruler. We need to establish a reference zero and a model before we can assign negative numbers such as −13.6 eV to bound states.

3. A historical bridge: from Bohr's levels to quantum wavefunctions

Bohr's 1913 model allowed stationary states with quantized energies and explained the gross hydrogen spectrum using a simple one-electron Coulomb attraction. Its appeal lay in connecting spectral lines with differences between permitted energies. However, a classical circular orbit is not an adequate modern description of an electron. The Schrödinger wave equation replaced the orbital path with a wavefunction, whose spatial probability distribution we studied in Node 10.

For a single electron bound to a pointlike nucleus in the nonrelativistic Coulomb model, the Schrödinger equation gives the same basic principal-shell energy dependence as Bohr's expression. This agreement is important historically, but it does not mean the models are identical: wave mechanics also predicts angular states, nodes, probability distributions and additional transition information.

Later high-resolution spectroscopy uncovered smaller splittings beyond this elementary pattern. Fine structure, hyperfine structure and the Lamb shift remind us that even the exact mathematical solution of a simplified Coulomb Hamiltonian is not the last word on the real hydrogen atom.

4. What does 'energy of an electron' mean when the atom is bound?

Imagine first an electron and a positive nucleus infinitely far apart and at rest relative to each other. Take that separated-particle configuration as the zero of potential and total relative energy. Bringing the particles together allows attractive electric potential energy to become negative. A bound stationary state has a total energy below the separated-particle threshold. Thus a value such as −13.6 eV does not mean that energy is a physically impossible negative substance; it means the bound system lies 13.6 eV below the chosen ionization reference.

To ionize the atom, energy must be supplied to raise the system from its bound state to the continuum threshold. For ideal hydrogen with energy E₁≈−13.6 eV, the required minimum is approximately +13.6 eV. For its n=2 state E₂≈−3.4 eV, the threshold is only about +3.4 eV in the elementary model.

Eionization threshold = 0;   ΔEion = 0 − Ebound > 0
The zero is a reference for separated electron and ion; energy differences, not the arbitrary absolute zero, determine physical work.

An orbital energy in a true one-electron Hamiltonian is an eigenvalue for the state. In a many-electron atom, the words “orbital energy” become more subtle. We will explicitly distinguish model orbital eigenvalues from measurable differences between whole-atom electronic states.

5. Why the electron's kinetic energy matters alongside attraction

A naive beginner might reason that electrostatic attraction always prefers the electron to sit directly on the nucleus, making its energy decrease without limit. That argument leaves out quantum kinetic energy. A wavefunction squeezed into a very narrow region varies rapidly in space; such localization is associated with a large spread in momentum and a large expectation of kinetic energy. The competition between Coulomb attraction and quantum kinetic energy sets a finite characteristic scale for the bound atom.

In nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, the kinetic-energy operator involves the spatial curvature of the wavefunction; the potential energy from a point nucleus varies as −1/r. Finding stationary energies therefore means solving for wavefunctions that satisfy a differential equation, normalization and appropriate boundary conditions. Only selected bound energies result.

This balance does not authorize a literal picture of an electron oscillating back and forth along an orbit. It tells us why atomic bound states are neither infinitely collapsed nor arbitrary in size, connecting the uncertainty principle from an earlier node to the energy levels that spectroscopy probes.

6. The one-electron Coulomb Hamiltonian

For a hydrogen-like ion containing one electron and a nucleus of charge +Ze, the nonrelativistic relative-motion Hamiltonian consists of kinetic energy and the attractive Coulomb potential. The electron–nucleus reduced mass μ accounts for the fact that the nucleus is not perfectly fixed. The operator acting on the spatial wavefunction is the mathematical rule whose eigenvalues are the stationary energies.

Ĥ = −(ℏ²/2μ)∇² − Ze²/(4πε₀r),   Ĥψ = Eψ
μ = mₑM/(mₑ+M); Z is nuclear charge number; ε₀ is vacuum permittivity; ℏ is reduced Planck constant; r is electron–nucleus separation.

Notice what is not in this Hamiltonian: no electron–electron repulsion, because the system has only one electron. That missing term becomes decisive in helium, lithium, carbon and the rest of the many-electron world. For the one-electron problem, rotational symmetry and the special 1/r dependence allow the wavefunction to separate into radial and angular parts and give an unusually simple bound-state energy formula.

The equation describes the idealized nonrelativistic point-nucleus model. Fine structure, Lamb shifts, hyperfine effects and finite nuclear size require additional physics; this distinction becomes important when discussing what “degenerate” means in real measurements.

7. The hydrogenic energy formula and what every symbol means

Solving the Coulomb Schrödinger equation with normalizable bound-state solutions leads to energies labelled by the positive principal quantum number n. The compact result is remarkable: within this ideal Hamiltonian, energy depends on n and Z but not independently on l or ml. All allowed angular orbitals with the same n share the same energy.

En = −[μZ²e⁴/(2(4πε₀)²ℏ²)] · 1/n² ≈ −13.6057 (μ/mₑ) Z²/n² eV
n=1,2,3,…; Z≥1; bound levels of a one-electron Coulomb atom/ion. 13.6057 eV refers to an infinite-nuclear-mass reference with electron mass mₑ.

The electronvolt, eV, is a convenient energy unit for atoms: 1 eV = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J exactly because the elementary charge is exact in the SI. For hydrogen, reduced mass makes the actual gross-structure value slightly different from the infinite-mass 13.6057 eV coefficient. At ordinary teaching precision one often writes En≈−13.6/n² eV for hydrogen.

The negative sign indicates a bound state; Z² means stronger nuclear attraction in a hydrogen-like one-electron ion; n² in the denominator means higher n levels become progressively less negative and approach the ionization threshold from below.

8. What the formula predicts for the first four hydrogen levels

Now apply the one-electron approximation to hydrogen with Z=1 and μ≈mₑ. At n=1 the electron is most tightly bound and E₁≈−13.6 eV. At n=2 the energy is −13.6/4≈−3.40 eV. At n=3 it is −13.6/9≈−1.51 eV. At n=4 it is −13.6/16≈−0.850 eV. A more negative value means a lower bound-state energy, not a larger energy available for spontaneous emission.

9. Visualize the ladder without confusing negative energy with low photon frequency

Draw the bound energies on a vertical axis with zero at the top. The n=1 line is far below zero. The n=2, n=3, n=4 and higher lines climb toward it. Because energies approach zero as −1/n², they become more crowded near the threshold. A transition upward requires absorbing energy; a transition downward can release energy.

A frequent error is to think “−13.6 is numerically larger in magnitude than −3.4, so n=1 must be a higher energy.” Physical ordering uses actual signed numbers: −13.6 < −3.4. Therefore n=1 is lower, more tightly bound; n=2 is higher, less tightly bound. The magnitude of binding energy is a separate positive quantity |Eₙ|.

10. Why hydrogen's energy depends only on n in the ideal model

From Node 09 we know that n=2 permits 2s and three 2p spatial orbitals. Their shapes are strikingly different. Yet the Coulomb formula assigns all four the same gross energy because each has the same principal n=2. This is a model-specific degeneracy: distinct quantum states share an energy eigenvalue.

The result is more than a statement that “all orbitals in a shell look identical.” They do not. The one-electron central Coulomb potential has special mathematical symmetry that makes the energy independent of l, ml and spin in the simplest spin-free description. A generic central potential need not preserve the same accidental n degeneracy.

Hydrogen-like ions such as He⁺ and Li²⁺ retain the same gross pattern (scaled by Z² and reduced mass) because they have one electron, not because they happen to resemble hydrogen chemically. Ordinary neutral helium has two electrons, so the one-electron hydrogenic formula does not describe its full electronic energies.

11. Count the states sharing a hydrogen principal-shell energy

Within fixed n, allowed l values run from 0 through n−1. Each l permits 2l+1 magnetic orbital states. Adding gives the number of spatial states in the shell: n². If electron spin is included as an independent two-state degree of freedom while ignoring spin-dependent splittings, the number doubles to 2n² spin-orbital states. This count does not say that hydrogen contains 2n² electrons.

gspatial(n) = Σl=0n−1(2l+1) = n²;   gspin-orbital(n) = 2n²
Hydrogenic gross-structure energy degeneracy in a spin-independent Coulomb Hamiltonian; number of independent available states, not population.

For n=2, spatial states =4 (one 2s and three 2p). With spin there are eight basis spin-orbitals. For n=3, there are nine spatial states (one 3s, three 3p, five 3d), or eighteen including two spin states each. The quantum numbers provide a clean consistency check on an energy diagram: if it shows 2s and 2p at the same gross energy, its degeneracy must be understood as a count of distinct states, not as a drawing of overlapping clouds.

12. What 'degenerate' does and does not mean

Two states are degenerate when their model energies are equal. They may still differ in angular quantum numbers, wavefunction shapes and transition properties. Degeneracy does not mean the states are physically identical, and it does not mean they have equal occupancy in a particular many-electron ground state.

The phrase “2s and 2p have the same energy” must be accompanied by its assumptions: the nonrelativistic one-electron Coulomb Hamiltonian without the small correction terms. If someone reads a high-resolution hydrogen spectrum, they may find splittings that this elementary Hamiltonian deliberately omits.

Similarly, a multi-electron atom may still have degeneracies protected by rotational symmetry, such as magnetic sublevels within an isolated atomic term before external fields and certain interactions are considered. It is incorrect to assume that electron–electron repulsion simply destroys every degeneracy of every kind.

13. Why spin, fine structure and Lamb shift are not contradictions

Real hydrogen is not perfectly described by an electron moving nonrelativistically in a featureless 1/r potential around an infinitely heavy point nucleus. Relativistic kinetic effects and spin–orbit coupling produce fine-structure splittings. The interaction of magnetic moments produces hyperfine structure, while quantum electrodynamic corrections help explain the Lamb shift. Precision spectroscopy observes these differences.

A particularly famous example is the small separation of hydrogen's 2S1/2 and 2P1/2 levels. The simple Coulomb Schrödinger theory predicts the same n energy for both, but experiment resolves a Lamb shift of about 1058 MHz (expressed as frequency equivalent, E=hν). This is vastly smaller than the main 1s-to-2s gross separation, but scientifically real.

For our educational level, the key discipline is to label the model when claiming degeneracy. Later work in advanced atomic spectroscopy can treat total angular momentum, relativistic corrections and radiative effects in detail. The presence of fine splittings does not invalidate the value of the n-dependent gross-energy model; it identifies the model's scope.

14. Photon energies as differences between stationary states

A photon absorbed by an atom can raise it from one allowed energy state to another when the interaction permits that transition. The energy absorbed is ΔE=Ehigher−Elower>0. In emission, the atom loses energy and a photon with energy Ehigher−Elower can be emitted. The same positive gap appears in both directions, though actual transition strengths and selection rules require more information than the gap alone.

Ephoton = hν = hc/λ = |Efinal − Einitial|
h Planck constant (J·s), c speed of light in vacuum (m·s⁻¹), ν frequency (s⁻¹), λ vacuum wavelength (m).

For practical atomic exercises, hc≈1239.842 eV·nm. This conversion allows the energy gap calculated in electronvolts to be translated into an approximate vacuum wavelength. The appearance of a line at a particular wavelength is therefore an experimental trace of a quantum-state energy difference, not evidence that an electron must have followed a visible classical trajectory.

15. Why a numerical line wavelength does not imply every transition is allowed

Suppose we calculate the gap between the 2s and 1s states of ideal hydrogen. Even if their energies are different, a direct single-photon electric-dipole transition between s and s orbitals is forbidden by the basic Δl=±1 dipole selection rule. The 2s state can decay through other processes, including two-photon emission, so “forbidden” does not mean absolutely impossible for every interaction.

Many high-school calculations ask only for the energy difference or corresponding photon wavelength if a transition were to exchange one photon; that is a mathematical calculation. A scientifically complete claim about an observed spectral line needs the transition's interaction mechanism and quantum selection rules. This chapter focuses on energy structure and uses real allowed examples when treating common single-photon hydrogen spectra.

Students who distinguish level spacing from transition probability are better prepared to read actual spectra of biological pigments, atoms and coordination complexes, where an energetically possible transition can be weak or absent under a given spectroscopy technique.

16. A worked hydrogen transition: from n=3 to n=2

The well-known Balmer-series line associated with hydrogen's n=3→2 transition illustrates the link between energy and light. In the gross model, E₃≈−13.6/9=−1.511 eV and E₂≈−13.6/4=−3.400 eV. The atom loses the positive amount E₃−E₂≈1.889 eV. Using hc≈1239.842 eV·nm gives a vacuum wavelength near 656 nm at introductory precision.

ΔE = 13.6 eV(1/2² − 1/3²) = 13.6×5/36 ≈ 1.889 eV;   λ≈656 nm
Introductory infinite-mass, nonrelativistic hydrogen approximation. Precision wavelengths require reduced-mass and fine-structure conventions.

Here the units cancel correctly: (eV·nm)/(eV)=nm. The line is in the red region of visible light. Actual hydrogen lines have fine-structure components, and the wavelength quoted in air differs from the vacuum wavelength; a three-digit teaching calculation should not be presented as a precision NIST measurement.

This example also illustrates why total-state energy differences should come first: we determine which energy is higher, compute a positive photon energy, and only then interpret the result as emitted light.

17. The series limit and the relationship to ionization

For any fixed lower principal level nf, transitions from successively higher initial n approach a limit as ni→∞. Because E∞→0, the limiting photon gap is |Enf|, the energy needed to ionize from that lower level in the inverse process. For the Lyman series with lower n=1, the limit is approximately 13.6 eV; for the Balmer series with lower n=2, the limit is about 3.4 eV in the same approximation.

At higher n the levels become closer together; spectral lines in a series therefore crowd toward the convergence limit. The series does not continue as bound-state transitions beyond the ionization threshold; there the electron can be freed with nonnegative excess kinetic energy. The limiting behaviour is a direct consequence of Eₙ∝−1/n², not an arbitrary empirical stopping rule.

1/λ = ReffectiveZ² (1/nf² − 1/ni²),   ni>nf

Balmer and Rydberg had found powerful regularities before modern wave mechanics explained their origin. Understanding this historical order prevents the modern equation from appearing to be a formula invented merely for examination problems.

18. Changing Z: why He+ and Li2+ are closer to hydrogen than helium is

A hydrogen-like species has one electron, not necessarily one proton. He⁺ has Z=2 and one electron; Li²⁺ has Z=3 and one electron. In the simple Coulomb model, at equal n their binding-energy magnitudes scale as Z², allowing for the reduced-mass correction. Thus a He⁺ level is about four times as deeply bound as the corresponding hydrogen level, and Li²⁺ is about nine times as deeply bound.

For n=1, the elementary estimates are H: −13.6 eV, He⁺: −54.4 eV, Li²⁺: −122.4 eV. For He⁺ at n=2, −13.6×4/4=−13.6 eV: numerically the same teaching approximation as hydrogen's ground-state energy, even though the spatial wavefunctions and the ions are very different systems. Neutral helium contains two electrons and cannot be treated with this same one-electron formula for its whole atom.

As Z grows, relativistic and finite-nuclear-size effects can matter increasingly, so Z² is not an all-purpose precision law for highly charged heavy ions. Within the elementary model, however, the scaling is exact and provides excellent conceptual and numerical practice.

19. An energy diagram comparing H and He+ at the same n

Compare H and He⁺, both with a single electron. Each has the same allowed sets of n, l and ml, and the same type of within-n gross degeneracy. But the He⁺ energy scale is expanded by about a factor of four. That means the n=2 to n=1 gap in He⁺ is about four times larger than hydrogen's corresponding gap; the photon frequency is about four times greater, and the vacuum wavelength about four times smaller.

This comparison teaches three variables at once: quantum labels determine which levels exist; nuclear charge sets the strength of the Coulomb binding; and the measured photon wavelength depends on the energy difference. It also prevents the common error “He⁺ has an n=1 state with two electrons because helium usually has two electrons.” The plus sign matters: He⁺ has lost one electron and has only one left.

20. What changes when a second electron is added?

Now move from H (one electron) toward neutral He (two electrons). Each electron is attracted to the positive nucleus, but the electrons also repel each other. The many-electron Hamiltonian contains an additional positive pairwise Coulomb term, dependent on electron–electron separation. This destroys the simple separation into independent, exact hydrogenic one-electron problems.

Ĥ = Σi[−ℏ²∇i²/(2mₑ) − Ze²/(4πε₀ri)] + Σi<j e²/(4πε₀rij)
Schematic nonrelativistic fixed-nucleus N-electron Hamiltonian; r_ij is distance between electrons i and j. Spin and relativistic terms omitted.

Electron–electron repulsion influences the distribution and energy of the entire electronic state. In general there is no exact way to label each interacting electron as independently having Eₙ=−13.6Z²/n² eV while ignoring the others. We need approximations, numerical methods and careful comparison with experiments to describe these many-particle systems.

Do not say simply “electrons repel, so every electron has positive energy.” Bound multi-electron atoms have negative total electronic energy relative to separated nuclei and electrons in a suitable reference convention. Repulsion raises energy relative to an unrealistically noninteracting system, but does not eliminate nuclear attraction or binding.

21. The first approximation: one electron feels the others on average

A useful model replaces the complicated instantaneous interactions with an effective potential felt by one electron. The effective field includes attraction to the nucleus and the average influence of other electrons. Depending on the calculation, this is described through central-field approximations, self-consistent-field methods or Kohn–Sham-like orbitals. The resulting orbital energies are eigenvalues of the chosen effective one-electron operator, not independent measured pieces of the exact many-electron energy.

Why does the potential need to be solved self-consistently? The distribution of the electrons changes the effective field, but the field in turn changes their orbitals. Approximate methods iteratively adjust them until the chosen criterion is satisfied. This is conceptually different from drawing hydrogen orbitals once and then simply assigning several electrons to them without any energetic effect.

For a beginner, the important picture is that the effective nuclear attraction can vary with radius and orbital character. The next two sections show how this leads to a qualitative separation of 2s and 2p energies that were equal in the ideal hydrogenic model.

22. Core electrons influence the attraction experienced by outer electrons

Consider a lithium atom: the nucleus has charge +3e, and its ground configuration contains a compact 1s² core plus a more extended outer electron. When the outer electron is far from the nucleus and the two core electrons are largely inside its radius, their negative charge partly offsets the nuclear attraction. When the outer electron penetrates closer to the nucleus, the geometry of the charge distribution changes and the cancellation is not simply the same.

This behaviour is often described as shielding or screening. But do not replace it with an exact universal statement that every electron always feels Z minus the number of inner electrons, regardless of position. The field depends on electron distribution and the approximation used. The concept of effective nuclear charge Zeff makes this quantitative and is the full subject of Node 1.A.2.12.

Here we need only one consequence: two orbitals with the same n can sample different parts of the effective potential, so their approximate energies need not be equal.

23. Why 2s and 2p usually split in multi-electron atoms

The 2s orbital has spherical angular symmetry and a radial wavefunction that can have substantial amplitude closer to the nucleus, inside a compact core region. A 2p orbital has a different radial and angular structure; its wavefunction vanishes at the exact nucleus in the nonrelativistic central-field picture. Because the two states sample different radial portions of the effective field, the energy corrections need not be the same.

In introductory examples such as neutral lithium, 2s is stabilized relative to 2p. Thus we can write E(2s) < E(2p) for that comparison, whereas ideal one-electron Coulomb hydrogen has E(2s)=E(2p) at the gross-structure level. The phrase “greater penetration makes s lower” is a useful explanatory trend under comparable circumstances, not a universal algebraic theorem about every orbital in every ion.

The very mechanism that produces splitting in multi-electron atoms is absent from the simple hydrogenic Hamiltonian because that system has no electron core. This is why it is scientifically meaningful to compare the *two models* rather than declaring that one diagram is wrong.

24. The single most important comparison diagram

Read these two panels as different physical models. In the left panel, the electron experiences an ideal central Coulomb field with no other electrons. The 2s and 2p levels are degenerate in gross energy. In the right panel, a representative many-electron atom has a core and a non-Coulombic effective potential; in a common case such as lithium, the 2s orbital lies lower than 2p.

This diagram should not be read as a universal statement that 2s is always a fixed number of electronvolts below 2p for every atom, ion and occupation. Electron–electron interaction effects, configuration, term structure and environment all matter. Its scientific use is to explain why n-only ordering ceases to be exact.

25. Why orbital energy is not a fixed property of the label

A label such as 3p identifies a kind of atomic state or approximate orbital, but not a unique numerical energy independent of the species. Hydrogen 3p, He⁺ 3p, neutral sodium 3p and a 3p-like component of a molecule have different physical environments and generally different energies. Even within a given element, removing an electron changes the potential and often the positions of the approximate orbital levels.

Therefore, an unqualified sentence like “the 3p orbital has energy −1.51 eV” is correct only for an explicitly identified hydrogenic approximation with Z=1, n=3 and the chosen reference. It is not a universal data value for all atoms. The same warning applies to memorized arrow diagrams used for electron filling.

One useful habit is to add a context phrase to any energy comparison: hydrogen in its nonrelativistic gross model, neutral lithium in a typical ground-state effective-field picture, or a particular transition-metal ion in its specified configuration.

26. Why a multi-electron atom's total energy is not just a sum of exact hydrogen energies

Suppose we attempt to estimate helium by placing two electrons separately into hydrogenic 1s energies with nuclear charge Z=2. This ignores the fact that the two electrons repel one another and that their probability distributions are correlated. Simply doubling −54.4 eV would make the atom unrealistically overbound in that crude noninteracting model.

A more realistic theory has an expectation value of the full Hamiltonian and must include electron–electron interactions. In approximate orbital theories, summing one-electron orbital eigenvalues alone commonly double-counts parts of the interaction energy or omits necessary corrections. The atom's total electronic energy and an individual orbital's eigenvalue are not interchangeable objects.

This difference is crucial when making claims about bonding energies, ionization or electronic excitations. Those quantities compare energies of whole electronic states (and sometimes nuclear configurations), not merely labels printed beside isolated orbital cartoons.

27. Ground states, excited states and configurations

The ground state is the lowest-energy quantum state of the specified atom or ion under the specified physical conditions. An excited state has higher energy. A configuration describes a pattern of occupancy of orbitals or subshells in a chosen approximation, but a real many-electron atomic state can be a superposition of several configurations, especially where electron correlation matters.

For ideal one-electron hydrogen, the ground state is n=1, and every allowed n>1 bound state is excited. For many-electron atoms, selecting the ground configuration requires comparing possible occupancies, electron repulsion, exchange, spin and other interactions. Energy ordering of one-electron effective orbitals gives a helpful guide, but the total-energy criterion is the physical definition.

A state can be electronically excited while having the same total number of electrons as the ground state; excitation generally means supplying energy without necessarily ionizing the atom. Ionization changes the electron number and the identity of the ion. These are related, but different, physical processes.

28. An energy level diagram is a model, not a universal periodic-table staircase

Textbooks often show a ladder of orbitals labelled 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, and so on. This is a useful introductory guide for building common ground-state configurations. But there is no single numerical set of orbital energies that applies unchanged to hydrogen, calcium, neutral iron, Fe²⁺ and every other atomic species. Hydrogenic orbital energies follow n alone in the ideal model. Multi-electron energies depend on configuration and electron interactions.

The well-known 4s–3d crossover is the easiest demonstration of this limitation. Potassium and calcium fill a 4s-like subshell before 3d in their ordinary ground-state configurations; in transition-metal systems the relative orbital-energy picture changes as 3d fills and the atom or ion changes. A rigid universal “4s is below 3d” assertion is therefore insufficient.

We will return to this example more carefully below, but the complete calculation and exceptions of electron configurations belong in Nodes 13, 17, 18 and 19. Here the aim is to understand why the energetic landscape can change.

29. Why 4s may be favoured before 3d in K and Ca

Moving to potassium and calcium, one must compare total-energy consequences of different possible occupancies. The commonly observed neutral ground-state configurations place the next electron(s) in 4s: K has [Ar]4s¹ and Ca has [Ar]4s². An introductory explanation notes that the 4s radial function can penetrate into the core more strongly than 3d-like functions, which influences their relative effective energies at this point.

Yet “4s is lower for every atom” is not the conclusion. As nuclear charge and d occupation change, the effective potential changes, which can cause the 3d subshell to become more stabilized relative to 4s. This is why a transition-metal atom or ion cannot always be interpreted using the same diagram one used for potassium.

It is also a mistake to infer a complete configuration merely by choosing whichever single approximate orbital eigenvalue happens to be lowest in a calculation after the other electrons are already placed. The physical configuration minimizes the total energy with interactions and the correct electron number included.

30. The 4s–3d crossover is an electron-interaction story

Why can a level ordering change? In a many-electron atom, adding electrons changes not only the nucleus-to-electron attraction experienced by a subshell but also the repulsion and screening between orbital populations. As the first-row transition series develops, 3d orbitals generally respond strongly to the growing nuclear charge, and 4s electrons are more extended on average. The balance that favoured 4s occupation for K and Ca is not constant.

In particular, a typical transition-metal ion often loses 4s electrons before 3d electrons even though 4s was occupied early in the neutral filling narrative. This is not a paradox once one stops treating subshell eigenvalues as immutable numbers. Removing electrons changes the ion, its total energy, the self-consistent potential and the preferred occupancy.

We deliberately do not turn this section into a memorized list of every transition-metal configuration. The separate configuration chapters will establish those with careful element-by-element exceptions and explicit total electron counts.

31. Why an ion is a different energy problem from its neutral parent

Ionization is not the mechanical removal of a tiny coloured dot from a rigid subshell drawer while every other orbital remains unchanged. When a real electron is removed, the remaining electrons experience a different mean electric field and can relax into new distributions. The approximate orbital energies obtained for the neutral atom are not guaranteed to remain the same for the cation.

Consequently, statements about “the last electron added” during a neutral Aufbau construction and “the electron removed first” during cation formation need not refer to the same orbital. The 4s electrons in ordinary first-row transition-metal cations provide a standard example. A rigorous treatment compares the total energies of candidate ionic configurations and accounts for relaxation and electron interaction.

This is an especially useful lesson for a life-science learner because biologically important metals occur in multiple oxidation states and ligand environments. The electronic structure of an isolated neutral metal atom is only a starting reference, not a universal description of the metal in a protein.

32. Energy depends on electronic environment, not just atomic number

Even the same metal in the same formal oxidation state can have different spectra and reactivity in different ligands or solvents. Neighbouring atoms alter the electronic Hamiltonian, creating molecular orbitals and changing the relative energies of metal-derived states. These effects underpin many ideas in coordination chemistry, ligand-field theory and biological redox chemistry.

For example, an iron-containing protein centre is not a free isolated Fe atom. Nearby nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur donors, the geometry of the coordinating ligands and the surrounding protein all matter. A statement about 3d versus 4s in an isolated atom does not by itself predict the colour, spin state or function of such a centre.

The relevant educational bridge is that electronic energies are conditional on the Hamiltonian and the system. In later molecular-bonding lessons, we will examine how interactions and symmetry determine bonding and electronic transitions, building on the current atomic foundation.

33. Energy conservation and an electronic excitation diagram

Energy-level diagrams should distinguish energy difference from state occupancy. An upward arrow from a lower state to a higher one can represent absorption of a photon of the matching energy, provided the transition is allowed. A downward arrow can represent radiative emission, again depending on selection rules. Collisions can also excite or de-excite atoms, transferring energy without a photon of that exact transition energy being absorbed or emitted in the elementary one-photon way.

When drawing the diagram, label the vertical axis and the model. A diagram of gross hydrogen levels is not the same as a diagram of approximate 2s/2p orbital energies for lithium, or the complicated total-state energies of an iron complex. It is better to present one correctly labelled schematic for each physical situation than to paste all three into a single universal ladder.

34. Interpreting photoelectron spectroscopy and orbital energies

Photoelectron spectroscopy removes an electron from a neutral atom or molecule by absorption of a photon. The measured photoelectron kinetic energy, together with known photon energy and relevant experimental work-function/calibration terms, yields a binding or ionization energy. Importantly, the experiment probes a difference in energy between electronic states of systems with different electron counts.

In Hartree–Fock theory, Koopmans' theorem gives an approximate relationship between negative occupied orbital energies and vertical ionization energies when orbitals are treated as frozen. But electron relaxation and correlation can make the approximation inaccurate. Therefore, equating every displayed orbital energy exactly with an experimental ionization peak would overstate the model.

This is a useful example of scientific reasoning: an empirical binding-energy spectrum can test and constrain electronic structure models, but it does not turn approximate orbital energies into exact observables in every theoretical method.

35. The difference between total energies and model orbital eigenvalues

For a one-electron hydrogenic Hamiltonian, each orbital eigenvalue is a physical stationary total energy because there is only one electron. In an N-electron system, the true Hamiltonian has eigenvalues for whole N-electron states. Hartree–Fock orbitals are eigenfunctions of a self-consistent effective one-electron Fock operator; their eigenvalues are useful, but not simply the exact energy possessed separately by each electron.

Some introductory diagrams are best understood as qualitative effective orbital eigenvalue orderings. To calculate a spectroscopic excitation energy accurately, one generally needs a difference between total many-electron state energies, including the relevant electronic interactions. The same applies to ionization energies and chemical reaction energetics.

Physically, this matters because two configurations can interact, and electron repulsion and exchange contributions do not remain fixed when occupations change. “Fill the lowest individual orbital” works as a valuable approximate rule, but the actual ground state is defined by minimizing the complete system's energy.

36. Why electron repulsion does not make every subshell higher by the same amount

Different orbitals have different spatial probability distributions and overlap with other electrons in different ways. Their mean repulsive energies therefore need not be identical. Moreover, the occupied orbital populations modify the effective field, causing the shapes of the approximate orbitals themselves to relax. A correction for 2s can differ from one for 2p, and a correction for 4s can vary across the transition series.

A uniform upward shift of all orbital energies would leave every relative ordering unchanged, and thus could not explain why 2s/2p degeneracy is lifted or why crossovers appear. The essential physics is that the many-electron terms depend on spatial character and occupancy. We do not need the full many-dimensional integration machinery to understand why this changes the ordering.

37. Ionization energy, excitation energy and electron affinity are different

Ionization energy refers to removing an electron from a species to a separated state and forming an ion. Excitation energy usually refers to raising the same-electron-number system into a higher electronic state. Electron affinity concerns the energy change when an electron is added to a specified neutral species or another species, with conventions for sign requiring care.

For ideal H 1s, ionization needs about 13.6 eV, while a 1s→2p excitation needs about 10.2 eV. The latter leaves the electron bound. These numbers are distinct because the upper n=2 state still lies below the continuum. A student who writes “all absorption by hydrogen is ionization” has confused bound–bound transitions with bound–continuum transitions.

Later lessons will calculate trends in ionization energies across atoms, but their physical definition and the zero reference are essential here.

38. Why experimental electronic spectra need context

Atomic emission and absorption spectra can resolve individual lines corresponding to transitions between well-defined states. Molecular spectra are often more complicated because electronic transitions can be accompanied by vibrational and rotational changes, and electronic states interact with nuclear geometry. Solutions and proteins can add environmental broadening and shifts.

Therefore, a broad biological absorption band should not be identified with the exact n=3→n=2 hydrogenic gap merely because its colour looks similar. The correct response asks what system was measured, what electronic states are involved, which interactions contribute, and what experimental conditions apply.

This distinction shows why learning the simplest atomic model is valuable even for biology: it teaches the logical method of connecting measured photon energy to differences between energy levels, while making the boundaries of that model visible.

39. Biological bridge: energy gaps in chromophores

Biological chromophores such as those involved in photosynthesis and vision absorb light through electronic transitions. Their molecular energy gaps reflect interactions among many electrons and nuclei, not the simple hydrogenic Eₙ formula. Yet the core relation ΔE=hν=hc/λ remains fundamental: shorter-wavelength photons carry larger energy, and an absorption peak indicates the energy scale of an accessible transition under the measurement conditions.

Consider two visible bands, one near 450 nm and one near 650 nm. At the same one-photon interpretation, the 450 nm photon has greater energy because energy varies inversely with wavelength. The exact molecular excited-state energies, transition strengths and photochemical outcomes cannot be deduced from wavelength alone, but the energy comparison is valid.

This example preserves the lesson's boundary: learn electronic energy differences here, and let later topics develop molecular orbitals, chromophore chemistry and excited-state dynamics.

40. Biological bridge: metal oxidation states and redox proteins

Many enzymes and electron-transfer proteins involve metal ions that can access distinct oxidation states or coordination environments. The electronic states relevant to Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ in a biological site differ because the electron count and surrounding field differ. This supports an important principle of the present node: changing electron number changes the energy problem, just as neutral atoms and their cations cannot be described by one permanently fixed orbital ordering.

It would be misleading to say that the oxidation potential of a biological iron centre can be calculated by inserting n=3 and Z=26 into −13.6Z²/n² eV. That formula assumes exactly one electron in a pure Coulomb nuclear field. A biological complex is a many-electron, many-nucleus system with substantial environmental and thermodynamic contributions.

41. Biological bridge: why spectroscopy of haem and pigments is richer

Haem proteins and other chromophore-containing biomolecules can show distinct absorption bands and redox-dependent spectral changes. An energy gap corresponding to a measured optical band is meaningful, but explaining the band's exact position and intensity requires molecular electronic states, selection rules and environmental effects. Atomic orbital energy trends offer background intuition about electron distributions and transition-metal centres without determining the full spectrum.

This is a useful boundary for examination writing: a short answer may ask only for photon energy from wavelength; a sophisticated answer about an actual biological spectrum needs structural and electronic information as well. The most scientifically honest student states what the simple energy relation can establish and what it cannot.

42. The strange but useful idea of a quantum defect

In alkali atoms, one outer electron moves in the field of a compact ionic core. At large distance the potential resembles Coulomb attraction, but close to the core it departs from a pure 1/r field. A helpful way to describe some high-lying series is with an effective principal quantum number n*=n−δl, where the quantum defect δl captures deviations from the hydrogenic series.

For low-l states, greater core penetration often produces larger deviations from a simple hydrogenic formula than for states that remain mostly outside the core. Core polarization can also contribute. The point is not to memorize a numerical δ for every atom; it is to see experimental evidence that orbital energy depends on more than n when an electron experiences a realistic core.

NIST discusses quantum defects as a practical way of organizing observed atomic term series. This advanced connection explains why the simple hydrogen model remains useful as a reference rather than a universal exact formula.

43. Why an ordinary 2s/2p energy diagram can hide term structure

Many-electron atoms often have multiple electronic terms arising from the same apparent subshell occupancy, differing in total spin and angular momentum coupling. These are energies of whole-atom electronic states, not merely independent electron boxes. Such term structure is one reason real spectroscopic line lists can be much richer than a one-line-per-orbital textbook drawing.

An introductory effective-orbital diagram may still illustrate the qualitative ordering of 2s and 2p, but it cannot replace an atomic term diagram with explicit state labels and selection rules. Students should be able to name the abstraction used rather than assuming that every observed line corresponds to a direct hop between two isolated one-electron boxes.

Detailed term symbols and coupling schemes are advanced atomic-physics material, but the principle that many-electron quantum states are collective should already be understood here.

44. What would happen if the potential were changed without changing n?

The hydrogenic degeneracy is special to the ideal Coulomb 1/r interaction. If a different central potential acts on the electron, states with the same n but different l generally need not have the same energy. The separation of radial and angular variables may still be possible because the potential is spherical, yet the resulting radial equations contain l-dependent terms and different solutions.

Many-electron effective atomic potentials illustrate this general principle. They are not exactly the same as −Ze²/(4πε₀r) at all distances. Different l orbitals sample that potential differently, leading to different effective orbital energies. Thus the loss of 2s–2p degeneracy is not some mysterious exception to quantum mechanics; it is a natural consequence of changing the Hamiltonian.

45. How scientists decide which energy model is adequate

Scientific modelling begins by asking what accuracy the question requires. If the problem asks for the order of gross hydrogen lines, the 1/n² pattern with reduced mass as needed is often sufficient. If it asks for a microwave-scale 2S–2P splitting, corrections beyond simple Schrödinger Coulomb theory are necessary. If it asks for an ionization energy of a many-electron atom, the hydrogenic Z² formula is not sufficient. If it asks about a metal in an enzyme, the protein and ligands must enter the description.

The best model is not always the most complicated one, but it must include the physical effects that dominate the requested observable. Learning to state assumptions before calculating prevents impressive-looking but meaningless numbers.

In CSIR-NET/SET questions, the wording often identifies the correct model with cues such as “hydrogen-like,” “one electron,” “in the absence of external fields,” “many-electron,” or “ground-state ion.” Read these qualifiers before selecting a formula.

47. How this lesson prepares the shielding and filling chapters

We have identified the essential new element of multi-electron atomic energy: electron–electron interactions modify the effective attraction and remove the special degeneracy of the simplest Coulomb model. We have not yet derived practical shielding estimates, detailed penetration trends or a quantitative effective nuclear charge, because the next node is dedicated to those mechanisms.

After shielding, the Academy will develop the approximate Aufbau (n+l) filling guide, Pauli exclusion, Hund's rule, ordinary ground-state configurations and meaningful exceptions. This separation of topics matters pedagogically. It prevents a student from learning “4s goes before 3d” as an unexamined command before understanding why the ordering can depend on the atom.

The transferable conclusion is simple: to predict an energy level, identify the physical system and the Hamiltonian or approximation first. Only then use a numerical formula or a qualitative energy diagram.
