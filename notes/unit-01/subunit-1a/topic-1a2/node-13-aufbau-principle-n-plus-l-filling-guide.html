Aufbau Principle and the (n + l) Filling Guide

A chronological journey from quantum-state constraints and periodic patterns to the Madelung rule, its correct use, and scientifically important exceptions.

Learning outcomes: Determine allowed subshells, calculate n+l indices and tie priorities, build common neutral ground configurations, audit capacities and electron counts, distinguish the filling guide from exact energy laws, and interpret Cr/Cu and 4s/3d caveats responsibly.

Contents · 1.A.2.13

1. A new question: how can we build an atom one electron at a time?

Imagine learning the atomic spectrum of hydrogen first. With a single electron, its allowed states and gross energies follow a clean model. Now consider a neutral atom with eleven, seventeen or twenty-six electrons. We can no longer put every electron into the same one-electron energy level without thinking about how states are occupied, how electrons interact and how their total arrangement affects the atom. The question changes from “What states are possible?” to “Which arrangement is energetically preferred when all electrons are present?”

Earlier Academy nodes introduced the quantum numbers, subshell capacities, shapes of orbitals, many-electron energy splitting, shielding and penetration. We now connect those ideas to a practical construction method. The method is called Aufbau, a German word for building up. It provides a useful first map for the configurations of many neutral atoms. The familiar diagonal chart and the numerical n+l guide help learners organize that map.

This is not a licence to treat every atom as if electrons move along fixed tracks or occupy permanently numbered energy boxes. A ground-state configuration is a description of a many-electron quantum system. The filling diagram is an instructional approximation to that system, not an animation of electron arrival or an exact ranking of orbital energies in every possible atom or ion.

2. What the old spectral puzzles taught us, and what they did not

Hydrogen spectra led scientists to quantized electronic energies. The quantum wave equation later explained allowed states with principal number n, angular momentum l and magnetic label ml. A hydrogenic electron can occupy the 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s and other states, but it does not have to visit them sequentially. Its quantum state is specified by the system's actual preparation and energy.

For an ideal one-electron Coulomb atom, the gross energy depends on n rather than on l. Thus hydrogen's 2s and 2p orbitals have the same gross level in that model. In an atom with several interacting electrons, 2s and 2p generally do not remain so simple. Shielding, penetration, electron repulsion, exchange and other contributions affect the relative energies of many-electron electronic states.

This distinction creates the need for a working order when constructing configurations of neutral atoms. Aufbau is a helpful way to organize common ground states, while the genuine ground state remains the electronic state with lowest total energy for the specified nucleus, charge and physical conditions.

3. A historical perspective: chemical patterns came before orbital boxes

The periodic table emerged from comparisons of elements and chemical behaviour before the modern quantum mechanical picture of orbitals existed. The discovery of spectral regularities, atomic number, electron spin and quantum states gradually supplied a physical explanation for periodic patterns. The Aufbau idea was a practical synthesis: as we consider successively larger neutral atoms, their low-energy configurations often resemble a previously completed core plus new electrons in additional subshells.

In early quantum theory, arranging the available quantum states and anticipating their occupation helped organize atomic spectroscopy and the periodic table. A more systematic diagonal prescription, often called the Madelung or (n+l) rule, subsequently became a classroom guide. It is an empirical regularity and heuristic for common neutral-atom ground configurations, not a theorem derived universally from the many-electron Schrödinger equation.

One historical subtlety matters: an easy mnemonic may remain widespread long after research establishes its exceptions. Modern teaching must preserve the usefulness of the mnemonic and also state its domain. Research on transition-metal atoms makes it especially important not to equate “a common filling order across elements” with “unchanging orbital eigenvalues inside each element.”

4. Three questions that must be kept separate

A thorough explanation distinguishes three things. First, what states are allowed? Quantum numbers and the Pauli principle constrain occupancy. Second, which configuration is the neutral ground state? That is an energy comparison for a specified atom, including interactions. Third, what order does a memorized filling chart display? The chart is a useful prediction procedure that works broadly but has known exceptions.

A fourth question often gets smuggled into the discussion: “Which electron will be removed first from a positive ion?” That depends on the new ion and its total energy, not only on the order in which we wrote the neutral configuration. A diagram that works for potassium does not automatically settle chromium, copper or Fe²⁺.

In this chapter we will master the guide without misrepresenting its physics. Detailed proofs of the Pauli exclusion principle, the origin of Hund's rule, complete element-by-element writing practice and transition-metal ion configurations are reserved for their own subsequent nodes. Here we use only enough of those ideas to perform responsible Aufbau examples.

5. The quantum-number prerequisite: n identifies the shell

The principal quantum number n can be any positive integer for bound atomic orbitals in the usual model: 1, 2, 3, and so on. It identifies the principal shell. A learner's first intuition may be to fill all of shell n=3 before entering shell n=4. But in common neutral ground-state configuration construction, the 4s subshell is filled before the 3d subshell. That feature is precisely why a principal-shell-only ordering is insufficient.

In ideal hydrogen, the gross energy increases with n. In interacting atoms, different subshells with different n can overlap or invert in their approximate orbital energies, depending on electron number and configuration. A whole-shell rule sorted only by n therefore misses the familiar 4s-before-3d stage of the neutral-atom Aufbau scheme.

Remember the difference between a shell number and a statement of electron population. An atom's n=4 shell can contain electrons while some allowed n=3 subshells remain unoccupied in a simple neutral filling construction. This does not break quantum-number rules; it reflects a comparison of subshell energetics rather than a prohibition against having n=4 electrons.

6. The second quantum number: l distinguishes s, p, d and f

Within a given shell n, permitted angular momentum quantum numbers are l=0, 1, …, n−1. The conventional letters are s for l=0, p for l=1, d for l=2, and f for l=3. Thus n=1 permits only 1s, n=2 permits 2s and 2p, n=3 permits 3s, 3p and 3d, while n=4 also permits 4f.

These are allowed kinds of orbitals. Their existence does not imply that a particular atom has electrons occupying every permitted subshell. For example, a neutral calcium atom has a familiar ground configuration ending in 4s², even though quantum mechanics also allows 3d and 4p states for an excited or otherwise different electronic configuration.

The diagonal construction uses n and l together, so recognizing both quantum labels is essential. When we compare 3d with 4s, the numbers and letters are not decorative: n and l determine the two entries we will add to obtain the mnemonic's ranking index.

7. How many orbitals does a subshell contain?

For a chosen n and l, the magnetic quantum number ml takes integer values from −l through +l, giving 2l+1 spatial orbitals within that subshell. Thus an s subshell contains one spatial orbital, p contains three, d contains five, and f contains seven. Under the ordinary nonrelativistic independent-orbital convention, each spatial orbital accommodates at most two electrons with opposite spin projections.

8. Pauli exclusion and the difference between a subshell and a box

The reason two electrons cannot occupy the exact same spin-orbital is the Pauli exclusion principle. In a simplified orbital-box diagram, two electrons may share one spatial orbital if their spin projections differ. A subshell contains several such spatial orbitals according to its l value. This is why 2p can accommodate six electrons in three spatial orbitals, not six electrons in one spatial box.

For today's construction, we treat maximum capacities as known constraints and mark each filled subshell with a superscript. A detailed examination of indistinguishable fermions, antisymmetric wavefunctions and allowed spin-pairing arrangements belongs in Node 1.A.2.14. We do not silently equate an up or down arrow with a little charged ball literally spinning about its own axis.

The minimum conceptual rule is that a correct ground-state construction must obey quantum-number availability, occupancy constraints and the full system's energy preference. The familiar Aufbau sequence is a useful first approximation to the last of those three ingredients.

9. What Aufbau means, stated carefully

In an elementary configuration-building algorithm, one writes a neutral atom's electron count and distributes electrons among available subshells, taking the lower entries in a conventional filling sequence first and respecting capacities. For common ground-state configurations this recipe is called the Aufbau principle or building-up procedure.

The phrase “electrons fill the lowest-energy orbital first” is useful if we make the implied approximation explicit: the comparison is between allowed arrangements of a specified system. Approximate orbital eigenvalues can shift with population, and the total energy of the atom is not simply the sum of immutable orbital labels. The sequence written on a classroom chart is therefore a ground-state configuration guide, not an exact spectroscopic energy-axis chart.

For the first few neutral atoms the recipe works smoothly. Hydrogen's ground configuration is 1s¹, helium's is 1s², lithium adds a 2s electron, and so on. The curious cases, beginning with apparent shell crossings and later transition metals, will reveal both the strength and the limits of the method.

10. Why we need a rule beyond increasing n

If we used only increasing n, we would place every n=3 orbital before every n=4 orbital. That would put 3d ahead of 4s. The familiar neutral sequence, however, passes from 3p to 4s and only then to 3d. The reason cannot be recovered from the principal number alone. It reflects the changing effective attraction experienced by subshells with different radial and angular character, as we studied in Nodes 11 and 12.

A compact mnemonic that reproduces much of this sequence is the sum n+l. Its success is partly connected to penetration and shielding trends, but it is not derived by simply adding two fundamental energies. The integers n and l have precise quantum meanings; their sum is used here as an ordering index rather than a directly measured physical energy.

Students often ask why nature would care about an arithmetic sum of quantum numbers. The careful answer is that the sum captures a broad regularity in many neutral atoms; the fundamental many-electron Hamiltonian does not contain a universal law that E is proportional to n+l.

11. The Madelung or n+l filling guide

For each allowed subshell, compute the positive integer n+l. The common Aufbau guide sorts subshells by increasing n+l. If two permitted subshells have the same n+l, the one with smaller n is placed first in the mnemonic. Because n and l are dimensionless quantum numbers, n+l is also dimensionless. It is an ordering key, not an energy in joules or electronvolts.

Order by (n+l) ascending; when tied, order by n ascending
Madelung / (n+l) rule: a conventional approximate neutral-atom filling guide, not an exact universal orbital-energy formula.

Consider 3p: n=3, l=1, sum 4. For 4s: n=4, l=0, sum 4. Because the sums tie, the lower principal n wins and 3p precedes 4s. For 3d: n=3, l=2, sum 5; 4s therefore precedes 3d in the mnemonic because 4 is less than 5.

A critical distinction: saying that 4s appears before 3d in this guide does not establish that an occupied 4s orbital remains lower in energy than 3d in every neutral transition-metal atom or cation. Species-specific electronic structure must decide such claims.

12. The tie-breaker is as important as the sum

Many memorized versions teach only “lower n+l first.” That is incomplete because real ties occur repeatedly. We already met 3p versus 4s. A second important pair is 3d (sum 5) versus 4p (sum 5): the mnemonic places 3d ahead because 3 is less than 4. Another pair is 4d (sum 6) versus 5p (sum 6), where 4d precedes 5p. And 4f, 5d, 6p and 7s all have sum 7 and are sorted by n in that order.

The tie-breaker is an algorithmic instruction, not the assertion that “all lower n states are always lower in total energy.” Its role is simply to make the mnemonic's ranking unambiguous for the permitted subshell set. If two entries have both identical n+l and identical n, they also have identical l and are the same named subshell, so there is no distinct tie to resolve.

It is often more reliable to calculate the pair (n+l, n) explicitly than to follow an untidy diagonal chart drawn from memory.

13. Read the first sequence rather than merely memorizing its appearance

Applying the two ordering criteria to allowed subshells gives the familiar beginning: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s. This line is a teaching procedure for many neutral-atom ground configurations, not a universal measured energy ladder.

Look for the moments where the principal quantum number seems to “go backward”: after 4s comes 3d, and after 5s comes 4d. The reason is that the sorting key n+l has increased, even though n itself decreased. Once you understand that ordering mechanism, the sequence no longer appears arbitrary.

For any individual atom, stop adding electrons when its total electron count is reached and respect capacities. Do not assume the chart predicts all exceptions correctly; a configuration should be checked against authoritative ground-state evidence when the species falls in a known exception region.

15. Visual diagram: discover the diagonals instead of drawing them blindly

Place shell number n down the rows and subshell letter l across the columns. The top-right cells do not all exist: 1p is forbidden because l cannot reach 1 when n=1; 2d is forbidden because l cannot reach 2 when n=2. A correct diagonal chart must include only permitted subshells.

To reconstruct the ranking, you can ignore the artistic direction of each arrow. Compute the index printed in each cell, gather equal-index entries and sort them by n. This method remains correct even when a textbook displays its diagonal arrows in a different orientation.

17. A stepwise procedure that never loses the electron count

Start with the species. Determine its nuclear Z and net charge to obtain the actual electron count, keeping neutral-atom examples separate from ionic cases. Next construct only the allowed subshells and arrange them by (n+l, n). Then place electrons into each subshell up to its capacity, stopping immediately when the required number is reached. Finally check that the sum of all superscripts equals the electron count, and whether the species is a known exception to the simple pattern.

Use the notation 1s²2s²2p⁶ and so forth. The leading integer identifies n, the letter identifies l, and the superscript gives the number of electrons occupying that subshell in the described configuration. The superscript does not change the allowed quantum numbers or the subshell's capacity.

For a real ground-state determination, check the accepted or measured configuration of the specified atom or ion. The algorithm is a predictor, not a substitute for actual spectroscopy or a sophisticated total-energy calculation.

18. A beginner worked sequence: hydrogen, helium and lithium

Begin with hydrogen, Z=1. There is one electron; the first subshell in the guide is 1s, so the ground configuration is 1s¹. Helium has Z=2 and two electrons; both occupy 1s under the familiar independent-orbital description, giving 1s². For lithium, Z=3, the 1s subshell holds two, and the third enters the next subshell 2s. Thus lithium's ground configuration is 1s²2s¹.

The calculation obeys two simple checks. First, subshell 1s holds at most two, not three. Second, total electron counts are 1, 2 and 3 respectively. There is no claim that the lithium atom is three noninteracting copies of hydrogen: its electrons interact, and the Aufbau notation is an approximate one-electron occupancy description of the true many-electron state.

The pattern allows an immediate chemical insight: lithium has a filled inner 1s² core plus one electron in an outer s subshell. That observation helps explain broad parallels with other alkali-metal atoms without yet predicting specific chemical reactions.

19. Beryllium and boron: a subshell reaches capacity and a new one begins

Beryllium has four electrons. In the simple ground-state guide, the first two fill 1s, and the next two fill 2s, giving 1s²2s². The 2s subshell contains one spatial orbital and is now at its occupancy limit. For boron, Z=5, the fifth electron belongs to the next available group, 2p, giving 1s²2s²2p¹.

Three useful distinctions emerge. First, n=2 shell is not “full” merely because 2s² is filled; it also permits 2p. Second, 2p is a subshell consisting of three spatial orbitals, not one box holding six particles. Third, the superscript 1 in 2p¹ counts electrons, not the angular momentum quantum number.

Students can now build a configuration from the allowed-state structure instead of simply memorizing symbols. But the detailed arrangements of electron spin among the 2p orbitals will be handled systematically in the later Pauli and Hund chapters.

20. Carbon through neon: counting correctly in a six-electron p subshell

Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and neon have nuclear charges 6 through 10. All follow the common 1s²2s² core in the neutral ground-state scheme, while the 2p population progresses from two to six. Their usual spectroscopic ground configurations can be written as C: 1s²2s²2p², N: 1s²2s²2p³, O: 1s²2s²2p⁴, F: 1s²2s²2p⁵, and Ne: 1s²2s²2p⁶.

Each formula passes the electron-count test. For example, oxygen has 2+2+4=8 and neon has 2+2+6=10. The 2p subshell cannot hold seven electrons in this ordinary model because it has only three spatial orbitals and two spin possibilities per orbital. When Ne reaches 2p⁶, the n=2 shell's s and p subshells are fully occupied.

What this notation does not yet specify is the detailed placement of spin arrows among the p orbitals. That depends on Pauli and Hund considerations, not on the n+l sequence alone. The distinction prevents students from treating the Aufbau guide as a complete theory of spin states.

21. Noble-gas shorthand is a bookkeeping device, not a change in physics

Long configurations quickly become cumbersome. A noble-gas abbreviation replaces an already completed core sequence with the symbol of a preceding noble gas in square brackets. Thus Li can be written [He]2s¹, sodium [Ne]3s¹, chlorine [Ne]3s²3p⁵, and potassium [Ar]4s¹. The square-bracket name represents the configuration of the core electrons, not an actual helium, neon or argon atom physically embedded inside the new atom.

This notation helps compare valence patterns between elements while keeping electron counts checkable. [Ne] represents ten electrons, [Ar] eighteen, and [Kr] thirty-six. You can reconstruct any full configuration by substituting the written core subshells and adding the outer superscripts.

A novice error is to use the next noble gas rather than the previous one, inadvertently assigning more electrons to the core than the atom possesses. Another is to forget the electrons inside the abbreviation when checking the atomic number.

22. Sodium and magnesium: the n=3 shell begins

Once neon's 2p subshell is filled, the next conventional neutral filling entry is 3s. Sodium (Z=11) has the abbreviated configuration [Ne]3s¹; magnesium (Z=12) has [Ne]3s². The 3s subshell accommodates two electrons, so the guide proceeds to 3p as the third-period sequence continues.

Notice that 3d is an allowed n=3 subshell, yet it is not the next subshell to be occupied in the common neutral Aufbau sequence. The n+l index of 3p is 4, that of 4s is also 4, and that of 3d is 5. This arithmetic will later explain why potassium and calcium have 4s population before the familiar transition-metal 3d stage.

Biological interest in Na and Mg begins with their valence electronic patterns and ionic behaviour, but real hydration and biochemical coordination cannot be calculated from the basic Aufbau superscripts alone.

23. Aluminium through argon: the 3p subshell

For Z=13 through 18, after [Ne]3s² the 3p population increases from one to six. In the common ground configuration scheme: Al is [Ne]3s²3p¹, Si is [Ne]3s²3p², P is [Ne]3s²3p³, S is [Ne]3s²3p⁴, Cl is [Ne]3s²3p⁵, and Ar is [Ne]3s²3p⁶.

Phosphorus, for example, contains 10+2+3=15 electrons. Its last occupied subshell is 3p, not 3d. Argon contains 10+2+6=18 electrons and has its n=3 s and p subshells filled, even though 3d remains unoccupied in this configuration.

This is a crucial milestone in the story: after argon, the next conventional entry is 4s. The 3d subshell exists, but the neutral Aufbau heuristic does not treat increasing shell number as the only ordering criterion.

24. The turning point: why 4s appears before 3d in the guide

Potassium has Z=19. Add one electron beyond [Ar], and the conventional simple ground configuration is [Ar]4s¹. Calcium has Z=20 and [Ar]4s². In a naive shell-by-shell list one might expect 3d to follow 3p because both have n=3. But the n+l mnemonic assigns 4s index 4 and 3d index 5, so it lists 4s first.

This observation is often expressed by saying “4s fills before 3d.” The statement is useful for the neutral potassium/calcium building sequence, but the physical explanation is more subtle than a fixed vertical energy chart. Penetration, screening and changing electron interactions influence configuration energetics; occupied d-block systems need not preserve the same ordering of orbital eigenvalues.

Research in chemical education specifically cautions against treating “4s before 3d” as an invariant energy ranking for all transition-metal atoms. The mnemonic predicts common occupancy progression, whereas true ground-state configuration arises from comparison of whole-atom electronic energies.

25. Compare 3p, 4s, 3d and 4p by actual calculations

To understand the crucial crossing, compute n+l for each contender. For 3p, the sum is 3+1=4. For 4s, it is 4+0=4, so 3p comes first by lower n. For 3d, it is 3+2=5. For 4p, it is 4+1=5, so 3d comes before 4p by lower n. The resulting local mnemonic sequence is 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p.

The explanation is entirely arithmetic once n and l are known; no diagonal-arrow chart is needed. But that arithmetic predicts order only within the stated empirical guide. It does not calculate a measured energy gap, imply the same gap in all elements or say which electron leaves first after ionization.

A frequent exam trap presents 4s and 3d as a tie because the first has n=4 and the second n=3. They are not tied: their sums are 4 and 5, respectively. It is 3p and 4s that are tied in n+l, not 4s and 3d.

27. What happens after calcium: entering the 3d series

After potassium and calcium, the conventional next subshell in the Aufbau list is 3d, able to hold ten electrons. This gives the familiar starting point for the first transition series. For many neutral first-row transition atoms, the ground configuration can be represented with an argon core and significant 3d and 4s populations. The exact populations, however, are not all predicted correctly by assigning two electrons to 4s and then filling 3d mechanically.

Chromium and copper are two well-known exceptions to that simple forecast, and other exceptions occur in heavier series. The underlying reason is that different many-electron configurations can have small total-energy differences, while electron repulsion, exchange, correlations and relativistic effects affect the preferred state.

The responsible instruction is to use the mnemonic to generate a starting hypothesis and then check known ground-state configurations. The later Academy nodes on actual electron configurations and exceptions will examine the detailed cases systematically rather than reusing the n+l chart as if it were a proof.

28. Why the principle should not be described as literal electron arrival

An atom's ground-state electronic structure is not normally obtained by experimentally building it in a visible timeline of electron arrivals. The Aufbau construction imagines increasing Z and adding electrons to organize a series of neutral atoms. But when Z changes, the Hamiltonian also changes. A potassium atom and a calcium atom have different nuclear charges; the electrons do not occupy the same unchanging potential.

Thus the familiar story “first the 4s box is filled, then an electron climbs into a 3d box” is pedagogically convenient but not a literal movie of what happens to the same atom as its electrons appear. A given neutral atom has a many-electron ground state appropriate to that species. Adding or removing an electron from it changes the system and can change its orbitals.

Taking the construction as an algorithm rather than a physical trajectory helps students avoid contradictions when later studying photoelectron spectra or electronic states of metal ions.

29. The total-energy principle behind an approximate occupancy rule

For each specified atom, the genuine ground electronic state minimizes its energy under the appropriate Hamiltonian. The electron–nucleus attraction competes with kinetic energy, electron–electron repulsion and exchange/correlation effects. Approximate orbitals are helpful because they compress a complex many-body state into more familiar occupancy symbols.

However, comparing a candidate [Ar]4s²3d⁴ configuration with another [Ar]4s¹3d⁵ is not merely asking which individual orbital label is lower on a pre-existing fixed chart. We must compare the total energies of the candidate many-electron states. The model orbital eigenvalues are themselves affected by electron occupation and relaxation.

This distinction is why the n+l guide can predict common sequences and still fail for particular atoms without quantum mechanics being inconsistent. An empirical rule can capture a pattern without being an exact consequence of every Hamiltonian.

30. Chromium: an instructive counterexample

Neutral chromium has Z=24. A direct application of the simplistic filling sequence after argon and a 4s² stage might give [Ar]4s²3d⁴. But the observed neutral ground configuration is commonly represented as [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, a total of 18+5+1=24 electrons. NIST atomic data and introductory chemistry sources confirm the ground configuration.

The familiar classroom mnemonic says that a half-filled 3d⁵ set enjoys additional stability. That captures a pattern but should not be treated as an independent universal law that every possible half-filled subshell automatically beats any alternative. Electron exchange, repulsion, correlation and the very small energetic balance between configurations determine the actual result.

This is the first major signal that the (n+l) chart is a starting point, not a complete predictive algorithm for all neutral ground states. Accurate assignments rely on electronic-structure theory and experimental spectroscopy.

31. Copper: a second counterexample with a filled d subshell

Neutral copper has Z=29. The naive Aufbau construction can be written [Ar]4s²3d⁹; the observed ground configuration is commonly represented as [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. The count is 18+10+1=29. The fully occupied 3d subshell is part of the energetic preference, but the true explanation requires considering total many-electron state energies, not a magical rule that “full d always wins.”

It is useful to contrast chromium and copper carefully. Chromium has 3d⁵4s¹, and copper has 3d¹⁰4s¹. Both differ from a simple 4s² prediction, but they do not demonstrate that every other d-block atom must also have 4s¹. For example, neutral zinc is commonly [Ar]3d¹⁰4s².

A beginner should first understand the identity and electron count of these exceptions, then explore their term structure and detailed energies in the dedicated exceptions chapter. Pretending that a single “half/full stability” slogan predicts every transition metal would mislead an advanced learner.

32. Why 4s electrons are commonly removed first from transition-metal cations

In many first-row transition-metal cations, the outer 4s population is removed before electrons from 3d when forming the commonly encountered positive ions. This seems paradoxical if a student reads the Aufbau sequence as asserting “4s is always energetically lower than 3d.” But it is not paradoxical once the atom's state and electron number are treated properly.

Ion formation changes the nuclear-electron-to-electron balance, the effective potential and the optimal configuration. In a d-block neutral atom, 3d-like orbitals may already be more stabilized than occupied 4s-like orbitals. Removing electrons changes the remaining electronic structure again. Thus the ion cannot be solved by blindly reversing a rigid neutral filling-arrow sequence.

For now, remember the conceptual warning. Detailed bookkeeping for Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and other ions belongs in Node 1.A.2.18, where charge states, correct counts and the exceptions can be treated carefully.

33. A controlled comparison: potassium is not chromium

Potassium has the neutral ground configuration [Ar]4s¹, making the common 4s-before-3d step a useful way to organize its electrons. Chromium has a different Z and a different ground configuration, [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Their relative orbital energetics cannot be assumed to be the same merely because both contain electrons with the label 4s.

The point extends to calcium, scandium and iron. Each atom has its own total electronic energy, and the approximate one-electron orbital energies can shift with nuclear charge and electron occupancy. A sequence of configurations across different Z is not one unchanging orbital-energy diagram.

The correct scientific method is to specify the atom and its charge state, use the n+l chart as an initial organized hypothesis, apply occupancy constraints, then check the known ground-state configuration where exceptions may arise. This approach remains useful from beginner exercises to higher-level electronic-structure interpretation.

34. Why periodic-table blocks emerge from capacities, not directly from n+l

The periodic table is commonly described in s, p, d and f blocks. Their characteristic widths—two, six, ten and fourteen—come from the number of orbital spin states in the corresponding subshells: 2(2l+1). The n+l sequence helps organize the approximate order in which these blocks appear as neutral atoms become larger.

For instance, the third period has eight elements associated with 3s and 3p filling in the simple scheme, rather than filling 3d immediately after 3p. The next period starts with the 4s block (two elements), then a 3d block (ten elements), then a 4p block (six), giving eighteen elements in that broad structural pattern.

Block labels are a pedagogical classification, not a complete statement of every atom's measured ground state. Actual transition and inner-transition configurations include exceptions, and some block definitions become subtle for individual elements. The orbital capacities remain exact constraints within the adopted spin-orbital description, even when the filling sequence needs refinement.

35. Why a subshell cannot be filled twice in the counting algorithm

When building a configuration, each named subshell appears once with an occupancy between zero and its capacity. If your notation includes both 3p⁴ and a later extra 3p² as separate entries, combine them to 3p⁶ before checking the total. The configuration is a distribution of electrons among subshells, not a chronological record of how many times the symbol was visited.

Likewise, a prefix such as [Ne] cannot be followed by 2p⁴ without carefully realizing that the [Ne] core already contains a full 2p⁶. A ground-state configuration with a [Ne] core describes the ten electrons inside that core, which should not be counted a second time.

These apparently elementary mistakes matter because a long electron configuration can look plausible while having the wrong particle count or an overfilled subshell. Good exam answers always include both checks.

36. A second method: use the ordered pair (n+l, n)

The diagonal chart can be replaced by a short mathematical comparison. For each valid subshell form the ordered pair (n+l, n). In a lexicographic sort, compare the first component; the smaller wins. If the first components match, compare the second; smaller n wins. This is a simple exact algorithm for reproducing the mnemonic, rather than a claim about exact physical energies.

For example 3d corresponds to (5,3), 4p to (5,4), and 5s to (5,5). These appear in precisely that order. The procedure generalizes to 4f (7,4), 5d (7,5), 6p (7,6) and 7s (7,7). You can generate the mnemonic systematically without relying on the direction of diagonals in a drawing.

All quantities are integers and dimensionless. If an exam asks for a numerical energy difference in eV, sorting these pairs is not an energy calculation and cannot give the requested result.

37. When two allowed subshells have the same n+l

The tie-breaker can be tested with cases that do not involve the familiar 4s/3d example. For 2p and 3s, both have sum 3, so 2p is listed first. For 3p and 4s, both have sum 4, so 3p first. For 4d and 5p, both have sum 6, so 4d first. For 4f and 7s, both have sum 7, so 4f first.

Do not choose the subshell with the smaller l just because it has s character. For a tie in n+l, the correct mnemonic criterion is smaller n. In a pair such as 3p and 4s, 3p has larger l but smaller n and comes first.

It is also wrong to apply the tie rule before confirming that the subshells are quantum-mechanically allowed. For example 2d has n=2 and l=2, which violates l≤n−1, so it is not an eligible entry in the sorting problem.

38. Why the diagonal chart has blank entries

At n=1, only l=0 is possible, so 1p, 1d and 1f are forbidden labels in the usual atomic orbital scheme. At n=2, s and p are permitted but 2d is not. At n=3, s, p and d are permitted, but 3f is not. Thus the diagonal table is triangular in its allowed-state region.

Adding forbidden labels to the chart is more serious than merely choosing a wrong order: the corresponding orbitals do not exist as allowed atomic states with those quantum numbers. The first step in a rigorous Aufbau exercise is therefore to establish the available states from n and l, not to draw an unrestricted rectangular grid.

A general subshell n,l is allowed if n≥1 is an integer and 0≤l≤n−1 is an integer. The configuration algorithm should never try to place electrons into an invalid subshell, no matter how attractive its artificially computed n+l sum might appear.

39. Capacities provide a second independent consistency test

Suppose someone writes a configuration containing 2p⁷. Even if the number of electrons in the entire expression seems right, 2p⁷ is impossible in the usual orbital-occupancy description: three spatial p orbitals can collectively accommodate at most six electrons. Similarly, 3d¹¹ exceeds its ten-electron capacity, while 4f¹⁵ exceeds fourteen.

The capacity check follows 2(2l+1), independent of which other subshells are filled or where they appear in the n+l sequence. This separation prevents students from believing that a subshell acquires new capacity when it appears later in the periodic table.

At the same time, a configuration may be below the capacity limit and still fail to describe the ground state because it places electrons in a higher-energy arrangement or ignores a known exception. Capacity is necessary, not sufficient, for ground-state correctness.

40. Neutral atom, anion and cation: keep charge in the electron count

A neutral atom has N=Z electrons. A cation with charge +q has N=Z−q, while an anion with charge −q has N=Z+q, where q is a positive integer expressing charge magnitude in units of the elementary charge. You must calculate N before distributing electrons into subshells.

Nelectrons = Z − Q/e
Q/e is the signed ionic charge number (+2 for a 2+ cation, −1 for a 1− anion). This counts electrons only; it does not set a universal ionic filling order.

For ionic ground-state problems, however, the neutral Aufbau chart cannot always be applied directly to the charged system. The relative energies, occupations and orbital relaxation can change on ionization. The equation for N is exact bookkeeping; the pattern of orbital occupancy is a physical prediction requiring additional consideration.

Thus we can safely compute the electron count of Fe²⁺, but we should not assume that simply taking the last two symbols from a rigid neutral Aufbau written sequence will automatically produce its correct electronic state.

41. Core and valence electrons: useful language, not an energy theorem

Electrons occupying filled inner subshells are often called core electrons, while those in outer or chemically active subshells are called valence electrons. For sodium, [Ne] represents ten core electrons and 3s¹ the ordinary valence pattern. For chlorine, [Ne] is a ten-electron core and 3s²3p⁵ contains seven valence electrons in the standard main-group description.

For transition metals, the d subshell can also participate in chemical bonding and electronic states, even though its principal n may be one less than that of the outermost s subshell. Therefore the statement “valence electrons are always exactly those with the largest n” is most reliable in a simple main-group context and needs refinement for d-block atoms.

The Aufbau procedure is a foundation for core–valence notation, but biochemical reactivity requires knowing actual molecular interactions, charge states and orbital energies, not simply counting a superscript in an isolated atom.

44. Why the 4f and 5d region needs more caution

At higher atomic numbers, the simple filling sequence includes 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s and beyond. The ordering-key arithmetic is straightforward: 4f has sum 7, 5d sum 7, 6p sum 7 and 7s sum 7, so the tie-breaker lists them by increasing n. However, real lanthanide, actinide and heavy transition-metal configurations can involve closely spaced subshells and substantial relativistic effects.

Therefore the extended diagonal chart is best treated as a broad organization of periodic-table regions, not a guaranteed atom-by-atom ground-state assignment at high Z. For example, the actual configurations of certain d-block and f-block atoms depart from naive filling patterns.

This chapter teaches how to compute the guide reliably, including its higher-index entries, while later nodes and authoritative atomic data provide the nuanced ground-state details. The aim is accurate conceptual scaffolding, not an impossibly long list of unverified memorized exceptions.

45. The difference between an excited configuration and a ground-state exception

Imagine a neutral atom described by an electronic occupancy different from the usual ground configuration. That occupancy might represent an excited state, but it might also represent a mistaken prediction. To distinguish the two, first identify the species and compare the energy of possible states. A “Madelung exception” is an observed or well-established ground configuration that does not match the simple mnemonic prediction; it is not evidence that the actual atom violates quantum mechanics.

An excited configuration can be prepared by absorbing energy or through collisions, and it may contain an electron in an otherwise empty allowed subshell. The Aufbau guide is not a law forbidding excited-state occupancy. It is a starting aid for neutral ground states.

This distinction becomes important in atomic spectroscopy and in biochemical photophysics: light-induced excited states are not errors in the ground-state configuration of a species.

46. Electron removal is a whole-system energy difference

When a neutral atom loses an electron, its ionization energy is the difference between the electronic energy of the ion plus separated electron and that of the neutral atom. The remaining electrons can relax and their orbitals change. The last subshell that appears in a simple configuration-building notation is not automatically the one whose electron is removed first in every situation.

For transition metals, 4s electrons are commonly removed before 3d electrons in relevant ion formation, a useful counterpoint to naive reversal of the (n+l) chart. For ions, one should determine the correct neutral ground-state configuration first and then use species-appropriate removal considerations, checking experimental or reliable theoretical configurations as necessary.

In this node, the lesson is the boundary of the mnemonic. A complete table of particular ionic ground configurations, including unusual oxidation states, is a later curriculum task rather than a by-product of this ranking exercise.

47. Why ion and molecule examples must not be shoehorned into the chart

A sodium ion in a biological fluid and a neutral gas-phase sodium atom have different electron counts and environments. A metal centre inside an enzyme is still more complicated: neighbouring ligands, metal–ligand covalency, spin-state energetics and surrounding protein fields affect its electronic structure. A gas-phase neutral-atom n+l chart is not a complete predictor of those states.

The chart remains valuable because it establishes what shell/subshell names mean and why common neutral valence patterns recur. It helps students recognize the starting orbital families relevant to bonds and ions. But an explanation of biological metal redox potential, for example, needs total free-energy differences in a molecular environment and cannot be derived from the ranking index n+l.

The academically responsible bridge is to teach the atomic method correctly now and connect it to more sophisticated molecular and biochemical models later, rather than claiming the simplest atomic shortcut already solves the biology.

48. An example from photosynthetic pigments: what is transferable?

Photosynthetic pigments absorb light because the molecule has electronic states separated by photon-scale energies. The relation E=hν remains true, and the atomic-orbital concept helps learners understand the idea of quantized electron states. But a chlorophyll molecule is a many-nucleus system; its optical transitions are not obtained by following the neutral-atom Madelung order across its constituent atoms.

What transfers from this chapter is the methodology of identifying allowed electronic arrangements, using quantum-number constraints as foundational language and recognizing that electron interactions determine actual energies. What does not transfer directly is a one-number orbital ranking index that magically predicts a molecular absorption wavelength.

This clear boundary prevents a common kind of overreach: using a diagram that is appropriate for isolated atoms to make unsupported quantitative claims about complex living systems.

49. Biological iron centres and the need to distinguish charge state

Iron can occur in several oxidation states and coordination environments in living systems. Even before ligand effects, neutral Fe and Fe²⁺ are different electronic systems with different electron numbers. Their orbital populations cannot be predicted by treating the neutral 4s-before-3d instructional sequence as one fixed universal energy map.

In haem proteins, actual electronic behaviour depends on the ligand field, covalency, spin and the protein environment. A sound biological explanation begins with reliable electron counts and appropriate ionic configurations, then moves to molecular orbital and ligand-field descriptions. An Aufbau chart alone is not an explanation of haem colour, O₂ binding or redox potentials.

Nevertheless, knowing why 3d-derived states occur in the transition-metal region gives an essential starting vocabulary for later bioinorganic chemistry.

50. Periodic trends are consequences of many-electron structure, not arrow directions

The periodic table displays recurring patterns in valence populations. Elements with similar outer configurations often share broad chemical features. For example, Li and Na have one s electron beyond noble-gas-like cores in their simple neutral ground descriptions. Halogens such as F and Cl have ns²np⁵ patterns. These regularities help organize chemical periodicity.

But trends in ionization energy, atomic radius, electron affinity and chemical reactivity reflect actual electronic energies and interactions, including shielding and penetration from Node 12. They are not determined solely by the direction of lines drawn across a Madelung grid.

51. A diagnostic exercise: errors that look mathematically plausible

Suppose a student writes the pair (n+l,n) for 3d as (5,3), for 4s as (4,4), and correctly sorts 4s first. Then the student concludes, “4s must be lower in energy than 3d in Fe²⁺.” The calculation of the mnemonic is correct; the physical conclusion is not justified. The student has silently changed from a neutral ground-state ranking heuristic to a universal ionic energy theorem.

Another student lists 2d with n=2 and l=2, obtains index 4 and inserts it between 3p and 4s. The index arithmetic is correct, but the state is forbidden because l cannot equal n for hydrogenic atomic orbitals. That student failed the allowed-state check before sorting.

A third student writes 3p⁷, counts the total electrons correctly and declares success. The superscript exceeds the p-subshell capacity, so the configuration is impossible in the standard orbital model even if its overall total happens to match Z.

54. Fully worked examples: n+l, capacities, configurations and model limitations

The following 61 original worked exercises proceed from orbital-label mathematics to neutral-atom construction and the scientifically important exceptions. Each calculation states the relevant species, checks total electrons or the permitted quantum numbers, and distinguishes the mnemonic from an exact orbital-energy measurement.

Worked example 1: Compute the ranking key for 1s

Detailed solution: A 1s state has principal n=1 and s means l=0. Therefore n+l=1. Its permitted quantum numbers satisfy l≤n−1, and the s capacity is 2(2×0+1)=2 electrons. It is the first entry in the conventional neutral filling chart.

Worked example 2: Compute the key for 2s

Detailed solution: For 2s, n=2 and l=0, giving n+l=2. Its capacity is two electrons. This places it after 1s, even though an atom with several electrons is not a simple collection of independent one-electron hydrogen levels.

Worked example 3: Compute the key for 2p

Detailed solution: Here n=2, l=1, so n+l=3. A p subshell contains 2l+1=3 spatial orbitals and at most six electrons. The subscript p is a family label, not a sign that the principal shell contains three electrons.

Worked example 4: Why does 2p precede 3s?

Detailed solution: Both 2p and 3s have sum 3: 2+1=3 versus 3+0=3. The conventional tie-breaker selects smaller n, so 2p (n=2) precedes 3s (n=3). Their subshell capacities are different, six and two respectively; capacity does not decide the tie.

Worked example 5: Why does 3p precede 4s?

Detailed solution: 3p has n=3, l=1, sum 4. 4s has n=4, l=0, also sum 4. Choose the lower n, so 3p comes first in the mnemonic. This is a sorting rule, not a universal statement about all ionic orbital energies.

Worked example 6: Why does 4s precede 3d in the mnemonic?

Detailed solution: 4s has key (n+l,n)=(4,4), while 3d has (5,3). Compare the first components: 4 is less than 5, so the 4s entry precedes 3d. The common mistake is to think these entries tie; they do not.

Worked example 7: Why does 3d precede 4p?

Detailed solution: 3d has (n+l,n)=(5,3), while 4p has (5,4). Their sums tie, so smaller n wins; 3d comes first. Do not use the letter order s,p,d,f to resolve this tie.

Worked example 8: Why does 4p precede 5s?

Detailed solution: 4p gives (5,4) and 5s gives (5,5). The index 5 ties and n=4 is smaller, so 4p precedes 5s. This explains the conventional completion of the 4p block before a 5s electron is placed in a neutral Aufbau chart.

Worked example 9: How are 4d, 5p and 6s ordered?

Detailed solution: Their keys are 4d:(6,4), 5p:(6,5), and 6s:(6,6). Equal sums require sorting by n. The mnemonic order is 4d, then 5p, then 6s. None of these index values is an energy in eV.

Worked example 10: How are 4f, 5d, 6p and 7s ordered?

Detailed solution: They all have n+l=7: 4+3, 5+2, 6+1, 7+0. The lower-n tie-breaker gives 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s. This is a broad construction sequence and needs explicit exception checking in heavy atoms.

Worked example 11: Check the alleged 2d subshell

Detailed solution: For n=2, allowed l values are 0 and 1. A d orbital requires l=2, which violates l≤n−1; therefore 2d is not an allowed atomic subshell. Computing a hypothetical n+l=4 does not legalize a nonexistent state.

Worked example 12: Check the alleged 3f subshell

Detailed solution: An f subshell needs l=3. For n=3, allowed l values are 0,1,2; therefore 3f is forbidden. Its apparent numerical key 3+3=6 is irrelevant because the state is not in the allowed set.

Worked example 13: How many electrons fit in one s subshell?

Detailed solution: s means l=0. There is 2l+1=1 spatial orbital, with two permitted spin projections in the ordinary occupancy model. Capacity=2 electrons. It does not grow just because the s subshell has a higher n.

Worked example 14: How many electrons fit in a p subshell?

Detailed solution: p means l=1. There are 3 spatial orbitals and two spin projections per spatial orbital, so the capacity is 3×2=6 electrons. A notation 3p⁷ would exceed that capacity even if the overall electron count looked correct.

Worked example 15: How many electrons fit in a d subshell?

Detailed solution: d means l=2. There are 5 spatial orbitals, each accommodating at most two spin-labelled electrons, so capacity=10. A label 3d¹⁰ is allowed as a filled d subshell; 3d¹¹ is not.

Worked example 16: How many electrons fit in an f subshell?

Detailed solution: f means l=3. Its seven spatial orbitals give a maximum of 14 electrons. The f block's characteristic width is fourteen in the common block picture; this is a capacity statement, not an assertion that all high-Z configurations follow the mnemonic without exception.

Worked example 17: Hydrogen: from one proton to 1s¹

Detailed solution: Neutral H has Z=N=1. The first allowed entry is 1s, which can hold two; place one electron and stop. The neutral ground occupancy 1s¹ passes both the capacity check and the total-electron check 1=Z.

Worked example 18: Helium: why the first subshell is full

Detailed solution: Neutral He has two electrons. Both occupy the 1s subshell in the ordinary ground-state representation, yielding 1s² and exhausting its capacity. We do not continue to 2s because the required two electrons have already been placed.

Worked example 19: Lithium: the first outer electron

Detailed solution: Neutral Li has Z=3. Place 1s² and stop using that subshell at capacity two. The remaining third electron enters 2s, producing 1s²2s¹ or [He]2s¹. Totals 2+1=3, with one s electron outside a filled inner core.

Worked example 20: Beryllium: a filled 2s subshell

Detailed solution: Neutral Be has four electrons. The first two fill 1s; the next two fill 2s, giving 1s²2s². The 2s capacity is exactly two, but the n=2 principal shell also permits 2p states that are unoccupied in this configuration.

Worked example 21: Boron: when 2p begins

Detailed solution: Neutral B has five electrons. 1s² and 2s² account for four. The fifth is assigned to 2p, giving 1s²2s²2p¹. The index of 2p is 3, making it the next entry after 2s (index 2).

Worked example 22: Carbon: keep the electron count visible

Detailed solution: Neutral C has six electrons. After 1s²2s² (four), two remain for 2p, giving 1s²2s²2p². The count 2+2+2=6 is exact. A separate Hund's-rule analysis will later decide the detailed spin-labelled spatial orbital occupancy.

Worked example 23: Nitrogen: three 2p electrons

Detailed solution: Neutral N has Z=7. Core 1s²2s² contributes four electrons; 2p receives the remaining three, producing 1s²2s²2p³. The p capacity is six, so there is no violation. Distribution among its three orbital boxes is a later Hund's-rule topic.

Worked example 24: Oxygen: 2p⁴ is not four orbitals

Detailed solution: Neutral O has eight electrons; 1s²2s²2p⁴ gives 2+2+4=8. A p subshell has three spatial orbitals but can contain four electrons because one spatial orbital may be doubly occupied in the standard model.

Worked example 25: Fluorine: one electron short of the n=2 closure

Detailed solution: Neutral F has nine electrons. 1s²2s²2p⁵ gives 9 and leaves one available place within the 2p capacity of six. The configuration does not imply fluorine is missing a proton; neutral F has Z=9.

Worked example 26: Neon: closure of the second principal shell

Detailed solution: Neutral Ne has Z=10. The configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶ totals 10, with the n=2 subshells filled. In an Aufbau construction for a heavier atom, [Ne] abbreviates these ten electrons; it does not add another ten on top.

Worked example 27: Sodium: first electron beyond [Ne]

Detailed solution: Neutral Na has Z=11. The ten-electron [Ne] core is already complete; the next neutral filling entry is 3s. Thus [Ne]3s¹ totals 10+1=11 and is preferred over writing [Ne]3p¹ in the ordinary ground state.

Worked example 28: Magnesium: the 3s capacity is reached

Detailed solution: Neutral Mg has Z=12. Begin with [Ne] (10) and allocate two remaining electrons to 3s, giving [Ne]3s². The capacity of 3s is two. The next conventional neutral element begins population of 3p.

Worked example 29: Aluminium: first 3p electron

Detailed solution: Neutral Al has Z=13. [Ne] contributes ten, 3s² gives two, and the thirteenth enters 3p: [Ne]3s²3p¹. The count is 10+2+1=13. 3d is permitted for n=3 but has a larger n+l key than 3p.

Worked example 30: Silicon: 3p² notation

Detailed solution: Neutral Si has fourteen electrons. The compact form [Ne]3s²3p² totals 10+2+2=14. It is an occupancy description for the neutral ground state and not a picture of two 3p electrons orbiting on definite tracks.

Worked example 31: Phosphorus: 3p³ without drawing spin arrows

Detailed solution: Neutral P has Z=15. [Ne]3s²3p³ totals 10+2+3=15. The superscript 3 counts three p electrons; the quantum state and detailed spin arrangement require rules beyond the n+l chart.

Worked example 32: Sulfur: 3p⁴

Detailed solution: Neutral S has Z=16. [Ne]3s²3p⁴ totals 10+2+4=16 and respects p's capacity six. The Aufbau algorithm allocates the number to the subshell, not to a specific single p orbital box.

Worked example 33: Chlorine: 3p⁵

Detailed solution: Neutral Cl has Z=17. [Ne]3s²3p⁵ totals 10+2+5=17. The five p electrons occupy a subshell with six possible spin-orbitals in a suitable model; that does not mean one p spatial orbital contains five electrons.

Worked example 34: Argon: [Ne]3s²3p⁶

Detailed solution: Neutral Ar has Z=18. The shorthand contributes 10+2+6=18 electrons, filling the 3s and 3p subshells. Its higher 3d states are allowed but not occupied in this usual ground configuration. The next neutral Aufbau entry is 4s.

Worked example 35: Potassium: why the notation ends 4s¹

Detailed solution: Neutral K has 19 electrons. The [Ar] core contributes 18; the next conventional filling entry is 4s because its n+l=4 precedes 3d with n+l=5. Hence [Ar]4s¹ totals 19, a species-specific neutral ground-state configuration.

Worked example 36: Calcium: 4s² before an ordinary 3d series

Detailed solution: Neutral Ca has 20 electrons. [Ar] accounts for 18 and 4s² for two. The usual ground configuration is [Ar]4s², despite the existence of 3d states. This construction does not prove that 4s stays below 3d in every later atom or ion.

Worked example 37: Scandium: where the common 3d stage begins

Detailed solution: Neutral Sc has Z=21. One familiar neutral ground representation is [Ar]3d¹4s², totalling 18+1+2=21. The mnemonic suggests entry into 3d after the K/Ca 4s stage; its placement is not a fixed claim about an arbitrary Fe ion's orbital energies.

Worked example 38: Titanium: check d-block count

Detailed solution: Neutral Ti has Z=22. [Ar]3d²4s² totals 18+2+2=22. The two d electrons are in the d subshell, which has a maximum of ten; one should not read 3d² as two distinct principal shells.

Worked example 39: Iron: audit a common neutral shorthand

Detailed solution: Neutral Fe has Z=26. Its usual ground configuration is [Ar]3d⁶4s², giving 18+6+2=26 electrons. This can be used for a neutral-atom count, but the order of removing electrons to form Fe²⁺ cannot be deduced by blindly deleting the last written text symbol.

Worked example 40: Chromium: compare naive forecast with observed ground state

Detailed solution: Neutral Cr has 24 electrons. Naive [Ar]4s²3d⁴ totals 18+2+4=24 but is not the ground occupancy; the accepted ground configuration is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, also 24 electrons. Correct electron count is necessary but does not guarantee minimum total energy.

Worked example 41: Copper: another known neutral exception

Detailed solution: Neutral Cu has 29 electrons. The naive [Ar]4s²3d⁹ count totals 29; the accepted ground configuration is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. The filled d subshell is associated with energy preference, but not a universal rule that every transition metal must have d¹⁰.

Worked example 42: Zinc: why Cr/Cu exceptions must not be overgeneralized

Detailed solution: Neutral Zn has Z=30, commonly [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². Count 18+10+2=30. The existence of Cu's [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ does not require zinc to have an identical 4s¹ pattern; each atom has its own total energy and electron number.

Worked example 43: Find a neutral element from [Ne]3s²3p⁵

Detailed solution: The [Ne] core contains ten electrons; 3s² contributes two; 3p⁵ contributes five. Total=10+2+5=17, so the neutral atom is chlorine, Z=17. A charge would change the identification from electron count alone, so the question's word “neutral” matters.

Worked example 44: Find a neutral element from [Ar]4s²

Detailed solution: The [Ar] core contains eighteen electrons and 4s² adds two. N=20, so a neutral atom is calcium (Z=20). This does not imply the 3d subshell is forbidden, only unoccupied in this common ground configuration.

Worked example 45: Find a neutral element from 1s²2s²2p³

Detailed solution: Count 2+2+3=7 electrons, so neutral Z=7 corresponds to nitrogen. The n=2 p subshell capacity is six, so p³ is valid. Electron counts are exact bookkeeping even when many-electron energies are approximate.

Worked example 46: Noble-gas shorthand expansion

Detailed solution: Expand [Ar]4s¹ by replacing [Ar] with its full eighteen-electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶. Then add 4s¹; the result has 19 electrons. It is incorrect to count [Ar] as an additional argon nucleus.

Worked example 47: Noble-gas shorthand compression

Detailed solution: Compress 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁴ by replacing the first ten electrons (1s²2s²2p⁶) with [Ne]. The result [Ne]3s²3p⁴ belongs to neutral sulfur. Verify 10+2+4=16.

Worked example 48: Why 3p⁷ is impossible

Detailed solution: A p subshell has l=1, three spatial orbitals and at most six electrons, so a 3p⁷ occupancy violates Pauli-respecting capacity. The correct repair is not to redefine p as seven orbitals; examine the electron count and next allowed subshell.

Worked example 49: Why 2d³ is impossible

Detailed solution: For n=2, l may be 0 or 1, not 2. The 2d label is forbidden before electron capacity is even considered. A valid d subshell begins at n=3 under the standard atomic quantum-number rules.

Worked example 50: Compare ordered pairs for 4f and 7s

Detailed solution: 4f has n=4,l=3, giving (7,4); 7s has n=7,l=0, giving (7,7). Both sum to 7, so smaller n selects 4f before 7s in the guide. This does not mean every real heavy atom exactly follows the schematic order.

Worked example 51: Why 5s comes after 4p

Detailed solution: 4p gives (n+l,n)=(5,4) and 5s gives (5,5). Their n+l values tie, so lower n=4 wins. The result demonstrates a different tie from 4s/3d, which have unequal sums.

Worked example 52: Check neutral electron count versus cation count

Detailed solution: Fe is Z=26, so neutral Fe has 26 electrons, Fe²⁺ has 24 and Fe³⁺ has 23. This arithmetic is exact. The actual ion configurations require appropriate removal and total-energy rules, not simply the neutral chart used for calcium.

Worked example 53: Check anion electron count without treating Z as changed

Detailed solution: Cl is Z=17; Cl⁻ has eighteen electrons because an electron has been added, while Z remains seventeen protons. Electron count is 18, but its charge state matters when interpreting ground-state occupation and chemistry.

Worked example 54: Why a neutral 4s-before-3d rule cannot predict Fe²⁺ energies

Detailed solution: The neutral n+l mnemonic is a cross-element occupancy guide, not a constant set of orbital eigenvalues. Fe²⁺ has a different electron number and interacting electronic structure from neutral K, Ca and Fe. One must consider species-specific energy and electron-removal behaviour rather than reading a universal 4s-lowest ladder.

Worked example 55: Compute the number of spin-orbitals in n=3

Detailed solution: For n=3, allowed l=0,1,2 contribute 1+3+5=9 spatial orbitals. Doubling for spin gives 18 spin-orbitals (under the standard model). Yet the usual neutral argon ground configuration does not occupy 3d; capacity and ground occupancy are different notions.

Worked example 56: Check the first twenty neutral atoms' total Aufbau occupancy

Detailed solution: The conventional sequence through Ca has capacities 1s² (2), 2s² (2), 2p⁶ (6), 3s² (2), 3p⁶ (6), 4s² (2). Sum 2+2+6+2+6+2=20, matching Ca's Z. Note the 3d subshell remains empty even though it belongs to the n=3 shell.

Worked example 57: A transition-metal exception is not an excited state by definition

Detailed solution: Chromium's [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ is an accepted neutral ground-state configuration despite differing from the simplistic Aufbau prediction. Therefore, calling it an “excited” configuration merely because it disagrees with the arrow chart confuses an empirical heuristic with the actual minimum-energy state.

Worked example 58: Why the rule is not E=n+l in eV

Detailed solution: The integers n and l are dimensionless quantum numbers; their sum is dimensionless. Energy requires a Hamiltonian, physical constants, electron interactions and a unit. A numerical key 5 for 3d cannot be interpreted as a measured 5 eV orbital energy.

Worked example 59: How many subshells does n=4 permit?

Detailed solution: Allowed l=0,1,2,3, giving 4s,4p,4d,4f. Their spatial orbital counts are 1+3+5+7=16, and spin-orbital capacity is 32. That does not mean the fourth period occupies all n=4 orbitals before n=5.

Worked example 60: What does [Ne] actually represent?

Detailed solution: [Ne] abbreviates the full electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶ (10 electrons). It represents a core pattern, not a separate neon atom inside sodium. Using it in [Ne]3s¹ yields 11 electrons for neutral sodium.

Worked example 61: Why n+l ties should be checked explicitly

Detailed solution: For 3p and 4s, the sums both equal four; the tiebreak uses smaller n to choose 3p. For 3d and 4s, the sums are five and four, so there is no tie. Writing the two keys prevents a subtle but common exam error.

57. Thirty-five conceptual pitfalls corrected

A good correction explains the assumption behind the mistake. These are especially useful when a memorized Madelung chart seems to contradict spectroscopy or ionic configurations.

Misconception 1: Aufbau is a movie of electrons arriving in a fixed atom. It is a model construction across species and configurations, not the literal time evolution of a given atom.
Misconception 2: n+l is an energy in eV. It is a dimensionless integer sorting key; real energy comes from a species-specific Hamiltonian.
Misconception 3: The smaller n always fills before the larger n. The guide compares n+l first, and smaller n only in a tie; 4s is written before 3d in the conventional sequence.
Misconception 4: Every subshell with a calculable n+l exists. A label is valid only if 0≤l≤n−1. 1p, 2d and 3f remain forbidden regardless of arithmetic.
Misconception 5: 4s and 3d have equal n+l. 4s has index 4 and 3d has index 5. The classic tie is 3p with 4s or 3d with 4p.
Misconception 6: Lower l always wins an n+l tie. The tie-break is smaller n. In a 3p/4s tie, 3p wins despite its larger l.
Misconception 7: One p subshell contains six spatial orbitals. It contains three spatial orbitals; six is its capacity including two spin projections for each.
Misconception 8: A d subshell can take eleven electrons when n is large. The maximum remains ten electrons, independent of the permitted principal n.
Misconception 9: An atom with n=4 occupied must have every n=3 subshell full. Neutral potassium and calcium have 4s electrons while 3d remains unoccupied in their ordinary ground representations.
Misconception 10: Every n=2 subshell must be filled before an atom can have n=3 electrons in any state. The guide describes common ground-state ordering; excited configurations may occupy other allowed states if prepared with energy.
Misconception 11: A valid electron count proves a ground configuration is correct. The configuration must also obey capacities and be the actual lowest-energy electronic state of that species.
Misconception 12: A configuration containing 2p⁷ becomes valid if the atom has enough protons. p has capacity six by allowed orbital/spin states. Increasing proton count cannot create a seventh p spin-orbital in that subshell.
Misconception 13: The [Ne] symbol denotes an embedded neon atom. It abbreviates the ten-electron core configuration, not another nucleus or an independent atom.
Misconception 14: A noble-gas core may be counted twice when expanding shorthand. Its internal electrons are already included in the bracket; expansion must replace, not add, the core.
Misconception 15: A superscript in 3p⁴ means l=4. The superscript counts electrons; p fixes l=1, and the leading 3 fixes n=3.
Misconception 16: Aufbau ordering alone determines spin arrangement in p orbitals. Pauli and Hund considerations govern detailed spin/spatial occupancy; they are separate rules.
Misconception 17: Pauli exclusion is the same principle as n+l sorting. Pauli constrains indistinguishable-fermion state occupancy; Madelung approximates a neutral filling sequence.
Misconception 18: Hund's rule is already proved by drawing three p boxes. Hund's rule involves many-electron spin and exchange energetics; it requires a separate explanation.
Misconception 19: The 4s box is always below the 3d box for every atom. Relative approximate orbital energies depend on atom, ion and occupancy; fixed universal level diagrams are misleading.
Misconception 20: The last subshell written in neutral Aufbau always loses an electron first. Ionization can reorganize electronic states; transition-metal cations often lose 4s electrons first.
Misconception 21: Neutral Fe and Fe2+ have identical orbital-energy ordering because Z is unchanged. Their electron numbers differ; remaining electrons relax and the energy balance changes.
Misconception 22: Chromium's [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ breaks quantum mechanics. It is a valid experimentally supported ground configuration, demonstrating limitations of the simple mnemonic.
Misconception 23: Copper's [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ is a forbidden exception. It respects quantum capacities and electron count; it is the accepted neutral ground representation.
Misconception 24: The half-filled or fully-filled slogan predicts all elements perfectly. It helps rationalize certain examples but is not a universal total-energy law and fails as an unrestricted prediction algorithm.
Misconception 25: Cr and Cu have 4s¹ so Zn must also have 4s¹. Zn commonly has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². Each atom's ground energy preference must be considered separately.
Misconception 26: n+l supplies exact spectral transition wavelengths. It gives no electronvolt-level separations or transition matrix elements; spectroscopy needs actual electronic-state energies.
Misconception 27: The ordering of subshells does not depend on nuclear charge. Approximate one-electron and total-state energies change as Z and occupancy change; the mnemonic is a cross-element heuristic.
Misconception 28: A transition-metal ion is simply a neutral atom with the last written superscript reduced. Electron removal and the resulting ionic ground state require species-specific physics, not a mechanical deletion.
Misconception 29: Every d-block atom uses the naive neutral 4s²3d^k pattern. Cr, Cu and further heavier atoms are established exceptions; exact ground assignments should be checked.
Misconception 30: All physically possible electronic configurations are ground states. Higher-energy occupancies can exist as excited states but are not the minimum-energy configuration.
Misconception 31: The same chart correctly predicts molecular excited-state energies. Molecular electronic structure has multiple nuclei and interacting electrons; isolated neutral atomic filling guides do not provide its optical gaps.
Misconception 32: A chlorophyll pigment's colour can be computed from its constituent atoms' n+l indices. Its absorption involves molecular electronic excitations and environment-dependent state energies, not sums of atomic quantum labels.
Misconception 33: The width of the d block is ten because n+l sometimes equals ten. The ten-electron capacity follows five spatial d orbitals times two spin labels; n+l plays no role in that count.
Misconception 34: An ion's charge changes the nuclear proton number. Ionization adds or removes electrons; Z remains the number of protons in the nucleus.
Misconception 35: The Academy's 22 electronic-configuration nodes are mandated by CSIR. The syllabus specifies broad coverage; the 22-node sequence is the Academy's instructional design, not an official exam-board count.

58. Twelve scientific and biological case studies

Each case follows what was observed or established, what the neutral Aufbau construction predicts, and what remains beyond this approximate model. The examples connect the sequence to chemistry without pretending the mnemonic is a complete molecular theory.

Case 1: The periodic table was observed before the electron-filling diagram was invented

The familiar chemical periodicity of elements preceded the full quantum explanation of orbitals. Early classifications grouped elements using properties such as valence behaviour and atomic weight, while later identification of atomic number and quantum states provided a more physical organization. The modern n+l chart is a retrospective way to account for many configurations across the periodic table. We should not confuse the chronological scientific evidence with the classroom narrative that scientists simply wrote a diagonal chart and then discovered chemistry. The regular widths of the s, p, d and f blocks are rooted in quantum numbers and allowed spin states; actual element-by-element ground configurations rely on experimental observations and many-electron energy theory.

Scope and evidence: The case teaches that a useful explanatory model can be developed after the empirical patterns it organizes; a mnemonic does not generate experimental evidence by itself.

Case 2: From neon to sodium: the first clear core-plus-valence example

Neutral neon has 1s²2s²2p⁶, ten electrons. Neutral sodium adds a proton and an electron relative to the preceding element of the periodic sequence, yielding a ground configuration commonly written [Ne]3s¹. The shorthand reveals a completed core and one outer s electron. It also demonstrates why a neutral Aufbau sequence can be used to recognize recurring valence patterns. But the atom's nuclear charge changes across the sequence: Na is not Ne plus a passive electron added to an unchanged nuclear potential. Shielding and electron repulsion adjust to the new system.

Scope and evidence: The accepted configuration is supported by atomic spectroscopy and common reference treatments; this case does not assert that the additional electron travelled physically through fixed slots.

Case 3: Potassium and calcium: a crossing of shell numbers without forbidden states

Potassium, Z=19, has the familiar neutral ground configuration [Ar]4s¹; calcium, Z=20, has [Ar]4s². The 3d subshell is allowed by quantum numbers, but the conventional neutral filling sequence passes through 4s before population of 3d. Computing the ordered keys shows why the mnemonic does so: 4s=(4,4), 3d=(5,3). This is a useful example of why filling solely by principal shell number is insufficient. Yet the accepted K/Ca ground states do not prove that all later d-block ions retain a 4s-lower-than-3d orbital energy ordering.

Scope and evidence: The same diagram must not be presented as a universal measured spectrum; the energy landscape changes across species.

Case 4: A chromium spectrum distinguishes a heuristic from the actual ground state

A student following the naive filling algorithm predicts neutral Cr (Z=24) as [Ar]4s²3d⁴. The electron count is right, but NIST lists the neutral ground configuration as [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. The apparent discrepancy is not that an electron forgot a fundamental rule. The ground state of the 24-electron system reflects the total energy of interacting electrons, including exchange and repulsion. A common teaching slogan invokes half-filled d-shell stability, which is a useful shorthand but not a universally sufficient energy calculation. In accurate work the experimental configuration takes precedence over the simple algorithm.

Scope and evidence: A correct scientific explanation distinguishes an approximate predicted occupancy from verified ground-state assignment.

Case 5: Copper provides another exception but not a second universal rule

For neutral copper, Z=29, the mechanical Aufbau pattern predicts [Ar]4s²3d⁹, whereas the accepted ground configuration is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Both count 29 electrons and obey the subshell-capacity limits, but only one is the observed ground occupation. A filled d subshell is associated with the preferred energy arrangement, together with the many-electron balance. This observation is sometimes exaggerated into the incorrect claim that every d-block element will sacrifice an s electron to obtain a filled d subshell whenever possible. Zinc, with [Ar]3d¹⁰4s², is an immediate reminder that the electronic system must be assessed separately.

Scope and evidence: Do not use a half/full mnemonic to manufacture configurations of every transition-metal atom; authoritative data and total-state energies matter.

Case 6: The 4s/3d ionization question in transition metals

In many first-row transition-metal cations, electrons are removed from the occupied 4s region before 3d, although a neutral fill chart lists 4s before the initial 3d stage. This seems contradictory if one has treated the chart as fixed energies. In reality, changing electron number changes the effective field and the energies of the states. A neutral atom's Aufbau occupancy construction across different elements is not the same task as minimizing the energy of its particular cation. This case prepares an ion-configuration chapter where the real charges, candidate configurations and their energetics can be examined in detail.

Scope and evidence: The apparent paradox disappears when the neutral chart is labelled a heuristic and ion formation is treated as a new many-electron problem.

Case 7: The niobium counterexample warns against stopping at chromium and copper

OpenStax also discusses neutral niobium, for which a simple Aufbau forecast [Kr]5s²4d³ differs from an observed ground-state representation [Kr]5s¹4d⁴. This shows that Cr and Cu are not the only exceptions and that the deeper problem is the small energy separation between possible subshell occupations. The accepted ground state depends on the total electronic balance, including repulsion and rearrangement. The existence of such cases at higher atomic number makes it especially unsafe to assume that one diagonal order exactly predicts the full periodic table.

Scope and evidence: The case supports a general model-validity lesson, not an exhaustive list of all transition-metal anomalies.

Case 8: A molecular pigment is not an atom with a longer Aufbau chart

Consider chlorophyll, where absorption of light drives electronic excitation. Atomic Aufbau theory provides vocabulary for shells, orbitals and the importance of electron interactions. But a chlorophyll molecule contains many nuclei and a conjugated electronic structure. Its absorption bands are differences between molecular electronic states, with vibrational and environmental effects. There is no physically justified procedure that inserts its constituent atoms' n+l indices into a sum to predict those bands. To understand the pigment, one must move to molecular orbital and excited-state theory.

Scope and evidence: The value of the atomic chapter is to establish quantum-state language and its limitations before biological photophysics.

Case 9: Haem proteins require actual ions, not the neutral iron filling diagram alone

An iron-containing protein centre can have different oxidation and coordination states. Neutral gas-phase iron has a common configuration [Ar]3d⁶4s², but Fe²⁺ inside a molecular site is not the same electron-number system, and its electronic levels are affected by ligand interactions, covalency, spin and the protein environment. A scientist cannot calculate haem oxygen affinity or redox potential from n+l alone. Nevertheless, correct neutral-atom and ionic electron counting builds foundational intuition for why transition-metal d-derived orbitals are chemically important.

Scope and evidence: The biochemical mechanism requires ligand-field, molecular and thermodynamic reasoning beyond this neutral-atom heuristic.

Case 10: Sodium and potassium in membranes: repeating outer patterns

Neutral sodium is commonly [Ne]3s¹ and potassium [Ar]4s¹. Both have a single s electron outside a filled noble-gas-like core in their usual neutral ground-state descriptions, a pattern that underlies broad chemical similarities. When they form Na⁺ and K⁺, however, their electronic structures and interactions with water differ. Ion-channel selectivity depends on dehydration costs, coordinating atoms, protein structure and dynamics. Their common ns¹ starting pattern cannot alone predict membrane transport behaviour.

Scope and evidence: The correct biological bridge is from atomic configuration to ion electronic properties and then to molecular hydration and channel interactions.

Case 11: Element identification from a spectral term is more than counting subscripts

A neutral element can often be identified from its accepted ground-state configuration by adding occupied subshell electron counts. For example [Ne]3s²3p⁵ totals seventeen and identifies chlorine. But spectroscopy can reveal term multiplicities, spin/orbital angular momentum coupling and excited configurations that cannot be read from a single Madelung rank. Two states with the same electron count can differ energetically and spectroscopically. When an assignment is needed, chemical and spectroscopic evidence determines the actual state.

Scope and evidence: Electron count is exact bookkeeping. Configuration and term assignment require specific atomic-state information.

Case 12: Chemistry grows from validated approximations, not from universal arrows

Many early neutral elements follow an Aufbau sequence and can be taught through clear incremental examples. Then chromium, copper, niobium and metal ions demonstrate that simple instruction does not eliminate exchange, correlation and competing electronic configurations. A scientifically mature approach uses the simplest model that addresses the question, states its limits, and switches to experimental or higher-level theory when needed. This is an especially valuable habit for life-science learners because real enzymes, pigments and biomolecules have far more complex environments than free neutral atoms.

Scope and evidence: A useful mnemonic can organize learning without becoming a false universal physical law.

59. Twelve integrative challenges with detailed solutions

These tasks combine allowed-state logic, ordered-pair sorting, capacities, real species and scientific model selection. Try writing the explanation before reading its solution.

Integrative challenge 1: Rebuild the local diagonal ordering without any arrows

Problem: Starting from the allowed subshells 3p, 4s, 3d and 4p, assign n+l, resolve ties and explain what the resulting sequence does not prove.

Solution: 3p has (4,3), 4s (4,4), 3d (5,3) and 4p (5,4). Sort first on n+l and then n, giving 3p→4s→3d→4p. The result reproduces the neutral filling mnemonic; it is not a measured species-independent set of orbital energies in eV.

Integrative challenge 2: Prove the capacity of the full n=4 shell

Problem: List every permitted l value for n=4, count spatial orbitals and determine the maximum spin-orbital occupancy.

Solution: l may be 0,1,2,3. Counts are 1,3,5,7 spatial orbitals, totalling 16=n². With two spin projections each, maximum occupancy is 32 electrons. This is a capacity for available quantum states; it does not mean every atom with 4s occupation has its n=4 shell filled.

Integrative challenge 3: Construct a neutral ground configuration and audit the result

Problem: Write phosphorus (Z=15) by the conventional sequence, convert to noble-gas shorthand and verify the subshell capacities.

Solution: The neutral construction is 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³. Electron count 2+2+6+2+3=15. The shorthand is [Ne]3s²3p³. All subshell occupancies lie within s=2 and p=6 capacities. Detailed spatial spin alignment of p³ requires Hund's rule, which is not fixed by n+l alone.

Integrative challenge 4: Identify the source of a seemingly plausible invalid configuration

Problem: A proposed neutral element with 22 electrons is assigned 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d². Is its count valid? Does this automatically prove it is the ground state?

Solution: The superscripts total 22 and all named subshells are allowed and below capacity. It is a common ground representation for neutral titanium. However, the fact that a notation passes count and capacity checks does not, by itself, prove minimum total energy for an arbitrary atom; one must identify the species and check the actual ground assignment.

Integrative challenge 5: Distinguish allowed-state logic from diagonal sorting

Problem: A student computes 2d as (n+l,n)=(4,2) and inserts it before 3p. Explain the mistake completely.

Solution: For n=2, l can be 0 or 1 only. d requires l=2, so the proposed 2d subshell is not permitted. The ordered-pair rule applies only to allowed subshells. The student computed a numerical sorting key for a nonexistent state and therefore violated the prerequisite quantum-number constraint.

Integrative challenge 6: Explain a real exception using evidence rather than a slogan

Problem: Compare the naive and the accepted chromium ground configurations, check electron counts and state what must determine the preferred one.

Solution: The naive guide produces [Ar]4s²3d⁴ and the accepted neutral Cr ground state is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Each has 18+6=24 electrons, and each respects capacities. The actual ground state is determined by lower total electronic energy, including interacting-electron effects. Calling 3d⁵ 'half-filled and stable' may help memory, but accurate preference is not derivable from the chart alone.

Integrative challenge 7: Avoid over-generalizing from copper to zinc

Problem: Compare the ground-state shorthands of Cu and Zn and explain why Cu does not imply Zn must also have 4s¹.

Solution: Neutral Cu (Z=29) is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹, totalling 29. Neutral Zn (Z=30) is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s², totalling 30. They are different nuclei and electronic systems. A full d subshell can coexist with either one or two s electrons depending on the species and energetic balance; neither arrangement can be forced on the other by a simple slogan.

Integrative challenge 8: Connect n+l to periodic-block widths without confusing the concepts

Problem: Explain why an ordinary d block contains ten positions and where the 3d entry occurs relative to 4s and 4p.

Solution: The d capacity is 2(2l+1)=2×5=10 from l=2 and spin. The mnemonic assigns 4s index 4 and 3d/4p index 5; with smaller n first in a tie, it gives 4s→3d→4p. The d block's width comes from state multiplicity, not from n+l=5 or the length of a diagonal arrow.

Integrative challenge 9: Separate neutral atom count from cation configuration

Problem: Iron has Z=26. Calculate its neutral and +2 electron counts, state the common neutral shorthand and explain why the +2 configuration needs an additional physical rule.

Solution: Neutral Fe has 26 electrons, Fe²⁺ has 24. Neutral Fe is commonly [Ar]3d⁶4s². The ion's remaining electrons can reorganize, and occupied 4s electrons are commonly removed before 3d for first-row transition-metal cations. That cannot be deduced by simply assuming the neutral mnemonic is an immutable energy ranking.

Integrative challenge 10: Test the high-index tie family

Problem: Calculate ordered keys for 4f,5d,6p,7s. Which are permitted and what is their ranking in the mnemonic?

Solution: Each is permitted because l is 3,2,1,0 respectively and l≤n−1. Their keys are (7,4),(7,5),(7,6),(7,7), so ascending n yields 4f→5d→6p→7s. Heavy-atom ground configurations can depart from the naive construction because relativistic and many-electron energies matter.

Integrative challenge 11: Make a correct boundary statement for biochemistry

Problem: Why would a chlorophyll absorption peak not be calculable from the n+l of its atoms, despite atomic orbitals being foundational?

Solution: n+l is an approximate neutral-atom subshell ordering key. Chlorophyll is a multi-nuclear molecule with molecular electronic states, vibrational coupling and environmental effects; the photon energy is a difference between those states. Atomic quantum numbers give foundational concepts, but a molecular excited-state model and experiment are needed for its actual absorption spectrum.

Integrative challenge 12: Diagnose a three-error configuration solution

Problem: A student says '3d is forbidden before 4s because n=3 is lower, and a p subshell can hold seven electrons if Z is high.' Find all independent errors.

Solution: First, 3d is allowed for n=3,l=2; its absence in early neutral configurations is an occupancy choice, not a forbidden quantum state. Second, 4s precedes 3d in the mnemonic because indices are 4 and 5, not because 3d has lower n. Third, a p subshell always has three spatial orbitals and at most six electrons; high nuclear charge does not make 2p⁷ valid.
