1. The puzzle that Pauli leaves unanswered

In the previous chapter we learned why two electrons cannot share the same spin-orbital. That restriction tells us why [↑↑] within one orbital box is impossible. But consider two electrons that must be placed among the three 2p spatial orbitals of an isolated carbon atom. Both [↑↓][ ][ ] and [↑][↑][ ] respect Pauli's exclusion principle. Why is the second kind of pattern central to carbon's observed ground state? This is not a question of legal versus illegal electron states. It is a question of which allowed many-electron state has the lower energy.

We can imagine a laboratory spectrum of carbon, nitrogen or oxygen: the spectral lines arise from specific differences between many-electron energy levels. Experimental levels show characteristic spin and orbital angular-momentum patterns that cannot be captured merely by counting the maximum occupancy of a p subshell. A new organizing principle is needed to predict which way partially filled, equal-energy orbitals are occupied in a low-energy state.

Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity offers that principle. In its familiar orbital-box form, electrons occupy the equal-energy orbitals of a subshell singly with parallel spin projections before an orbital receives a second electron. We will build this rule from the difference between orbital availability and energy preference, connect it to exchange symmetry, test it against measured atomic term symbols, and learn exactly where the simplified rule stops being reliable.

2. From spectral observations to Hund's 1920s rules

As experimental atomic spectroscopy improved during the early twentieth century, physicists saw that electron-rich atoms produced groups of spectral lines associated with several coupled angular momenta. Pauli's exclusion principle clarified which electronic states were forbidden. But spectra still contained multiple allowed terms belonging to the same configuration, often with different energies. A rule about state exclusion could not decide which permitted term was lowest.

Friedrich Hund formulated empirical rules in the 1920s to organize the ordering of atomic terms. What general-chemistry courses call Hund's rule is chiefly an introductory consequence of his first rule: within the relevant configuration, the state with maximum total electron spin S generally has the lowest energy. The rule applies in the appropriate free-atom, LS-coupling and configuration comparison context; it is not a universal statement about every molecule, complex or strongly perturbed atom.

The modern interpretation connects spin symmetry, the antisymmetric wavefunction required for fermions, Coulomb interactions and orbital relaxation. The historical lesson is important: first came patterns in observed spectra and the need to compare atomic energy levels; the familiar rows of arrows were developed as a convenient representation for one part of the resulting quantum theory.

If an electron is moved from a lower-energy subshell to a higher one solely to increase parallel spin, one cannot claim Hund requires the promotion: an energy comparison across different subshells is necessary.

4. What a degenerate orbital set really means

The word degenerate means that different states or orbitals have the same energy in the chosen Hamiltonian or approximation. In a spherical, field-free central-potential approximation, the three p orientations belonging to a given n,l subshell have the same one-electron orbital energy. The five d spatial orbitals of one subshell similarly form a degenerate set. Their orbital labels differ, allowing more than one electron to have the same spin projection without breaking Pauli.

Do not confuse equal orbital energies in a simplified one-electron picture with equal total energies of all multi-electron arrangements. When electrons interact, different spin-orbital occupancy combinations can have distinct total energies even if the starting orbital energies are equal. That difference is exactly why Hund's rule is meaningful.

Degeneracy can also be lifted. External fields, molecular bonding, crystal or ligand environments and strong spin-orbit coupling change the relevant electronic problem. In such cases it may not be correct to draw three truly equal-energy orbitals and apply a universal “one arrow in each first” instruction without asking what the energy separation actually is.

5. Recall the spatial orbital labels and Pauli's limit

An elementary orbital is identified by n,l,ml. Within a p subshell, l=1 and the magnetic labels are −1,0,+1, giving three distinct spatial orbitals. A d subshell has l=2, with five ml labels. Each spatial orbital has two spin states ms=+½ or −½. Thus a p subshell accommodates up to six electrons and a d subshell up to ten in the usual atomic orbital approximation.

Every box in a diagram denotes one spatial orbital. One arrow denotes an occupied spin-orbital. Two opposite arrows in one box correspond to different spin-orbitals with the same spatial label, and are Pauli-allowed. Two identical arrows in one box duplicate a full spin-orbital and are forbidden. However, arrows with the same orientation in different boxes are completely Pauli-allowed because the spatial orbital labels differ.

Hund's rule begins only after this validity check. It addresses the preferred allocation of electrons across the available degenerate boxes, not the fundamental capacity of any box.

6. The introductory rule: singly first, with parallel spin

Hund's first rule in introductory orbital notation: For electrons distributed within degenerate orbitals of a partially filled subshell in the usual free-atom ground-state treatment, place one electron in each available spatial orbital with the same spin projection before adding a second electron to any orbital. This gives the largest compatible total spin within the relevant configuration.

If there is only one s spatial orbital, there is no set of multiple degenerate orbitals within that s subshell over which to distribute singles, so Hund's familiar box-spreading instruction adds nothing. In a p subshell, the first three electrons can occupy the three distinct p orbitals separately with aligned spins. The fourth must become a second occupant of one box if it remains in that p subshell; Pauli then demands its spin projection differ from that of the first occupant.

A consistent arrow convention is useful: draw the singly occupied boxes ↑ ↑ ↑, then add ↓ arrows to produce pairs. Reversing every arrow, using ↓ ↓ ↓ before adding ↑, is physically equivalent in a field-free setting. The absolute arrow direction is not what matters; the parallel relationship among the singles is.

7. A first worked story: carbon's 2p² electrons

Carbon's familiar neutral configuration is 1s²2s²2p². The 1s and 2s orbitals are doubly occupied, so they contribute no net spin in the simple paired-shell picture. The essential puzzle is where to put the two 2p electrons. A single p box can hold a pair [↑↓], but we have three p boxes of equal one-electron energy in the standard free-atom orbital model.

The ordinary Hund-favoured arrangement is [↑][↑][ ]. Both electrons occupy different p spatial orbitals with aligned spin projections. A paired arrangement [↑↓][ ][ ] is not Pauli-forbidden but does not represent the usual lowest-energy high-spin pattern. The two unpaired electrons can combine to total S=1 in the triplet term; the field-free carbon ground level is experimentally classified as 2s²2p² ³P₀ by NIST.

The orbital drawing captures a spin-coupled feature of the ground-state configuration but not the entire angular-momentum eigenstate. It does not independently determine the letter P or the fine-structure quantum number J=0. The full term symbol requires orbital angular momentum coupling and the other Hund rules, which we discuss only as a carefully bounded advanced connection later.

9. Why the third p electron remains unpaired at first

After placing two electrons in different p orbitals, a third 2p electron has an available third spatial orbital. Keeping the electron singly occupied there with the same chosen spin projection produces [↑][↑][↑] for p³. Nothing in Pauli forces pairing while a third distinct orbital is available, and the high-spin preference of Hund's first rule selects the familiar three-singles pattern.

Neutral nitrogen's ground configuration includes 2p³. NIST lists its ground term as ⁴S°₃/₂. The superscript 4 tells us the spin multiplicity is four, corresponding to total S=3/2. A simple ↑↑↑ orbital-box picture has three aligned spin-½ contributions and is consistent with that high-spin value. But the exact atom is described by an antisymmetrized many-electron state and its total angular-momentum coupling, not three independently identifiable electron arrows.

Students frequently misread “parallel spins” to mean that electrons must all have different spin quantum numbers. Actually, different p orbital labels permit equal ms. The Pauli principle is satisfied because the complete spin-orbital labels differ. Hund's rule then helps explain why such a parallel-spin assignment is energetically preferred in the stated free-atom context.

10. Why the fourth p electron must pair in p⁴

For a p subshell there are only three spatial orbitals. After a p³ arrangement has singly occupied all three, a fourth p electron cannot enter a fourth p spatial orbital because none exists. It must become the second occupant of one of the three p boxes if the subshell occupancy is p⁴. By Pauli, its spin projection must be opposite to the first electron in that box.

Thus the familiar p⁴ high-spin occupancy pattern is [↑↓][↑][↑]. It contains one paired spatial orbital and two singly occupied ones. There are two unpaired electrons, so the simple aligned-spin counting picture gives a total-spin value S=1, corresponding to a triplet. Neutral oxygen's evaluated ground level has configuration 2p⁴ and term ³P₂, consistent with a triplet ground term.

The fourth electron does not “prefer to pair” because pairing has become universally favourable in an energetic sense; pairing is required by the finite number of p spatial orbital labels if four electrons occupy that subshell. Which exact spin-coupled term and fine-structure level is lowest needs a more complete account than the box diagram.

12. Worked diagnostic: p² is not necessarily a pure singlet when paired

It is tempting to say that [↑↓][ ][ ] “is the singlet state” while [↑][↑][ ] “is the triplet state.” That shortcut is incomplete. A single orbital-box arrangement is usually a determinant or an occupation schematic, whereas a pure total-spin eigenstate generally requires a spin-adapted combination of determinants. When two electrons occupy the same spatial orbital, their spin state is indeed necessarily a singlet. But when they occupy different orbitals, both singlet and triplet spin couplings are possible, and different combinations need not be represented by just one simple picture.

For p², full atomic term analysis gives allowed terms such as ³P, ¹D and ¹S. Hund's first rule favours the maximum-S term (³P) for the ordinary free-atom configuration. The box diagram with two parallel arrows is a useful high-spin microstate representative, not an exhaustive description of the term and its fine structure.

When a question gives only a box diagram and asks how many electrons are singly occupied, answer that literal diagram. When it asks for a physical atomic term, multiplicity or energy ordering, determine whether the assumptions justify identifying a particular spin-coupled state. Those are different levels of analysis.

13. The d subshell supplies five orbitals, not three

For d, l=2 and ml can be −2,−1,0,+1,+2. There are five spatial d orbitals. In the elementary free-atom degenerate-shell treatment, the first five d electrons occupy these five orbitals singly and with parallel spin projections. The sixth begins opposite-spin pairing. This is the d analogue of the p rule, but the half-filled maximum now occurs at d⁵.

The usual sequence of high-spin box occupancies for d¹ through d⁵ is one through five separate ↑ arrows. For d⁶ through d¹⁰, add ↓ arrows to the already singly occupied orbitals while respecting a maximum of two electrons per box. Thus d⁵ has five unpaired electrons, d⁶ has four, d⁷ has three, d⁸ has two, d⁹ has one, and d¹⁰ has none in this idealized single-subshell sequence.

Do not automatically identify these diagrams with the ground states of every transition-metal ion or complex. In free atoms, 3d and 4s occupations compete; within ligand fields, d orbitals may split significantly in energy. A d⁶ transition-metal complex, for example, may have high-spin or low-spin states depending on the relevant orbital splitting and pairing costs.

15. How many unpaired electrons does the rule predict?

If a degenerate subshell has g spatial orbitals, then its maximum occupancy is 2g. With k electrons in the simple high-spin box construction, the number of singly occupied orbitals first grows as k for 0≤k≤g, then decreases as 2g−k for g≤k≤2g. The compact expression is u = min(k, 2g−k). This counts unpaired electrons in the high-spin orbital-box picture, not the number of spin-orbitals or the atom's full magnetic response.

u = min(k, 2g−k), 0 ≤ k ≤ 2g
g = number of equivalent spatial orbitals; k = their electron count; assumes the high-spin single-subshell box pattern.

For p⁵, g=3 and k=5, hence u=min(5,6−5)=1. For d⁷, g=5 and k=7, hence u=min(7,10−7)=3. These are useful quick checks, but they become unsafe when k is not the actual subshell electron count or the orbitals have split so that lower-spin pairing can beat spreading.

Keep the vocabulary exact. “One unpaired electron” means that, in the chosen orbital-occupation approximation, one spatial orbital is singly occupied. It does not assert that a physical electron is literally unbound or that the total magnetic moment equals one fixed universal value.

16. Spin quantum number S: adding aligned spin-½ contributions

Each electron has intrinsic spin s=½. Two spin-½ particles can couple to total spin S=0 (singlet) or S=1 (triplet). Three can couple to S=½ or S=3/2, among other coupling routes. In the maximum-spin state of u effectively unpaired, aligned spin-½ electrons within one open subshell, the highest total spin is S=u/2. Doubly occupied spatial orbitals contribute a spin singlet in the simple pair picture and therefore no net total spin.

Smax = u/2
Spin multiplicity = 2S + 1 = u + 1
For a simple maximum-spin open-subshell arrangement with u aligned unpaired electrons.

For p², u=2 implies S=1 and multiplicity 3, which is why the high-spin term is triplet. For p³, u=3 implies S=3/2 and multiplicity 4, called a quartet. For d⁵, u=5 implies S=5/2 and multiplicity 6, a sextet. For p⁴, u=2 again gives S=1; the partial shell is beyond half filling but the high-spin open-shell state can still be a triplet.

Be careful with notation. The symbol S in the expression 2S+1 denotes total spin quantum number, not the chemical symbol for sulfur; the notation ³P uses the superscript 3 for multiplicity, not for the number of electrons or principal shell.

17. Spin multiplicity is not the number of unpaired electrons

The formula 2S+1 counts the possible values of the total-spin projection MS for a given S in the absence of spin-dependent splitting. For S=1, MS=−1,0,+1, so the multiplicity is three. That is why the term is called a triplet. In the simple high-spin orbital picture of p² there are two unpaired electrons, yet the multiplicity is three, not two. Likewise three parallel unpaired spin-½ electrons yield a quartet, not a triplet.

Multiplicity is a property of a well-defined total-spin term and its spin-projection possibilities. A generic orbital box diagram with arbitrarily opposed singly occupied arrows may not itself be an eigenstate of S². In the intended school-level high-spin construction, however, u well-defined parallel singles provide a reliable route to S=u/2 and multiplicity u+1.

Actual spectral fine-structure lines may further split according to orbital angular momentum and spin-orbit coupling. The multiplicity count is not a promise that exactly that many isolated spectral lines must be visible under every experimental condition.

19. What does the word 'exchange' mean here?

In quantum mechanics identical electrons cannot be treated as labelled marbles. The full electronic wavefunction is antisymmetric when any two electron labels are exchanged. When the spin part of a two-electron state is symmetric, as in a triplet, the corresponding spatial part must be antisymmetric. When the spin is antisymmetric, as in a singlet, the spatial part can be symmetric. These different spatial symmetries lead to different electron-pair probability patterns and different expectations for the electron-electron interaction.

Exchange in this context does not mean two electrons literally swap paths while an observer watches. It is the mathematical consequence of indistinguishability and the symmetry of the electronic quantum state. In a simple model with two occupied distinct spatial orbitals, exchange contributes an energy difference between spatially symmetric and antisymmetric combinations.

The presence of an exchange contribution helps make high-spin configurations of many free atoms energetically favourable. But any universal claim that “parallel spins always lower all electrons' Coulomb energy” would be too crude: real total energies also include kinetic energy, electron-nuclear attraction, orbital relaxation, correlation and interactions with the environment.

20. A two-electron model: direct and exchange Coulomb terms

To see a controlled mathematical example, choose two distinct, normalized, orthogonal spatial orbitals a and b with the same one-electron energy ε in a simplified Hamiltonian. Define the direct Coulomb integral Jab as the average of e²/(4πε₀r₁₂) over the product density |a(1)|²|b(2)|². Define the exchange integral Kab using the cross product a*(1)b*(2)a(2)b(1), integrated over the electron coordinates. Both J and K have units of energy; they are integrals, not arbitrary numbers equal to the electron count.

For the symmetric spatial combination [a(1)b(2)+b(1)a(2)]/√2, appropriate to a spin singlet, the model electron-electron contribution is Jab+Kab. For the antisymmetric spatial combination [a(1)b(2)−b(1)a(2)]/√2, appropriate to a spin triplet, it is Jab−Kab. These expressions assume the chosen orbitals are orthonormal, and neglect the redistribution of the orbitals as the spin state changes.

Esinglet = 2ε + Jab + Kab
Etriplet = 2ε + Jab − Kab
ΔE = Esinglet − Etriplet = 2Kab
Restricted two-electron equal-orbital-energy model; r₁₂ is electron separation; e charge magnitude; ε₀ vacuum permittivity.

For the ordinary repulsive Coulomb kernel the exchange form K is non-negative for the relevant orbitals, and typically positive when spatial overlap is appreciable. The toy calculation therefore illustrates why the triplet can lie below the corresponding singlet. It is not a universal formula for actual term separations in all atoms or complexes: different orbital occupations, correlation, symmetry, spin-orbit effects and environments can change the physical comparison.

21. What an exchange hole actually means—and what it does not

Because a same-spin electron pair must have an antisymmetric spatial amplitude, the joint probability of finding two identical-spin electrons at exactly the same position is suppressed in an appropriate nonrelativistic description. In approximate mean-field language this tendency is associated with an exchange hole in the conditional probability around an electron. It is a statistical feature of the many-electron state, not a classical exclusion bubble or a force directed away from each electron.

When electrons are in different spatial orbitals and have the same spin projection, the antisymmetric spatial combination affects how often they are near one another. This can change their Coulomb interaction energy. But other terms in the total energy remain important, and exchange cannot be reduced to the slogan “parallel spins have less repulsion in all circumstances.” A real electron distribution also responds to kinetic energy, nuclear attraction and correlation beyond one determinant.

The model is most useful here as a conceptual bridge between observed free-atom high-spin preferences and the mathematical indistinguishability introduced in Node 14. It does not replace detailed physical chemistry of open-shell molecules, ligand-field theory or many-electron spectral calculations.

22. Why we do not artificially promote an electron across a subshell

A common incorrect strategy is to maximise the count of upward arrows across all subshells before considering their energies. For instance, one might move a 2s electron into a 2p orbital in a light atom merely to create an extra unpaired spin. That imagined promotion may increase the total energy far more than the gain associated with different spin couplings of degenerate p orbitals.

Hund's familiar singly-first rule is designed for an open set of degenerate orbitals within a given electronic configuration. It does not command occupation of a high-energy orbital in another subshell simply because a new upward arrow could be drawn. A legitimate ground-state prediction considers orbital energy differences, repulsion, exchange and the system as a whole.

Similarly, the relative energetic preference of 4s and 3d occupation is species-dependent. The diagonal Aufbau guide and Hund's intra-subshell distribution rule should never be combined to claim a universally exact configuration for all transition metals and ions. Our later Nodes 17–19 treat those cases individually using experimental and theoretical context.

23. From orbital diagrams to actual atomic term symbols

A spectroscopic term symbol such as ³P encodes total-spin and total-orbital-angular-momentum quantum numbers in an LS-coupling picture. The superscript 3 is the multiplicity (2S+1), so S=1. The letter P means total orbital angular momentum L=1. If a subscript J is appended, such as ³P₀ or ³P₂, it labels total angular momentum after including spin–orbit coupling.

Hund's three classical rules are often summarized as follows for the appropriate atomic configuration and coupling regime: (1) maximum S is preferred; (2) for a given S, the maximum L is preferred among admissible terms; (3) for a less-than-half-filled subshell the smallest allowed J tends to be lowest, while for a more-than-half-filled subshell the largest J tends to be lowest. These are useful atomic-term rules within their domains, not mechanical laws valid for every element, excited configuration, complex or highly relativistic regime.

Our current Node focuses on rule one and its box-diagram consequences. We mention rules two and three only to explain why carbon has ³P₀ while oxygen has ³P₂. We do not pretend the boxes themselves derive total L or all spin-orbit terms. More exact coupling analysis belongs in advanced atomic spectroscopy.

26. Spin alignment and an external magnetic field

One reason the arrows ↑ and ↓ can mislead is that students may imagine a fixed laboratory direction. The drawings choose an arbitrary spin quantization axis, usually convenient in a field-free model. All arrows reversed describes an equivalent orientation of a high-spin state in an idealized absence of magnetic fields. A field can lift degeneracies and influence which spin projection is energetically favoured relative to the field, but it does not turn Hund's first rule into a law about permanent spatial arrows.

Moreover, measured paramagnetism is a response of a quantum system to a magnetic field and temperature. Unpaired electrons often contribute to paramagnetism, but the magnitude of susceptibility and the effective magnetic moment can depend on orbital angular momentum, spin–orbit coupling, exchange interactions, level splittings and molecular environment. Counting singles in one atomic subshell is therefore a useful qualitative clue, not a universal quantitative formula for every species.

27. High-spin and low-spin: a real boundary in coordination chemistry

The equal-energy premise becomes especially important in coordination complexes. Consider six electrons distributed across five metal d-like orbitals. In an isolated-degenerate-shell illustration, d⁶ has four singles and one pair, the high-spin pattern [↑↓][↑][↑][↑][↑]. In an idealized octahedral ligand field, however, the d-like orbitals are split into a lower three-orbital group (t2g) and an upper two-orbital group (eg). If that energy gap is large enough compared with the effective pairing costs, the electrons may prefer to pair within the lower group.

A low-spin d⁶ octahedral model has t2g⁶ eg⁰, with all six electrons paired and total S=0 in that simplified description. A high-spin d⁶ octahedral model has t2g⁴ eg², with four unpaired electrons and S=2. Both obey Pauli. The difference comes from competing orbital splitting, pairing interactions and total-energy considerations. Actual spin state requires details of metal oxidation state, ligands, geometry and possible covalency.

This example is relevant to biological metal centres but belongs mainly to later coordination and molecular-structure lessons. Its purpose here is simply to prevent a dangerous overgeneralization: Hund's familiar free-atom box rule cannot be applied blindly across orbitals with significantly different energies.

28. The limited role of Hund's rule in real molecules and proteins

In molecular oxygen O₂, a pair of electrons occupies two nearly degenerate antibonding π* molecular orbitals singly in the simplest molecular-orbital picture. This high-spin arrangement is consistent with the triplet ground state and the molecule's paramagnetism. Hund's principle helps describe occupation of the near-degenerate pair, but proper molecular spectroscopy and bond properties require molecular orbital theory and the actual electronic Hamiltonian.

Metal-containing proteins can display multiple spin states influenced by chemical surroundings. For example, the electronic properties of iron centres in heme proteins depend on ligand binding, oxidation state, geometry and orbital splitting. The idea “unpaired electrons often affect magnetic and spectroscopic response” is useful, but Hund's box rule does not single-handedly predict oxygen affinity, an enzyme's mechanism or the biological role of a metalloprotein.

Organic radicals offer another instructive case. A singly occupied molecular orbital can carry an unpaired electron. Its spin and EPR behaviour cannot be inferred merely from the number of boxes in an atomic p subshell, and the radical's delocalized spin density may spread over several atoms. We retain Hund's rule as a reliable local intuition when a nearly degenerate shell model is appropriate, not as a complete theory of living systems.

29. A safe, repeatable method for any p or d orbital-box problem

First establish the given subshell electron count rather than guessing it from the element. A notation such as p⁴ means exactly four electrons in that p subshell; the entire element configuration might include many more electrons. Second count the number g of spatial orbitals: p gives three, d gives five, f gives seven. Third check that the proposed count lies between zero and 2g and that no single box has more than two arrows.

Fourth, when the question explicitly asks for the idealized high-spin ground occupancy within the degenerate subshell, put one arrow in each box until g electrons are placed; only then add opposite arrows. Fifth count singly occupied boxes. If the prompt also asks for maximum S and spin multiplicity, use S=u/2 and 2S+1=u+1 under the high-spin convention. If it asks for the actual atom or coordination complex, check whether energy splitting or configuration exceptions may invalidate a bare occupancy prediction.

Last, distinguish your conclusions: “Pauli-allowed” is a state-validity conclusion; “Hund-favoured under the stated approximation” is an energy-preference conclusion; “experimentally established ground term” is a claim that must be supported by spectroscopy or authoritative atomic data.

30. Use a counterexample to avoid a memorized false law

Suppose a hypothetical atom has two lower orbitals separated by only 0.01 eV and the relevant effective exchange and pairing effects favour one electron in each. It may display a high-spin state. Now imagine the same electron count in a chemical environment where the upper orbital is raised by several electronvolts while the effective pairing penalty remains smaller. A paired lower-orbital configuration could become energetically preferable. Pauli allows either provided the complete spin-orbital states are distinct.

The numbers in that thought experiment are illustrative, not measured data for a named molecule. The reasoning demonstrates the limitation: a high-spin state depends on relative total energies, not on an absolute command to maximize spin at any cost. The same caution prevents students from applying Hund's first rule to force an extra 3d electron out of a substantially lower-energy 4s state, or vice versa, without species-specific evidence.

Historically, Hund's rules organize many observed free-atom spectral term patterns. Their success is not a proof that every interacting quantum system has the highest spin ground state. Quantum chemistry must determine the actual competing energies.

31. How a paired-orbital energy penalty differs from exclusion

An orbital can be doubly occupied only by opposite-spin electron states. That is the Pauli restriction. The energetic cost of placing two electrons in the same spatial orbital is a different matter: their spatial probability distributions affect Coulomb repulsion, and the total state responds through orbital relaxation and correlation. In elementary models one often introduces a qualitative pairing energy, but it is not a universal constant for all atoms or all orbitals.

In a degenerate p or d subshell, spreading single electrons through different orbitals can change the interaction energy favourably and permit a larger total spin. Yet deciding between that arrangement and a pair in one orbital requires an energy comparison. Pauli says [↑↑] is impossible in one spatial orbital; Hund may favour [↑][↑] over [↑↓][ ] in equivalent spatial orbitals. Neither says two oppositely oriented arrows attract one another in a classical way.

32. Interpret p⁴, p⁵ and d⁷ diagrams without false certainty

In p⁴ the familiar high-spin occupancy has two unpaired electrons, but the choice of which p box contains the pair is not a physical selection of a preferred laboratory direction in a spherical atom. In p⁵ one unpaired electron remains, and in p⁶ all boxes are fully paired. The boxes are distinguishable labels for a convenient orbital basis, not physical compartments that electrons move between along a visible trajectory.

Likewise, d⁷ in a degenerate high-spin scheme has three unpaired electrons, because seven electrons distributed over five spatial orbitals require at least two pairs and leave three singles in the Hund-favoured pattern. However, simply reading “d⁷” from an oxidation-state guess is risky for a specific transition-metal compound: the actual electronic structure can involve metal-ligand covalency, effective orbital energy splitting or mixed configurations.

Use the unpaired-count formulas for well-specified model questions. For actual spectroscopic ground terms, consult evaluated atomic level data or an explicitly justified quantum-chemical model.

33. Review: the scientific chain from observation to rule

We began with an experimentally motivated question: different Pauli-allowed arrangements within an open shell do not all have the same measured energy. Hund's empirical term-ordering rules provided a powerful organizing idea. In the introductory free-atom subshell model, the high-spin arrangement places one electron in each degenerate orbital with parallel spin before pairing. Pauli restricts two per spatial orbital, while exchange symmetry and electron interactions help account for energetic differences among allowed states.

We then made the idea quantitative. A p subshell has three spatial orbitals, so its idealized maximum-spin unpaired counts across p⁰–p⁶ are 0,1,2,3,2,1,0. A d subshell has five, so d⁵ reaches five unpaired electrons in the simple high-spin model. With u aligned unpaired spin-½ electrons, S=u/2 and multiplicity 2S+1=u+1. NIST term symbols for neutral carbon, nitrogen and oxygen support the main predictions while reminding us that J and L require more detailed angular-momentum coupling.

The limitations are as important as the rule: arrows are spin-projection labels, orbital boxes are approximations, exchange is not classical swapping, and actual ground states depend on the full Hamiltonian. The next chapter will combine the orbital filling principles to build complete neutral ground configurations from hydrogen through calcium, without pretending that a set of mnemonics explains every element or ion.

34. Boundary between this node and the next topics

This chapter establishes how to draw and interpret a ground-state orbital diagram for a specified partial subshell. It does not exhaustively construct the full electron configuration of every element from H through Ca, which is the learning objective of Node 1.A.2.16. It does not derive all transition-metal 3d/4s exceptions or their ion configurations, handled in later nodes. And it does not attempt full ligand-field diagrams, heme-protein biophysics or molecular orbital spectroscopy.

Keeping these boundaries clear also makes the present lesson more teachable. We understand the reason for spreading electrons across a degenerate subshell, we know the formal and empirical evidence for the rule, and we have reliable tools for drawing p and d high-spin diagrams. The next lesson can therefore focus on combining valid shell capacities, approximate energy order and intra-subshell spin assignment to form full neutral atomic configurations.
