Unit 01 · Sub-unit 1.A · Topic 1.A.2 · Node 16

Ground-State Electron Configurations from Hydrogen to Calcium

One neutral atom at a time: a rigorous, accessible journey through twenty configurations and their electronic logic.

Learning outcomes: Construct full and noble-gas configurations for H–Ca, draw s/p orbital box diagrams, apply Aufbau, Pauli and Hund, count electrons independently, identify mistakes and distinguish neutral atoms from ions and excited states.

Contents · 1.A.2.16

1. The puzzle: how do twenty electrons arrange themselves?

A configuration such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ can look like a cryptic formula. Yet it is a compact answer to a genuine scientific question: how are the electrons in an isolated atom distributed among the accessible quantum states at the lowest total energy? Earlier chapters introduced atomic spectra, orbitals, quantum numbers and three filling principles. Our next job is to combine these ideas into a method that works reliably from hydrogen to calcium.

Imagine increasing the atomic number by one at each step. A neutral atom at each step also contains one additional electron. At first the 1s orbital fills; later new subshells become occupied. Rather than learning twenty unconnected strings, we will watch how the occupation pattern grows and why the order changes at potassium.

2. Ground state is an energy statement, not a drawing style

The ground state is the lowest-energy state of an isolated atom. Other electronic states can exist, and energy from light or collisions can excite them. A subshell configuration names how many electrons occupy 1s, 2s, 2p and other subshells; it does not fully specify all the coupled quantum properties of a many-electron atom.

A legal arrow diagram is not automatically the ground state. For example, an electron promotion may preserve the total electron number and satisfy Pauli exclusion but still place the atom in an excited configuration. The filling sequence taught here summarizes the observed ground-state occupations of the first twenty neutral elements. Modern atomic spectroscopy and many-electron calculations provide evidence beyond the mnemonic itself.

3. First identify the species: neutral atom, ion or excited atom?

Atomic number Z counts protons. The electron count of an electrically neutral atom is exactly Ne = Z. Carbon has Z = 6, so a neutral carbon atom has six electrons. Sodium has Z = 11, so neutral sodium has eleven. The mass number of an isotope counts protons plus neutrons; it does not tell us how many electrons a neutral atom has.

This chapter does not assign ionic electron configurations. The symbols Na, Mg, K and Ca below denote neutral free atoms, whereas Na⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ are different species with different electron counts. Understanding the neutral configuration is necessary but insufficient for explaining the chemistry of solvated biological ions. Ionic occupancy and electron removal will receive a dedicated treatment in Node 19.

4. Three principles, three different questions

The Aufbau approach supplies an approximate ordering of subshell occupation in a low-energy neutral atom. Pauli exclusion states that two electrons cannot occupy the same complete spin-orbital quantum state; in a simple orbital-box diagram one spatial orbital holds at most an up-and-down pair. Hund's first rule helps distribute electrons in a set of approximately equivalent orbitals, favouring single occupations with parallel spins before pairing.

Do not use Hund's rule to jump an electron into a much higher-energy subshell just to increase the number of unpaired electrons. Do not use Pauli's principle as a complete energy-ordering rule. And do not treat the familiar Aufbau list as an exact universal law for every ion and transition metal. Each principle has an appropriate scope.

5. Decode every symbol in 3p⁴

The digit 3 is the principal quantum number n and identifies the third shell. The letter p means orbital angular-momentum quantum number l = 1. The superscript 4 tells us that four electrons occupy the entire 3p subshell, not just one orbital. A 3p subshell contains three different spatial orbitals; its four electrons are distributed among those states.

The expression 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ therefore contains 2 + 2 + 6 = 10 electrons. That addition is not optional decorative arithmetic: it is the easiest independent check of a proposed neutral configuration. A correct answer must simultaneously respect orbital capacity, the observed occupation pattern and the required electron number.

6. Subshell capacity comes from counting allowed quantum states

For an s subshell l = 0, so ml can take one value; there is one spatial orbital. For a p subshell l = 1, ml takes −1, 0 and +1; there are three spatial orbitals. Each spatial orbital permits two electron spin states, conventionally shown by ↑ and ↓. The resulting capacities are s² and p⁶. The general independent-orbital capacity of a subshell is 2(2l + 1).

A shell capacity 2n² follows when all allowed subshells for that principal number are counted. It is an upper bound, not the order in which a many-electron atom must fill shells. This is why calcium's n = 3 shell still lacks occupied 3d states while the neutral atom contains electrons in 4s.

7. The precise H–Ca sequence

For this set of twenty neutral ground states, the occupation sequence is 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s. The successive capacities are 2, 2, 6, 2, 6 and 2; their sum is 20. The 1s subshell fills by helium, 2s by beryllium, 2p by neon, 3s by magnesium, 3p by argon, and 4s by calcium.

This is a sequence of occupied subshell types and observed neutral ground configurations, not a claim that exactly the same orbital energies hold for all charge states or chemical environments. Scandium, Z = 21, introduces occupied 3d states in the next chapter.

8. Why potassium uses 4s rather than 3d

After argon has filled 3p, one might incorrectly write the next electron in 3d merely because the principal number is smaller. Neutral potassium actually has [Ar] 4s¹, and neutral calcium has [Ar] 4s². The 4s occupied ground states are supported by observed atomic data. Qualitatively, penetration, shielding and the resulting many-electron energy balance help explain why a simple principal-quantum-number ordering fails.

The assertion '4s is always lower in energy than 3d' is too broad. Effective orbital energies change with nuclear charge, electron occupancy and ionization. This lesson makes the narrow, evidence-backed claim for neutral K and Ca. The nuanced 3d/4s cases beyond calcium and electron loss from transition-metal atoms belong in Nodes 17–19.

9. The five-step configuration algorithm

First read atomic number Z. Second set the neutral electron count to Z. Third fill only allowed subshells in the H–Ca sequence without exceeding each capacity. Fourth, when the question requests orbital boxes, use Pauli-compatible opposite arrows in a doubly occupied box and Hund-compatible single arrows across equivalent p boxes before pairing. Fifth, sum every superscript and compare it with Z.

The final step is a mathematical consistency test: for 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴ the sum is 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 16, so the notation accounts for neutral sulfur's sixteen electrons. The count alone does not prove the state has lowest energy, but it quickly rejects many false answers.

10. An orbital box is a state label, not a tiny planetary track

A spatial orbital is a quantum state that is described by a wavefunction; in elementary diagrams, it is represented by a box. An s subshell has one box and a p subshell has three. ↑ and ↓ represent spin projections, not literal clockwise and anticlockwise spinning charge. The box boundaries do not imply that the electron is confined to a rectangular chamber.

Equivalent p orbitals may be drawn in any convenient left-to-right order. For oxygen, the arrangements [↑↓][↑][↑] and [↑][↑↓][↑] indicate the same simple subshell occupation pattern. A reader should infer permitted occupation and unpaired counts, not the electron's exact position or trajectory.

11. Hydrogen and helium: the 1s story

Hydrogen is the first neutral atom, with Z = 1. Its electron occupies 1s and we write 1s¹. In box notation it is a single ↑ in the 1s box. Helium has Z = 2 and fills 1s², drawn [↑↓]. Both electrons occupy the same spatial orbital with opposite spin projections. They do not have identical complete sets of four quantum numbers.

At helium the n = 1 shell has reached its allowed capacity. The abbreviation [He] used for later elements refers to this closed 1s² core. Helium itself is conventionally written 1s² in a full configuration; a bracketed core is a shorthand for the inner electrons, not an actual helium atom inside a larger atom.

12. Lithium and beryllium: an s subshell in a new shell

Lithium (Z = 3) retains the filled 1s² core and adds one 2s electron, producing 1s² 2s¹ or [He] 2s¹. Beryllium (Z = 4) adds the second 2s electron, giving 1s² 2s² or [He] 2s². Notice that neither needs a 2p electron yet, although the 2p orbitals are allowed quantum states at n = 2.

This gives the first striking pattern: the next shell begins with an s occupation. The two-electron [He] core stays intact in the usual configurations of the second-period atoms that follow.

13. Boron, carbon and nitrogen: the first three p positions

Boron, Z = 5, adds the first electron to the 2p subshell: 1s² 2s² 2p¹. Carbon, Z = 6, is 2p² after the same inner 1s² 2s² core. Nitrogen, Z = 7, is 2p³. For the free-atom high-spin orbital-box model, carbon's two p electrons occupy separate boxes with parallel arrows; nitrogen's three p electrons occupy all three p boxes singly.

This is an opportunity to separate notation from diagrams. The superscript '3' in 2p³ tells us the electron count of the p subshell. It does not say that all three electrons occupy a single orbital. Hund's rule, applied after Pauli, tells us the preferred introductory box pattern.

14. Oxygen, fluorine and neon: pairing in 2p

Oxygen has eight electrons, with 2p⁴: three p boxes become singly occupied before the fourth electron pairs with one of them, giving [↑↓][↑][↑]. Fluorine adds a fifth p electron, giving [↑↓][↑↓][↑]. Neon has 2p⁶ and all three boxes paired, [↑↓][↑↓][↑↓]. The entire neon configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, totaling ten electrons.

A p subshell has three orbitals rather than six. Its maximum of six electrons arises because each of the three orbitals has two spin possibilities. For an isolated p occupation with k between 0 and 6, the simple maximum-spin diagram has min(k, 6−k) unpaired p electrons. That result is not a universal model of molecular magnetism.

15. The neon core: a useful turning point

At Ne, Z = 10, both 2s and 2p are full. The 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ configuration is a closed-shell reference, and a ten-electron [Ne] core is an efficient shorthand for heavier elements. Students should distinguish a full subshell from an entire closed shell: beryllium fills 2s, but neon fills both 2s and 2p.

The sequence also reveals periodic repetition. Lithium begins the second shell with 2s¹, while sodium will begin the third with 3s¹ after the larger closed [Ne] core. Such parallels help explain later group behaviour, although configuration similarity is not by itself a complete chemical mechanism.

16. Sodium and magnesium start the third shell

Sodium (Z = 11) adds 3s¹ to the ten-electron [Ne] core, so its neutral ground configuration is [Ne] 3s¹. Magnesium (Z = 12) pairs a second electron in the 3s orbital, giving [Ne] 3s². Expanded, magnesium is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s². Its electron total is 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 12.

Both Na and Mg commonly appear in biological discussions as ions. Yet Na in this neutral table is not Na⁺, and Mg here is not Mg²⁺. Keeping the atom's charge in view is part of correct scientific notation, not a minor formality.

17. Aluminium through phosphorus: occupation of 3p

After magnesium fills 3s, aluminium (Z = 13) begins 3p¹, silicon (Z = 14) gives 3p², and phosphorus (Z = 15) gives 3p³. All three retain [Ne] 3s². Their expanded configurations are respectively 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹, the same prefix with 3p², and the same prefix with 3p³.

The 3p orbitals form another set of three possible spatial states. Thus the pattern from B to N repeats, with the principal quantum number raised from 2 to 3. The broad analogy is useful for understanding periodicity; it does not make a silicon atom identical to carbon.

18. Sulfur, chlorine and argon complete the third p set

Sulfur (Z = 16) has [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. Its 3p box pattern is [↑↓][↑][↑]; the fourth p electron forms the first pair. Chlorine (Z = 17) has [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵, with two p pairs and one single electron. Argon (Z = 18) has [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶, with all three 3p orbitals fully occupied.

The ten-electron neon core plus eight additional electrons in 3s and 3p produces the eighteen-electron argon core. That becomes a useful shorthand for the next two atoms. Do not mistake this completed 3p subshell for an occupied 3d subshell: neutral argon's ground configuration has no 3d electrons.

19. Potassium and calcium: the endpoint of this chapter

Potassium has nineteen electrons, and the neutral ground state is [Ar] 4s¹. Calcium has twenty and is [Ar] 4s². This is the experimentally supported continuation after 3p⁶. Neither neutral ground-state occupation includes 3d electrons; the first neutral atom in the next sequence with 3d occupation is scandium, Z = 21.

For Ca the full subshell expression is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s², and the sum 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 20 confirms the electron total. The core notation [Ar] 4s² is concise and complete. We will not treat neutral Ca as if it were the physiologically abundant Ca²⁺ ion.

21. What [He], [Ne] and [Ar] actually mean

A bracketed noble-gas symbol stands for exactly the electrons of that earlier closed-shell neutral atom: [He] = 1s² = 2 electrons, [Ne] = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ = 10 electrons and [Ar] = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ = 18 electrons. Writing [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵ is exactly equivalent to expanding the ten-electron core and appending seven more electrons.

The bracket is shorthand, not evidence of a helium, neon or argon nucleus hidden in the atom. Do not replace the entire argon configuration with [Ar] in an answer that asks you to show how the subshells are occupied; expand the earlier [Ne] core or the complete expression when needed. For atoms preceding helium there is no earlier noble-gas core.

22. Expand and compress without losing electrons

To expand [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵, write out the [Ne] core: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵. The count 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 equals 17, so the neutral element is chlorine. To compress 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p², recognize that the first ten electrons make up [Ne], giving [Ne] 3s² 3p² for silicon.

Choose the largest preceding closed noble-gas core, not an arbitrary convenient number. [Ne] stands for ten electrons, not ten additional electrons; add the suffix only once. A short configuration should retain every electron outside the chosen core.

23. Shell population and subshell occupation are different summaries

For calcium, the full subshell occupation is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s². Grouping terms by principal quantum number gives the shell-population sequence 2, 8, 8, 2. For sulfur it is 2, 8, 6; for neon it is 2, 8. These sequences summarize totals by shell, but do not identify s versus p orbitals.

A shell total is not the same as a full configuration and does not make 2n² a mandatory stepwise filling law. The third principal shell has available d states but neutral Ca still fills 4s first. A question asking about 3p electrons requires the 3p superscript specifically, not the total of all n = 3 electrons.

24. Orbital diagrams: six procedures that prevent errors

Write the correct subshell configuration before drawing boxes. Give every occupied s subshell exactly one spatial box, and each occupied p subshell exactly three. Fill the earlier completed subshells as [↑↓]. For the open p subshell place one parallel-spin arrow in each box before pairing, consistent with Hund's first rule. Pauli requires opposite arrows for a two-electron box. Finally count arrows and confirm that the diagram contains Z electrons.

For oxygen the compact valence portion is 2s [↑↓], 2p [↑↓][↑][↑]. Chlorine has 3s [↑↓], 3p [↑↓][↑↓][↑]. Permuting the equivalent p boxes does not invalidate a correct pattern. These drawings depict occupations, not exact electron positions.

25. Periodic repetition: why Li resembles Na and B resembles Al

Li has [He] 2s¹; Na has [Ne] 3s¹; K has [Ar] 4s¹. Be, Mg and Ca have an outer s² in these isolated neutral ground configurations. Similarly B, Al have p¹ after s², while C/Si, N/P, O/S and F/Cl show repeated p², p³, p⁴ and p⁵ patterns at successive principal shell numbers.

This is an important precondition for periodic trends. It does not mean that two elements with similar outer occupation have equal sizes, identical ionization energies or the same biology. Such quantitative chemistry depends on nuclear charge, shielding, bonding and environment, topics developed in later units.

26. Atomic spectroscopy is evidence; the filling chart is a model

Real atomic energy levels can be measured from the wavelengths of light emitted and absorbed, together with other experimental techniques. Evaluated compilations such as NIST's atomic data identify neutral ground-state configurations. The H–Ca list agrees with those evaluated assignments; the simple order provides a convenient classroom construction.

A configuration such as 2p⁴ does not fully specify the total angular momentum quantum numbers of a free atom. Spectroscopic term symbols encode finer details than the subshell shorthand. Those distinctions help prevent the mistaken belief that a single arrow diagram describes the entire exact many-electron wavefunction.

27. Do not confuse an isotope with a new electronic element

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 both have Z = 6, so free neutral atoms of either isotope have six electrons and the same occupation 1s² 2s² 2p² in the introductory ground-state model. Oxygen-16 and oxygen-18 similarly share Z = 8 and the same neutral electron count. A neutron change can alter nuclear behaviour and certain isotope-sensitive properties without adding a neutral electron.

This is especially relevant to biochemical isotope-tracer experiments: a labelled atom in a molecule is not necessarily a free neutral atom, but its chemical identity is set by proton number, not mass number. Distinguish isotope notation, ionic charge and electronic excitation instead of combining them.

28. Original diagram: compare the shell totals without losing the 4s story

A principal-shell tally becomes especially revealing after argon. The n = 3 count remains eight for neutral K and Ca, while the n = 4 count changes from zero to one and two. The diagram below is therefore a visualization of totals, not a replacement for the underlying subshell notation or an assertion that the third shell has a maximum of eight quantum states.

29. Worked construction 01: Hydrogen (H)

Start with Z = 1, hence 1 electrons for neutral H. No predecessor is needed; this is the first atom. The single electron goes into 1s, giving one unpaired s electron. The full answer is 1s¹, and its permitted compact form is 1s¹.

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=1; the sum is 1, which matches Z = 1. Its one occupied 1s spatial orbital is singly occupied. No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

30. Worked construction 02: Helium (He)

Start with Z = 2, hence 2 electrons for neutral He. Relative to neutral Hydrogen, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. A second electron pairs in 1s, closing the n = 1 shell. The full answer is 1s², and its permitted compact form is 1s².

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=2; the sum is 2, which matches Z = 2. The 1s box now contains opposite spin arrows. No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

31. Worked construction 03: Lithium (Li)

Start with Z = 3, hence 3 electrons for neutral Li. Relative to neutral Helium, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. The filled 1s core is retained and a new 2s subshell begins. The full answer is 1s² 2s¹, and its permitted compact form is [He] 2s¹.

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=2 + 2s=1; the sum is 3, which matches Z = 3. No open p subshell is left in the highest occupied shell of this neutral ground-state configuration. No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

32. Worked construction 04: Beryllium (Be)

Start with Z = 4, hence 4 electrons for neutral Be. Relative to neutral Lithium, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. The second 2s electron completes the 2s orbital, without entering 2p. The full answer is 1s² 2s², and its permitted compact form is [He] 2s².

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=2 + 2s=2; the sum is 4, which matches Z = 4. No open p subshell is left in the highest occupied shell of this neutral ground-state configuration. No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

33. Worked construction 05: Boron (B)

Start with Z = 5, hence 5 electrons for neutral B. Relative to neutral Beryllium, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. With 2s occupied by two electrons, the additional electron enters the 2p set. The full answer is 1s² 2s² 2p¹, and its permitted compact form is [He] 2s² 2p¹.

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=2 + 2s=2 + 2p=1; the sum is 5, which matches Z = 5. The 2p subshell contains 1 electron(s), and its introductory box diagram has 1 unpaired 2p electron(s). No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

34. Worked construction 06: Carbon (C)

Start with Z = 6, hence 6 electrons for neutral C. Relative to neutral Boron, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. The second 2p electron occupies a different degenerate p orbital in the ground-state box picture. The full answer is 1s² 2s² 2p², and its permitted compact form is [He] 2s² 2p².

Audit the subshell occupancies: 1s=2 + 2s=2 + 2p=2; the sum is 6, which matches Z = 6. The 2p subshell contains 2 electron(s), and its introductory box diagram has 2 unpaired 2p electron(s). No ionic or excited configuration has been substituted for the requested neutral atom.

35. Worked construction 07: Nitrogen (N)

39. Worked construction 11: Sodium (Na)

Start with Z = 11, hence 11 electrons for neutral Na. Relative to neutral Neon, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation. 40. Worked construction 12: Magnesium (Mg)

41. Worked construction 13: Aluminium (Al)

Start with Z = 13, hence 13 electrons for neutral Al. Relative to neutral Magnesium, this step adds one electron to the developing ground-state occupation.
