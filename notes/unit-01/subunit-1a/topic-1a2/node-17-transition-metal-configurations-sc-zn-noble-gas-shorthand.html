1. The unexpected question after calcium

At the end of the previous chapter, calcium had twenty electrons arranged as [Ar] 4s². Its fourth-shell s subshell was filled, but the third-shell d subshell had not yet gained an electron. Now imagine the next neutral atom, scandium, whose nucleus carries twenty-one protons. Where can the additional electron be accommodated? The next available ground-state occupancy is not a new 4p electron. It is [Ar] 3d¹ 4s². This simple fact creates the central scientific problem of the first transition series.

A beginner may expect electron shells to fill in a neat numerical order: all of shell 3 before shell 4, then shell 5. Experimental electronic structures do not follow that rule. The relative energies of orbitals depend on the whole atom—nuclear charge, the other electrons and their mutual interactions. The 4s subshell becomes occupied for potassium and calcium; the 3d subshell begins filling from scandium. Understanding this sequence requires us to connect the quantum numbers studied earlier with actual observed neutral-atom configurations.

Our story covers ten elements, atomic numbers 21–30, with their shared argon core. We will learn how to reconstruct the configurations, understand what a configuration does and does not tell us, and recognise why a simple filling slogan can fail. A separate following chapter investigates chromium and copper in depth, and a later chapter deals with ions.

2. The idea of a series, rather than ten unrelated facts

From scandium (Sc) through zinc (Zn), the nuclear charge rises one unit at a time. We can compare neutral atoms while keeping a useful common starting point: the electron arrangement of argon, [Ar], containing eighteen electrons. This lets us focus on the three to twelve electrons outside that core rather than writing every inner subshell anew.

A consistent pattern appears: electrons populate the 3d and 4s subshells together. Several elements follow the simple pattern [Ar] 3dk 4s², while chromium and copper adopt different measured distributions. The apparent irregularities become intelligible once we stop confusing a learning-order diagram with a universal law of energy.

The phrase first transition series commonly refers to the row Sc–Zn in introductory teaching. In strict chemical terminology, the definition of a transition element depends on an incomplete d subshell in the atom or in a common cation; zinc's filled d shell is an important classification qualification, not a mistake in our chosen Sc–Zn learning span.

3. Why argon is the natural shorthand core

Argon, atomic number 18, has the occupied subshells 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶. Counting their superscripts gives 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 18. When we write [Ar], these eighteen electrons are already included. A symbol in square brackets is not a new subshell; it is an abbreviation for the complete electronic configuration of a noble-gas atom.

For example, [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² describes a neutral iron atom with 18 + 6 + 2 = 26 electrons. Writing 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s² means exactly the same occupation pattern. The abbreviated form becomes most useful when we want to compare a long row without losing sight of the d and s electrons.

Always count what the bracket hides: [He] = 2; [Ne] = 10; [Ar] = 18; [Kr] = 36. For Sc–Zn in the present chapter, the relevant core is [Ar], not [Ne] and not [Kr].

4. A careful reminder about shells and subshells

The symbol 3d refers to principal quantum number n = 3 and orbital angular-momentum quantum number l = 2. The symbol 4s refers to n = 4 and l = 0. The numbers label states, not a mandatory order in which all electrons must be inserted in every environment. A 3d subshell has five spatial orbitals, each capable of holding at most two electrons of opposite spin, so its capacity is ten. A 4s subshell has one spatial orbital and holds at most two electrons.

Those capacities come from quantum-number counting: for a d subshell, ml = −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, five possible values. Each has two possible spin projections, ms = +½ or −½. Therefore 5 × 2 = 10 possible one-electron states. For s, ml = 0 only, so there are two spin states. Notice that these are state counts; they do not tell us the complete energy of a many-electron atom.

5. Why 4s appears before the first 3d electron

Potassium, Z = 19, has [Ar] 4s¹, and calcium, Z = 20, has [Ar] 4s² in their neutral ground states. In many beginner filling charts, 4s is drawn before 3d. Such a chart is a useful summary of how the first neutral configurations are built, but its arrows are not timeless energy inequalities. The spatial distributions of 4s and 3d differ, and the energies of the whole many-electron states change as Z rises.

The 4s orbital penetrates regions close to the nucleus in a way that can make it energetically competitive before substantial 3d occupation. As nuclear attraction and electron–electron interactions change across Sc–Zn, the balance changes. It is therefore more accurate to say neutral potassium and calcium occupy 4s before neutral scandium begins 3d occupation than to say “4s is always below 3d”.

Orbital energy, an ionisation experiment, and the order in which we write terms in a configuration are three different things. This distinction will protect you from an especially common mistake when you later study Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.

6. How five d orbitals accommodate a population

We often draw five equal-height boxes for the five 3d spatial orbitals of an isolated atom in a spherically symmetric environment. A single arrow means one electron with a chosen spin projection; two opposite arrows in one box mean a pair. Hund's first rule is the useful starting point: when occupying orbitals of the same subshell in the introductory free-atom model, populate separate orbitals with parallel spin before pairing them.

Thus d¹ means one singly occupied orbital; d³ means three singly occupied orbitals; d⁵ means five singly occupied orbitals; d⁶ means one pair and four singly occupied orbitals; d⁸ means three pairs and two singly occupied orbitals; and d¹⁰ means five pairs. None of these sketches specifies a literal orbit around the nucleus, and none by itself supplies the full atomic term or the electronic state of a molecule.

7. Original diagram: orbital capacity is not orbital energy

Capacity and energy must be kept separate. A 3d subshell can hold ten electrons, but that does not require every neutral atom to reach d¹⁰ before any electron enters 4s. Likewise, the fact that two 4s electrons are present in iron does not mean they must remain there when iron forms an ion. Capacity is fixed by quantum numbers; occupation is established by the energetics of the particular species.

9. Original data graphic: a growing d population

The diagram makes two useful features visible. First, 3d occupancy generally rises as we move across the row. Second, the number in 4s is not always two. A plot of occupancy is not a plot of energy: it tells us what the evaluated ground-state configurations are, not why their total energies differ by a particular number of joules.

10. Scandium starts the d series

Scandium has Z = 21. Its eighteen-electron argon core leaves three electrons. The observed neutral state is [Ar] 3d¹ 4s²: one electron occupies a 3d spatial orbital and two share the 4s orbital with opposite spins. Sc is a particularly clean introductory example because there is no competition over which of the five 3d boxes must pair. A free Sc atom's fine-structure ground level is described spectroscopically as ²D3/2; that label is not the same thing as the symbol 3d¹.

Audit for Z = 21: [Ar] 3d1 4s2 → 18 + 1 + 2 = 21 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 1.

11. Titanium adds one 3d electron

Titanium has Z = 22 and therefore four electrons beyond [Ar]. Its neutral ground occupation is [Ar] 3d² 4s². In the introductory box picture, the two 3d electrons occupy different orbitals with parallel spins, consistent with Hund's first rule. This tells us the simple occupancy, while the observed atomic term arises from the allowed coupling of orbital and spin angular momenta. The answer is obtained by scientific comparison with the observed ground state, not just by drawing a second arrow.

Audit for Z = 22: [Ar] 3d2 4s2 → 18 + 2 + 2 = 22 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 2.

12. Vanadium reveals the d³ pattern

Vanadium has Z = 23: [Ar] 3d³ 4s². Three different 3d orbitals are occupied once in the usual box model, and the 4s pair remains. The count 18 + 3 + 2 = 23 works immediately. A classifying question about vanadium should distinguish the three d electrons from the five electrons beyond the argon core. Which count is called valence depends on the model and chemical context; it is not safe to equate valence with the largest n alone in transition-metal chemistry.

Audit for Z = 23: [Ar] 3d3 4s2 → 18 + 3 + 2 = 23 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 3.

13. Chromium: mark the observation, reserve the mechanism

Chromium has Z = 24, but its experimentally assigned neutral ground configuration is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. A naive continuing pattern [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² contains the same twenty-four electrons, yet it is not the observed ground occupation. Electron count is therefore necessary but not sufficient to select the lowest-energy state. Chromium has five singly occupied d orbitals plus a 4s electron in a simple arrow model; its neutral ground term is ⁷S₃. We will analyse the close competing many-electron states rigorously in Node 18 rather than use the unsupported claim that “half-filled is always stable.”

Audit for Z = 24: [Ar] 3d5 4s1 → 18 + 5 + 1 = 24 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 5.

14. Manganese has the d⁵ 4s² pattern

Manganese, Z = 25, is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² in its isolated neutral ground state. Five 3d spatial orbitals can each be occupied singly in the model, while 4s is paired. It is instructive to compare manganese with chromium: both have d⁵, but their neutral 4s populations differ. Manganese therefore has seven electrons beyond [Ar], while chromium has six. This comparison makes clear that the d exponent alone is not the total count outside the core.

Audit for Z = 25: [Ar] 3d5 4s2 → 18 + 5 + 2 = 25 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 5.

15. Iron and the first d pair

Iron has Z = 26. NIST gives [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² for the neutral free atom. Following the introductory Hund pattern, the sixth 3d electron makes the first pair, leaving four singly occupied 3d orbitals. The 4s orbital holds a pair. The construction is [Ar] (18) + d⁶ (6) + s² (2) = 26. Iron's chemical fame comes mainly from its ions and coordinated species, which are different systems; do not transfer a free Fe atom's box pattern uncritically to haem or an iron–sulfur protein.

Audit for Z = 26: [Ar] 3d6 4s2 → 18 + 6 + 2 = 26 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 4.

16. Cobalt continues pairing in 3d

Cobalt has Z = 27 and neutral [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s². In the isolated-orbital introductory model, seven d electrons mean two paired boxes and three singly occupied boxes. The four-subshell history and total electron count remain unchanged: an argon core of eighteen, a d population of seven and s population of two. Students often call 3d⁷ “seven unpaired” because they read the exponent as an unpaired count. That is incorrect once pairing begins.

Audit for Z = 27: [Ar] 3d7 4s2 → 18 + 7 + 2 = 27 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 3.

17. Nickel: eight electrons, five orbital spaces

Neutral nickel has Z = 28 and [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s². Five 3d orbitals can accommodate eight electrons as three pairs and two single occupations in the common Hund box representation. Thus a d⁸ shell is not completely filled: it needs two more d electrons to reach d¹⁰. Nickel's evaluated neutral ground state can be represented by this dominant configuration, but the detailed electron-correlation and spectral-level story goes beyond a two-number shorthand.

Audit for Z = 28: [Ar] 3d8 4s2 → 18 + 8 + 2 = 28 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 2.

18. Copper: another experimentally observed shift

For Z = 29, copper's neutral ground occupation is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ rather than the simple extrapolation [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s². With all five 3d spatial orbitals doubly occupied, the d subshell has no unpaired electrons in the basic picture, but the 4s¹ electron remains unpaired. A student who says “copper has no unpaired electrons because d is full” ignores its 4s electron. The relative energies and why the actual configuration differs from naive filling are left for Node 18.

Audit for Z = 29: [Ar] 3d10 4s1 → 18 + 10 + 1 = 29 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 0.

19. Zinc: the end of this selected row

Zinc has Z = 30 and neutral [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s². Both the 3d subshell and the 4s subshell are filled in the free-atom occupation pattern. Counting 18 + 10 + 2 = 30 confirms the assignment. Zinc sits in the d block, and the introductory “Sc to Zn” row includes it, but according to the common IUPAC definition it is not a transition element because neither the neutral atom nor its usual Zn²⁺ ion has an incomplete d subshell. This is a classification boundary, not an exception to the electron-count rule.

Audit for Z = 30: [Ar] 3d10 4s2 → 18 + 10 + 2 = 30 electrons; 3d orbitals singly occupied in the introductory Hund picture: 0.

20. Original orbital illustration: three different d populations

Scandium has a single d electron; iron's sixth d electron must share one of the five spatial orbitals already occupied; zinc's d subshell is fully paired. The boxes explain a common test question: the exponent in d⁸ is not the number of unpaired electrons. If five boxes receive one electron each, only the remaining three of the eight electrons form additional pairs, leaving two single occupations.

21. Why two notations can be equally correct

Some reference tables write [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², while classroom diagrams may show [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶. For a configuration considered purely as an occupation record, these forms communicate the same populated subshells and the same electron counts. Neither string tells the reader that an electron was physically placed at a particular time nor supplies a universal 3d–4s energy comparison.

A clear convention is to present first-series ground configurations in [Ar] 3da 4sb order, matching NIST's neutral-state table. If an examination answer uses [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ for Fe, it represents the same occupancy; the mathematical order of factors or labels is not a new atomic state. Still, never change the exponents, because those determine which quantum states are populated.

22. What does the word ground state assert?

A neutral atom has many allowed excited states, and its electrons are not rigid beads in separate boxes. A ground-state configuration denotes the occupied subshells associated with its lowest-energy atomic state, usually expressed as the dominant configuration in an appropriate atomic-state description. We compare the ground state with possibilities whose occupancies or couplings may differ.

When students obtain a configuration from a simple filling algorithm, they have produced a prediction. We validate the prediction with experimentally established spectroscopy and reliable atomic reference data. Chromium and copper show why that extra step matters. NIST's evaluated tables make ground-state assignments for neutral atoms; their separate positive-ion column demonstrates why we should not casually copy the neutral pattern to a cation.

23. From spectral evidence to quantum configurations

Atomic spectra contain lines connecting allowed energy levels. Measuring wavelengths gives photon energy differences through ΔE = hc/λ, with h as Planck's constant, c the speed of light and λ the photon wavelength. The positions and intensity patterns of many transitions, interpreted using angular-momentum selection rules and atomic theory, support the identification of atomic energy levels and their configurations.

For instance, a spectral term such as ⁵D₄ for the neutral iron ground level provides more information about total spin and angular momenta than the shorthand [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² alone. We should not confuse an electron-occupation label with a fully specified quantum state. Term symbols and fine structure are significant subjects, but they cannot be reconstructed with certainty from nothing more than the diagonal Aufbau chart.

Always distinguish independent evidence from instructional approximation. Our diagrams are teaching models that agree with important occupation counts; atomic spectroscopy and modern calculations test much richer descriptions.

24. How electron interactions change orbital energy discussions

In a one-electron hydrogen-like atom, energy depends primarily on n in the nonrelativistic Coulomb model. In a many-electron atom, the electron feels the attraction of the nucleus and repulsion from other electrons. Inner-electron shielding, the radial distribution of different orbitals, electron exchange, spin–orbit effects and correlation change relative state energies. Thus 3d and 4s cannot be treated as static shelves with permanent vertical positions.

In the central-field approximation we speak of orbital energies, but the ground atomic term is found by considering the total energy of the many-electron state. Changing the number of 3d and 4s electrons also changes the self-consistent electrostatic environment. A simplistic energy sketch can suggest a trend; it cannot prove that Cr must be 3d⁵4s¹ or Cu must be 3d¹⁰4s¹.

25. Neutral atoms versus ions: a deliberately marked boundary

The neutral first-series configurations are often used as starting points for predicting cations. This is a useful application but not the same question. Ionisation removes electrons and changes the energy balance and orbital occupations. In a common classroom treatment, transition-metal cations lose the outer ns electrons before the (n−1)d electrons. Yet specific ions and charge states require care, especially when multiple low-energy atomic states compete.

This chapter teaches neutral isolated atomic ground states only. Node 19 will teach electron removal carefully and will examine examples including Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺. The distinction is biologically crucial: iron in haem and zinc in enzymes are usually present as coordinated ions, not as free neutral gas-phase atoms.

26. d-block position is not an automatic chemical definition

By periodic-table position, scandium through zinc occupy the first row of the d block. That educational label is convenient because this region tracks the involvement of 3d orbitals. The strict IUPAC-style chemical definition of a transition element instead refers to an element whose atom has an incomplete d subshell or which can produce cations with an incomplete d subshell. Under this definition, zinc is excluded because neutral Zn is d¹⁰ and its usual Zn²⁺ cation is d¹⁰.

The boundary also helps explain why a chapter on “transition-metal configurations Sc–Zn” does not claim that all ten elements are chemically identical. Their oxidation states, bonding, biological functions and magnetic properties differ. A shared orbital-building scheme is a useful starting framework, not a theory that predicts every property of the row.

27. A real biological connection: iron, copper and zinc

Iron is indispensable in oxygen transport, respiration and many enzymes, but the relevant electron structures are those of Fe ions in coordination environments. Ligands around a metal can split the energies of d orbitals and change spin states. The neutral isolated Fe atom's [Ar] 3d⁶4s² is therefore the starting vocabulary, not a stand-in for the electron configuration of haem iron in every oxidation and spin state.

Copper occurs in redox enzymes, including electron-transfer proteins. The free neutral Cu atom has [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹, but biological copper chemistry frequently involves Cu(I) or Cu(II) states bound to donor atoms from proteins. Zinc commonly provides structural or catalytic functions in enzymes; its chemistry frequently involves Zn(II), a d¹⁰ ion that does not generally undergo redox cycling in biological conditions. None of these realities can be inferred from a neutral box diagram alone.

These comparisons justify why life-science students should master neutral-atom notation and then study ionic configurations, coordination chemistry and ligand fields as separate, connected steps.

28. A reliable five-stage method for any Sc–Zn problem

Stage 1: Identify the element and its atomic number Z. For a neutral atom, the electron count equals Z. Stage 2: Expand the 18-electron [Ar] core mentally; the number outside is Z − 18. Stage 3: Determine the experimentally established 3d and 4s ground-state populations rather than applying a mnemonic blindly. Stage 4: Check that 0 ≤ d ≤ 10 and 0 ≤ s ≤ 2, and that 18 + d + s = Z. Stage 5: If asked about unpaired 3d electrons, use the introductory Hund orbital model, not the raw d superscript.

The method detects impossible exponents and arithmetic mistakes. It also highlights a limitation: both [Ar] 3d⁴4s² and [Ar] 3d⁵4s¹ total twenty-four electrons, so simple counting alone cannot choose the correct chromium ground state. Independent evidence remains necessary.

29. Thirty fully worked examples: method before memorisation

These original worked exercises build several different abilities: identifying a neutral configuration, auditing electron counts, reading shorthand, interpreting orbital occupancy, and deciding when a claim overreaches the evidence.

Worked example 1. Fe, from Z to configuration

A neutral Fe atom has 26 electrons. Remove the 18-electron argon core: 26 − 18 = 8. The evaluated free-atom ground state divides these eight as 3d⁶ and 4s². Therefore Fe = [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²; check 18 + 6 + 2 = 26.

Worked example 2. Co electron audit

Co has Z = 27, so there are 27 − 18 = 9 electrons beyond [Ar]. The neutral evaluated occupation is 3d⁷4s². Summing 18 + 7 + 2 gives 27; this verifies count but does not establish energy by itself.

Worked example 3. Ni, pairing question

Ni is [Ar] 3d⁸4s². Five orbital boxes receive one d electron each; three more electrons pair in three boxes. Therefore two d orbitals remain singly occupied in the elementary free-atom Hund diagram.

Worked example 4. Zn, capacity test

Zinc has [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s². In 3d, five orbital boxes each contain two electrons, while 4s contains two. The d subshell has zero singly occupied boxes and the total electron number is 18 + 10 + 2 = 30.

Worked example 5. Sc without the full Aufbau chart

For Sc (Z = 21) there are 3 electrons beyond [Ar]. NIST assigns 3d¹4s². To explain it, refer to the changing relative 3d/4s state energies after Ca instead of the false claim that all n = 3 states must fill first.

Worked example 6. Ti orbital box sketch

Titanium is [Ar] 3d²4s². Place two up arrows in two distinct d boxes and an up/down pair in 4s in the introductory representation; the d single-occupation count is two.

Worked example 7. V number of electrons beyond the core

Vanadium has Z = 23. The expression [Ar]3d³4s² gives three d plus two s = five beyond the argon core. Five is not the number of unpaired d electrons; that number is three in the simple d³ model.

Worked example 8. Cr, distinguish counts from energy

Two candidates [Ar]3d⁴4s² and [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ each sum to 24. NIST's measured neutral-ground occupation is the latter; an electron-count test cannot distinguish their relative energies.

Worked example 9. Mn versus Cr

Mn has [Ar]3d⁵4s², Cr has [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Both have five d electrons, but the total outside [Ar] differs: 7 for Mn and 6 for Cr. Atomic number distinguishes the cases.

Worked example 10. Cu, a deceptive d¹⁰ problem

Copper is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. The 3d subshell is filled, so all five d orbitals are paired. The 4s electron remains singly occupied; the neutral free-atom configuration is not wholly paired.

Worked example 11. Shorthand to expanded notation

Write [Ar] as 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶. Substituting this in [Ar]3d⁶4s² gives the expanded Fe configuration; the occupied subshells and 26-electron total stay unchanged.

Worked example 12. Expanded to shorthand

Starting from 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁷4s², recognise the first five subshells as [Ar]. The remaining 3d⁷4s² identifies cobalt, because 18 + 7 + 2 = 27.

Worked example 13. Detect an impossible 3d exponent

The notation [Ar]3d¹¹ cannot be an ordinary subshell occupation. A d subshell has five orbitals and two spin states per orbital, so its maximum occupancy is ten.

Worked example 14. Detect an impossible 4s exponent

The expression [Ar]3d⁶4s³ violates the 4s capacity. One s spatial orbital has two available spin states; three electrons cannot occupy that same 4s subshell.

Worked example 15. Read 3d⁸ correctly

The 3 labels n, the d labels l = 2, and superscript 8 counts occupied electron states in that subshell. It does not name the fourth period, eight d orbitals or eight unpaired spins.

Worked example 16. Atomic number from a shorthand

For [Ar]3d⁷4s², Z = 18 + 7 + 2 = 27 for a neutral atom. That is Co. You must know whether the atom is neutral; an ion may have a different electron count for the same nuclear Z.

Worked example 17. How many d orbital spaces remain singly occupied in d⁶?

Five boxes are occupied singly first. The sixth electron pairs with one already present, giving 1 paired box + 4 singly occupied boxes; four d electrons remain unpaired in the simple box model.

Worked example 18. How many d electrons are needed after Ni to fill d?

Ni has d⁸, and the capacity is d¹⁰. Two more d electrons would fill it; this question concerns capacity, not the actual neutral sequence of Cu without considering its 4s difference.

Worked example 19. Same electrons, different written order

[Ar]4s²3d⁸ and [Ar]3d⁸4s² have equal subshell populations. Both denote neutral Ni's ground-state occupation; writing 3d first avoids implying a universal filling timetable.

Worked example 20. Why d¹⁰ is not a noble gas

Zinc's [Ar]3d¹⁰4s² has full 3d and 4s subshells, but the chemical family is not a noble gas. Its nuclear charge and the absence of 4p⁶ distinguish it from krypton, [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p⁶.

Worked example 21. Argon core arithmetic

Verify [Ar] = 1s² + 2s² + 2p⁶ + 3s² + 3p⁶ = 18. Avoid adding [Ar] to the expanded argon subshells a second time.

Worked example 22. Spectroscopic term versus occupancy

Neutral Fe's ground term is often listed as ⁵D₄, while the configuration is [Ar]3d⁶4s². The term describes total angular-momentum coupling; the occupancy notation does not specify the level by itself.

Worked example 23. Identify a category mistake

A student claims “4s is always lower in energy because it was filled first.” The claim does not follow: relative energies change with species and occupancy, and the Aufbau chart is a teaching approximation.

Worked example 24. Check Sc orbital capacities

Sc has 3d¹ and 4s², both legal occupancies. One d orbital is singly occupied; the other four d orbitals are vacant in the box sketch. A filled 4s orbital cannot accept a third electron.

Worked example 25. Cobalt d pairing count

For d⁷, distribute five single electrons then pair two of them. The result is two paired d boxes and three singly occupied d boxes, not seven unpaired electrons.

Worked example 26. Compare Mn and Fe d populations

Neutral Mn is d⁵4s² and Fe is d⁶4s². Both use [Ar]; their atomic numbers are 25 and 26. Increasing d occupancy from five to six creates the first paired d box in the elementary Hund picture.

Worked example 27. Count a Cu electron correctly

The copper expression [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ contains 18 + 10 + 1 = 29 electrons. Do not force 4s² just because 4s holds a maximum of two: capacity is a maximum, not an obligation.

Worked example 28. Core and outside-core are not synonyms for chemistry

For Zn, 30 − 18 = 12 electrons occur outside [Ar]. That does not imply twelve chemically equivalent 'outer-shell' electrons; 3d and 4s have different spatial and energetic roles.

Worked example 29. What does a filled d subshell imply?

In the elementary free-atom d¹⁰ box model, five 3d orbitals each hold an opposite-spin pair, giving zero singly occupied 3d orbitals. This statement alone does not predict bulk material magnetism.

Worked example 30. Why an ion question needs a new starting point

A question asks for Fe²⁺ while quoting neutral Fe = [Ar]3d⁶4s². First recognise that Fe²⁺ has 24 electrons, not 26; determining its actual subshell distribution is the separate ionic-configuration task in Node 19.

30. Twelve scientific and biological cases

Each case below tests where a neutral-atom occupation is useful and where it stops being a satisfactory physical description.

Case 1. An observed scandium spectrum

Sc I means neutral scandium, whereas Sc II means singly ionised scandium in spectroscopic notation. The neutral spectrum supports assigning the Sc I ground-state [Ar]3d¹4s²; quoting the Sc II configuration would answer a different question.

Case 2. Vanadium in an alloy

A neutral isolated vanadium atom has [Ar]3d³4s², but metallic vanadium in an alloy is a collective electronic system. A simple single-atom orbital box cannot alone predict the alloy's band structure, conductivity or magnetic response.

Case 3. Chromium as a prediction test

An elementary Aufbau mnemonic produces d⁴4s² for Cr; comparison with evaluated neutral-state spectroscopy rejects this as the ground assignment. The scientific lesson is to distinguish prediction from empirical validation.

Case 4. Manganese in biology

Mn-containing enzymes typically coordinate manganese as ions with ligand-dependent states. Free neutral Mn's d⁵4s² pattern offers vocabulary for electron counting, but ligand geometry, oxidation state and spin determine the enzyme's electronic description.

Case 5. Iron in haem

Oxygen-binding haem iron interacts with the porphyrin and ligands; its state changes with oxidation, bonding and spin environment. The neutral Fe ground configuration must not be pasted unaltered into a haem description.

Case 6. Cobalt in cobalamin

Cobalamin contains a coordinated cobalt centre, not an isolated neutral gas-phase Co atom. Its observed chemistry requires coordination bonding and changes in oxidation state; neutral [Ar]3d⁷4s² is only a preliminary reference.

Case 7. Nickel metalloenzymes

Some enzymes use nickel centres with unusual coordination and oxidation chemistry. The neutral Ni atom's [Ar]3d⁸4s² cannot alone determine a protein's magnetic behaviour or catalytic pathway.

Case 8. Copper electron transfer

Copper centres in proteins frequently interconvert between different oxidation states. The neutral Cu d¹⁰4s¹ anomaly does not make every copper-containing enzyme a one-unpaired-electron system.

Case 9. Zinc structural enzyme

Zinc ions commonly help stabilise protein domains or provide Lewis-acid catalysis. The neutral Zn d¹⁰4s² and common Zn(II) d¹⁰ statements describe different species, even though both have a filled 3d shell.

Case 10. Why NIST has two columns

A reference compilation displays separate neutral and positive-ion occupations because ionisation can rearrange orbital populations. Never select the first cation column to answer a question about a free neutral atom.

Case 11. Spectroscopy and atomic terms

Atomic spectral lines establish energy-level differences; identifying terms such as ⁵D₄ also uses selection rules and theoretical interpretations. An observed line is evidence, not a microscopic photograph of a 3d electron.

Case 12. Classification of zinc

Zinc belongs to the first d-block row but is usually not counted as a transition element under the incomplete-d criterion. This explains why curricular convenience and strict chemical definitions need not match exactly.

31. Twenty-five high-value misconceptions and corrections

A misconception is corrected not by replacing one slogan with another, but by identifying which scientific rule or observation it misuses.

Pitfall 1: “All n = 3 subshells fill before n = 4.”

Potassium and calcium have occupied 4s before 3d begins to fill in scandium; n alone does not set the filling order.

Pitfall 2: “The 4s orbital is always lower in energy than 3d.”

Many-electron orbital energies depend on atomic and ionic state; there is no universal 4s–3d ranking.

Pitfall 3: “[Ar] means eighteen additional electrons on top of 1s²…3p⁶.”

The bracket already represents those eighteen inner electrons; expanding and then adding it double-counts.

Pitfall 4: “Every Sc–Zn atom has 4s².”

Neutral Cr and Cu have 4s¹ according to NIST.

Pitfall 5: “Chromium must be d⁴4s² because Z = 24.”

Electron counting alone permits several distributions; spectroscopy establishes the d⁵4s¹ neutral ground state.

Pitfall 6: “Copper must be d⁹4s² because Z = 29.”

The evaluated neutral Cu occupation is d¹⁰4s¹.

Pitfall 7: “A d electron count equals the number of unpaired electrons.”

A d⁸ Hund diagram has only two singly occupied d orbitals; d¹⁰ has none.

Pitfall 8: “Every d-block element satisfies the strict transition-element definition.”

Zinc has a filled d subshell in its atom and common Zn²⁺ ion.

Pitfall 9: “A full d shell is the same as a noble-gas configuration.”

Zn is d¹⁰ but lacks the complete 4p⁶ shell of krypton and is not a noble gas.

Pitfall 10: “Writing 4s before 3d describes a different Fe state.”

[Ar]4s²3d⁶ and [Ar]3d⁶4s² have the same subshell occupations.

Pitfall 11: “The 3d label means three d orbitals.”

The 3 specifies principal shell n; a d subshell has five spatial orbitals.

Pitfall 12: “The 3d subshell can hold eleven electrons.”

Five spatial orbitals times two spin states gives ten maximum.

Pitfall 13: “4s holds an unlimited number until the next row.”

An s subshell has one spatial orbital, so its electron capacity is two.

Pitfall 14: “A configuration uniquely determines all spectral lines.”

Energy levels, couplings, transitions and their selection rules require information beyond a bare occupancy string.

Pitfall 15: “All measured atoms obey the simplest filling mnemonic.”

The Cr and Cu neutral-ground occupations are counterexamples.

Pitfall 16: “Neutral atom is interchangeable with its usual biological ion.”

Ions have different electron numbers and coordination environments.

Pitfall 17: “The magnetic behaviour of an alloy equals one free-atom box diagram.”

Bulk magnetism also involves electronic bands, interactions and collective ordering.

Pitfall 18: “An iron protein must contain Fe d⁶4s².”

That is the isolated neutral Fe occupation, not the full electronic description of an ion in a ligand field.

Pitfall 19: “Eight electrons outside [Ar] mean eight unpaired electrons.”

In Fe, those eight are d⁶4s²; the elementary box model has four singly occupied d orbitals and a paired 4s.

Pitfall 20: “If the electrons add up, the ground-state configuration is proved.”

Total count checks arithmetic and capacity but cannot compare state energies.

Pitfall 21: “Hund's rule proves Cr and Cu exceptions on its own.”

The energetic competition includes electron interactions and radial distributions; an isolated slogan is not a complete explanation.

Pitfall 22: “Spin-up arrows are literal tiny classical spinning balls.”

Arrows label spin projections in a quantum model, not classical rotations.

Pitfall 23: “3d and 4s are separate rigid containers in real space.”

They describe quantum orbitals with spatial probability distributions; electrons are indistinguishable.

Pitfall 24: “NIST's first positive-ion column is the neutral column.”

The table separates neutral atomic states from first cation states.

Pitfall 25: “All first-row metals have exactly the same biological role.”

Different ionisation states, bonding geometries and ligand environments produce very different biological functions.

32. Fifty original CSIR-NET/SET-style short-answer and conceptual questions

These are original revision problems, not claimed previous-year examination questions. Try each prompt before opening its explanation. The answer is concise, but the scientific reason is included.

Question 1. A neutral atom has [Ar]3d⁶4s². Identify Z.

Answer: 26. 18 + 6 + 2 = 26, the neutral atomic number of iron.

Question 2. Which neutral atom has [Ar]3d⁷4s²?

Answer: Co. The electron count is 18 + 7 + 2 = 27, cobalt.

Question 3. What does [Ar] contribute to an electron count?

Answer: 18. The noble-gas core covers 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶.

Question 4. How many 3d spatial orbitals exist?

Answer: 5. For l = 2, m_l has five values: −2 through +2.

Question 5. Maximum electrons in the 3d subshell?

Answer: 10. Five spatial orbitals each accept two opposite spin states.

Question 6. Maximum electrons in the 4s subshell?

Answer: 2. The s subshell contains one spatial orbital with two spin projections.

Question 7. Write the observed neutral Sc shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d¹4s². NIST assigns one electron to 3d, with the 4s pair already present.

Question 8. Write the observed neutral Ti shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d²4s². 18 + 2 + 2 = 22; two 3d electrons occupy the ground configuration.

Question 9. Write neutral V shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d³4s². 18 + 3 + 2 = 23, vanadium.

Question 10. Write neutral Mn shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d⁵4s². Manganese has 25 electrons, including five in 3d and two in 4s.

Question 11. Write neutral Fe shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d⁶4s². Its free neutral ground state has 26 electrons.

Question 12. Write neutral Ni shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d⁸4s². It contains 28 electrons and does not have a filled 3d subshell.

Question 13. Write neutral Zn shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². 18 + 10 + 2 = 30; both listed subshells are full.

Question 14. Write the measured neutral Cr shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. The naive d⁴4s² prediction does not give neutral chromium's ground state.

Question 15. Write the measured neutral Cu shorthand.

Answer: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Its d subshell is fully occupied while its 4s has one electron.

Question 16. How many electrons lie outside [Ar] in Ni?

Answer: 10. 28 − 18 = 10; eight occupy 3d and two occupy 4s.

Question 17. How many electrons lie outside [Ar] in Cu?

Answer: 11. 29 − 18 = 11; ten occupy 3d and one occupies 4s.

Question 18. In the basic d⁵ Hund pattern, how many singly occupied d orbitals?

Answer: 5. Each of the five degenerate spatial boxes is singly occupied before pairing.

Question 19. In the basic d⁶ Hund pattern, how many singly occupied d orbitals?

Answer: 4. The sixth d electron creates the first opposite-spin pair.

Question 20. In the basic d⁷ Hund pattern, how many singly occupied d orbitals?

Answer: 3. Two paired orbital boxes and three single boxes contain seven d electrons.

Question 21. In the basic d⁸ Hund pattern, how many singly occupied d orbitals?

Answer: 2. Three pairs plus two singles contain eight d electrons.

Question 22. In d¹⁰, how many singly occupied d orbitals?

Answer: 0. All five boxes are paired.

Question 23. Does 4s²3d⁶ differ in occupied subshells from 3d⁶4s²?

Answer: No. Reordering subshell terms in notation does not change their electron exponents.

Question 24. Does electron count alone select Cr's ground state?

Answer: No. Both d⁴4s² and d⁵4s¹ contain 24 electrons; energies and evidence decide.

Question 25. What is wrong with an exponent 4s³?

Answer: Capacity violation. An s subshell has one spatial orbital and two possible spin states.

Question 26. How many electrons does a neutral Co atom contain?

Answer: 27. Its nuclear charge Z = 27 and neutrality imply 27 electrons.

Question 27. Does a free Fe atom represent all states of biological iron?

Answer: No. Biological iron normally exists in ligand-bound ionic or electronically complex environments.

Question 28. Does [Ar]3d¹⁰4s² describe a noble gas?

Answer: No. This is neutral Zn; krypton additionally contains a filled 4p subshell.

Question 29. Why is Sc–Zn called a d-block span?

Answer: 3d occupation. Across this region, the 3d subshell participates in ground-state electron populations.

Question 30. Under the common incomplete-d criterion, is Zn a transition element?

Answer: No. Neutral Zn and its usual Zn²⁺ ion have full 3d¹⁰.

Question 31. For free neutral Cr, how many 4s electrons?

Answer: 1. NIST gives 3d⁵4s¹.

Question 32. For free neutral Mn, how many 4s electrons?

Answer: 2. The ground configuration is 3d⁵4s².

Question 33. What exactly does the superscript in 3d⁷ count?

Answer: 7 electrons. The superscript denotes subshell occupancy, not orbital number or unpaired count.

Question 34. What physical quantity does an atomic spectral wavelength determine?

Answer: Photon energy difference. Use ΔE = hc/λ for an assigned emission or absorption transition.

Question 35. Does a diagonal Aufbau arrow prove an exact total energy ordering?

Answer: No. It is an introductory heuristic; correlated atomic-state energies need fuller theory.

Question 36. Name one independently evaluated source of neutral configurations.

Answer: NIST. NIST's electronic-configuration compilation includes a neutral column and separate cation data.

Question 37. Why is an isolated atom not the same as a bulk metal?

Answer: Different interacting system. A solid has band formation and many-atom interactions not captured by a free-atom occupation alone.

Question 38. How many occupied d orbitals does neutral Sc have in its box diagram?

Answer: 1. Sc has d¹, so only one of five spatial d orbital boxes is occupied.

Question 39. How many occupied d orbital boxes does neutral Mn have?

Answer: 5. Its d⁵ ground occupation singly fills all five boxes in the introductory model.

Question 40. Which has more d electrons, Co or Ni, and by how many?

Answer: Ni by one. Co is d⁷ and Ni d⁸.

Question 41. Identify the error: neutral Cu is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s².

Answer: It has 30 electrons. This gives Zn's count, not Cu's Z = 29.

Question 42. Identify the error: neutral Fe has [Ar]3d⁶4s¹.

Answer: It has 25 electrons. The total is 18 + 6 + 1 = 25, not Fe's 26.

Question 43. Which two neutral Sc–Zn ground states have 4s¹?

Answer: Cr and Cu. They are NIST's 3d⁵4s¹ and 3d¹⁰4s¹ assignments.

Question 44. Why can't d⁸ fit in four orbital boxes without violating Pauli?

Answer: Capacity 8 is possible but placement not ground Hund pattern. Four boxes could hold eight by pairing, but that would leave one empty and is not the introductory lowest-spin-compatible Hund occupation of five equal-energy d orbitals.

Question 45. How does a positive ion differ from its neutral parent?

Answer: Fewer electrons. Removing electrons changes electron count and often the preferred subshell population.

Question 46. When using [Ar]3d⁵4s², what is the atom's Z if neutral?

Answer: 25. 18 + 5 + 2 = 25, manganese.

Question 47. Could Zn be d¹⁰ but still form compounds?

Answer: Yes. A filled 3d subshell does not imply chemically inert noble-gas behaviour.

Question 48. What is the main scope of the next Node 18?

Answer: Cr/Cu exceptions. The next chapter explains the detailed energy competition and limits of simple filling predictions.

Question 49. What is the main scope of Node 19?

Answer: Ionic configurations. It addresses changes in electron populations on ionisation and 4s–3d removal issues.

Question 50. What is the safest order of solving an Sc–Zn neutral problem?

Answer: Identify Z, assign observed occupation, audit capacity/count. Arithmetic verifies a configuration, while empirical reference helps select the ground state.

33. Ten multiple-choice problems with explanations

Choose a single best answer. Each distractor is designed to reveal a specific conceptual error, not simply a spelling mistake.

MCQ 1. Which row is the measured neutral Fe occupation?

  1. [Ar]3d⁶4s²
  2. [Ar]3d⁷4s¹
  3. [Ar]3d⁵4s³
  4. [Ne]3d⁶4s²

Correct option A. The correct answer has 26 electrons and agrees with NIST. The second has a different subshell occupation despite the same count; the third violates 4s capacity; the fourth uses an incorrect core.

MCQ 2. Which configuration is measured for neutral chromium?

  1. [Ar]3d⁴4s²
  2. [Ar]3d⁵4s¹
  3. [Ar]3d⁶4s⁰
  4. [Ar]3d³4s³

Correct option B. Cr neutral ground is d⁵4s¹. The d⁴4s² option is a naive mnemonic result; the d⁶ option is not its ground state; 4s³ violates capacity.

MCQ 3. Which sample has a completely paired 3d subshell in the elementary neutral free-atom picture?

  1. V
  2. Fe
  3. Cu
  4. Ni

Correct option C. Neutral Cu has d¹⁰. V d³, Fe d⁶ and Ni d⁸ leave singly occupied 3d orbitals in the simple Hund model.

MCQ 4. Which proposed electron count is correct for neutral Co?

  1. 18 + 7 + 2 = 27
  2. 18 + 7 + 2 = 26
  3. 10 + 7 + 2 = 27
  4. 18 + 7 + 3 = 28

Correct option A. The [Ar] core is 18 electrons; adding d⁷ and 4s² yields 27. The other options use arithmetic errors, the wrong core, or impossible 4s occupancy.

MCQ 5. A d⁷ free-atom Hund box pattern has how many singly occupied d orbitals?

  1. 7
  2. 5
  3. 3
  4. 2

Correct option C. Distribute five single electrons first, then pair two of them; three single boxes remain.

MCQ 6. Which claim about [Ar]3d⁶4s² is supported?

  1. It describes neutral Fe's occupied subshells
  2. It proves 4s is always lower energy
  3. It describes Fe²⁺ unchanged
  4. It fixes all Fe molecular spin states

Correct option A. The shorthand records the neutral free-atom occupation. Energy ordering, ion formation and molecular spin require more context.

MCQ 7. Which element is in the Sc–Zn d-block span but usually excluded by the incomplete-d transition-element definition?

  1. V
  2. Fe
  3. Zn
  4. Co

Correct option C. Neutral Zn and common Zn²⁺ have d¹⁰, so zinc fails the incomplete-d criterion while still occupying the d block.

MCQ 8. A neutral atom has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Which is it?

  1. Ni
  2. Zn
  3. Cr
  4. Cu

Correct option D. 18 + 10 + 1 = 29 electrons, the neutral Cu ground occupation.

MCQ 9. Which statement best represents the role of an orbital diagram?

  1. A literal electron path
  2. An exact atomic energy measurement
  3. A schematic model of electron occupation and spin projections
  4. A complete chemical bonding model

Correct option C. Box-and-arrow diagrams represent the allowed orbital occupancies, not physical trajectories or complete many-electron interactions.

MCQ 10. Why cannot one select the Cr ground state by adding superscripts alone?

  1. All candidates have different Z
  2. More than one subshell arrangement can have the same electron count
  3. 4s holds ten electrons
  4. Nuclear charge is unknown

Correct option B. d⁴4s² and d⁵4s¹ both total 24, but only the second is the evaluated neutral-ground occupation.

34. Ten integrative challenges with reasoned solutions

The final set combines occupancy, evidence, spin diagrams and the limits of an atomic model. Solutions are provided for careful self-assessment.

Integrative challenge 1. Reverse identification

Problem: An anonymous neutral atom from Sc–Zn has eight electrons outside [Ar], with six in d and two in s. Identify the atom and explain how you excluded Mn and Co.

Reasoned solution: Z = 18 + 8 = 26, so it is Fe, d⁶4s². Mn has seven outside [Ar], while Co has nine.

Integrative challenge 2. Two possible configurations

Problem: A student writes two Cr candidates, d⁴4s² and d⁵4s¹. Design a reasoned way to choose, without appealing to electron-count arithmetic.

Reasoned solution: Both contain 24 electrons. Consult evaluated spectroscopy or trustworthy neutral ground-state reference data; measured Cr is d⁵4s¹. Energy comparison requires more than the simple filling mnemonic.

Integrative challenge 3. A shorthand expansion

Problem: Convert neutral Ni's shorthand to every occupied subshell, explain the core and independently count all electrons.

Reasoned solution: Ni = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁸4s². The first five subshells are [Ar] = 18. Sum 2+2+6+2+6+8+2 = 28.

Integrative challenge 4. Unpaired comparison

Problem: Compare the elementary free-neutral-atom 3d singly occupied counts for V, Fe and Zn, and explain why this is not a bulk magnetic prediction.

Reasoned solution: V d³ → 3, Fe d⁶ → 4, Zn d¹⁰ → 0. Bulk magnetism depends on many-atom interactions, electronic bands and environment.

Integrative challenge 5. Find a false inference

Problem: A text says Cu d¹⁰ implies all electrons are paired. Identify the missing subshell and correct the statement.

Reasoned solution: Neutral copper is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. The 3d electrons are all paired in the basic model, but the 4s¹ electron is not.

Integrative challenge 6. Metal–protein transfer test

Problem: Why would pasting free Fe = d⁶4s² directly into a haem diagram be scientifically inadequate?

Reasoned solution: Haem contains coordinated iron in a specific oxidation and ligand field state. Electron number, orbital energy splitting and spin may differ from free neutral Fe.

Integrative challenge 7. d-block versus transition element

Problem: Explain how zinc can be a d-block element in the studied row without satisfying the strict incomplete-d transition-element criterion.

Reasoned solution: Classification by periodic location and classification by incomplete d occupation are distinct conventions. Neutral Zn and common Zn²⁺ are d¹⁰.

Integrative challenge 8. Counterexample to a fixed order

Problem: Use K, Ca, Sc and Cr to explain why '4s always holds two before d fills' fails as a universal statement.

Reasoned solution: K = [Ar]4s¹; Ca = [Ar]4s²; Sc = [Ar]3d¹4s²; neutral Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Relative energies and occupations are state dependent.

Integrative challenge 9. Spectroscopic vs configuration label

Problem: Why does knowledge of neutral Fe's [Ar]3d⁶4s² not by itself determine the label ⁵D₄?

Reasoned solution: The configuration gives occupied subshells; the spectroscopic term additionally depends on coupling of electron angular momenta and energy-level details.

Integrative challenge 10. Design an error-proof answer

Problem: A friend claims [Ar]3d⁷4s³ for Z = 28. Diagnose both arithmetic and quantum-state errors; give the correct neutral Ni occupation.

Reasoned solution: 18+7+3=28 is arithmetically possible but 4s³ exceeds its capacity of two. The measured neutral Ni ground state is [Ar]3d⁸4s².

35. Consolidation: what we now know and what is still open

We started with a problem: the calcium arrangement [Ar]4s² is followed by scandium's [Ar]3d¹4s². The sequence demonstrated that orbital labels and rigid shell-by-shell filling are not the same thing. In a many-electron atom, the energetically preferred arrangement must be examined for each species. The evaluated neutral configurations across Sc–Zn are readily compared once we recognise the eighteen-electron [Ar] core, ten-electron 3d capacity and two-electron 4s capacity.

The pattern is useful but not mechanically uniform. Sc, Ti, V, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni and Zn follow [Ar]3dk4s² with the appropriate k; neutral Cr and Cu are [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Arithmetic validates electron counts; spectroscopy and evaluated atomic data identify ground configurations. The 3d arrow diagrams convey intuitive occupancies but cannot on their own establish every spectral term, ion, complex or magnetic property.

Node 1.A.2.19 then separates neutral atoms from ions and examines electron removal. Those two investigations build directly on the careful experimental and conceptual distinctions developed here.
