1. A prediction fails: the scientific problem

Imagine that you have just learned to construct electron configurations for the first transition series. Scandium, titanium and vanadium suggest a tidy sequence: keep the eighteen-electron argon core, place two electrons in 4s, and then add electrons to 3d. If that pattern continued without interruption, a neutral atom with twenty-four electrons would be [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². Yet the evaluated ground-state configuration of neutral chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. Five elements later, simple sequential filling would give copper [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s², whereas the observed ground state is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

These are not arithmetic mistakes. The proposed and observed configurations each contain the correct total number of electrons. Their difference concerns how electrons distribute among nearly competing subshells in the lowest-energy state. The scientific task is to explain why a broadly useful filling guide does not uniquely determine every real atom's ground state.

This lesson begins with the predictions, examines the measured ground states, develops a careful physical explanation, and finally tests the boundaries of that explanation. We will repeatedly distinguish a teaching rule, an observed fact and a model used to interpret that fact. Next Node 19 will investigate ions and the 4s–3d question; it should not be silently answered by transferring the neutral-atom pattern to every charged species.

2. What is meant by the ground state?

A free atom has many possible quantum states. The ground state is its lowest-energy allowed state under specified conditions, usually the isolated, nonrelativistic-to-relativistic atomic problem without an externally imposed field. A configuration is a compact occupancy description, not the full wavefunction: 3d⁵ 4s¹ says how many electrons occupy the 3d and 4s subshells in the dominant description. It does not provide every spin–orbital coupling, electron correlation or mixing coefficient.

An atom can absorb energy and reach an excited state without changing its chemical identity. Therefore a mathematically allowed occupancy and an experimentally identified ground-state occupancy are different claims. Two candidate arrangements can both satisfy electron-counting rules while only one is the lowest-energy arrangement. Spectroscopy and critically evaluated atomic structure data tell us which assignment is supported.

Even the phrase “an electron moves from 4s to 3d” is best interpreted as a comparison of two candidate distributions. It need not mean a literal electron was first placed in one orbital and physically migrated during the atom's formation. The atom reaches an allowed state through physical processes; our diagram is a convenient bookkeeping device.

3. Recover the prerequisites: n, l, m and spin

In a simple independent-electron picture, 4s has principal quantum number n = 4 and azimuthal quantum number l = 0. There is one spatial 4s orbital, with at most two electrons of opposite spin projections. The 3d subshell has n = 3, l = 2; its five allowed magnetic quantum numbers, −2, −1, 0, +1 and +2, correspond to five spatial orbitals, each with capacity two. Thus the d subshell has maximum occupancy ten, not five.

Pauli's exclusion principle prevents two identical fermions from occupying the same spin orbital. In an elementary box diagram, Hund's first rule favors an unpaired, same-spin arrangement across degenerate orbitals within a configuration before pairing is introduced. But neither principle by itself says that the energetic cost of 4s occupancy must always be smaller than 3d occupancy. For that, the interaction of all electrons with the nucleus and with one another matters.

4s capacity = 2   |   3d orbitals = 2l + 1 = 5   |   3d capacity = 2(2l + 1) = 10

4. The guide we are testing: Madelung (n + l)

The familiar Madelung or n + l order places lower values of n + l before higher values, with lower n first in a tie. A 4s orbital has n + l = 4; a 3d orbital has n + l = 5. For constructing many introductory neutral-atom configurations, that generates the sequence ...3p → 4s → 3d → 4p. It correctly helps us anticipate potassium and calcium and provides a good starting point through much of the first transition series.

It is not a theorem derived from the exact many-electron Schrödinger equation, nor does it assert a fixed universal ranking of orbital energies in every atom or ion. The electrostatic potential experienced by an electron changes as the nuclear charge and electron distribution change. Consequently, applying the order as if it were an inviolable physical law confuses an empirical construction mnemonic with a complete model of energy.

The practical method is disciplined: use the filling guide to propose a candidate; check capacities and total electrons; compare against authoritative ground-state data; then explain any important disagreement. This is how a scientific model should be used—successes and failures both provide information.

5. Chromium begins with twenty-four electrons

Chromium has atomic number Z = 24, so its electrically neutral atom has 24 electrons. The argon core accounts for 18 of them, leaving six beyond [Ar]. Sequentially assigning 4s² and then 3d⁴ uses all six: 18 + 2 + 4 = 24. That is the elementary predicted configuration, [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².

In the evaluated free-atom ground-state assignment, the outer distribution is 3d⁵ 4s¹: 18 + 5 + 1 = 24. Electron conservation is not violated. The change is a reorganization of occupation: relative to the simple prediction, the number of 3d electrons increases by one and the number of 4s electrons decreases by one. Both candidates satisfy the separate capacities, and neither can be rejected by electron counting alone.

The comparison creates a second question: why does the observed state have lower total energy? “Because it has five d electrons” names a pattern, but the stronger explanation must examine spin alignment, exchange and the energetic balance among electron–electron repulsions, electron–nucleus attraction and orbital reorganization.

6. Copper begins with twenty-nine electrons

Copper has Z = 29, leaving 29 − 18 = 11 electrons beyond the argon core. The simple sequence assigns 4s² and 3d⁹: 18 + 2 + 9 = 29. The evaluated ground state instead has [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, still 18 + 10 + 1 = 29. Here the d subshell is completely filled while the 4s subshell contains one electron.

The observed arrangement is not a general law that a full d subshell will always be obtained whenever it is reachable by shifting a single electron. Both the starting energy of the orbitals and the entire electron interaction energy have to favor that outcome. Copper is an especially clear lesson because the familiar “full subshell” mnemonic points to the observed result while not, by itself, providing a quantitative explanation.

Notice that a 3d¹⁰ subshell contains five pairs of opposite-spin electrons and has no net spin from the closed 3d shell in the simple coupling model; the remaining 4s¹ electron contributes a spin one-half. Chromium's ground term instead has a strikingly high total spin, which we shall connect to its six singly occupied outer spin orbitals.

8. Original diagram: the chromium alternatives

In the upper row a paired 4s orbital accompanies four singly occupied 3d orbitals. In the lower row all five d orbitals are singly occupied and 4s contains one electron. This second arrangement is consistent with chromium's high-spin ground term. The schematic itself does not prove an energy difference: it shows what the two candidate configurations mean.

9. Original diagram: copper and the closed d shell

The visual difference is one electron in an outer spin orbital, yet the many-electron state involves a different total arrangement. Both candidate configurations have one unpaired electron in their simplest box pictures, so copper's exception cannot be explained merely by saying it has “more unpaired electrons.” This is a useful contrast with chromium.

10. How experiments, rather than mnemonics, settle the question

Atoms emit and absorb light at discrete wavelengths because transitions connect quantized energy levels. Spectral line positions and intensities, selection rules, Zeeman splitting and patterns across related atomic states allow physicists to build and refine assignments of energy levels, angular momenta and dominant electron configurations. A simple spectroscope does not directly display an orbital occupancy label; the assignment comes from interpreting a network of spectral evidence with atomic theory.

The NIST Atomic Spectra Database reports critically evaluated ground states, levels and spectral lines. For neutral chromium (Cr I), the ground term is 3d⁵4s ⁷S₃; for neutral copper (Cu I) it is 3d¹⁰4s ²S₁/₂. Here “I” names the neutral spectrum in spectroscopic convention, and the superscripted term symbol describes spin and orbital angular momentum coupling. We will unpack the useful parts without pretending that term symbols can be deduced from a mnemonic alone.

The important epistemic distinction is this: the configurations have experimental grounding, and quantum models explain them with varying degrees of accuracy. If a basic rule and the evaluated spectra disagree, the basic rule is the approximation that needs revision, not the experimental dataset.

11. Reading Cr I and Cu I correctly

In spectroscopy, Cr I denotes neutral chromium, Cr II singly ionized chromium, and so on. The roman numeral is one more than the positive charge for the ionization stage. This notation must not be confused with oxidation numbers such as Cr(III) in chemistry or with the 3d electron count. Similarly, “Cu I” in an atomic spectra table means neutral copper, while copper(I) in an oxidation-state name conventionally means copper in oxidation state +1.

The complete ground-state label for Cr I includes the term ⁷S₃. The 7 is spin multiplicity 2S + 1, not electron count; the letter S corresponds to total orbital angular momentum L = 0; and subscript 3 indicates total angular momentum J = 3. The copper ground term ²S₁/₂ has S = 1/2, L = 0 and J = 1/2. None of these symbols changes the basic 24- and 29-electron bookkeeping.

12. Chromium's high spin: beyond five d electrons

For chromium's observed 3d⁵4s¹ arrangement, an elementary high-spin diagram places five electrons singly in five 3d orbitals and one singly in 4s. These six spin projections can align to yield a maximum total spin S = 6 × 1/2 = 3. The associated multiplicity is 2S + 1 = 7, consistent with the evaluated ⁷S ground term. This is a useful link between Hund's rule and spectroscopic evidence.

Why does spin alignment matter energetically? Electrons are indistinguishable fermions. The total electronic wavefunction must be antisymmetric under exchange. For same-spin electrons, the spatial part of the state carries corresponding antisymmetry; this changes the pair probability and can reduce certain electron–electron repulsion contributions. The term exchange stabilization refers to an energetic effect of the antisymmetrized many-electron state; it is not a separate classical attractive force between parallel spins.

Importantly, a full quantitative calculation also accounts for the rest of the energy. A higher spin multiplicity does not guarantee a lower total energy across every choice of subshell occupation, every element or every chemical environment.

13. Copper's closed 3d shell: a different balance

For copper, the observed 3d¹⁰4s¹ state puts ten electrons in the five d orbitals, making a closed d subshell. In a simple orbital description the d subsystem contributes net S = 0 and L = 0; the single 4s electron gives S = 1/2 and L = 0, matching the ground term ²S₁/₂. The proposed d⁹4s² candidate has a d hole with a different angular-momentum structure. Merely counting unpaired electrons would miss this difference.

The closed subshell is frequently described as “especially stable.” That is a useful qualitative clue, but one should ask: stable relative to which alternative and in which atom? The actual energy difference emerges from the combination of one-electron contributions and many-electron interactions, including exchange and correlation. The radial distributions of 3d and 4s electrons and their responses to nuclear charge also matter.

Do not explain copper by mechanically counting ten “exchange pairs” or by declaring that any full d subshell must outrank every competing configuration. Copper's experimentally identified ground state is the fact; the closed-shell story is a partial interpretation of its energy balance.

14. A total-energy comparison is the correct mathematical question

For a fixed nucleus and fixed total electron number, call the two possible configurations A and B. The physical ground state is the lowest-energy allowed state after the wavefunctions have been treated consistently. The useful comparison is the total energy difference, not the energy of one isolated 4s orbital subtracted from the energy of one isolated 3d orbital. In a many-electron atom, orbital energies depend on the electron density and on the approximation used to calculate them.

ΔE = Etotal(B) − Etotal(A)
ΔE < 0 favors B over A. Both energies refer to the same atom and electron count.

For chromium, define A = [Ar]3d⁴4s² and B = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. For copper, define A = [Ar]3d⁹4s² and B = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. A sufficiently accurate full calculation of all relevant allowed atomic states identifies the lower-energy state. The sign of ΔE cannot be established simply from electron count, from the n + l value, or from the fact that B has a familiar half/full d occupancy.

When configurations strongly interact, a real atomic eigenstate can mix contributions from more than one configuration. A single label then denotes a useful dominant character rather than a rigid literal partition of six or eleven distinguishable particles.

15. Where does total electronic energy come from?

A nonrelativistic fixed-nucleus model for N electrons includes their kinetic energies, the attraction between each electron and the nucleus, and the repulsion between each electron pair. The kinetic energy cannot be thrown away: tighter localization in space affects momentum and thus kinetic energy. Electron repulsion cannot be thrown away either, especially in transition-metal atoms where near-lying subshell arrangements compete.

The sum over pairs runs over distinct i < j and avoids double-counting. This Hamiltonian is a model; precise work may include finite nuclear mass, relativistic effects and spin-dependent interactions. The central lesson for chromium and copper is that the energy is a property of an interacting many-electron state, not an immutable list of energy costs attached to orbital labels.

16. Orbitals change as the atomic environment changes

The 4s orbital in a potassium atom is not energetically identical to an orbital called 4s in neutral chromium or a chromium ion. As protons are added across the period and electrons redistribute, the effective nuclear attraction, screening and radial shapes change. The 3d orbitals can contract and interact differently with other electrons; 4s remains comparatively spatially extended in many transition-metal contexts. These trends influence relative energies and energetic reorganization.

Our labels 3d and 4s remain useful quantum-chemical descriptions, but they do not imply that nature follows a fixed assembly conveyor belt. Even in self-consistent-field calculations, different occupancies can cause orbitals to relax into somewhat different shapes. This is why a simplistic “one 4s electron costs x and one 3d electron costs y” explanation has limited reliability.

A thoughtful exam response therefore distinguishes the aufbau construction order from the energy ordering of fully formed electronic states. They are connected but not identical.

17. Exchange is helpful but not a one-line formula

In introductory lessons, an exchange-energy cartoon suggests that equal-spin electrons spread among different orbitals can reduce average Coulomb repulsion. The number of same-spin pairs among n aligned electrons is C(n,2) = n(n − 1)/2. For five aligned d spins, that number is 10; for four, it is 6. The calculation can help a student see why additional aligned spins alter the structure of interactions.

Yet that combinatorial number is not an actual exchange energy. An energy contribution depends on integrals over spatial orbitals, their overlap, the nucleus and other electrons. Nor is it legitimate to compare “10 versus 6” and conclude that chromium must choose d⁵4s¹ in every physical situation. Other changes, including pairing, kinetic energy, electron–nucleus attraction, intershell interaction and orbital relaxation, must enter the total balance.

Model caution: Count spin pairs to understand the mechanism qualitatively; do not present the spin-pair count as a measured energy or a universal prediction of ground-state occupations.

18. Electron correlation and the limits of independent orbitals

An independent-particle picture approximates each electron as moving in an average potential. The actual correlated motion of electrons is more intricate: their instantaneous positions and motions are statistically linked by electrostatic interaction and by fermionic antisymmetry. Electron correlation in quantum chemistry generally refers to effects beyond a chosen mean-field approximation, such as Hartree–Fock. Different atomic terms and configurations gain different amounts of correlation energy.

This matters when two candidate states are relatively close in energy. An approximation may obtain broad orbital patterns correctly yet get a fine energetic ordering wrong. Reliable atomic theory may need configuration interaction, multiconfiguration methods, and appropriate relativistic corrections. A one-line table of orbital energies is therefore not a proof of chromium or copper's ground state.

For life-science students the deepest takeaway is not to memorize computational method names. It is to understand why more than a one-electron picture is needed when an apparent exception appears.

19. Why is “half-filled stability” a limited mnemonic?

A half-filled d subshell means d⁵: each of the five spatial d orbitals contains one electron in the elementary high-spin representation. Chromium's ground state has this d⁵ population, while manganese has d⁵4s². The observation that both can contain d⁵ does not tell us how many 4s electrons each should possess. Six electrons outside [Ar] in Cr and seven in Mn face different nuclear charges and different energetic comparisons.

To call d⁵ “stable” without a reference state is incomplete. A half-filled arrangement may offer favorable high-spin exchange characteristics and orbital symmetry features, but the total energy still compares entire states. A neighboring atom might favor the d⁵ configuration while retaining an extra 4s electron, as Mn does. The accurate statement is that partially filled subshells and their spin coupling affect energetics; they do not override all other contributions.

Exam questions often exploit this confusion by presenting an unqualified rule that every atom possessing d⁴ should spontaneously become d⁵. The correct challenge is always: what atom, what charge, what ground-state evidence and what alternative state?

20. Why is “fully filled stability” also incomplete?

Similarly, copper is d¹⁰4s¹ and zinc is d¹⁰4s². Both have a closed 3d subshell; only one has a singly occupied 4s. In another row of the periodic table, palladium's evaluated ground state is [Kr]4d¹⁰ with no 5s electron. This is a direct counterexample to any universal rule that a transition atom with a full d shell must also have exactly one outer s electron.

Words such as “stable” are often shorthand for “lower total energy than nearby alternatives under defined conditions.” They do not identify a single term that can be added mechanically. The actual energetic balance depends on nuclear charge, shell extent, electron repulsion, coupling and relaxation. Treating every d¹⁰ configuration as an identical chemical entity also overlooks the different oxidation states and environments that atoms can occupy.

Thus chromium and copper should be presented as important, familiar counterexamples to a simplified filling guide, not as evidence that an equally absolute half/full-sublevel law replaces it.

21. The experiment and model form a coherent story

The same intellectual method appears throughout science. A model predicts a pattern. An accurate measurement reveals a deviation. A better theory explains why the pattern worked where it did and failed where it did not. In this case, an orbital-order mnemonic gives useful starting configurations, while an interacting-electron description and spectroscopic observation determine a more accurate neutral-atom ground-state assignment.

22. Beyond Cr and Cu: other periods test the mnemonic

Chromium and copper are the best-known first-row exceptions, but the broader periodic table is less orderly than two isolated corrections suggest. NIST's evaluated neutral-atom list includes Mo: [Kr]4d⁵5s¹ and Ag: [Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹, as well as Nb: [Kr]4d⁴5s¹, Ru: [Kr]4d⁷5s¹ and Pd: [Kr]4d¹⁰. For example, a naïve continuation from [Kr]4d³5s² would not predict Nb's observed distribution.

The examples matter because they show that “half-filled or full” does not account for the entire set. Ruthenium has d⁷5s¹, neither a half-filled nor a full d subshell. Palladium has d¹⁰5s⁰, which a rigid one-electron shift slogan would miss. Additional rows bring relativistic and correlation effects of increasing importance; we need not calculate them to recognise the limits of the simple guide.

23. How neutral atoms differ from ions and molecules

This chapter's values refer to isolated, neutral atoms. Removing an electron, introducing an electric field, forming a coordination complex or placing the element in a solid changes the Hamiltonian, energy levels and in some cases the appropriate electronic description. One cannot infer an ion's complete configuration by applying the neutral-atom Madelung sequence as a rigid reverse procedure.

For example, the biological metal centers of enzymes are not simply neutral Cr or Cu atoms with an [Ar] core floating in vacuum. They are embedded in ligands, with definite oxidation states and bonding interactions. In a protein's copper site, the oxidation state and coordination environment influence the effective d-electron count, spin character, absorption spectrum and reactivity. These are authentic applications of electronic-structure thinking, but require chemical bonding and ligand-field theory in addition to neutral atomic tables.

We reserve formal ion-configuration construction and the 4s-versus-3d removal issue for Node 19. Keeping this boundary prevents a student from carrying a useful neutral-atom rule into a new physical problem without checking its assumptions.

24. Chromium's biological and environmental context

Chromium occurs in chemically distinct forms with different biological consequences. The essential point here is not to derive chromium chemistry from the neutral Cr I ground configuration. In materials and environmental chemistry, changes of oxidation state alter electron counts and bonding, while different chemical species have very different toxicity profiles. Hexavalent chromium species are established toxicological concerns; this cannot be inferred from the neutral atom's high-spin d⁵4s¹ label alone.

When a biological text mentions “chromium,” ask whether it means neutral Cr, a particular oxidation state, or a defined coordinated compound. The presence of the same element does not imply the same electronic structure or biological effect. This is a practical example of how precise configuration language protects scientific reasoning.

The methodological connection is valuable: use the NIST neutral Cr configuration as a foundational reference, then add oxidation-state, ligand and solvent information before drawing a biochemical conclusion.

25. Copper sites connect electronic structure to biology

Copper is part of biological electron-transfer systems and redox enzymes. Copper-bearing proteins can exploit accessible oxidation-state changes and metal–ligand covalency; the energetic arrangement of electrons in a coordinated site helps shape spectra and function. A free neutral atom with [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ is not a valid stand-in for a complex copper site in an enzyme.

For example, comparing Cu(I) and Cu(II) in a biochemical context requires their charge states and the identity and geometry of coordinating ligands. The distinction can connect to optical absorption, magnetic response and redox chemistry; the relevant explanations come from ionic and molecular electronic structure, not merely from the isolated neutral-atom box diagram. Later nodes build these additional layers.

One robust lesson transfers directly: occupancy depends on the physical system. A student's answer should name the system being modeled before proposing a configuration.

26. Common test formats: counting, comparison and reasoning

On a basic test you may be asked to write the observed neutral configurations of chromium and copper. On an analytical question you may be given the rule-based forms and asked to correct them while preserving the electron count. A deeper question may ask which statement is more defensible: “half-filled shells always dominate” or “the balance of interacting-electron energies determines the ground state.” The second captures the scientific logic.

A term-symbol question may ask for chromium's spin multiplicity. With six aligned outer electrons in the high-spin picture, S = 3, so the multiplicity is 2S + 1 = 7. For copper d¹⁰4s¹, the d shell contributes no total spin and the outer electron gives S = 1/2, hence multiplicity 2. These claims are consistent with evaluated ground-state terms, but detailed term calculations for arbitrary configurations require angular-momentum coupling theory.

Another question may offer the measured first ionization energy and ask whether it equals the orbital-promotion difference between d⁴4s² and d⁵4s¹. It does not: ionization changes the number of electrons, whereas that configuration comparison concerns alternative states of the same neutral atom.

27. A simple decision algorithm for exam answers

First identify the species: element, proton count and net charge. If the species is neutral Cr or Cu, start from Z = 24 or 29; if it is an ion, do not automatically apply this chapter's neutral values. Next build the [Ar] core (18 electrons). Compute the remaining count. Write the rule-based candidate to understand why an exception is being discussed. Finally replace the candidate with the evaluated free-neutral-atom ground-state occupancy and check that its electron total equals Z.

After the arithmetic, add a sentence separating the empirical result from the explanation: these ground states are supported by spectroscopy and many-electron atomic structure; half/full occupancy and exchange considerations help explain the trend, but a fixed n + l ranking does not predict every case. This short procedure avoids both memorization without understanding and overcomplicated claims that cannot be justified in an exam.

Sanity checks: Cr must sum to 24, Cu to 29; neither 4s orbital holds more than two electrons; neither 3d subshell holds more than ten; and the label “neutral” must accompany any direct use of the NIST configurations.

28. Two different uses of the word 'stability'

Students encounter “stability” in several contexts: low total energy of an isolated ground state, resistance to ionization, oxidation-state preference, bond energy and kinetic persistence against reaction. These are not interchangeable. Cr has a ground state d⁵4s¹, but that observation by itself does not prove that chromium is chemically unreactive, nor that it has the highest ionization energy among its neighbors.

For example, NIST reports a first ionization energy near 6.7665 eV for Cr and 7.72638 eV for Cu, both referenced to their respective ground atoms and ions. The measured quantities are not the energy differences between the two neutral configuration candidates pictured earlier. In particular, it would be a scientific error to subtract these ionization energies to calculate the energy gained by a notional 4s-to-3d shift.

Use the phrase “lower-energy ground-state configuration” for our central question, and identify any new meaning of stability explicitly before discussing biological chemistry.

29. A disciplined treatment of uncertainty and exactness

We can state with high confidence that the evaluated neutral atomic ground states are d⁵4s¹ for Cr and d¹⁰4s¹ for Cu. We cannot infer a precise energy gap between hypothetical configurations from the occupancy symbols alone. Those gaps depend on the electronic state, term coupling and computational or experimental method; treating one schematic “promotion energy” as a universal constant is unjustified.

In theoretical language, the many-electron wavefunction is the quantity that characterizes the atomic state. Orbital occupation numbers provide a useful representation, and the dominant configuration is often well defined for teaching purposes. But orbitals are not labeled electrons, and a real interacting atom does not necessarily inhabit one pure independent-particle determinant.

Therefore “chromium gains stability because 3d is half filled” is acceptable only as an explicitly qualitative introduction. A rigorous answer adds the interacting-energy balance and the spectroscopic identification of the ground state.

30. From chromium and copper to the next learning problem

The first transition-series lesson began with a regular-looking pattern after calcium. Chromium and copper showed that the regularity is useful but not universal. We have now connected electron counting, orbital capacity, Hund-type spin arrangements, total energies, evaluated spectral evidence and the limits of simple filling rules. Importantly, the true starting point is not a claim that nature “breaks a rule,” but recognition that a simple model had a limited domain.

The next natural question is what happens when a transition-metal atom becomes positively charged. Must the electron removed come from whichever subshell a construction diagram filled last? Can we carry the neutral ground-state configuration unchanged into a protein's metal site? Those questions lead to Node 1.A.2.19: Electronic Configurations of Ions and the 4s–3d Issue, and require a fresh comparison of ionic energies and actual chemical environments.

31. Worked examples I — constructing and checking Cr/Cu

Worked example 1. Chromium's missing electrons

Solution. Start with the argon core: 18 electrons. Neutral Cr has 24, so 6 remain. The simple candidate allocates 4 to 3d and 2 to 4s; the observed ground state allocates 5 to 3d and 1 to 4s. Both total 24; only one is the evaluated lowest-energy assignment.

Worked example 2. Copper's eleven electrons after [Ar]

Solution. Neutral Cu has 29 electrons, 11 more than [Ar]. A d⁹s² proposal has 9 + 2 = 11; the actual d¹⁰s¹ has 10 + 1 = 11. The sole difference is distribution. Neither a different core nor a lost electron is involved.

Worked example 3. Audit a false Cr answer

Solution. A student writes [Ar]3d⁶4s¹ for neutral chromium. Count 18 + 6 + 1 = 25: this is one electron too many. The count alone disproves the neutral configuration, even before comparing it with NIST's d⁵4s¹ ground-state assignment.

Worked example 4. Audit a false Cu answer

Solution. The suggested configuration [Ar]3d¹¹4s⁰ is impossible in a single-subshell orbital description because d has five spatial orbitals, each with two spin orbitals, giving a capacity of ten. It also totals 29, which shows that a correct electron count does not guarantee an allowed configuration.

Worked example 5. Madelung comparison for the two subshells

Solution. For 4s, n = 4 and l = 0, so n+l = 4. For 3d, n = 3 and l = 2, so n+l = 5. The simple rule therefore prioritizes 4s before 3d in a construction exercise. This does not rank every actual many-electron atomic state.

Worked example 6. Five d orbitals are ten places

Solution. For d, l = 2 and the permitted m values are −2, −1, 0, +1 and +2, giving five spatial orbitals. Each permits two opposite spin projections. Thus 5 × 2 = 10 spin orbitals and a capacity of ten electrons.

Worked example 7. Unpaired spins in observed Cr

Solution. A high-spin box representation of d⁵4s¹ has one electron in each of five 3d orbitals and one electron in 4s. All six can be aligned in the maximal-spin state. The model gives six unpaired electrons and S = 6/2 = 3, consistent with the ⁷S term's multiplicity.

Worked example 8. Multiplicity of chromium's high-spin state

Solution. Using S = 3, the multiplicity is 2S+1 = 2(3)+1 = 7. In the ground term ⁷S₃, the leading seven is not the number of d orbitals or the number of valence electrons; it is the spin multiplicity.

Worked example 9. Multiplicity in copper's ground state

Solution. For d¹⁰4s¹, the five d orbitals are paired and contribute S = 0 in the closed-shell model. The unpaired 4s electron gives S = 1/2. Thus 2S+1 = 2, matching the evaluated term ²S₁/₂.

Worked example 10. Compare hypothetical and actual Cu unpaired counts

Solution. A high-spin schematic for d⁹4s² has one unpaired d electron and a paired 4s; d¹⁰4s¹ has paired d and one unpaired 4s. Both have one unpaired electron. Therefore 'more unpaired electrons' cannot be the explanation of Cu's ground-state choice.

32. Worked examples II — spin, energy and comparison

Worked example 11. Compute aligned d-spin pairs for d⁴

Solution. Four aligned electrons have C(4,2) = (4×3)/2 = 6 distinct pairs. This is a combinatorial number, not six units of exchange energy. Real exchange contributions depend on spin orbitals and their overlap integrals.

Worked example 12. Compute aligned d-spin pairs for d⁵

Solution. Five aligned electrons have C(5,2) = (5×4)/2 = 10 pairs. Four extra combinations relative to d⁴ suggest a change in exchange-related terms, but the entire atomic energy comparison must include electron–nucleus attraction, kinetic energy and other interactions.

Worked example 13. Count aligned outer spins in Cr's diagram

Solution. The observed high-spin Cr diagram has six singly occupied outer spin orbitals. C(6,2) = 6×5/2 = 15 same-spin pairs across 3d and 4s in the simple picture. The total 15 differs from the ten d–d pairs; neither is by itself an energy.

Worked example 14. Check manganese as a control

Solution. For Mn, Z = 25 and the NIST neutral configuration is [Ar]3d⁵4s². Calculate 18 + 5 + 2 = 25. Even though 3d is half-filled, manganese keeps two 4s electrons. This falsifies a claimed universal 'half-filled d implies 4s¹' rule.

Worked example 15. Check zinc as a control

Solution. For Zn, Z = 30; [Ar]3d¹⁰4s² totals 18 + 10 + 2 = 30. The d shell is full, but 4s is also full. Consequently a full d shell does not require an outer s occupancy of exactly one.

Worked example 16. Contrast a candidate with a ground state

Solution. Both [Ar]3d⁴4s² and [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ are electron-count-consistent configurations for Cr. Calling one the 'ground state' is an additional physical assertion about the lowest available total energy; it is not determined from counting alone.

Worked example 17. Interpret a negative ΔE

Solution. Suppose a hypothetical calculation gives E(B) − E(A) = −0.15 eV for two properly defined states of one atom. Because ΔE is negative, B is lower in energy by 0.15 eV. This is an illustration of sign convention, not a measured Cr or Cu energy gap.

Worked example 18. Interpret a positive ΔE

Solution. If an illustrative model gives E(B) − E(A) = +0.08 eV, B is higher and A is energetically favored among the two. Reversing the subtraction would reverse the sign without changing the physical ordering. State your convention before interpreting numbers.

Worked example 19. Convert an illustrative gap to joules

Solution. An illustrative 0.20 eV energy separation is 0.20 × 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.204353268×10⁻²⁰ J. The electronvolt is an energy unit; do not confuse eV with an orbital occupancy count. This conversion is not a reported Cr/Cu gap.

Worked example 20. Do not misuse ionization energy

Solution. Cr's first ionization energy is about 6.7665 eV. It refers to producing a separated electron and a singly ionized product from the neutral ground atom. It does not equal the energy difference between two alternate configurations of the same 24-electron neutral chromium atom.

33. Worked examples III — shells, terms and external checks

Worked example 21. Convert chromium's 4s occupancy into counting

Solution. The observed Cr configuration uses one 4s electron, not two. In the elementary picture the 4s orbital therefore remains singly occupied, while all five d orbitals are singly occupied. The 4s subscript is a principal quantum number, not a count of four electrons.

Worked example 22. Determine copper's d-hole count

Solution. A d subshell has ten possible spin-orbital places. Observed Cu d¹⁰ has 10−10 = 0 d holes; the simple predicted d⁹ arrangement has one d hole. The difference in d-hole count is relevant to possible term structures but is not a complete energy proof.

Worked example 23. Expand the argon core

Solution. [Ar] equals 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶: 2+2+6+2+6 = 18 electrons. Hence Cr's full neutral configuration ends ...3p⁶3d⁵4s¹ and Cu's ends ...3p⁶3d¹⁰4s¹. Ordering the written subshell labels does not mean electrons have chronological identities.

Worked example 24. Find the ground-state label for Cr I

Solution. In atomic spectroscopic notation the Roman numeral I corresponds to the neutral atom. Therefore Cr I refers to the 24-electron species, not Cr⁺ or chromium with oxidation number +1. The evaluated ground designation includes d⁵4s ⁷S₃.

Worked example 25. Find the ground-state label for Cu I

Solution. Cu I refers to neutral copper's spectrum and has 29 electrons. It is not interchangeable with the chemical oxidation-state name copper(I), often written Cu(I), which conventionally describes an oxidation state of +1. Context and notation prevent this common ambiguity.

Worked example 26. What does the 'S' term letter mean?

Solution. In ⁷S₃ and ²S₁/₂, capital S is the term letter for total orbital angular momentum L = 0. It is different from the italic spin quantum number S used in 2S+1; confusing the two gives incorrect derivations of spectral multiplicities.

Worked example 27. Recover chromium's total angular momentum label

Solution. For the reported ⁷S₃ ground level, the letter S means L = 0 and 7 means spin S = 3. When L = 0, the total angular momentum J is 3, agreeing with the subscript. This uses the evaluated term; it is not derived from n+l.

Worked example 28. Recover copper's J label

Solution. For ²S₁/₂ the spin is S = 1/2 and orbital L = 0. The total angular momentum is J = 1/2, as shown by the reported subscript. Do not interpret the denominator 2 as an electron count.

Worked example 29. Cross-check molybdenum's neutral configuration

Solution. NIST lists Mo as [Kr]4d⁵5s¹. Krypton has 36 electrons; 36+5+1=42, the atomic number of Mo. This provides a second d⁵s¹ pattern in a different row, but one example does not establish a universal rule for all metals.

Worked example 30. Cross-check silver's neutral configuration

Solution. Ag has Z=47 and NIST lists [Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹, totaling 36+10+1=47. Its apparent analogy to copper is useful; interpretation still depends on period-specific energies and the many-electron problem.

34. Worked examples IV — scope, ions and scientific inference

Worked example 31. Cross-check palladium's neutral configuration

Solution. Pd has Z=46 and is [Kr]4d¹⁰ in the evaluated neutral ground state: 36+10=46. The number of 5s electrons is zero. This one case directly refutes 'every d¹⁰ transition metal must be ns¹'.

Worked example 32. Cross-check niobium's neutral configuration

Solution. Nb has Z=41 and NIST [Kr]4d⁴5s¹: 36+4+1=41. Its d shell is neither half-filled nor full. The configuration shows why a story based solely on d⁵ or d¹⁰ misses other exceptions.

Worked example 33. Cross-check ruthenium's neutral configuration

Solution. Ru has Z=44 and NIST [Kr]4d⁷5s¹: 36+7+1=44. Since d⁷ is neither d⁵ nor d¹⁰, a universal half/full stability mnemonic cannot generate every neutral transition-metal ground state.

Worked example 34. Distinguish zero-spin shell from a full atom

Solution. Cu's closed 3d subshell has S=0 in the basic model, but the whole ground atom has a remaining 4s electron and S=1/2. Saying 'copper is spin zero because d¹⁰ is filled' silently ignores the remaining electron.

Worked example 35. Set up the Coulomb pair sum

Solution. For N = 3 electrons, the electron–electron term has C(3,2)=3 distinct pairs: (1,2), (1,3), (2,3). A sum over i<j counts each once. The number of repelling pairs is not itself enough to compute an energy.

Worked example 36. Check the dimensions of the Coulomb term

Solution. The Coulomb energy factor e²/(4πε₀r) has units joules: charge squared divided by permittivity and distance. An electron-configuration exponent is dimensionless. Energy comparisons require physical energy units, not mere numerical occupancy differences.

Worked example 37. Explain why a one-electron orbital subtraction fails

Solution. A formula such as E(3d)−E(4s) ignores that the state has many interacting electrons and each candidate can have different relaxed orbitals. ΔE of two whole atomic states includes all contributions. Orbital differences can guide intuition but cannot automatically replace the total-state calculation.

Worked example 38. Relate a qualitative model to spectroscopy

Solution. The observed Cr I ⁷S₃ and Cu I ²S₁/₂ labels are supported by evaluated atomic spectroscopy. Hund-style boxes can help interpret these terms after the fact; the boxes alone cannot determine every precise level separation or reliably identify any experimental spectral line.

Worked example 39. Identify the proper limit of a biochemical claim

Solution. A student infers that the neutral atom Cr I configuration directly predicts toxicity of a chromium compound. Reject the inference: oxidation state, molecular bonding, solubility and chemical species matter. Neutral atom occupancy is a starting reference, not a toxicological property.

Worked example 40. Prepare for ionic configurations without solving them

Solution. From a neutral Cr or Cu configuration one can correctly say that an ion will have a different electron total and may have different subshell energetics. The specific removal sequence and ground-state ion configurations require ionic evidence and are the subject of Node 19, not a universal reverse-filling slogan.

35. Scientific cases I — observation, evidence and test answers

Case 1 — a conflicting exam answer

A mock answer key insists that chromium must be [Ar]3d⁴4s² because 4s lies before 3d in the filling diagram. As a student reviewer, separate the correct arithmetic from the incorrect ground-state claim. The candidate contains 24 electrons and is not forbidden, but NIST's neutral free-atom compilation identifies d⁵4s¹ as the ground state. A complete correction says that the construction order is a guide, not a complete total-energy minimization.

Case 2 — a mistaken explanation for Cu

A tutor says copper's d¹⁰s¹ ground state occurs because it has two unpaired electrons instead of one. Draw the candidate d⁹s² and actual d¹⁰s¹ orbital boxes. Each simple diagram contains only one unpaired electron. The explanation must therefore rely on interacting-state energetics and closed-shell features, not an incorrect raw count.

Case 3 — the Mn counterexample

A student believes every atom with d⁵ must have s¹. Compare neutral Cr d⁵s¹ with neutral Mn d⁵s². Both have a d⁵ subshell, but the latter atom has one more proton and one more electron. Each total-energy landscape changes with Z. The contrast makes the limitations of an unconditional half-filled slogan immediately visible.

Case 4 — an atomic-spectra table

A lab handout labels a line series Cr I and another Cr II. The student thinks the Roman numerals state oxidation number rather than stage of ionization. Explain that stage I is neutral and stage II is singly ionized, while a chemical Cr(III) descriptor is a different convention. A correct interpretation starts by specifying the species before applying any occupancy statement.

Case 5 — a spin term in an examination

A question lists ⁷S₃ for the Cr I ground level. Interpret it as spin multiplicity seven, L=0 and J=3. This fits a high-spin six-electron picture for d⁵4s¹, but the superscript 7 is not seven 3d electrons: d contains only five in the observed Cr configuration. The term label adds information beyond occupancy.

Case 6 — an ab initio computation

Two atomic calculations disagree on the lowest Cr configuration while both conserve 24 electrons. The discrepancy might reflect methods with different treatments of correlation, orbital optimization or relativistic terms. Inspect the total energies for comparable physical states, methodological assumptions and benchmarks against spectroscopy. Do not declare the one with simpler subshell order automatically correct.

36. Scientific cases II — biological and conceptual boundaries

Case 7 — ionization data misapplied

A student takes Cr's 6.7665 eV first ionization energy and calls it the energetic gain from d⁵s¹ relative to d⁴s². Ionization removes an electron from the neutral system and leaves a cation, so it compares states with different electron numbers. The supposed rearrangement compares neutral alternatives with the same N=24. No such equality follows.

Case 8 — a protein copper site

A researcher describes a copper center in an enzyme and a textbook shows neutral Cu as d¹⁰4s¹. The researcher cannot substitute that isolated free-atom state for a coordinated metal center. Oxidation state, donor atoms, geometry and covalency must be specified before discussing d occupation, redox activity or magnetic properties. This distinction is central to linking atomic physics with biochemistry.

Case 9 — neutral palladium

A student extends a rule from Cu and Ag and predicts palladium 4d¹⁰5s¹. That would total 36+10+1=47, not palladium's 46 electrons. NIST instead lists neutral Pd [Kr]4d¹⁰. The case combines a simple arithmetic failure with a warning that “full d means one outer s” is not a universal pattern.

Case 10 — incomplete experimental reasoning

Someone claims that a spectrometer directly photographed five chromium d electrons. Spectroscopy measures transitions and other observables; level assignments use selection rules, term structure and models across observations. Explain that critical evaluation of these data gives the ground configuration, but no single line is a microscopic photo of occupancy. The distinction helps students understand scientific inference.

Case 11 — comparing element and oxidation state

A paper says 'chromium(VI)' while an atomic reference states 'Cr I'. These are not interchangeable species; chemical chromium(VI) denotes an oxidation state in a compound, while spectroscopic Cr I is neutral. Toxicity, bonding and spin may differ sharply. A good research note records exact species, ligands and conditions before invoking electronic configuration.

Case 12 — selecting the better rule

A student proposes replacing aufbau with a new universal rule: every atom must promote an s electron until a d shell is half or fully filled. Evaluate Nb, Ru, Pd, Mn and Zn. They do not fit the simplistic replacement. A stronger method uses the aufbau order as a first guess, checks the electron count, consults critically evaluated ground states and recognises many-electron interactions.

Question: A dataset lists [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ for Cr and [Ar]3d⁵4s² for Mn. Does identical d occupation imply identical total spin?

Answer and reasoning: No. The outer s occupancies differ and extra electron spin can pair or add angular momentum; the term states are not determined solely from d count. Ground Cr has a ⁷S term and ground Mn a ⁶S term in the cited table.

Practice Q42 · Data interpretation

Question: A student sees Cu I with IE=7.72638 eV. What quantity is measured, and what does it not give?

Answer and reasoning: It is the first ionization energy of the neutral ground atom into a separated cation and electron, using the chosen energy reference. It is not the total-energy gap between neutral d⁹s² and d¹⁰s¹ candidate states.

Practice Q43 · Data interpretation

Practice Q44 · Data interpretation

Question: An atomic source lists Cr I and Cr II on adjacent rows. How many electrons do those labels imply for the two stages?

Question: A graph shows a higher calculated energy for d⁴s² than for d⁵s¹ for a fixed neutral atom. What may you conclude?

Answer and reasoning: Within that calculation and among those states, the d⁵s¹ state is energetically favored. The graph does not prove the energy method is accurate without validation, nor does it identify the lowest state among every possible configuration.

Practice Q46 · Data interpretation

Question: A research note calls the Cr configuration 'observed.' Does that mean electrons were photographed sitting in labeled d boxes?

Answer and reasoning: No. Spectral transitions, term patterns and theoretical interpretation support the assigned quantum state. The box diagram is a visualization of an occupancy model; the inference is indirect but scientifically rigorous.

Practice Q47 · Data interpretation

Question: One report gives copper a single unpaired electron, another describes a diamagnetic copper-containing compound. Are the reports necessarily contradictory?

Answer and reasoning: No. The first concerns neutral free atomic Cu in its ground state; the second concerns a compound with different charge, bonding and electron pairing. Without matching species and physical conditions, magnetic conclusions cannot be transferred.

Practice Q48 · Data interpretation

Question: A spectrum has line positions with distinct term labels but no element identifier. Can you infer that every line belongs to neutral Cr?

Answer and reasoning: No. Atomic identification requires wavelength patterns, selection rules, ionization stage assignments and corroborating analysis. A term symbol alone does not uniquely specify an element or ion.

Practice Q49 · Data interpretation

Question: A student finds Cr's ground configuration in two references written '4s¹3d⁵' and '3d⁵4s¹'. Are these different states?

Answer and reasoning: Not necessarily. Both notation orders indicate one 4s and five 3d electrons with an [Ar] core. The written order is not a timeline of electron insertion; compare occupancies and terms, not typography.

Answer and reasoning: Check total-energy convergence, method, electron correlation, term symmetries, possible configuration interaction and experimental spectra. Near-degeneracy makes simplistic one-orbital energy statements particularly unreliable.

43. Thirty misconceptions corrected — occupancy and quantum rules

Pitfall 1. An exception violates Pauli.

Neither chromium nor copper violates Pauli. The observed d and s occupations remain within orbital capacities and their spin occupations can be antisymmetrized. The conflict is only with a simplified energy-order mnemonic, not with quantum exclusion.

Pitfall 2. Cr has 23 electrons because 4s lost one.

The 'one electron shift' describes a comparison between two 24-electron neutral candidates. Chromium still has Z=24 protons and 24 electrons. Count the [Ar] core plus both outer subshells before describing any configuration.

Pitfall 3. Cu has 30 electrons because d is full.

A complete d subshell adds ten to the 18-electron argon core, and the 4s¹ adds one: 18+10+1=29. The 30-electron neighbor zinc has d¹⁰4s²; the distinction is precisely one extra 4s electron.

Pitfall 4. The superscript 7 in Cr ⁷S₃ is electron count.

It is spin multiplicity 2S+1, not occupancy. Chromium's d count is five, outer electron count six, and atomic number 24. Keeping these three types of number separate prevents several common errors.

Pitfall 5. Cu is diamagnetic because its d shell is full.

Neutral free Cu d¹⁰4s¹ still has a 4s unpaired electron. Compound magnetic behavior depends on oxidation state and bonding; an isolated neutral atom's shell picture cannot be exported to every copper-containing material.

Pitfall 6. Every d⁵ atom is 4s¹.

Manganese is [Ar]3d⁵4s². The d⁵ pattern occurs alongside different s populations, depending on the atom's total energy and Z. Do not let a half-filled-subshell mnemonic replace the actual neutral ground-state table.

Pitfall 7. Every d¹⁰ metal has ns¹.

Palladium's neutral [Kr]4d¹⁰ has no 5s electron, while zinc's neutral [Ar]3d¹⁰4s² has two 4s electrons. These cases bracket copper's d¹⁰4s¹ and refute a universal s¹ inference.

Pitfall 8. Madelung is a quantum number selection rule.

The n+l construction is an empirical pedagogical pattern for many ground-state occupancies, not a selection rule governing radiative transitions or a rigorous theorem fixing all many-electron levels. Transition selection rules have a different theoretical basis.

Pitfall 9. The atom first fills then literally shifts one electron.

The diagram compares occupancies for competing states. It does not establish an actual chronological path followed by distinguishable electrons during atom formation; electrons are indistinguishable quantum particles.

Pitfall 10. Exchange is a force attracting same-spin electrons.

Exchange reflects antisymmetry of fermionic wavefunctions and changes allowed spatial correlations and energy. It is not a new classical attractive force; electrostatic repulsion between electrons remains repulsive.

44. Thirty misconceptions corrected — exchange and evidence

Pitfall 11. Exchange-pair count equals exchange energy.

C(n,2) is dimensionless, while exchange energy has joule or eV units and depends on orbital integrals. More counted pairs may be suggestive but cannot by themselves determine which total atomic state lies lower.

Pitfall 12. Higher total spin always wins.

Hund-type tendencies hold under particular orbital and energetic assumptions. Different radial distributions, pairing, crystal fields, correlations and kinetic energy contributions can change the preferred state. An unqualified spin maximization law is incorrect.

Pitfall 13. Orbital energies are universal constants.

The energy and shape of an orbital in a many-electron approximation depend on Z, electron count, occupancy, method and environment. A 4s label does not carry one immutable energy across K, Cr, Cu and their ions.

Pitfall 14. Closed d shell means chemically inert copper.

A d¹⁰ closed subshell in a neutral atom does not make all copper chemistry inert. Oxidation states, ligand binding and electron-transfer energetics govern compounds. Atomic ground configuration is only one ingredient.

Pitfall 15. NIST reports the hypothetical promotion energy.

NIST's neutral ground-state occupancy and first ionization energy are not the same as a comparison between hypothetical d⁴s² and d⁵s¹ states. To know a state-to-state gap, the states and corresponding levels must be specified.

Pitfall 16. All spectroscopic observations are direct orbital photos.

Spectra measure electromagnetic transitions and related observables. The assigned configurations and term levels emerge from interpreting systematic evidence with atomic quantum theory, not from imaging fixed electrons in box positions.

Pitfall 17. Cr I is chromium(I) chemistry.

Roman numeral I in atomic spectral notation means neutral; the oxidation-state notation Cr(I) refers to a formal +1 state in compounds. A specialist must always examine the naming context.

Pitfall 18. All states are accurately one configuration.

Configuration interaction can mix independent-particle configurations. A leading [Ar]d⁵s¹ label summarizes dominant occupation character and remains useful; it need not be an exact pure product state.

Pitfall 19. A correct electron count proves an allowed configuration.

The proposed d¹¹s⁰ for neutral copper totals 29 with [Ar] but violates the d capacity of ten. Both electron count and quantum number constraints are necessary checks, before even asking about the energy ordering.

Pitfall 20. A configuration can tell whether a compound is toxic.

Toxicity depends on chemical species, exposure, dose, biological processing and environmental conditions. Comparing neutral chromium with a chromate compound as though they share one electronic and chemical identity is unjustified.

45. Thirty misconceptions corrected — notation and scientific scope

Pitfall 21. The 4s exponent is the shell number.

In 4s¹, 4 is principal quantum number n and superscript 1 is the electron occupancy. Confusing these numbers can produce impossible electron totals or misunderstanding of the number of available orbitals.

Pitfall 22. d⁵ means five d orbital pairs.

A d⁵ subshell in a high-spin box representation occupies five separate d orbitals singly; a fully paired d subshell would require ten electrons. Distinguish orbitals, electrons, electron pairs and aligned-spin combinations.

Pitfall 23. Negative total energy means unstable.

Reference choices make absolute electronic energies negative in bound-atom models. Relative energies of properly comparable states, not sign of a single number in isolation, determine which candidate is lower.

Pitfall 24. Electron removal reverses filling in every ion.

A neutral-atom construction sequence is not an automatic ionization algorithm. Charge changes the electron–electron balance and subshell energies, so ionic ground states require a separate analysis, developed in Node 19.

Pitfall 25. Cu d¹⁰s¹ has ten unpaired spins.

The ten d electrons are paired across five spatial orbitals in the elementary closed-shell picture. Only the s¹ electron is unpaired in the neutral ground state; ten is a d population, not spin count.

Pitfall 26. A 7S term means orbital L=7.

Term letter S means L=0, independent of the leading multiplicity. ⁷S₃ has multiplicity seven, total orbital angular momentum L=0 and J=3.

Pitfall 27. The label d⁹ necessarily means a measured Cu excited level.

A proposed occupancy is not by itself an identified spectral level. The actual level energies and term assignments require calculations and spectroscopy; do not invent a numerical excitation energy for a convenient cartoon.

Pitfall 28. Copper's exception can be explained solely by spin count.

Both proposed d⁹s² and observed d¹⁰s¹ have one unpaired electron in simple orbital diagrams. The difference is total electronic structure and energetic interaction, not a change from zero to many unpaired spins.

Pitfall 29. All periodic-table exceptions are d⁵ or d¹⁰.

NIST lists Nb d⁴s¹ and Ru d⁷s¹, which do not fit that classification. Further evidence beyond the famous Cr/Cu examples guards against replacing one simplistic rule with another.

Pitfall 30. The same subshell occupancy guarantees the same biology.

Electron arrangements in free atoms, ions, proteins and solids respond to different nuclear/electron counts and potentials. Even when a simplified d count coincides, ligands, geometry, covalency and redox context can profoundly change behavior.

46. Ten advanced integrative challenges — physical reasoning

Integrative challenge 1. Challenge 1 — test two competing chromium explanations

Problem: Compare hypothesis H₁: 'd⁵ always forces 4s¹' and H₂: 'the total energies of interacting electronic states determine ground occupancy, with d⁵ spin effects among the contributions.' Use chromium and manganese as independent tests.

Reasoned solution: Cr I has [Ar]d⁵s¹, while Mn I has [Ar]d⁵s². Both exhibit d⁵, disproving H₁'s universality. H₂ accommodates different nuclear charges, orbital relaxation, pairing and electron interactions. A rigorous answer should identify data (the evaluated ground configurations), a model (state energies) and the limitation of using half-filled shells as a complete cause.

Integrative challenge 2. Challenge 2 — evaluate an invented copper state table

Problem: A model reports E(d⁹s²)=−1000.40 eV and E(d¹⁰s¹)=−1000.58 eV for neutral copper, while a competing calculation reverses the ordering. Interpret the first model and explain what you must check before deciding which prediction is credible.

Reasoned solution: Within the first model, ΔE=−1000.58−(−1000.40)=−0.18 eV, so d¹⁰s¹ is lower. The numbers are hypothetical, not measured Cu levels. Check whether both energies describe comparable total states and consistent approximations, whether correlation and relaxation were treated well, and whether predicted terms agree with evaluated spectroscopy. NIST lists the neutral Cu d¹⁰s¹ ground state.

Integrative challenge 3. Challenge 3 — reconcile counting and occupancy across Cr, Mn, Cu, Zn

Problem: Using [Ar]=18, calculate each neutral atom's 3d and 4s populations and total electron count; then explain why adjacent elements do not form a single s-occupancy sequence.

Reasoned solution: Cr 5+1+18=24, Mn 5+2+18=25, Cu 10+1+18=29 and Zn 10+2+18=30. The 3d populations in each pair coincide but the 4s populations differ; the nuclear charge and total electron number also change. Therefore predicting a particular s exponent from d half/full status alone is invalid.

Integrative challenge 4. Challenge 4 — interpret chromium's term and box diagram

Problem: From Cr I's reported ⁷S₃, find S, L and J, then construct a basic occupancy diagram consistent with the reported d⁵s¹ configuration. What does your construction fail to prove?

Reasoned solution: 2S+1=7 gives S=3; S as the orbital term letter gives L=0; J=3. Five singly occupied 3d orbitals plus one singly occupied 4s orbital with aligned spins provide a representative high-spin picture. The boxes do not determine absolute energies, calculate correlation or prove that no other configuration mixes into the true eigenstate.

Integrative challenge 5. Challenge 5 — use counterexamples to revise a rule

Problem: A classroom poster claims: 'All transition-metal anomalies occur because an s electron produces a half-filled or full d shell.' Use Nb, Ru and Pd to design a corrected one-sentence poster.

Reasoned solution: Nb is [Kr]4d⁴5s¹, Ru is [Kr]4d⁷5s¹ and Pd is [Kr]4d¹⁰ with no 5s electron. Neither the Nb nor Ru d count is half/full, and Pd refutes obligatory remaining s¹. A better sentence is: 'Near-lying s and d configurations compete; many-electron total energies determine observed ground states, for which Madelung ordering is only a useful guide.'

47. Ten advanced integrative challenges — applications and synthesis

Integrative challenge 6. Challenge 6 — separation of physical energy scales

Problem: Explain why Cr's first ionization energy cannot be subtracted from Cu's first ionization energy to obtain the Cr d⁴s²→d⁵s¹ stabilization. Specify the systems and reference states involved.

Reasoned solution: Cr ionization removes an electron from Cr I to produce Cr II plus a separated electron; Cu ionization similarly links different Cu charge states. Their difference compares two distinct elements' ionization thresholds, not two configurations of neutral Cr with the same 24 electrons. To estimate the latter gap, define two neutral Cr states and compare their total energies or identified atomic levels.

Integrative challenge 7. Challenge 7 — correlate spectral designations with chemistry

Problem: A lab notebook calls both neutral atomic Cu I and protein-bound Cu(I) 'Cu I'. Explain the ambiguity and propose a scientifically precise notation scheme.

Reasoned solution: Use 'Cu I' only when clearly referring to the first (neutral) atomic spectrum in spectroscopy. For chemical coordination sites, write 'Cu(I)' for oxidation state +1 and name the complex, ligands and geometry. Neutral isolated Cu is [Ar]d¹⁰4s¹, but Cu(I) in a protein cannot be assigned that neutral free-atom state; different charge and bonding context changes the model.

Integrative challenge 8. Challenge 8 — evaluate a purported exchange proof

Problem: Someone counts 15 aligned-spin pairs for Cr's six outer spins and 6 pairs for an imagined d⁴s² high-spin picture, and concludes the energy gain is 9 eV. Identify every unsupported step.

Reasoned solution: The pair counts are dimensionless combinations, not energies. Exchange integrals vary across orbital pairs and differ between spatially relaxed configurations; electron–nucleus attraction, kinetic, direct Coulomb and correlation terms also change. No conversion from nine extra pairs to nine electronvolts exists without a quantitative Hamiltonian and wavefunctions. The ground-state occupation must be evaluated against actual evidence.

Integrative challenge 9. Challenge 9 — biological boundary case

Problem: A biochemistry article claims a copper enzyme is paramagnetic because neutral Cu has one unpaired 4s electron. Critique the inference and outline the experimental information that would be useful.

Reasoned solution: The statement transfers a free-atom configuration into a coordinated metal site without specifying the charge state or ligand environment. Identify Cu oxidation state, spin coupling to ligands, local geometry, covalency and possible interactions with nearby metal centers. Magnetic susceptibility, EPR or optical spectroscopy where appropriate can help, alongside molecular theory. The neutral Cu label alone does not determine protein magnetism.

Integrative challenge 10. Challenge 10 — explain a scientific 'exception' to a beginner

Problem: Write a compact scientific explanation connecting electron counts, the n+l prediction, observed Cr and Cu ground states, and why the model must be limited. Then state the next problem to study.

Reasoned solution: Both simple candidate and observed configurations conserve each neutral atom's electron count: Cr has 24 and Cu has 29. The n+l guide builds d⁴s² and d⁹s², while evaluated states are d⁵s¹ and d¹⁰s¹. The discrepancy arises because interacting-state total energies cannot be inferred from a fixed filling order; exchange, correlation and orbital relaxation matter. Next investigate what changes for ions and why electron-removal rules are not simply reverse filling.
