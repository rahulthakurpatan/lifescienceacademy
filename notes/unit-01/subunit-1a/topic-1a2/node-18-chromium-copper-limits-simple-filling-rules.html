Chromium, Copper and the Limits of Simple Filling Rules

How two neutral atoms exposed the limitations of a filling guide—and why spectroscopy, total energy and quantum interactions offer a better explanation than a memorized exception.

Learning outcomes: reconstruct Cr and Cu ground states; compare them to simple Madelung predictions; audit electron numbers and d-orbital capacities; interpret high-spin Cr and closed-shell Cu; understand total-energy, exchange and correlation arguments; use reliable NIST evidence; identify why half/full slogans fail outside their context; and distinguish neutral atoms from ions and coordination compounds.

1. A prediction fails: the scientific problem

Imagine that you have just learned to construct electron configurations for the first transition series. Scandium, titanium and vanadium suggest a tidy sequence: keep the eighteen-electron argon core, place two electrons in 4s, and then add electrons to 3d. If that pattern continued without interruption, a neutral atom with twenty-four electrons would be [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². Yet the evaluated ground-state configuration of neutral chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. Five elements later, simple sequential filling would give copper [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s², whereas the observed ground state is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

These are not arithmetic mistakes. The proposed and observed configurations each contain the correct total number of electrons. Their difference concerns how electrons distribute among nearly competing subshells in the lowest-energy state. The scientific task is to explain why a broadly useful filling guide does not uniquely determine every real atom's ground state.

This lesson begins with the predictions, examines the measured ground states, develops a careful physical explanation, and finally tests the boundaries of that explanation. We will repeatedly distinguish a teaching rule, an observed fact and a model used to interpret that fact. Next Node 19 will investigate ions and the 4s–3d question; it should not be silently answered by transferring the neutral-atom pattern to every charged species.

2. What is meant by the ground state?

A free atom has many possible quantum states. The ground state is its lowest-energy allowed state under specified conditions, usually the isolated, nonrelativistic-to-relativistic atomic problem without an externally imposed field. A configuration is a compact occupancy description, not the full wavefunction: 3d⁵ 4s¹ says how many electrons occupy the 3d and 4s subshells in the dominant description. It does not provide every spin–orbital coupling, electron correlation or mixing coefficient.

An atom can absorb energy and reach an excited state without changing its chemical identity. Therefore a mathematically allowed occupancy and an experimentally identified ground-state occupancy are different claims. Two candidate arrangements can both satisfy electron-counting rules while only one is the lowest-energy arrangement. Spectroscopy and critically evaluated atomic structure data tell us which assignment is supported.

Even the phrase “an electron moves from 4s to 3d” is best interpreted as a comparison of two candidate distributions. It need not mean a literal electron was first placed in one orbital and physically migrated during the atom's formation. The atom reaches an allowed state through physical processes; our diagram is a convenient bookkeeping device.

3. Recover the prerequisites: n, l, m and spin

In a simple independent-electron picture, 4s has principal quantum number n = 4 and azimuthal quantum number l = 0. There is one spatial 4s orbital, with at most two electrons of opposite spin projections. The 3d subshell has n = 3, l = 2; its five allowed magnetic quantum numbers, −2, −1, 0, +1 and +2, correspond to five spatial orbitals, each with capacity two. Thus the d subshell has maximum occupancy ten, not five.

Pauli's exclusion principle prevents two identical fermions from occupying the same spin orbital. In an elementary box diagram, Hund's first rule favors an unpaired, same-spin arrangement across degenerate orbitals within a configuration before pairing is introduced. But neither principle by itself says that the energetic cost of 4s occupancy must always be smaller than 3d occupancy. For that, the interaction of all electrons with the nucleus and with one another matters.

4s capacity = 2   |   3d orbitals = 2l + 1 = 5   |   3d capacity = 2(2l + 1) = 10

4. The guide we are testing: Madelung (n + l)

The familiar Madelung or n + l order places lower values of n + l before higher values, with lower n first in a tie. A 4s orbital has n + l = 4; a 3d orbital has n + l = 5. For constructing many introductory neutral-atom configurations, that generates the sequence ...3p → 4s → 3d → 4p. It correctly helps us anticipate potassium and calcium and provides a good starting point through much of the first transition series.

It is not a theorem derived from the exact many-electron Schrödinger equation, nor does it assert a fixed universal ranking of orbital energies in every atom or ion. The electrostatic potential experienced by an electron changes as the nuclear charge and electron distribution change. Consequently, applying the order as if it were an inviolable physical law confuses an empirical construction mnemonic with a complete model of energy.

The practical method is disciplined: use the filling guide to propose a candidate; check capacities and total electrons; compare against authoritative ground-state data; then explain any important disagreement. This is how a scientific model should be used—successes and failures both provide information.

5. Chromium begins with twenty-four electrons

Chromium has atomic number Z = 24, so its electrically neutral atom has 24 electrons. The argon core accounts for 18 of them, leaving six beyond [Ar]. Sequentially assigning 4s² and then 3d⁴ uses all six: 18 + 2 + 4 = 24. That is the elementary predicted configuration, [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².

In the evaluated free-atom ground-state assignment, the outer distribution is 3d⁵ 4s¹: 18 + 5 + 1 = 24. Electron conservation is not violated. The change is a reorganization of occupation: relative to the simple prediction, the number of 3d electrons increases by one and the number of 4s electrons decreases by one. Both candidates satisfy the separate capacities, and neither can be rejected by electron counting alone.

The comparison creates a second question: why does the observed state have lower total energy? “Because it has five d electrons” names a pattern, but the stronger explanation must examine spin alignment, exchange and the energetic balance among electron–electron repulsions, electron–nucleus attraction and orbital reorganization.

6. Copper begins with twenty-nine electrons

Copper has Z = 29, leaving 29 − 18 = 11 electrons beyond the argon core. The simple sequence assigns 4s² and 3d⁹: 18 + 2 + 9 = 29. The evaluated ground state instead has [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, still 18 + 10 + 1 = 29. Here the d subshell is completely filled while the 4s subshell contains one electron.

The observed arrangement is not a general law that a full d subshell will always be obtained whenever it is reachable by shifting a single electron. Both the starting energy of the orbitals and the entire electron interaction energy have to favor that outcome. Copper is an especially clear lesson because the familiar “full subshell” mnemonic points to the observed result while not, by itself, providing a quantitative explanation.

Notice that a 3d¹⁰ subshell contains five pairs of opposite-spin electrons and has no net spin from the closed 3d shell in the simple coupling model; the remaining 4s¹ electron contributes a spin one-half. Chromium's ground term instead has a strikingly high total spin, which we shall connect to its six singly occupied outer spin orbitals.

8. Original diagram: the chromium alternatives

In the upper row a paired 4s orbital accompanies four singly occupied 3d orbitals. In the lower row all five d orbitals are singly occupied and 4s contains one electron. This second arrangement is consistent with chromium's high-spin ground term. The schematic itself does not prove an energy difference: it shows what the two candidate configurations mean.

9. Original diagram: copper and the closed d shell

The visual difference is one electron in an outer spin orbital, yet the many-electron state involves a different total arrangement. Both candidate configurations have one unpaired electron in their simplest box pictures, so copper's exception cannot be explained merely by saying it has “more unpaired electrons.” This is a useful contrast with chromium.

10. How experiments, rather than mnemonics, settle the question

Atoms emit and absorb light at discrete wavelengths because transitions connect quantized energy levels. Spectral line positions and intensities, selection rules, Zeeman splitting and patterns across related atomic states allow physicists to build and refine assignments of energy levels, angular momenta and dominant electron configurations. A simple spectroscope does not directly display an orbital occupancy label; the assignment comes from interpreting a network of spectral evidence with atomic theory.

The NIST Atomic Spectra Database reports critically evaluated ground states, levels and spectral lines. For neutral chromium (Cr I), the ground term is 3d⁵4s ⁷S₃; for neutral copper (Cu I) it is 3d¹⁰4s ²S₁/₂. Here “I” names the neutral spectrum in spectroscopic convention, and the superscripted term symbol describes spin and orbital angular momentum coupling. We will unpack the useful parts without pretending that term symbols can be deduced from a mnemonic alone.

The important epistemic distinction is this: the configurations have experimental grounding, and quantum models explain them with varying degrees of accuracy. If a basic rule and the evaluated spectra disagree, the basic rule is the approximation that needs revision, not the experimental dataset.

11. Reading Cr I and Cu I correctly

In spectroscopy, Cr I denotes neutral chromium, Cr II singly ionized chromium, and so on. The roman numeral is one more than the positive charge for the ionization stage. This notation must not be confused with oxidation numbers such as Cr(III) in chemistry or with the 3d electron count. Similarly, “Cu I” in an atomic spectra table means neutral copper, while copper(I) in an oxidation-state name conventionally means copper in oxidation state +1.

The complete ground-state label for Cr I includes the term ⁷S₃. The 7 is spin multiplicity 2S + 1, not electron count; the letter S corresponds to total orbital angular momentum L = 0; and subscript 3 indicates total angular momentum J = 3. The copper ground term ²S₁/₂ has S = 1/2, L = 0 and J = 1/2. None of these symbols changes the basic 24- and 29-electron bookkeeping.

12. Chromium's high spin: beyond five d electrons

For chromium's observed 3d⁵4s¹ arrangement, an elementary high-spin diagram places five electrons singly in five 3d orbitals and one singly in 4s. These six spin projections can align to yield a maximum total spin S = 6 × 1/2 = 3. The associated multiplicity is 2S + 1 = 7, consistent with the evaluated ⁷S ground term. This is a useful link between Hund's rule and spectroscopic evidence.

Why does spin alignment matter energetically? Electrons are indistinguishable fermions. The total electronic wavefunction must be antisymmetric under exchange. For same-spin electrons, the spatial part of the state carries corresponding antisymmetry; this changes the pair probability and can reduce certain electron–electron repulsion contributions. The term exchange stabilization refers to an energetic effect of the antisymmetrized many-electron state; it is not a separate classical attractive force between parallel spins.

Importantly, a full quantitative calculation also accounts for the rest of the energy. A higher spin multiplicity does not guarantee a lower total energy across every choice of subshell occupation, every element or every chemical environment.

13. Copper's closed 3d shell: a different balance

For copper, the observed 3d¹⁰4s¹ state puts ten electrons in the five d orbitals, making a closed d subshell. In a simple orbital description the d subsystem contributes net S = 0 and L = 0; the single 4s electron gives S = 1/2 and L = 0, matching the ground term ²S₁/₂. The proposed d⁹4s² candidate has a d hole with a different angular-momentum structure. Merely counting unpaired electrons would miss this difference.

The closed subshell is frequently described as “especially stable.” That is a useful qualitative clue, but one should ask: stable relative to which alternative and in which atom? The actual energy difference emerges from the combination of one-electron contributions and many-electron interactions, including exchange and correlation. The radial distributions of 3d and 4s electrons and their responses to nuclear charge also matter.

Do not explain copper by mechanically counting ten “exchange pairs” or by declaring that any full d subshell must outrank every competing configuration. Copper's experimentally identified ground state is the fact; the closed-shell story is a partial interpretation of its energy balance.

14. A total-energy comparison is the correct mathematical question

For a fixed nucleus and fixed total electron number, call the two possible configurations A and B. The physical ground state is the lowest-energy allowed state after the wavefunctions have been treated consistently. The useful comparison is the total energy difference, not the energy of one isolated 4s orbital subtracted from the energy of one isolated 3d orbital. In a many-electron atom, orbital energies depend on the electron density and on the approximation used to calculate them.

ΔE = Etotal(B) − Etotal(A)
ΔE < 0 favors B over A. Both energies refer to the same atom and electron count.

For chromium, define A = [Ar]3d⁴4s² and B = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. For copper, define A = [Ar]3d⁹4s² and B = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. A sufficiently accurate full calculation of all relevant allowed atomic states identifies the lower-energy state. The sign of ΔE cannot be established simply from electron count, from the n + l value, or from the fact that B has a familiar half/full d occupancy.

When configurations strongly interact, a real atomic eigenstate can mix contributions from more than one configuration. A single label then denotes a useful dominant character rather than a rigid literal partition of six or eleven distinguishable particles.

15. Where does total electronic energy come from?

A nonrelativistic fixed-nucleus model for N electrons includes their kinetic energies, the attraction between each electron and the nucleus, and the repulsion between each electron pair. The kinetic energy cannot be thrown away: tighter localization in space affects momentum and thus kinetic energy. Electron repulsion cannot be thrown away either, especially in transition-metal atoms where near-lying subshell arrangements compete.

H = Σi [−(ℏ²/2me)∇i² − Ze²/(4πε₀ri)] + Σi<j e²/(4πε₀rij)
Fixed-nucleus nonrelativistic Coulomb Hamiltonian; i and j index electrons, Z is nuclear charge, r is separation, ℏ = h/2π.

The sum over pairs runs over distinct i < j and avoids double-counting. This Hamiltonian is a model; precise work may include finite nuclear mass, relativistic effects and spin-dependent interactions. The central lesson for chromium and copper is that the energy is a property of an interacting many-electron state, not an immutable list of energy costs attached to orbital labels.

16. Orbitals change as the atomic environment changes

The 4s orbital in a potassium atom is not energetically identical to an orbital called 4s in neutral chromium or a chromium ion. As protons are added across the period and electrons redistribute, the effective nuclear attraction, screening and radial shapes change. The 3d orbitals can contract and interact differently with other electrons; 4s remains comparatively spatially extended in many transition-metal contexts. These trends influence relative energies and energetic reorganization.

Our labels 3d and 4s remain useful quantum-chemical descriptions, but they do not imply that nature follows a fixed assembly conveyor belt. Even in self-consistent-field calculations, different occupancies can cause orbitals to relax into somewhat different shapes. This is why a simplistic “one 4s electron costs x and one 3d electron costs y” explanation has limited reliability.

A thoughtful exam response therefore distinguishes the aufbau construction order from the energy ordering of fully formed electronic states. They are connected but not identical.

17. Exchange is helpful but not a one-line formula

In introductory lessons, an exchange-energy cartoon suggests that equal-spin electrons spread among different orbitals can reduce average Coulomb repulsion. The number of same-spin pairs among n aligned electrons is C(n,2) = n(n − 1)/2. For five aligned d spins, that number is 10; for four, it is 6. The calculation can help a student see why additional aligned spins alter the structure of interactions.

Yet that combinatorial number is not an actual exchange energy. An energy contribution depends on integrals over spatial orbitals, their overlap, the nucleus and other electrons. Nor is it legitimate to compare “10 versus 6” and conclude that chromium must choose d⁵4s¹ in every physical situation. Other changes, including pairing, kinetic energy, electron–nucleus attraction, intershell interaction and orbital relaxation, must enter the total balance.

Model caution: Count spin pairs to understand the mechanism qualitatively; do not present the spin-pair count as a measured energy or a universal prediction of ground-state occupations.

18. Electron correlation and the limits of independent orbitals

An independent-particle picture approximates each electron as moving in an average potential. The actual correlated motion of electrons is more intricate: their instantaneous positions and motions are statistically linked by electrostatic interaction and by fermionic antisymmetry. Electron correlation in quantum chemistry generally refers to effects beyond a chosen mean-field approximation, such as Hartree–Fock. Different atomic terms and configurations gain different amounts of correlation energy.

This matters when two candidate states are relatively close in energy. An approximation may obtain broad orbital patterns correctly yet get a fine energetic ordering wrong. Reliable atomic theory may need configuration interaction, multiconfiguration methods, and appropriate relativistic corrections. A one-line table of orbital energies is therefore not a proof of chromium or copper's ground state.

For life-science students the deepest takeaway is not to memorize computational method names. It is to understand why more than a one-electron picture is needed when an apparent exception appears.

19. Why is “half-filled stability” a limited mnemonic?

A half-filled d subshell means d⁵: each of the five spatial d orbitals contains one electron in the elementary high-spin representation. Chromium's ground state has this d⁵ population, while manganese has d⁵4s². The observation that both can contain d⁵ does not tell us how many 4s electrons each should possess. Six electrons outside [Ar] in Cr and seven in Mn face different nuclear charges and different energetic comparisons.

To call d⁵ “stable” without a reference state is incomplete. A half-filled arrangement may offer favorable high-spin exchange characteristics and orbital symmetry features, but the total energy still compares entire states. A neighboring atom might favor the d⁵ configuration while retaining an extra 4s electron, as Mn does. The accurate statement is that partially filled subshells and their spin coupling affect energetics; they do not override all other contributions.

Exam questions often exploit this confusion by presenting an unqualified rule that every atom possessing d⁴ should spontaneously become d⁵. The correct challenge is always: what atom, what charge, what ground-state evidence and what alternative state?

20. Why is “fully filled stability” also incomplete?

Similarly, copper is d¹⁰4s¹ and zinc is d¹⁰4s². Both have a closed 3d subshell; only one has a singly occupied 4s. In another row of the periodic table, palladium's evaluated ground state is [Kr]4d¹⁰ with no 5s electron. This is a direct counterexample to any universal rule that a transition atom with a full d shell must also have exactly one outer s electron.

Words such as “stable” are often shorthand for “lower total energy than nearby alternatives under defined conditions.” They do not identify a single term that can be added mechanically. The actual energetic balance depends on nuclear charge, shell extent, electron repulsion, coupling and relaxation. Treating every d¹⁰ configuration as an identical chemical entity also overlooks the different oxidation states and environments that atoms can occupy.

Thus chromium and copper should be presented as important, familiar counterexamples to a simplified filling guide, not as evidence that an equally absolute half/full-sublevel law replaces it.

21. The experiment and model form a coherent story

The same intellectual method appears throughout science. A model predicts a pattern. An accurate measurement reveals a deviation. A better theory explains why the pattern worked where it did and failed where it did not. In this case, an orbital-order mnemonic gives useful starting configurations, while an interacting-electron description and spectroscopic observation determine a more accurate neutral-atom ground-state assignment.

22. Beyond Cr and Cu: other periods test the mnemonic

Chromium and copper are the best-known first-row exceptions, but the broader periodic table is less orderly than two isolated corrections suggest. NIST's evaluated neutral-atom list includes Mo: [Kr]4d⁵5s¹ and Ag: [Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹, as well as Nb: [Kr]4d⁴5s¹, Ru: [Kr]4d⁷5s¹ and Pd: [Kr]4d¹⁰. For example, a naïve continuation from [Kr]4d³5s² would not predict Nb's observed distribution.

The examples matter because they show that “half-filled or full” does not account for the entire set. Ruthenium has d⁷5s¹, neither a half-filled nor a full d subshell. Palladium has d¹⁰5s⁰, which a rigid one-electron shift slogan would miss. Additional rows bring relativistic and correlation effects of increasing importance; we need not calculate them to recognise the limits of the simple guide.

23. How neutral atoms differ from ions and molecules

This chapter's values refer to isolated, neutral atoms. Removing an electron, introducing an electric field, forming a coordination complex or placing the element in a solid changes the Hamiltonian, energy levels and in some cases the appropriate electronic description. One cannot infer an ion's complete configuration by applying the neutral-atom Madelung sequence as a rigid reverse procedure.

For example, the biological metal centers of enzymes are not simply neutral Cr or Cu atoms with an [Ar] core floating in vacuum. They are embedded in ligands, with definite oxidation states and bonding interactions. In a protein's copper site, the oxidation state and coordination environment influence the effective d-electron count, spin character, absorption spectrum and reactivity. These are authentic applications of electronic-structure thinking, but require chemical bonding and ligand-field theory in addition to neutral atomic tables.

We reserve formal ion-configuration construction and the 4s-versus-3d removal issue for Node 19. Keeping this boundary prevents a student from carrying a useful neutral-atom rule into a new physical problem without checking its assumptions.

24. Chromium's biological and environmental context

Chromium occurs in chemically distinct forms with different biological consequences. The essential point here is not to derive chromium chemistry from the neutral Cr I ground configuration. In materials and environmental chemistry, changes of oxidation state alter electron counts and bonding, while different chemical species have very different toxicity profiles. Hexavalent chromium species are established toxicological concerns; this cannot be inferred from the neutral atom's high-spin d⁵4s¹ label alone.

When a biological text mentions “chromium,” ask whether it means neutral Cr, a particular oxidation state, or a defined coordinated compound. The presence of the same element does not imply the same electronic structure or biological effect. This is a practical example of how precise configuration language protects scientific reasoning.

The methodological connection is valuable: use the NIST neutral Cr configuration as a foundational reference, then add oxidation-state, ligand and solvent information before drawing a biochemical conclusion.

25. Copper sites connect electronic structure to biology

Copper is part of biological electron-transfer systems and redox enzymes. Copper-bearing proteins can exploit accessible oxidation-state changes and metal–ligand covalency; the energetic arrangement of electrons in a coordinated site helps shape spectra and function. A free neutral atom with [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ is not a valid stand-in for a complex copper site in an enzyme.

For example, comparing Cu(I) and Cu(II) in a biochemical context requires their charge states and the identity and geometry of coordinating ligands. The distinction can connect to optical absorption, magnetic response and redox chemistry; the relevant explanations come from ionic and molecular electronic structure, not merely from the isolated neutral-atom box diagram. Later nodes build these additional layers.

One robust lesson transfers directly: occupancy depends on the physical system. A student's answer should name the system being modeled before proposing a configuration.

26. Common test formats: counting, comparison and reasoning

On a basic test you may be asked to write the observed neutral configurations of chromium and copper. On an analytical question you may be given the rule-based forms and asked to correct them while preserving the electron count. A deeper question may ask which statement is more defensible: “half-filled shells always dominate” or “the balance of interacting-electron energies determines the ground state.” The second captures the scientific logic.

A term-symbol question may ask for chromium's spin multiplicity. With six aligned outer electrons in the high-spin picture, S = 3, so the multiplicity is 2S + 1 = 7. For copper d¹⁰4s¹, the d shell contributes no total spin and the outer electron gives S = 1/2, hence multiplicity 2. These claims are consistent with evaluated ground-state terms, but detailed term calculations for arbitrary configurations require angular-momentum coupling theory.

Another question may offer the measured first ionization energy and ask whether it equals the orbital-promotion difference between d⁴4s² and d⁵4s¹. It does not: ionization changes the number of electrons, whereas that configuration comparison concerns alternative states of the same neutral atom.

27. A simple decision algorithm for exam answers

First identify the species: element, proton count and net charge. If the species is neutral Cr or Cu, start from Z = 24 or 29; if it is an ion, do not automatically apply this chapter's neutral values. Next build the [Ar] core (18 electrons). Compute the remaining count. Write the rule-based candidate to understand why an exception is being discussed. Finally replace the candidate with the evaluated free-neutral-atom ground-state occupancy and check that its electron total equals Z.

After the arithmetic, add a sentence separating the empirical result from the explanation: these ground states are supported by spectroscopy and many-electron atomic structure; half/full occupancy and exchange considerations help explain the trend, but a fixed n + l ranking does not predict every case. This short procedure avoids both memorization without understanding and overcomplicated claims that cannot be justified in an exam.

Sanity checks: Cr must sum to 24, Cu to 29; neither 4s orbital holds more than two electrons; neither 3d subshell holds more than ten; and the label “neutral” must accompany any direct use of the NIST configurations.

28. Two different uses of the word 'stability'

Students encounter “stability” in several contexts: low total energy of an isolated ground state, resistance to ionization, oxidation-state preference, bond energy and kinetic persistence against reaction. These are not interchangeable. Cr has a ground state d⁵4s¹, but that observation by itself does not prove that chromium is chemically unreactive, nor that it has the highest ionization energy among its neighbors.

For example, NIST reports a first ionization energy near 6.7665 eV for Cr and 7.72638 eV for Cu, both referenced to their respective ground atoms and ions. The measured quantities are not the energy differences between the two neutral configuration candidates pictured earlier. In particular, it would be a scientific error to subtract these ionization energies to calculate the energy gained by a notional 4s-to-3d shift.

Use the phrase “lower-energy ground-state configuration” for our central question, and identify any new meaning of stability explicitly before discussing biological chemistry.

29. A disciplined treatment of uncertainty and exactness

We can state with high confidence that the evaluated neutral atomic ground states are d⁵4s¹ for Cr and d¹⁰4s¹ for Cu. We cannot infer a precise energy gap between hypothetical configurations from the occupancy symbols alone. Those gaps depend on the electronic state, term coupling and computational or experimental method; treating one schematic “promotion energy” as a universal constant is unjustified.

In theoretical language, the many-electron wavefunction is the quantity that characterizes the atomic state. Orbital occupation numbers provide a useful representation, and the dominant configuration is often well defined for teaching purposes. But orbitals are not labeled electrons, and a real interacting atom does not necessarily inhabit one pure independent-particle determinant.

Therefore “chromium gains stability because 3d is half filled” is acceptable only as an explicitly qualitative introduction. A rigorous answer adds the interacting-energy balance and the spectroscopic identification of the ground state.

30. From chromium and copper to the next learning problem

The first transition-series lesson began with a regular-looking pattern after calcium. Chromium and copper showed that the regularity is useful but not universal. We have now connected electron counting, orbital capacity, Hund-type spin arrangements, total energies, evaluated spectral evidence and the limits of simple filling rules. Importantly, the true starting point is not a claim that nature “breaks a rule,” but recognition that a simple model had a limited domain.

The next natural question is what happens when a transition-metal atom becomes positively charged. Must the electron removed come from whichever subshell a construction diagram filled last? Can we carry the neutral ground-state configuration unchanged into a protein's metal site? Those questions lead to Node 1.A.2.19: Electronic Configurations of Ions and the 4s–3d Issue, and require a fresh comparison of ionic energies and actual chemical environments.
