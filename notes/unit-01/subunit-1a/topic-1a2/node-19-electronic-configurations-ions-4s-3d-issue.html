1. The question that appears when an atom becomes an ion

In the previous lesson, we found the experimentally established ground-state configurations of neutral chromium and copper, and learned why the approximate Aufbau filling order cannot determine every atomic state. But living cells, minerals and salts rarely contain isolated neutral transition-metal atoms. Iron in a heme site, copper in an electron-transfer protein, sodium in the cytoplasm and chloride in extracellular fluid are encountered in charged or chemically bonded environments. Before discussing their biological behavior, we need to know how a change of electron number changes electronic configuration.

Consider iron. Neutral iron has twenty-six electrons and the familiar free-atom ground configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². Iron(II), written Fe²⁺, has twenty-four electrons and is commonly described as [Ar] 3d⁶. Iron(III), Fe³⁺, has twenty-three and is [Ar] 3d⁵ in the free-ion atomic configuration. Why is 4s occupied in the neutral atom, but not in the common +2 and +3 ions? Is there a contradiction in the usual filling diagram?

The central answer is that a filling mnemonic predicts neither universal ionization paths nor fixed orbital energies. Every change of charge changes the many-electron problem. We will learn a reliable bookkeeping procedure first, apply it to main-group ions, then use evaluated atomic data and physical reasoning to tackle the 4s–3d issue in transition metals. We will also learn precisely where the useful classroom rule stops being universal.

2. Review: an electron carries charge −e

A proton has electric charge +e and an electron has charge −e, with e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb in SI units. The atomic number Z counts protons and defines the element. Forming an ion ordinarily changes the number of electrons, not the number of protons in the nucleus. Thus a species with twenty-six protons remains iron whether it has 26, 25, 24 or 23 electrons.

Let N be the electron count and let z be the signed ionic charge measured in units of e. Charge conservation then gives z = Z − N. Therefore N = Z − z. The sign is essential: a cation with charge +2 has two fewer electrons than the neutral atom, whereas an anion with charge −2 has two more. This equation is independent of the orbital model and works as a nonnegotiable audit for every configuration.

Q = (Z − N)e   ⇒   N = Z − z
Q: net charge in coulombs; z = Q/e: signed charge number; Z: proton count; N: electron count.

For Fe²⁺, Z=26 and z=+2, giving N=26−2=24. For O²⁻, Z=8 and z=−2, giving N=8−(−2)=10. When students confuse a negative charge with “subtract two electrons,” their answers can be wrong even if their orbital capacities are correct.

3. Ions versus isotopes: two changes that must not be confused

A change in the number of neutrons produces a different isotope of the same element. A change in the number of electrons produces a different charge state. The proton count remains Z in both cases. For example, a particular iron isotope can lose two electrons to form Fe²⁺ without changing its number of nuclear protons or neutrons; ionization is an electronic process, not nuclear decay.

The mass of an electron is tiny relative to a proton or neutron, so the mass of an ion may be extremely close to that of the neutral atom; that is not a reason to call it neutral. The physical and chemical properties can change substantially because the electron distribution and electrostatic interactions change. This distinction is fundamental in mass spectrometry, metal-binding biology and ionic transport.

Unit-safe notation: The superscript in Fe³⁺ is ionic charge, not three electrons in an orbital. The superscript in 3d⁵ counts five d electrons, and the leading 3 is the principal quantum number. These different superscripts encode different quantities.

4. The three-stage ion-configuration method

The most reliable student procedure separates facts that should not be mixed. First, identify the nuclear charge Z and the desired signed ion charge z. Compute N = Z−z. Second, begin from a checked neutral ground-state configuration if the task asks you to reason from the parent atom; note that a change of N may reorganize orbitals. Third, write or verify an allowed configuration for the target ion and audit its total electron count, orbital capacities and actual species context.

For common main-group examples, removing outer-shell electrons or filling the available valence orbitals is an effective construction rule. For common first-row transition-metal cations, the neutral ns electrons are usually removed before (n−1)d electrons when writing elementary +2 and +3 configurations. But the second step is an empirical shortcut, not an inviolable theorem about every successive free-ion ground state.

When there is a reliable evaluated ground-state table for a free ion, prefer that evidence over an extrapolated rule. Also distinguish a free ion from an ion in an ionic crystal or a metal center bound to a protein. All can have the same formal charge while experiencing different electronic potentials.

5. Why a cation has fewer electrons

A cation is positively charged because it has fewer electrons than protons. Neutral sodium has Z=11 and configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹. Removing the 3s electron leaves Na⁺ with ten electrons, 1s²2s²2p⁶, conveniently written [Ne]. The charge is +e because the eleven protons are balanced by only ten electrons.

For magnesium, Z=12 and neutral [Ne]3s² becomes Mg²⁺ with ten electrons after losing both outer 3s electrons. In calcium, neutral [Ar]4s² becomes Ca²⁺=[Ar]. These are ordinary main-group examples in which the highest occupied principal shell loses its s electron or electrons, giving the electron count of a noble gas.

But the fact that a resulting ion has a noble-gas-like configuration does not mean its formation is automatically energetically favorable in any imaginable environment. Ion formation involves ionization energies, electron affinities and surrounding interactions; ionic compounds may be stabilized through lattice energy, solvation or chemical bonding.

6. Why an anion has more electrons

An anion has more electrons than protons. A neutral chlorine atom, Z=17, has [Ne]3s²3p⁵. If it gains one electron it has eighteen electrons, and chloride Cl⁻ is [Ar]. Fluorine has Z=9 and F⁻ has ten electrons, [Ne]. The electron gained is accommodated in the incompletely filled valence subshell without exceeding Pauli capacity.

Neutral oxygen has eight electrons, 1s²2s²2p⁴. As a formal oxide ion O²⁻, the count is 8−(−2)=10, so the idealized configuration is 1s²2s²2p⁶ = [Ne]. Sulfide S²⁻ similarly has eighteen electrons, [Ar]. The arithmetic is simple, but important physical qualifications apply when we ask whether these multiply charged anions survive as isolated gas-phase particles.

For a negative ion, never read the minus sign as “remove.” You can test your intuition by thinking about charge: adding a negatively charged electron makes the net charge more negative, so the electron total rises.

7. Isoelectronic species share an electron count

The word isoelectronic means “having the same number of electrons,” although it does not imply identical nuclear charges or identical energies. Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, F⁻ and the formal oxide O²⁻ all have ten electrons. Ar, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ and the formal sulfide S²⁻ all have eighteen. They can share closed-shell occupancy patterns under suitable conditions, yet their proton numbers differ, so the attraction experienced by their electrons is not the same.

Consider the neon-like set: O²⁻ has Z=8, F⁻ has Z=9, Ne has Z=10, Na⁺ has Z=11 and Mg²⁺ has Z=12. Their electron count N is constant at ten, but the nuclear charge is not. This makes them a useful conceptual sequence for discussing electron attraction and ion size later, when the Academy reaches periodic properties.

For the present topic, the safest conclusion is narrower: you can recognize the same electron count and standard closed-shell shorthand without claiming equal radii, equal ionization energies or identical chemical behavior.

9. A crucial caveat: oxide in a crystal is not a stable free dianion

An electron-counting exercise often asks for O²⁻=[Ne]. That is the standard formal ion description within oxides such as magnesium oxide. It is scientifically incomplete to conclude that an isolated O²⁻ gas-phase particle is stable simply because it has ten electrons. Adding the first electron to O(g) is energetically favorable in the usual electron-affinity convention, but adding a second to already negative O⁻(g) is strongly unfavorable. An isolated oxide dianion is not stable against losing an electron in vacuum.

Inside an ionic solid, attractions to neighboring cations and the electrostatic environment stabilize oxide-like negative charge. Real solids also display polarization and sometimes significant covalency, so perfect point charges are idealizations. The crystal's stability is a property of the whole material, not proof that a free O²⁻ ion has an independently stable gas-phase ground state.

This qualification is not an optional curiosity: it shows how chemical context can decide whether a formally allowed electron count corresponds to a stable physical species. The analogy will help when we study metal centers in proteins, where ligands likewise alter electronic energies.

10. Why the simple filling diagram predicts 4s before 3d

The familiar Madelung or n+l diagram orders subshells for a useful neutral-atom construction sequence. For 4s, n+l=4+0=4; for 3d, n+l=3+2=5. The mnemonic therefore places 4s earlier. Potassium is [Ar]4s¹ and calcium is [Ar]4s², while the first-row transition series subsequently involves the 3d subshell.

This sequence makes an effective introductory construction method, but orbital energies are not universal numbers attached permanently to the labels “4s” and “3d.” Different nuclear charge, electron occupation, shielding, electron–electron repulsion, relaxation and correlation alter the many-electron energies. An atom with one charge is not an identical energetic system to its cation.

Thus the phrase “4s fills before 3d” describes a helpful pattern in building many neutral configurations. It does not imply that when a transition metal loses electrons, the 3d electrons must be removed before the 4s electrons in a mechanical reversal of the sequence.

11. The key distinction: Aufbau order is not reverse ionization order

The elementary rule for many first-row transition-metal cations says to remove the neutral 4s electrons before 3d electrons. For neutral iron, [Ar]3d⁶4s², the common +2 ion is [Ar]3d⁶ rather than [Ar]3d⁴4s². In other words, the two 4s occupancies disappear while the d⁶ population remains for Fe²⁺. The +3 ion then has [Ar]3d⁵.

Students sometimes protest: “But we put electrons into 4s before 3d; surely we must pull them out of 3d first.” That treats the filling sequence as a reversible chronological operation. Electrons are indistinguishable quantum particles, and the state after ionization is determined by the allowed states and total energies of a different electron-number system. Simple occupancy arrows are a model comparison, not a literal film of individual electrons being removed.

For common neutral Fe, Cu, Zn and their simple cations, the 4s-first removal convention is useful; later we will see NIST-confirmed counterexamples to simplistic one-by-one removal descriptions in some singly charged atoms.

12. What changes in energy when electron number changes?

In an independent-electron approximation, each orbital is computed in an effective potential that depends on the nuclear charge and all other electrons. Remove an electron and the remaining electrons see a different screening environment; the orbitals can contract or relax. The energy of 3d relative to 4s can change, and a reorganization of occupation can accompany ionization. A statement like “4s is universally higher energy than 3d” is as misleading as “4s is universally lower.”

The total-energy question is more reliable. Let EN be the neutral ground-state energy and EN−1 the corresponding positive-ion ground-state energy, with a free electron at the zero kinetic-energy reference. Then the first ionization energy in a gas-phase isolated-atom idealization is IE₁ = EN−1 − EN (using a consistent energy zero). To obtain the next ion, compare the next electron count and ionic ground state separately.

IE₁ = Eground(X⁺) − Eground(X)
Both energies are total energies of different electron-count species referenced consistently; IE is positive for a bound electron.

These differences are not simply a subtraction of two fixed orbital-energy labels and should not be confused with the energy gap between two excited configurations of the same ion.

14. Iron from neutral Fe to Fe²⁺: a full construction

Begin with Z(Fe)=26 and neutral configuration [Ar]3d⁶4s²; [Ar] contains eighteen electrons. The free atom has 18+6+2=26 electrons. For Fe²⁺, z=+2 and N=26−2=24. The commonly established free-ion configuration is [Ar]3d⁶, giving 18+6=24. In the standard textbook construction, remove the two neutral 4s electrons.

Do not accidentally write [Ar]3d⁴4s². Although this also totals twenty-four, it applies the wrong assumption about the ionic ground state. The corrected assignment keeps a 3d⁶ population with 4s occupancy zero. If a diagram is requested, the five 3d orbitals can be depicted with six electrons consistent with Pauli and Hund-type high-spin filling, but a fully interacting ground level has more structure than five independent boxes.

For a biological iron center, the ionic shorthand d⁶ becomes a starting point, not a complete prediction of the protein's observed spin or bonding. Crystal-field splitting, covalent bonding and ligand geometry affect the detailed electronic states.

15. Iron from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺: one more electron

For Fe³⁺, the signed charge is +3. Its electron count must be 26−3=23. Starting from the already established Fe²⁺ configuration [Ar]3d⁶, the next electron-count reduction gives [Ar]3d⁵, which has 18+5=23 electrons. The standard description leaves the 4s subshell empty and removes one d electron in the additional ionization.

The 3d⁵ designation is familiar from chromium and manganese, but it does not mean Fe³⁺ and neutral Cr have identical overall electronic structures. Fe³⁺ has Z=26 with 23 electrons; neutral Cr has Z=24 with 24 electrons and also has a 4s¹ population. Different nuclear charge, charge state and environment mean different total energies and spectroscopic levels.

Two practical safeguards are to verify the ion's charge and to count every electron in its configuration. The correct Fe³⁺ notation is not [Ar]3d³4s² (wrong ionic ground-state assignment), nor is it [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ (too many electrons for Fe³⁺).

16. Copper from [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ to Cu⁺

Unlike the neutral iron atom, free neutral copper has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Its atomic number is 29. A copper cation Cu⁺ has 29−1=28 electrons, so the standard ground configuration is [Ar]3d¹⁰ (18+10=28). This provides an especially transparent example of outer 4s occupancy disappearing while the full d shell remains in the free positive ion.

The d¹⁰ ion has no d vacancies in its simple closed-subshell picture. The whole isolated ion is a closed-shell electronic system with 28 electrons. But “Cu⁺ has no unpaired 3d electrons” should not be exaggerated into “every Cu(I)-containing molecule must have no magnetic response of any sort.” Ligands, coupled radicals or other components can contribute, and formal oxidation state is not a direct measurement of local electron populations.

Notice the contrast between the spectroscopic label Cu II, which denotes a singly charged atomic copper ion, and the chemical notation Cu(II), which denotes formal oxidation state +2. They differ by one Roman numeral if compared without understanding the conventions.

17. Cu²⁺ and the missing 3d electron

For copper(II) as a simple monatomic free ion, z=+2 and N=29−2=27. The standard configuration is [Ar]3d⁹, giving 18+9=27. Relative to Cu⁺=[Ar]3d¹⁰, this second electron-count reduction removes one electron from the d subshell. In an elementary box diagram, d⁹ has one unpaired electron, but the magnetic response of a particular copper(II) complex depends on the ligand environment and exchange coupling.

It is misleading to start from an incorrectly predicted neutral copper state [Ar]3d⁹4s² and remove two 4s electrons, then congratulate oneself for obtaining d⁹. The final answer happens to be the expected Cu²⁺ configuration, but the starting neutral configuration is wrong. A high-quality explanation begins with the experimentally established neutral Cu ground state and compares separately the +1 and +2 charge states.

Transition-metal ions also show changing chemical stabilities: the oxidation state most common in a protein or aqueous system is not determined solely by whether a d subshell is filled. Redox energetics, donors, solvent and ligands all participate.

With the +1 state, the neutral 4s¹ occupancy is absent and the d shell stays full. With +2, the d shell has one vacancy. 19. Chromium and manganese ions: further practice with exceptions

Neutral chromium has [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. For Cr²⁺, N=24−2=22, and the standard free-ion configuration is [Ar]3d⁴. For Cr³⁺, N=21, so it is [Ar]3d³. These common textbook configurations follow from the neutral one-electron 4s occupancy and subsequent reduction of d population. The electron count checks are 18+4=22 and 18+3=21.

Neutral manganese has [Ar]3d⁵4s². The customary Mn²⁺ ionic configuration is [Ar]3d⁵: 25−2=23 and 18+5=23. Mn³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁴ in the basic free-ion picture. The similarity between Mn²⁺ d⁵ and Fe³⁺ d⁵ is important because the species are isoelectronic in the strict electron-count sense: each has 23 electrons; the nuclear charge, however, is different.

Do not conflate oxidation state, ionic configuration and actual spin state in a ligand field. A free-ion d⁵ high-spin pattern is not automatically the spin state of every d⁵ coordination compound.

21. The surprising limitation: not every first cation is obtained by simply erasing 4s

The standard classroom method “remove 4s first” is often correct, but NIST's evaluated first-cation ground states reveal that a naïve electron-erasure algorithm can fail even when an ion still has the expected total number of electrons. From neutral vanadium [Ar]3d³4s², simply removing a 4s electron suggests [Ar]3d³4s¹. Yet NIST lists free V⁺ as [Ar]3d⁴. Similarly, neutral cobalt [Ar]3d⁷4s² has evaluated Co⁺=[Ar]3d⁸, and neutral nickel [Ar]3d⁸4s² has evaluated Ni⁺=[Ar]3d⁹. In each, occupancy reorganizes so the ground cation has no 4s electron.

Thus it is more precise to teach “outer s occupancy is typically removed or diminished, and d–s reorganization can accompany ionization” than to promise that every electron removed from a neutral transition atom leaves all other electron subshell occupancies unchanged. For iron, NIST lists Fe⁺=[Ar]3d⁶4s¹; for copper, Cu⁺=[Ar]3d¹⁰. Both familiar patterns coexist with the counterexamples.

These are not mistakes in a database. They are evidence that the true target is the lowest-energy state of the ion, not the output of a mechanical erasure operation. The ion may settle into a different occupancy pattern because its total electronic energy has changed.

23. Why this does not invalidate the 4s-first classroom rule

For many simple +2 ions of first-row transition metals, the familiar [Ar]3dⁿ configurations are reliable and widely used. Saying “remove the outer 4s electrons first” is a practical way to reach them from neutral 3dⁿ4s² configurations. What fails is the stronger claim that every intermediate first cation, every excited state and every chemical complex is created by one unrearranged electron deletion from a fixed set of orbitals.

Free ions can reorganize after each ionization step because the Hamiltonian and ground-state competition differ from those of the parent atom. In particular, different total spins, angular-momentum terms and orbital relaxation may change which configuration is lowest. A reliable data source such as NIST provides the evaluated ground assignment for a specific ionization stage. A student should accept that evidence rather than force-fit it to a textbook mnemonic.

For a typical exam asking Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺, the standard configurations remain the essential answers. For an advanced prompt that asks whether the rule is exact for all charge states, examples such as V⁺, Co⁺ and Ni⁺ establish that it is not.

24. Spectroscopic notation: Fe I, Fe II and Fe III

In atomic spectroscopy a Roman numeral indicates the ionization stage. A neutral atom is stage I. A singly charged positive ion is stage II, and a doubly charged ion is stage III. Therefore Fe I = neutral Fe, Fe II = Fe⁺ and Fe III = Fe²⁺. Fe³⁺ would be Fe IV. Students working across chemistry and spectroscopy often miss this offset.

In chemistry, by contrast, Fe(II) ordinarily means a formal iron oxidation state of +2 and Fe(III) means +3. These are not automatically the same as the labels Fe II or Fe III in a NIST atomic spectrum. A chemical Fe(II) complex has ligands, whereas NIST Fe III refers to the spectrum of an isolated doubly charged Fe atom/ion.

The distinction matters when reading a paper or database. If you search NIST for the free Fe²⁺ ion, the relevant atomic spectrum is Fe III, not Fe II. Always identify which convention is being used before extracting a configuration.

25. Electron count versus chemical oxidation number

A monatomic free ion Fe²⁺ has actual net charge +2 and therefore exactly 24 electrons. A metal in a covalent or coordination complex labeled iron(II), however, has a formal oxidation state assigned by a bookkeeping convention. Electron density is shared and redistributed through metal–ligand bonds; an oxidation-state label does not assert that every region around iron has a perfectly integer localized electron charge.

This is why the free-ion d count is a starting point for ligand-field models rather than an exact measurement of the density on the metal in a protein. A formal Fe(II) center is conventionally d⁶ and Fe(III) d⁵ in the usual ionic electron-counting model, but metal–ligand covalency and delocalization complicate the precise occupancy.

This distinction is especially valuable for biochemistry, where heme iron, iron–sulfur clusters and copper proteins are frequently introduced with oxidation numbers that describe electron transfer. Do not identify oxidation state directly with spin, geometry or ionic radius without the additional chemical conditions.

26. Why some atoms can lose multiple electrons

It takes energy to remove a bound electron from an isolated atom, and successive ionizations typically require increasingly large energies. A first ionization creates a cation; a second must remove an electron from an already positive ion, generally against stronger electrostatic binding. However, whether particular oxidation states are accessible or stabilized in compounds depends strongly on bonding, hydration and redox conditions.

Iron commonly forms +2 and +3 oxidation states because its chemistry can stabilize different electron populations in different environments. Copper commonly occurs as +1 and +2; metal–ligand chemistry determines the relative stability and biochemical role. Neither trend can be deduced solely from an “octet rule,” which is mainly a useful introductory guideline for many main-group systems.

Be careful with wording: saying a metal forms Fe³⁺ in water does not mean naked, uncoordinated Fe³⁺ ions float without solvent interactions. The hydrated and complexed environments change the free energy, bonding and in some cases acid–base chemistry.

27. A short derivation: from charge to configuration audit

Start from total charge Q. Each of Z protons contributes +e and each of N electrons contributes −e, giving Q = Ze − Ne = (Z−N)e. Divide by e to obtain z = Z−N. Rearranging yields N = Z−z. No quantum mechanics is needed for this conservation step; it is straightforward signed charge bookkeeping.

Next let ni be the occupation of atomic spin orbital i. For a single-determinant, independent-particle configuration, each spin orbital satisfies ni ∈ {0,1}, and the total electron number is N = Σ ni. Spatial subshell occupation numbers are sums over available spin orbitals. In the common orbital picture, s, p, d and f have capacities 2, 6, 10 and 14 respectively.

N = Σ ni;   ni ∈ {0,1};   N = Z − z
Occupation-number bookkeeping; the full interacting wavefunction may mix configurations, but every physical state has the appropriate total electron number.

With these two checks, you can reject impossible answers before asking more subtle energetic questions. For example, [Ar]3d¹¹ for a 29-electron ion violates d capacity even if the overall count looks tempting.

28. Pauli and Hund still operate when charge changes

Ion formation does not suspend quantum mechanics. Every electron remains a spin-half fermion; spin orbitals cannot be multiply occupied by indistinguishable electrons in violation of Pauli. Within a suitable set of degenerate spatial orbitals, Hund's first rule can guide how unpaired spins are drawn before pairing. But the energy spacing of orbitals and the effects of chemical ligands can change when the atom is charged or coordinated.

Take the simple Fe³⁺ d⁵ configuration. In a free-ion high-spin schematic, five d orbitals each contain one electron. For free Fe²⁺ d⁶ the basic d-box representation has five singly occupied orbitals before the sixth pairs in one, giving four unpaired electrons in a high-spin model. Yet a ligand field can favor low-spin or high-spin states depending on splitting and pairing energies. That comparison belongs in greater depth to the later magnetism and bonding lessons.

Therefore a single d count alone gives an allowed occupation number, not necessarily one universal magnetic moment across all biological compounds.

29. Case study: hemoglobin and iron oxidation states

Heme proteins offer a compelling reason to learn electron-counting precisely. In hemoglobin, iron in the heme environment binds oxygen through a complicated metal–ligand electronic structure. A formal Fe(II) versus Fe(III) distinction is biologically consequential: ferric methemoglobin does not bind oxygen in the same functional manner as the normal oxygen-binding ferrous form. The iron center is not a free isolated Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ gas-phase ion.

Our basic shorthand maps formal Fe(II) to d⁶ and Fe(III) to d⁵ in the ionic counting convention. However, the spin and charge distribution of oxygenated heme are influenced by covalency and charge-transfer descriptions. It would be incorrect to treat the oxygen-bound protein as nothing more than a simple free Fe²⁺ ion with a 4s orbital removed.

The educational connection is powerful: conservation of electron number and oxidation-state bookkeeping provide the starting language, while a complete biological mechanism requires spectroscopy, molecular orbitals, ligand field and protein environment.

30. Case study: copper proteins and oxidation-state cycling

Copper participates in biological electron-transfer and catalytic systems, often through changes between formal Cu(I) and Cu(II). In a simple ionic model, Cu(I) corresponds to d¹⁰ and Cu(II) to d⁹. This can help students anticipate differences in spin and spectroscopic behavior, but the exact redox potential and electronic structure depend on ligand donor atoms, geometry, covalency and coupling to other centers.

The important conceptual shift is to ask what electron count is being assigned to the metal site and under what convention. A redox step does not necessarily correspond to an electron entering or leaving a localized textbook orbital untouched by covalent interactions. Transition-metal sites are many-electron molecular systems.

Later lessons examine core/valence electron conventions and magnetic properties in more depth. Here we limit ourselves to correctly interpreting the ion charge and baseline d configuration.

31. Case study: iron–sulfur proteins are not free ions

Iron–sulfur clusters contain iron centers coordinated by sulfide and cysteine ligands and act in biological electron transfer. Their redox states are often described using formal combinations of Fe(II) and Fe(III), but electrons and spins may be distributed over multiple interacting centers. Writing [Ar]3d⁶ or [Ar]3d⁵ for an isolated ionic reference does not directly determine the observed cluster spectrum or magnetic coupling.

One should therefore avoid reading formal metal charges as literal images of localized electrons. In a cluster, electron exchange and coupling can be central to function. The beginner's job is to keep charge and electron-count logic consistent while recognizing that the chemical environment introduces new physics.

These biological examples are not decorative analogies; they are where the distinction between ion bookkeeping and full electronic state becomes indispensable.

32. The role of solvation and lattice energy

The stability of an ion is inseparable from environment. A hydrated Fe³⁺ species in water is stabilized by polar solvent interactions and coordination with water molecules or other ligands. In an ionic crystal, the combined attraction among cations and anions contributes greatly to stability. In a gas-phase reference experiment, an isolated ion has none of these surroundings.

The oxide O²⁻ example makes this especially vivid: a formal neon-like electron count is easy to write, yet the free gas-phase dianion is unstable. In a solid oxide, the surrounding lattice can strongly stabilize oxide-like charge. An appropriate energy comparison must include the entire physical system rather than pretending a free ionic configuration solves a materials thermodynamics problem.

For NET/SET-style reasoning, this guards against two false extremes: saying electron configurations never matter to chemical stability, and saying they alone determine every observed stability trend.

33. What an ionization energy actually measures

In atomic spectroscopy, the ionization threshold represents an energy difference from a particular atomic or ionic state to the corresponding separated higher-charge ion plus an electron at the chosen reference. If we denote the ground energies of neutral X and cation X⁺ as E(X) and E(X⁺), the first threshold is IE₁ = E(X⁺) − E(X) with zero-energy separated electron. The second is IE₂ = E(X²⁺) − E(X⁺).

These are differences between total energies of different electron-count states. In a hypothetical calculation a neutral configuration may contain a 4s² occupancy, while the cation ground state may have a reorganized d and 4s population. The ionization energy automatically includes that reorganization if both ground states have been correctly determined.

IE₂ = Eground(X²⁺) − Eground(X⁺)
The second ionization starts from the singly charged ion's ground state, not from a second electron deleted mechanically from the neutral drawing.

34. Charge, oxidation state and ionic radius: keep the boundaries

An ion may become more compact after electron loss, but the extent depends on species, occupancy and environment; a simple electron-counting calculation alone does not give a precise radius. Equally, a formal Fe(III) oxidation number in a complex is a bookkeeping label, not an actual measurable point charge at an iron nucleus. The physical distribution of electrons in a molecule is continuous.

These distinctions keep us from making sweeping claims such as “Fe³⁺ is always smaller than Fe²⁺ in every coordination environment” without specifying ionic radius conventions, coordination number and spin state. The next topic on periodic properties will examine trends and their limitations more systematically.

For this Node, be rigorous about what is directly established: the charge number, the electron count, the standard ion shorthand and the need to confirm electronic ground states under specified physical conditions.

35. A systematic exam workflow for main-group ions

For a main-group species, determine Z and the sign of charge. Count N = Z−z. Write the corresponding neutral configuration if needed. For a cation, identify the outer electrons removed under the usual low-energy construction; for an anion, add electrons to available valence orbitals without overfilling a subshell. Then sum the superscripts and compare with N.

Examples illustrate the sequence. Na⁺: Z=11, z=+1, N=10, [Ne]. Mg²⁺: Z=12, z=+2, N=10, [Ne]. Cl⁻: Z=17, z=−1, N=18, [Ar]. O²⁻ as a formal oxide species: Z=8, z=−2, N=10, [Ne]. All four checks should be explicit when you are first learning the subject.

When a question asks why a particular gas-phase multiply charged anion is unstable, the standard octet bookkeeping alone is not the answer. Discuss electron affinity and environmental stabilization instead of inferring stability from an apparent noble-gas electron count.

36. A systematic exam workflow for transition-metal ions

For a first-row transition-metal ion, begin with the correct neutral free-atom configuration, including Cr and Cu exceptions, and calculate the target electron count. For the standard classroom +2 and +3 cases, remove the neutral 4s population before reducing the 3d population, and verify the result against known ionic configurations. If the task concerns a particular free singly charged ion, look for evaluated data—because some first cations reorganize beyond simply losing one 4s electron.

For Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺, the resulting configurations are [Ar]3d⁶, [Ar]3d⁵, [Ar]3d¹⁰ and [Ar]3d⁹. To check, add 18 to the d exponent: 24, 23, 28 and 27. Compare with Z−z: Fe 26−2=24, 26−3=23; Cu 29−1=28, 29−2=27.

A good final answer includes the species, its charge, the configuration, an electron-count check and, when relevant, a short qualification that orbital energies can reorganize between charge states.

38. Misleading shortcuts: a preliminary diagnostic

There are three recurrent errors in ion-configuration questions. The first is a sign error: treating O²⁻ as if oxygen had lost two electrons. The second is a sequence error: taking the reverse of a neutral Aufbau construction as an exact electron-removal law. The third is a context error: transferring a free atomic ion's ground-state occupancy into an aqueous complex or protein as if ligands had no effect.

These errors can be detected with different tools. Charge conservation catches the sign error; evaluated ionic ground-state data and correct 4s/3d treatment catch the sequence error; chemical context and awareness of delocalization catch the context error. None is repaired by simply repeating “full and half-filled shells are stable.”

We will now practice the calculations, interpretation and explanations that transform those warnings into exam-ready competence.

40. Worked examples I — main-group cations and anions

Worked example 1. Na⁺ from neutral sodium

Solution and check. Sodium has Z=11. The +1 charge means N=11−1=10 electrons. Neutral Na=[Ne]3s¹; the cation has lost its one 3s electron and is [Ne]. Check: 2+2+6=10, matching the charge equation.

Worked example 2. Mg²⁺ from magnesium

Solution and check. Mg has Z=12 and neutral [Ne]3s². For +2, N=12−2=10. Removing the two outer 3s electrons gives Mg²⁺=[Ne]. It is isoelectronic with neon but contains two more protons than Ne.

Worked example 3. K⁺ from potassium

Solution and check. K has Z=19 and neutral [Ar]4s¹. Its +1 cation has 18 electrons and the closed-shell configuration [Ar]. A common error is [Ne] because the student remembers the previous noble gas too early; always count explicitly.

Worked example 4. Ca²⁺ from calcium

Solution and check. Neutral Ca=[Ar]4s² with Z=20. Ca²⁺ has 20−2=18 electrons. Remove the 4s² occupancy to obtain [Ar]. Its charge +2 is not the same as its 4s exponent, even though both happen to be 2 in the neutral atom.

Worked example 5. Al³⁺ from aluminium

Solution and check. Al has Z=13 and neutral [Ne]3s²3p¹. For Al³⁺, N=10. Removing the 3p electron and two 3s electrons yields [Ne]. The highest n=3 valence occupancies disappear while the 10-electron core remains.

Worked example 6. F⁻ from fluorine

Solution and check. Fluorine Z=9 has [He]2s²2p⁵. One added electron produces F⁻ with 10 electrons, [Ne]. Because z=−1, N=9−(−1)=10. This is a useful sign check before placing the electron in 2p.

Worked example 7. Cl⁻ from chlorine

Solution and check. Cl has Z=17, neutral [Ne]3s²3p⁵. For Cl⁻, N=17−(−1)=18. Add one electron to make 3p⁶ and obtain [Ar]. A chloride ion is not a neutral argon atom because its Z remains 17.

Worked example 8. Formal O²⁻ electron count

Solution and check. O has Z=8 and neutral 1s²2s²2p⁴. O²⁻ has N=8−(−2)=10, giving the textbook [Ne] formal oxide configuration. Qualify: O²⁻ as an isolated free gas-phase dianion is unstable; it is stabilized in condensed-phase materials.

Worked example 9. Formal S²⁻ electron count

Solution and check. S has Z=16, so S²⁻ has 18 electrons, [Ar]. This formal closed-shell assignment is useful for ionic compounds but does not mean a free gas-phase S²⁻ dianion is stable. The environment matters to thermodynamics.

Worked example 10. P³⁻ and the noble-gas pattern

Solution and check. P has Z=15, and P³⁻ would have 15−(−3)=18 electrons, formally [Ar]. It illustrates negative charge increasing electrons. Its formal ionic occupancy in compounds is not evidence for a stable isolated free triply charged P ion.

41. Worked examples II — iron, copper and other common metal ions

Worked example 11. Fe²⁺ from neutral iron

Solution and check. Neutral Fe has Z=26 and [Ar]3d⁶4s². Fe²⁺ requires 24 electrons. The standard configuration [Ar]3d⁶ totals 18+6=24; in the usual classroom picture the two 4s electrons are removed.

Worked example 12. Fe³⁺ from Fe²⁺

Solution and check. Fe³⁺ has N=26−3=23. Starting from Fe²⁺=[Ar]3d⁶, one further electron-count reduction gives [Ar]3d⁵ with 18+5=23. Writing [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ would give 24, the wrong electron count.

Worked example 13. Cu⁺ from neutral copper

Solution and check. Neutral Cu is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ and Z=29. Cu⁺ has N=28. Its free-ion ground configuration is [Ar]3d¹⁰, which has 18+10=28 electrons. Retaining 4s¹ would wrongly leave 29.

Worked example 14. Cu²⁺ from Cu⁺

Solution and check. Cu²⁺ has 29−2=27 electrons. Starting from Cu⁺=[Ar]3d¹⁰, the expected second reduction gives [Ar]3d⁹, totaling 27. It has one d vacancy within a ten-electron-capacity d subshell.

Worked example 15. Cr²⁺ from neutral chromium

Solution and check. Neutral Cr is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, Z=24. Cr²⁺ has 22 electrons and [Ar]3d⁴. One 4s occupancy and one 3d occupancy are absent relative to neutral Cr; 18+4=22.

Worked example 16. Cr³⁺ from Cr²⁺

Solution and check. Cr³⁺ has N=24−3=21, with standard [Ar]3d³ configuration. The sum 18+3=21. Incorrect [Ar]3d²4s¹ also totals 21 but is not the standard ground-state assignment.

Worked example 17. Mn²⁺ from manganese

Solution and check. Neutral Mn has Z=25 and [Ar]3d⁵4s². Mn²⁺ has 25−2=23 electrons, [Ar]3d⁵. Its simple high-spin free-ion 3d⁵ picture has five singly occupied d orbitals, but spin of coordination complexes can require more context.

Worked example 18. Co²⁺ from cobalt

Solution and check. Cobalt Z=27 has neutral [Ar]3d⁷4s². Co²⁺ has 25 electrons. The common free-ion configuration [Ar]3d⁷ totals 18+7=25; it is not the same as NIST Co⁺=[Ar]3d⁸, illustrating how successive states may reorganize.

Worked example 19. Ni²⁺ from nickel

Solution and check. Nickel Z=28 has neutral [Ar]3d⁸4s². Ni²⁺ has 26 electrons and common [Ar]3d⁸, totaling 18+8=26. NIST lists Ni⁺=[Ar]3d⁹, so a simple 'erase one 4s each step' trajectory is not an exact description of all intermediate free-ion ground states.

Worked example 20. Zn²⁺ from zinc

Solution and check. Neutral Zn has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s² with Z=30. Zn²⁺ has 28 electrons, [Ar]3d¹⁰. It shares an electron count and standard occupancy with Cu⁺, but their proton counts (30 and 29) and charges are different.

42. Worked examples III — reverse charge problems and ionic checks

Worked example 21. Charge of a 24-electron iron species

Solution and check. For iron Z=26 and N=24, z=Z−N=26−24=+2. It is Fe²⁺. This reverse calculation is a useful test when a problem gives only proton number and electron count.

Worked example 22. Charge of a 27-electron copper species

Solution and check. For Cu, Z=29 and N=27. z=29−27=+2, so the species is Cu²⁺. The d⁹ occupancy then supplies a standard ionic configuration consistent with 18+9=27.

Worked example 23. Identify an ion with 18 protons and 20 electrons

Solution and check. Z=18 identifies argon, and z=18−20=−2. The formal species is Ar²⁻, not Ca²⁺. Electron count alone does not establish a stable ground state; this charge assignment does not imply Ar²⁻ is a stable isolated ion.

Worked example 24. Compare the neon-like set

Solution and check. O²⁻: 8−(−2)=10; F⁻:9−(−1)=10; Ne:10; Na⁺:11−1=10; Mg²⁺:12−2=10. They are isoelectronic by count. Their nuclear charges differ, so energetics and actual sizes need not match.

Worked example 25. Find the error in Na⁺=1s²2s²2p⁵

Solution and check. The proposed occupancy totals 2+2+5=9 electrons. Na⁺ has 11−1=10. Correct the 2p exponent from five to six: Na⁺=1s²2s²2p⁶.

Worked example 26. Find the error in Fe³⁺=[Ar]3d⁶

Solution and check. That proposal gives 18+6=24 electrons, but Fe³⁺ has 23. Remove one d electron from Fe²⁺=[Ar]3d⁶: Fe³⁺=[Ar]3d⁵.

Worked example 27. Find the error in Cu²⁺=[Ar]3d¹⁰

Solution and check. The proposal totals 28 electrons but Cu²⁺ has 27. The 3d exponent must be nine in the standard free-ion model; [Ar]3d⁹ totals 27.

Worked example 28. Madelung 4s versus 3d audit

Solution and check. 4s has n+l=4+0=4 and 3d has n+l=3+2=5, so the conventional filling guide places 4s earlier. This does not imply neutral Aufbau order is simply reversed for every ion; ionic energies are different.

Worked example 29. Check NIST V⁺

Solution and check. Neutral V has Z=23 and [Ar]3d³4s², 23 electrons. V⁺ must have 22. NIST first-cation ground [Ar]3d⁴ totals 18+4=22, while a deletion-only candidate [Ar]3d³4s¹ also totals 22 but is not the evaluated ground configuration.

Worked example 30. Check NIST Co⁺

Solution and check. Neutral Co=[Ar]3d⁷4s² and Z=27. Co⁺ has 26 electrons. NIST gives [Ar]3d⁸=18+8=26; merely deleting one 4s electron would give d⁷4s¹, also 26 but not the listed first-cation ground state.

43. Worked examples IV — spectroscopy, NIST evidence and applications

Worked example 31. Check NIST Ni⁺

Solution and check. Neutral Ni=[Ar]3d⁸4s² and Z=28. Ni⁺ must have 27 electrons. NIST lists [Ar]3d⁹=27, which shows d–s reorganization relative to simple 4s-only deletion.

Worked example 32. Contrast NIST Fe⁺ with Fe²⁺

Solution and check. Fe⁺ has Z=26, N=25 and the NIST ground configuration [Ar]3d⁶4s¹:18+6+1=25. Fe²⁺ has N=24 and [Ar]3d⁶. Thus Fe supports the familiar stepwise disappearance of 4s before d reduction.

Worked example 33. Term convention for Fe III

Solution and check. NIST spectroscopic Fe III is an Fe²⁺ ion with 24 electrons, because stage I means neutral, II means +1 and III means +2. Chemical Fe(III) instead means formal oxidation state +3 in a compound; do not confuse the two.

Worked example 34. Convert a first ionization energy in eV

Solution and check. An illustrative 8.0 eV ionization threshold equals 8.0×1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J=1.2817413072×10⁻¹⁸ J per ionization event. The unit is energy; 8.0 eV here is a teaching value, not a measured threshold for Fe or Cu.

Worked example 35. Difference between sequential ionization energies

Solution and check. A hypothetical ion has E(X)=−100 eV, E(X⁺)=−92 eV and E(X²⁺)=−73 eV at a consistent reference. IE₁=−92−(−100)=8 eV; IE₂=−73−(−92)=19 eV. These are total-energy differences across different N.

Worked example 36. Counting spin orbitals in d⁹

Solution and check. The d subshell has five spatial orbitals and ten spin orbitals. In a d⁹ configuration, nine places are occupied and one is vacant. A simple orbital diagram has one unpaired d electron, but ligand spin-coupling in a chemical complex may complicate net magnetism.

Worked example 37. Two ions with identical d counts

Solution and check. Fe³⁺ and Mn²⁺ both have [Ar]3d⁵ and 23 electrons (Fe:26−3, Mn:25−2). They differ in proton number, net charge and interactions; identical electron counts do not mean identical ionization energies or biochemical functions.

Worked example 38. Fe²⁺ versus Cr: same electron total?

Solution and check. Neutral Cr has 24 electrons while Fe²⁺ also has 26−2=24. Cr I ground has [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, whereas Fe²⁺ has [Ar]3d⁶. Their electron totals match but their Z and ground configurations differ.

Worked example 39. Sodium versus Mg²⁺: different N

Solution and check. Neutral sodium has 11 electrons and Mg²⁺ has 10. Na⁺ has 10 and is isoelectronic with Mg²⁺. Always read charge state, not just the element, when comparing electronic configurations.

Worked example 40. A practical biological bookkeeping check

Solution and check. A simplified heme model assigns Fe(II) d⁶ and Fe(III) d⁵ under ionic counting. Both refer to Z=26; changing formal oxidation state from +2 to +3 corresponds to one fewer electron in the standard reference. Ligand covalency means the actual spatial electron density is not an exact localized integer.

44. Scientific cases I — interpreting ions, spectra and materials

Case 1 — a laboratory spectroscopic spectrum

A student looks for the ground level of free Fe²⁺ in the NIST Atomic Spectra Database and selects “Fe II” because Fe(II) is iron(II) in chemical nomenclature. Explain the mistake: Fe II in spectroscopy is Fe⁺, and Fe III is Fe²⁺. Spectroscopic stage is one more than the positive charge; chemical oxidation state notation with parentheses is a different convention.

Case 2 — Fe²⁺ ion configuration in an exam

An answer sheet prints Fe²⁺=[Ar]3d⁴4s². The total number 18+4+2=24 is correct but ground-state assignment is wrong. Starting with neutral Fe=[Ar]3d⁶4s² and common ionization pattern gives Fe²⁺=[Ar]3d⁶. This distinguishes electron-count correctness from energy-grounded configuration correctness.

Case 3 — copper electron-transfer proteins

A protein alternates formal Cu(I) and Cu(II) states in a redox process. Use d¹⁰ and d⁹ as ionic reference counts, but recognize that local geometry and ligand covalency influence measured properties. The free Cu⁺/Cu²⁺ configurations cannot by themselves determine the protein's EPR spectrum or reduction potential.

Case 4 — an oxide structure

A student argues MgO cannot form because the isolated O²⁻ ion is energetically unstable. The premise about the free dianion is relevant, but the conclusion ignores the solid lattice and polarization, which stabilize oxide-like negative charge. Ionic compound stability is a property of the assembled system.

Case 5 — the isoelectronic sequence

A table presents F⁻, Ne and Na⁺, each with ten electrons. A learner claims they must have equal radii and chemical behavior. The equal electron number is real, but Z=9,10,11 changes effective attraction. Isoelectronic does not mean identical atoms or chemical species.

Case 6 — a protein heme site

A biochemistry answer directly equates Fe²⁺(g)=[Ar]3d⁶ with the actual orbital energy diagram of oxygen-bound hemoglobin. The reference electron count is a useful starting point, but iron is coordinated to a heme and other ligands and participates in metal–ligand charge sharing. A molecular description is necessary for oxygen-binding electronic structure.

45. Scientific cases II — biological and advanced ionic contexts

Case 7 — cobalt first-cation exception

A computational exercise predicts Co⁺=[Ar]3d⁷4s¹ by deletion of the first 4s electron from neutral Co. NIST instead reports free Co⁺=[Ar]3d⁸. Both candidates have 26 electrons, but the ground-state rearrangement reflects different ionic total energies; this is why 'remove 4s first' is not an exact no-reorganization algorithm.

Case 8 — nickel first-cation exception

Neutral Ni is [Ar]3d⁸4s². A naïve Ni⁺ answer d⁸4s¹ preserves total 27 electrons, but NIST reports [Ar]3d⁹ as the evaluated first-cation ground. The discrepancy is not arithmetic; it is quantum-state energetics. Ni²⁺ is conventionally [Ar]3d⁸.

Case 9 — ionic charge in mass spectrometry

A mass spectrometer may detect differently charged forms of the same element or molecule. The number of protons identifies the nucleus, while net ionic charge changes how the particle responds to electromagnetic fields. A mass-to-charge observation is not enough to infer every electronic term; electron accounting and state identification remain separate tasks.

Case 10 — iron–sulfur redox

A cluster undergoes a one-electron redox event. It is tempting to assign the electron entirely to a specific Fe atom by free-ion notation, but metal centers and ligands can share electronic density. Formal oxidation-state bookkeeping is valuable, while experimental EPR, Mössbauer or other evidence is needed for detailed electronic-state assignments.

Case 11 — octet and environment

One textbook exercise gives O²⁻=[Ne], another explains an unstable gas-phase O²⁻. These statements concern different levels of description. The first is formal electron counting in a material; the second concerns thermodynamic stability of an isolated gas-phase dianion. Neither should erase the qualification attached to the other.

Case 12 — evaluating an overgeneralized rule

A classroom poster reads 'For every transition-metal ion, remove all 4s electrons before any 3d electron, and nothing else changes.' The first clause is an effective introductory shortcut for common cases; the second is too strong. NIST V⁺, Co⁺ and Ni⁺ show first-ion ground configurations with d–s reorganization. Replace the poster with: 'Count charge; use 4s-first as a common starting rule, verify actual ionic ground states.'

Practice Q16 · MCQ

Question: Which statement about O²⁻ is the most scientifically defensible? A. It is stable as a free gas-phase dianion solely because of [Ne]; B. Its ionic reference configuration is [Ne], but gas-phase O²⁻ is unstable; C. It has six electrons; D. It is a neutral neon atom

Answer with reasoning: B. Formal O²⁻ counting gives ten electrons, but a second electron attachment to O⁻ in vacuum is unfavorable; solid-state stabilization changes the situation.

Practice Q17 · MCQ

Question: For transition-metal ionization, the physical energy difference should be taken between: A. a fixed 4s orbital and a fixed 3d orbital; B. one-electron configurations with incorrect N; C. total energies of the neutral and ionic states; D. spin arrows alone

Answer with reasoning: C. Ground-state ionization energy compares interacting electronic states with different N. Orbital-energy mnemonics or arrows alone do not supply the full ionization threshold.

Practice Q18 · MCQ

Question: What is the neutral copper ground configuration used to derive Cu⁺? A. [Ar]3d⁹4s²; B. [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹; C. [Ar]3d⁸4s³; D. [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²

Answer with reasoning: B. Cu I is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. A is a simplistic predicted but not observed ground state; C violates 4s capacity; D totals 30.

Practice Q19 · MCQ

Question: For Fe²⁺ in a protein, which factor is needed beyond formal d⁶ counting? A. Ligand identity and geometry; B. A change of Z to 24; C. A universal forced 4s²; D. Treating all electrons as independent fixed particles

Answer with reasoning: A. Ligand field, covalency and geometry affect detailed states. The iron nucleus still has Z=26; chemical bonding does not justify changing the proton count.

Practice Q20 · MCQ

Question: Which ion has the same standard d count as free Cu⁺ but different charge and Z? A. Zn²⁺; B. Fe²⁺; C. Cr³⁺; D. Co²⁺

Answer with reasoning: A. Zn²⁺ and Cu⁺ each have 28 electrons and [Ar]3d¹⁰ in the ionic reference picture. Their Z and ionic charges differ.

48. Assertion–reason — separate evidence from inference

These are original Academy practice problems, not verified questions from official past papers. Try to answer each before consulting the reasoning.

Practice Q21 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: Changing neutral Fe to Fe²⁺ changes proton number from 26 to 24. Reason: Ionization removes orbital electrons, not protons.

Answer with reasoning: Assertion false, reason true. Z remains 26 for iron while the electron count decreases to 24. Proton changes would change element identity and involve nuclear processes.

Practice Q22 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: Fe³⁺ is commonly [Ar]3d⁵. Reason: It has N=26−3=23 electrons and is conventionally described with the 4s occupancy absent.

Answer with reasoning: Both are true, and the reason explains the electron-count consistency of the standard Fe³⁺ configuration. Ground-state assignment also requires electronic-structure evidence, not count alone.

Practice Q23 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: O²⁻ has ten electrons in the formal ionic count. Reason: Adding two electrons to neutral O increases N from 8 to 10.

Answer with reasoning: Both true, and the reason explains the count. Neither establishes that O²⁻ is stable as a free gas-phase dianion.

Practice Q24 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: Every isoelectronic pair has equal radius. Reason: Isoelectronic means equal electron count, not equal nuclear charge.

Answer with reasoning: Assertion false, reason true. Different Z causes different attractions and energetics; electron count equality does not force equal radii.

Practice Q25 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: All first cations of d-block elements are obtained by deleting one s electron without reorganizing the remaining occupancies. Reason: The neutral and cationic Hamiltonians have identical electron number.

Answer with reasoning: Both false. N changes by one during ionization, and evaluated V⁺, Co⁺ and Ni⁺ ground states illustrate reorganization.

Practice Q26 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: NIST spectroscopic Cu II means a singly charged free copper ion. Reason: Ionization stage I denotes neutral atoms.

Answer with reasoning: Both true, and the reason supplies the indexing rule: stages increase by one for each positive charge. Chemical Cu(II), however, denotes formal +2 oxidation state.

Practice Q27 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: Cu⁺=[Ar]3d¹⁰ is a useful free-ion reference state. Reason: The neutral Cu I configuration is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ and Cu⁺ has one fewer electron.

Answer with reasoning: Both true; the reason motivates the simplified 4s loss. The ground-state occupancy is also corroborated by evaluated atomic data.

Practice Q28 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: An ionic crystal can stabilize formal O²⁻ even though free O²⁻ is unstable. Reason: Electrostatic and bonding interactions with surrounding ions change the total energy.

Answer with reasoning: Both true, and the reason explains why gas-phase anion stability cannot be assumed from a crystal's electron counting.

Practice Q29 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: A formal Fe(III) oxidation state in a coordination compound is identical to a free Fe³⁺ gas-phase ion. Reason: Covalent metal–ligand bonds can delocalize electronic density.

Answer with reasoning: Assertion false, reason true. The formal oxidation state is a useful bookkeeping convention but the actual molecular electronic state includes ligands.

Practice Q30 · Assertion–reason

Question: Assertion: Pauli exclusion remains valid for Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺. Reason: Electronic charge state changes the fermionic character of electrons.

Answer with reasoning: Assertion true, reason false. Electrons remain fermions regardless of ionic charge; Pauli does not cease or change because one electron was removed.

49. Numerical practice — charge, energy and orbital capacity

These are original Academy practice problems, not verified questions from official past papers. Try to answer each before consulting the reasoning.

Practice Q31 · Numerical

Question: An ion has Z=17 and charge −1. Calculate N and standard noble-gas shorthand.

Answer with reasoning: N=Z−z=17−(−1)=18. The species is Cl⁻, with [Ar]. The minus sign raises, rather than lowers, electron count.

Practice Q32 · Numerical

Question: An Fe species has 26 protons and 23 electrons. Find its charge and configuration.

Answer with reasoning: z=Z−N=26−23=+3, so Fe³⁺. The standard free-ion configuration is [Ar]3d⁵, 18+5=23.

Practice Q33 · Numerical

Question: Cu has Z=29. Find the electron counts and d populations of Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺.

Answer with reasoning: Cu⁺: N=28 and [Ar]3d¹⁰. Cu²⁺:N=27 and [Ar]3d⁹. Their d populations differ by one, consistent with the standard free-ion descriptions.

Practice Q34 · Numerical

Question: Compare the charge of a 28-electron species of Zn and a 28-electron species of Cu.

Answer with reasoning: Zn has Z=30, z=30−28=+2, giving Zn²⁺. Cu Z=29, z=29−28=+1, giving Cu⁺. Same electron count but different nuclei and charges.

Practice Q35 · Numerical

Question: A molecule-free free Ni⁺ ion has Z=28 and 27 electrons. Explain why [Ar]3d⁹ is count-valid.

Answer with reasoning: 18+9=27. NIST first-cation ground is [Ar]3d⁹; a simple [Ar]3d⁸4s¹ alternative also totals 27 but does not identify the evaluated lowest-energy state.

Practice Q36 · Numerical

Question: For a +3 iron ion, what fraction of the original 26 electrons have been removed?

Answer with reasoning: Three electrons are removed relative to neutral Fe. The fraction is 3/26≈0.1154 or 11.54%. This arithmetic is a bookkeeping exercise, not a claim that charge density is uniformly reduced by 11.54% in a complex.

Practice Q37 · Numerical

Question: A hypothetical atom has E₀=−150 eV, E₊=−141 eV, E₂₊=−120 eV. Calculate IE₁ and IE₂.

Answer with reasoning: IE₁=−141−(−150)=9 eV; IE₂=−120−(−141)=21 eV. The illustrative values are not measured data, and both differences use comparable total-energy reference states.

Practice Q38 · Numerical

Question: Convert the hypothetical 9 eV first ionization energy above to joules per event.

Answer with reasoning: 9×1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J=1.4419589706×10⁻¹⁸ J per event. An energy in eV is not a charge number or a subshell occupancy.

Practice Q39 · Numerical

Question: A 3d subshell contains nine electrons. How many available spin-orbital vacancies remain?

Answer with reasoning: With l=2, there are 2(2l+1)=10 spin orbitals. Nine occupied leaves one vacancy. That count is appropriate for the simple Cu²⁺ d⁹ reference model.

Practice Q40 · Numerical

Question: How many electrons are formally assigned to P³⁻ and why does this not establish gas-phase stability?

Answer with reasoning: N=15−(−3)=18, conventionally [Ar]. The count is exact for formal ionic charge, while gas-phase stability requires favorable electron binding, which is not supplied by an octet mnemonic.

50. Data interpretation — NIST, solids and biological ions

These are original Academy practice problems, not verified questions from official past papers. Try to answer each before consulting the reasoning.

Practice Q41 · Data interpretation

Question: A table shows NIST V neutral d³4s² and V⁺ d⁴. Does this contradict N=Z−z?

Answer with reasoning: No. Neutral V has 18+3+2=23 electrons and V⁺ has 18+4=22. Electron count is conserved across charge change; orbital occupancy reorganizes in the ionic ground state.

Practice Q42 · Data interpretation

Question: NIST lists Co⁺ d⁸ while an answer key gives d⁷4s¹. What is wrong with the key?

Answer with reasoning: Both candidates total 26 electrons, but the evaluated free Co⁺ ground state is [Ar]3d⁸. The key assumed that deleting exactly one neutral 4s electron leaves all other occupancies untouched.

Practice Q43 · Data interpretation

Question: NIST lists Ni⁺ d⁹. Which neutral-atom fact makes this initially surprising?

Answer with reasoning: Neutral Ni is [Ar]3d⁸4s²; a simple 4s-only first deletion suggests d⁸4s¹. The observed ionic ground d⁹ instead reflects state reorganization, not a change of element.

Practice Q44 · Data interpretation

Question: A paper labels Fe III, and the sample is an isolated spectrum. Does it mean formal oxidation state +3?

Answer with reasoning: No. Atomic spectral Fe III means the +2 free-ion stage; Fe(III) with parentheses is the chemical oxidation-state convention. The domain determines the meaning.

Practice Q45 · Data interpretation

Question: A chart says Na⁺, Ne and F⁻ all have N=10. Can you conclude equal ionization energies?

Answer with reasoning: No. Their nuclei have Z=11,10,9 and the ions differ in net charge. Same electron count does not imply equal total energies or ionization thresholds.

Practice Q46 · Data interpretation

Question: An MgO unit-cell diagram labels O²⁻ and a gas-phase spectroscopy source says O²⁻ is unstable. Explain the apparent disagreement.

Answer with reasoning: The unit-cell diagram represents formal oxide charge in a solid, stabilized by lattice interactions; the isolated gas-phase dianion lacks that stabilization. Both statements can be correct in their contexts.

Practice Q47 · Data interpretation

Question: A heme measurement gives a complex EPR response inconsistent with a naive free Fe³⁺ d⁵ box. What is the scientific response?

Answer with reasoning: Specify oxidation state, ligand field, spin coupling and covalency, then use spectroscopy and molecular models. Free-ion occupancy is a reference, not a complete description of the protein center.

Practice Q48 · Data interpretation

Question: An electronic-structure calculation gives different 4s/3d orbital energies for Fe and Fe²⁺. Is that automatically a model error?

Answer with reasoning: No. Electron count and screening differ between the two states; self-consistent orbital relaxation changes energies. The calculation still requires independent validation but fixed orbital energies are not expected.

Practice Q49 · Data interpretation

Question: A student identifies Cr²⁺ as [Ar]d⁴ but reports 23 electrons. Locate the contradiction.

Answer with reasoning: The configuration has 18+4=22 electrons, agreeing with Cr Z=24 and charge +2 (N=22). The reported 23 would correspond to Cr⁺, not Cr²⁺.

Practice Q50 · Data interpretation

Question: Two different species have 23 electrons: Mn²⁺ and Fe³⁺. Why can their spectra be different?

Answer with reasoning: Their Z differs (25 and 26), as do their net charges. The one-electron potential and electron interactions differ, so identical electron counts do not require identical spectral energy levels.

51. Integrated practice — reason through new situations

These are original Academy practice problems, not verified questions from official past papers. Try to answer each before consulting the reasoning.

Practice Q51 · Integrative

Question: Write an error-checking algorithm for an ion electron configuration in five steps.

Answer with reasoning: Identify Z; read signed z; compute N=Z−z; construct an allowed candidate using relevant species rules and verified neutral/ionic data; sum all occupancies and compare with N, then qualify whether the ion is free, solvated or chemically bound.

Practice Q52 · Integrative

Question: Show why Fe²⁺ d⁶ and Fe³⁺ d⁵ should not be memorized without charge conservation.

Answer with reasoning: Fe²⁺ must have 24 electrons, and [Ar]d⁶ has 18+6=24. Fe³⁺ must have 23, and [Ar]d⁵ has 18+5=23. Charge count independently checks the ionic assignments, while 4s/d energetics explain the occupancy choice.

Practice Q53 · Integrative

Question: Explain to a beginner how 4s can fill before 3d yet Fe²⁺ lacks 4s electrons.

Answer with reasoning: The first phrase refers to a useful order for constructing many neutral-atom configurations; it is not a reversible physical assembly timeline. Ionization changes the total-energy competition and often favors emptied 4s occupancy, as in Fe²⁺.

Practice Q54 · Integrative

Question: Give a counterexample to a universal 'delete one 4s only' first-ion rule.

Answer with reasoning: NIST lists V⁺=[Ar]3d⁴ from neutral V=[Ar]3d³4s²; Co⁺ d⁸ and Ni⁺ d⁹ provide further examples. All keep correct electron totals but reorganize occupation in the cationic ground state.

Practice Q55 · Integrative

Question: Relate formal oxide O²⁻=[Ne] to the thermodynamics of MgO formation.

Answer with reasoning: The [Ne] shorthand correctly assigns ten electrons to the formal oxide site, but a free O²⁻ gas-phase ion is unstable. The solid is stabilized by electrostatic lattice and chemical interactions; one must compare full-system energies.

Practice Q56 · Integrative

Question: Explain why Cu⁺ and Zn²⁺ can have equal d¹⁰ counts but different chemistry.

Answer with reasoning: Both reference ions have 28 electrons, yet Cu has Z=29 and charge +1 while Zn has Z=30 and charge +2. Different nuclear charge, charge state and ligand chemistry mean different energies and reactions.

Practice Q57 · Integrative

Question: Explain the distinction between Fe III and Fe(III) using a NIST query and a protein article.

Answer with reasoning: Fe III is the spectrum of isolated Fe²⁺ (stage three); Fe(III) in a protein article denotes the formal +3 oxidation state of coordinated iron. Correctly identifying the convention prevents a one-electron mismatch.

Practice Q58 · Integrative

Question: Can a free-ion d count predict the spin of all metal complexes? Give a reasoned answer.

Answer with reasoning: No. Ligand-field splitting, covalency, electron pairing and coupling between metal and ligand spins change detailed electronic states. A d count is a starting occupation-number model, not an environment-independent magnetic measurement.

Practice Q59 · Integrative

Question: Which physical quantities belong in an ionization-energy calculation?

Answer with reasoning: Use total ground-state energies of two adjacent charge states referenced to a separated electron, e.g. IE₁=E(X⁺)−E(X). Subtracting fixed Madelung numbers or isolated orbital diagrams does not calculate a verified threshold.
