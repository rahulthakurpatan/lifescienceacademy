1. A puzzle inherited from ionic configurations

In the previous chapter we learned to count electrons in ions and to avoid reading a neutral-atom filling diagram backwards. Yet a deeper question now appears. Why is sodium able to give up an electron so readily while neon remains comparatively unreactive? Why may iron participate in different oxidation states although the largest principal quantum number in neutral Fe is only four? The answer begins with a useful division between electrons protected within an atom and electrons most relevant to its chemistry.

We shall build the distinction carefully rather than treat it as a magic label. We will use quantum numbers and orbital occupancy, examine what experiments reveal, then explain why the conventional main-group count cannot be transferred unchanged to d-block elements. Finally we shall test our definitions against biological and exam examples.

Guiding question: Which electrons are we counting, for which chemical purpose, and under which convention?

2. The vocabulary: nucleus, shell, subshell and electron

A neutral atom has a nucleus with Z protons and Z electrons in quantized states. A principal shell is labelled by n = 1, 2, 3 and so on. Within it lie subshells labelled s, p, d and f when permitted by the quantum numbers. An electron configuration such as carbon's 1s² 2s² 2p² says where the occupied one-electron orbitals are grouped in an approximate atomic description; it does not draw fixed paths around the nucleus.

Electrons cannot be distinguished as permanently numbered individuals. Calling a group of them “core” or “valence” classifies occupied states in a model, not tiny tagged beads. In chemistry, this model gives a practical first answer to why some electrons are normally retained while others participate more directly in bonding, ionization or excitation.

3. Why chemists began to separate inner and outer electrons

Long before quantum mechanics, chemists found recurring combining patterns. Mendeleev organized elements by repeating chemical behavior in 1869. Early twentieth-century electron-based ideas, including Lewis's 1916 picture of shared electron pairs and Kossel's ionic interpretation, connected those repetitions to the outer electrons. Modern atomic spectroscopy and quantum mechanics later clarified why repeated orbital occupations matter.

The chronological lesson is important. A convenient outer-electron rule was inferred from chemistry; it was not a fully general theorem derived by assuming every atom should obey an octet. Quantum calculations describe all electrons, while chemical classifications pick out those especially relevant to a particular process. Historical shell diagrams are helpful for learning, but their circles are not physical electron trajectories.

4. What a core electron means

In introductory atomic chemistry, core electrons are electrons in inner, generally filled, relatively strongly bound orbitals that are usually not the main participants in ordinary low-energy chemical bonding. They contribute strongly to screening the nuclear charge experienced by outer orbitals. Their presence determines much of the radial potential in which the remaining electrons move.

The word “usually” matters: sufficiently energetic radiation can ionize a core electron, and highly specialized chemistry may blur any simple partition. Thus core does not mean chemically irrelevant under every circumstance, nor does it mean an electron has zero probability outside a perfectly bounded sphere. Core and valence orbitals have spatially overlapping probability distributions.

5. What a valence electron means

The simplest practical definition is that a valence electron is an electron occupying an outer orbital that can matter for chemical bonding or reactivity. For main-group atoms in their standard ground configurations, valence electrons usually occupy the highest principal shell n, within its ns and np orbitals. For transition metals the chemically significant (n−1)d electrons may be included alongside ns electrons.

There is no unique numerical “valence electron count” that works unchanged in every field of chemistry. A Lewis-dot exercise, an oxidation-state assignment, a transition-metal electron-counting scheme and an atomic-shell question can apply distinct conventions. Scientific accuracy requires stating the convention rather than treating a familiar number as a permanent property.

6. Read configurations as accounts of occupancy

Take 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. The superscripts sum to eleven, so it describes neutral sodium in its ground state. Its ten inner electrons form the same occupancy pattern as neon and the remaining 3s electron belongs to n = 3. The notation [Ne] 3s¹ therefore saves writing all ten inner electrons but does not turn the [Ne] core into an actual neon atom within sodium.

For sodium, the chemically useful division is ten core electrons and one valence electron. We must distinguish this accounting identity from the separate energetic question of how much energy is needed to remove that 3s electron.

7. Noble-gas shorthand and its limits

A bracketed noble gas represents an electronic configuration, not a sealed physical container. [He] is 1s², [Ne] is 1s²2s²2p⁶ and [Ar] adds 3s²3p⁶. These cores appear repeatedly because the electron configurations of many elements can be described as a noble-gas pattern plus occupations beyond it.

For the first two rows of main-group chemistry this is often exactly the core-versus-valence split students need. But the rule “every electron written after the previous noble gas is valence” fails as a universal principle: gallium is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p¹, and the filled 3d¹⁰ is conventionally treated as part of the inner core in basic Ga valence counting.

8. The main-group rule follows the highest occupied n

For typical s- and p-block ground-state atoms, count electrons in the highest occupied shell: nsᵃ npᵇ. Their main-group valence count is a+b. Since ns can hold two and np six electrons, familiar closed n≥2 main-group valence shells contain eight. Helium is the special first-shell case: n=1 offers only a 1s subshell, so its closed shell contains two electrons.

Nvalence, main group = N(ns) + N(np)
For ordinary ground-state s- and p-block atoms; n is the largest occupied principal quantum number.

This is a counting convention. It does not state that each of these electrons will be transferred, shared or removed in a single chemical event. It also must not be imposed on d-block atoms without examining their (n−1)d states.

9. Carbon: derive the familiar four-valence-electron result

Carbon has Z = 6 and 1s² 2s² 2p². The n=1 shell is an occupied inner 1s subshell containing two electrons. The highest occupied principal shell is n=2: 2s² contributes two and 2p² contributes two more. Carbon therefore has four valence electrons and two core electrons in this basic atomic convention.

Notice that carbon's 2p electrons occupy two different p orbitals in the elementary Hund arrangement, while 2s is paired. The number four is an electron count; carbon's actual bonding arrangements require additional quantum and molecular descriptions. A free-atom orbital box is not a ready-made picture of the directions of four covalent bonds.

10. How a single shell can contain different subshells

The phrase “same valence shell” must not be taken to mean “same orbital energy.” In many-electron atoms, ns and np orbitals within the same n generally have different energies because penetration and electron-electron interactions differ. Yet both contribute to the standard main-group valence-electron inventory. For sulfur, the 3s² and 3p⁴ together yield six valence electrons even though they are not a single degenerate group of orbitals.

The maximum occupancy of n=3 as a mathematical shell is eighteen, since it can also contain 3d. That does not make sulfur's standard main-group valence count eighteen: its configuration has 3s²3p⁴ and no 3d electrons. Shell capacity, actual occupancy and chemical-valence classification are three different questions.

11. From lithium to neon: changing one outer-electron pattern

In the second period, Li and Be successively fill 2s, then B through Ne add electrons to 2p. The normal main-group valence counts progress from one to eight. The shared 1s² core remains an excellent first approximation for these neutral atoms, but the increasing nuclear charge affects their outer electrons. That changing attraction will be developed in the next Topic on periodic properties.

12. Why the periodic groups echo outer electron counts

Group 1 elements typically have an outer ns¹ pattern, group 2 elements ns², and groups 13–18 usually have ns²np¹ through ns²np⁶. It follows that Na and K each have one outer-shell electron even though sodium's outer shell is n=3 and potassium's is n=4. Likewise oxygen and sulfur each have six in the standard neutral main-group count.

This recurrence is part of the electronic explanation of periodicity. It is not a guarantee of identical chemical reactions: atom size, charge density, energetics, the physical state and the reacting partners also matter. Counting valence electrons is an entry point to predicting trends, not a replacement for understanding chemical systems.

13. Helium and the octet are not contradictory

Helium is 1s². Its highest occupied shell contains two electrons, and that shell is complete: the n=1 level offers one 1s spatial orbital for two spin states. Neon and argon have filled outer ns²np⁶ configurations and therefore eight conventional valence electrons. The teaching phrase “noble gases have eight” always needs the helium exception.

The octet rule is a useful pattern for many simple main-group compounds, but it is not a fundamental law of all bonding. Hydrogen commonly follows a duet-like electron count; electron-deficient species, odd-electron molecules and hypervalent bonding discussions demand other models. We shall teach bonding carefully in Topic 1.A.4 instead of pretending valence count by itself fixes a molecule's structure.

14. Why inner electrons usually resist ordinary bonding changes

An inner orbital is ordinarily more tightly bound because its electron density spends more time near the nuclear region and is subject to a different effective attraction than outer electrons. Removing a 1s electron from an atom generally requires much more energy than removing a loosely held outer electron. That energetic separation makes the core-valence division useful in ordinary chemistry.

It would be wrong to say every valence electron is farther from the nucleus at every instant. Wavefunctions spread over a range of radii; s orbitals can penetrate closer to the nucleus than p or d orbitals. Distinguish average radial distributions and binding energies from the simplistic picture of concentric, hard-edged rings.

15. Shielding and penetration: the physical reason the split is useful

An electron experiences the attraction of the positive nucleus and repulsion from other electrons. Inner electrons reduce the net nuclear attraction experienced by the outer population; this is called shielding. Penetration describes how much an orbital's probability density extends into regions close to the nucleus. These effects help explain why, within a principal shell of many-electron atoms, s and p subshells need not be energetically equivalent.

These ideas were developed in our earlier shielding lesson. Here we need just their consequence: shielding and orbital energies create a practical separation between relatively inert inner configurations and more chemically responsive states. “Core” is not simply defined by spatial distance, and there is no universal radius at which an atom switches from core to valence.

16. The boundary can be probed experimentally

Atomic photoelectron spectroscopy provides direct evidence that different electron groups possess markedly different removal energies. Incident photons eject electrons, and their measured kinetic energies reveal binding energies after appropriate energy and apparatus corrections. A spectrum can thus show outer-valence features and much deeper core-level features. The details are species- and environment-dependent; we are not claiming all orbitals yield one sharp peak in every spectrum.

Ebinding ≈ hν − Ekinetic − φ
Common solid-sample photoemission bookkeeping: hν photon energy; Ekin measured kinetic energy; φ a work-function/reference correction according to setup. For isolated atoms conventions differ.

Core-level spectroscopy is used to investigate elemental composition and chemical environments. A core signal may shift when the local electronic environment changes, even though core electrons are not the primary electrons exchanged in a typical bond. Therefore 'core-like' does not mean 'never affected by chemistry'.

17. A subtle distinction: a core electron can still be excited

If sufficiently energetic light is absorbed, a core electron can be promoted or ejected. X-ray absorption near-edge methods and core-level photoemission investigate such events. These techniques do not contradict the ordinary claim that core orbitals play less direct roles in typical chemical bonding; they simply operate on very different energy scales.

Likewise in heavy elements, relativistic effects and the small energy separations between some subshells require more careful electronic descriptions. For the Academy's present lesson the main-group ground-state distinction is a reliable starting model, not a declaration that all core-valence assignments are absolute physical partitions.

18. Valence electron count is not valency

A free carbon atom has four conventional valence electrons, but the word valency historically refers to combining capacity. It is not synonymous with the total number of valence electrons, especially when multiple bonding patterns are possible. Oxygen has six valence electrons but commonly forms two covalent bonds in a simple neutral water molecule. Chlorine has seven valence electrons yet may form one bond in hydrogen chloride.

A separate concept, oxidation state, assigns formal electron ownership under a defined rule for compounds and ions. Sodium's conventional valence count is one, and Na⁺ has oxidation state +1, but that agreement is not an identity of definitions. Iron offers an even clearer warning: neutral Fe has eight in one broad d+s orbital inventory, while +2 and +3 are familiar oxidation states.

19. Why a neutral atom's configuration does not settle its bonding

An atomic configuration belongs to an isolated atomic or ionic state under defined conditions. When atoms bond, their orbitals interact; electron density and energy levels change. The fact that oxygen has a free-atom 2p⁴ occupation does not authorize copying four isolated-atom orbital arrows as the molecular orbital diagram for O₂. Similarly, the eight d+s electrons in neutral iron cannot be interpreted as eight independent bonds in an iron compound.

This limitation protects against a common exam trap: distinguish electron count, unpaired electron count, number of bonds, coordination number and oxidation state. All are important, but they ask different scientific questions.

20. Why the transition-metal story needs a second convention

Beginning with scandium, neutral first-row d-block ground-state configurations can be written using the [Ar] core and both 3d and 4s occupations. The d electrons have principal number n=3, whereas the s electrons may occupy n=4. A strict “largest n only” rule would count only 4s electrons, overlooking d states whose occupancy, energy and interactions are essential to transition-metal chemistry.

Consequently a broader 3d + 4s electron inventory is useful for many neutral transition-metal examples. It is not a guarantee that all those electrons are removed, involved equally in bonding or available for the same oxidation states. In more advanced molecular-electron counting, the relevant orbital participation depends on ligands, geometry and the electronic state.

21. The two counting questions, written side by side

For a neutral atom such as iron with [Ar]3d⁶4s², the largest principal quantum number is n=4; counting electrons strictly in this outermost shell gives two. Counting electrons in the chemically relevant 3d and 4s subshells gives six plus two, or eight. Both numerical answers can be defensible only after the wording of the question states which inventory is being used.

Noutermost = electrons with maximum occupied n;  Nd+s = N((n−1)d) + N(ns)
Two different conventions for suitable neutral d-block species; neither is automatically a fixed bond count or oxidation state.

22. Scandium and titanium: d-electron participation begins

Scandium (Z=21) has [Ar]3d¹4s² in the isolated neutral atom: a strict outermost-shell inventory is two, whereas a broad d+s count is three. Titanium (Z=22) has [Ar]3d²4s² and similarly gives two versus four. These examples show why replacing all d-block chemistry with the main-group 'outermost principal shell' shortcut discards information.

In compounds, however, the formal charge on a metal center and the actual metal–ligand orbital interactions must be analyzed separately. The common +3 state of scandium is consistent with removal or redistribution of electrons relative to an isolated atom, but does not make the number three a universal permanent measure of its bond count.

23. Chromium and copper prevent mechanical counting

The measured neutral ground states of chromium and copper are [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹, respectively, not the naive 3d⁴4s² and 3d⁹4s² filling guesses. For Cr, the largest-n shell contains one electron while the 3d+4s inventory contains six. For Cu the analogous numbers are one and eleven.

Copper's eleven in the inventory is not an oxidation state of +11 and should not be called its ordinary covalent valency. Neutral configurations reflect subtle total-energy balances including electron interactions; using the phrase 'half-filled and fully filled stability' alone is not a complete physical derivation. This distinction was the central topic of Node 18.

24. Iron: why its d electrons matter

Iron's neutral configuration is [Ar]3d⁶4s². Its conventional chemistry often involves Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ with familiar free-ion occupancies [Ar]3d⁶ and [Ar]3d⁵. The number of d electrons changes with oxidation state, and those electrons help determine electronic spectra, spin states and chemical reactivity in real environments.

But neither an oxidation state nor the isolated ion's d⁵ or d⁶ notation describes an exact metal-bound wavefunction in a protein. Electron density may be shared with ligands, and ligand fields reorganize d-orbital energies. The important conclusion is that d electrons are chemically consequential even though they do not belong to the highest principal shell of neutral Fe.

25. Zinc: why even the broad d+s count needs care

Neutral zinc is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². By strict highest-n counting it has two 4s electrons. A mechanical inventory of every electron after [Ar] gives twelve (3d¹⁰+4s²), yet introductory Zn chemistry commonly regards the filled 3d¹⁰ shell as comparatively core-like and emphasizes the 4s² pair for the familiar +2 oxidation state.

This is not a trick. It demonstrates that which orbitals are chemically active can differ from which orbitals are written after a noble-gas core. Zinc does not satisfy the standard IUPAC transition-element criterion of possessing an incomplete d subshell in an atom or giving cations with an incomplete d subshell. Its placement in the d block and the vocabulary of “transition element” are different classification questions.

26. Gallium: an especially useful main-group counterexample

Gallium, Z=31, has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p¹. If we mechanically count every electron written after [Ar], the answer is thirteen. But Ga is a main-group element and its standard valence shell is n=4, with 4s²4p¹: three valence electrons. The inner 3d¹⁰ group belongs to the conventional core in this simple count.

This example is a decisive test of whether a student understands the method rather than repeating a rule. The core here includes the [Ar] eighteen plus ten 3d electrons, yielding 28. Core plus valence is 28+3=31, exactly the atomic number.

27. Free ions force us to state the species and its charge

The previous chapter established that N = Z − z, where z is the signed charge number. Na⁺ has ten electrons and a closed [Ne]-like configuration. Mg²⁺ also has ten, and Cl⁻ has eighteen with an [Ar]-like configuration. A neutral-atom valence-electron count should not be silently transferred unchanged to those ions.

For Fe²⁺, the standard free-ion configuration is [Ar]3d⁶: the neutral atom's 4s pair is absent. The highest occupied n then changes from 4 to 3. An exam asking 'number of d electrons in Fe²⁺' has the clear answer six, but an unspecific request for 'valence of Fe²⁺' needs a convention. The net charge +2 and the d-electron count six must not be conflated.

30. What makes valence 'chemical' rather than merely 'geometric'

Chemically useful valence orbitals tend to participate in the accessible low-energy electronic rearrangements of a species. These can involve electron transfer, orbital mixing and redistribution of charge density. That is why transition-metal d orbitals may play a strong role even though their principal quantum number is lower than the neutral atom's highest occupied n.

In contrast, a purely geometric rule might count only electrons located beyond some imagined radius. No such universal dividing surface exists. An orbital is a probability-amplitude state, not a rigid planet-like path, and even electrons within the same subshell contribute to a collective, antisymmetrized many-electron state.

31. The biological motivation: familiar ions and metal sites

Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ are important biological ions. Their familiar ionic forms can be understood through charge counting and main-group electron configurations; the actual physiological roles depend on hydration, membrane transport and coordination environments, which are beyond this node's focus. Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ are isoelectronic with neon in simple ionic bookkeeping; K⁺ and Ca²⁺ with argon.

Iron and copper provide a complementary lesson. Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ and Cu⁺/Cu²⁺ illustrate that transition-metal d-electron occupancy matters in biological redox chemistry. We do not deduce biological electron-transfer potentials, protein color or enzyme mechanism from a free-ion box diagram alone. Those require ligand and molecular context and will be returned to in Node 22.

32. An evidence-based workflow for a new element

First identify the species: neutral atom or ion, and if relevant its environment. Second write or verify the electron configuration against reliable data rather than assuming an approximate filling rule is exact. Third establish the question: largest-n electrons, introductory main-group valence count, broad d+s inventory, d-electron count, or formal oxidation state. Fourth perform the count explicitly and compare its sum with the atomic electron number. Fifth explain any convention-dependent ambiguity rather than hiding it.

Reliable exam phrasing: “The neutral Fe atom has two electrons in its highest occupied principal shell, and eight electrons across its 3d and 4s subshells.” This sentence is much safer than an unexplained “Fe has eight valence electrons” in every setting.

33. Avoid an attractive but false universal rule

It is tempting to assert that all electrons outside the last noble-gas symbol are valence electrons. This often works for early main-group examples and for some broad neutral transition-metal inventories, but gallium and zinc show the limitation. It is equally tempting to assert that valence always means electrons in the largest n; Fe and other d-block elements show why that can miss chemical d participation.

The deeper idea is a model boundary: core-valence partitions simplify a many-electron problem and should be chosen for the process being described. The most transferable skill is not memorizing a single valence number for every element; it is recognizing which definition the question requires and defending the result.

37. Worked examples 19–24: the d-block convention

Work each example by first choosing the question's counting convention and then verifying the electron inventory. The explanations are original guided calculations, not transcribed examination questions.

Worked example 19. Neutral iron

Reasoning and result: Z=26; [Ar]3d⁶4s². Largest-n count two, wider d+s count eight, core [Ar] eighteen. 18+8=26 is the inventory check.

Worked example 20. Neutral cobalt

Reasoning and result: Z=27; [Ar]3d⁷4s². Highest-n two; broad d+s nine; [Ar] eighteen. The 3d⁷ is chemically important although n=3.

Worked example 21. Neutral nickel

Reasoning and result: Z=28; [Ar]3d⁸4s². Highest-n two and broad d+s ten. Ten is a count of occupied selected subshells, not an oxidation state.

Worked example 22. Neutral copper

Reasoning and result: Z=29; [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Highest-n one; broad d+s eleven. Cu⁺ has [Ar]3d¹⁰ and does not retain neutral 4s¹.

Worked example 23. Neutral zinc

Reasoning and result: Z=30; [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². Broad after-[Ar] inventory twelve; basic chemical valence typically emphasizes 4s² with filled 3d¹⁰ behaving core-like.

Worked example 24. Neutral gallium

Reasoning and result: Z=31; [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p¹. Standard main-group valence is 3; conventional core count is 31−3=28. Counting 13 after [Ar] would be the wrong main-group answer.

39. Worked examples 31–34: checking misconceptions

Work each example by first choosing the question's counting convention and then verifying the electron inventory. The explanations are original guided calculations, not transcribed examination questions.

Worked example 31. Isoelectronic Na⁺ and Ne

Reasoning and result: Na⁺: 11−1=10 electrons; Ne has Z=10. Both possess ten electrons and the 1s²2s²2p⁶ occupancy in the simple atomic configuration, but different nuclear charges.

Worked example 32. Isoelectronic Ca²⁺ and Ar

Reasoning and result: Ca²⁺: 20−2=18 electrons; Ar has 18. Their electron totals agree, but ionic radius and chemistry are not identical.

Worked example 33. 'Ga has 13 valence electrons' diagnosis

Reasoning and result: Find the hidden error: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p¹ includes thirteen electrons after [Ar], but Ga's conventional highest-n valence set is 4s²4p¹, three.

Reasoning and result: An inventory 3d⁶+4s²=8 is not a number of bonds, unpaired electrons or oxidation state. The chemical environment must be specified before making such an inference.

40. Scientific and biological cases 1–5

These cases connect the orbital bookkeeping to observations and real scientific applications. They are interpretive teaching cases, not claims that a simple isolated-atom diagram can replace molecular measurement.

Case 1: Mendeleev's repetition

The pattern of related behavior within chemical families motivated periodic classification before modern orbitals were understood. Electron configurations now explain one powerful part of the repetition; actual reactions still require chemistry-specific conditions.

Case 2: Lewis's electron pairs

Lewis's model connected shared electron pairs with main-group combining patterns. The valence count gives a start for Lewis structures, but a free atom's orbital boxes are not a complete molecular bond diagram.

Case 3: An alkali metal and a noble gas

A neutral sodium atom has [Ne]3s¹ whereas neon itself has [He]2s²2p⁶. One outer electron versus a filled outer group motivates a striking contrast, without turning the noble-gas core notation into a literal embedded noble-gas atom.

Case 4: Core photoemission

Harder-to-remove inner electrons give higher binding-energy features than valence states in photoelectron experiments, making core-versus-valence an experimentally useful energy-scale distinction rather than an arbitrary textbook color.

Case 5: Carbon in molecular biology

Carbon's four atomic valence electrons support the elementary four-electron Lewis accounting used in organic structures. Actual sigma/pi bonding and molecular geometry require molecular orbital and bonding concepts not delivered by atomic counting alone.

41. Scientific and biological cases 6–10

These cases connect the orbital bookkeeping to observations and real scientific applications. They are interpretive teaching cases, not claims that a simple isolated-atom diagram can replace molecular measurement.

Case 6: Magnesium in enzymes

Mg²⁺ has ten electrons, matching neon's electron count. Its ability to stabilize charged biochemical groups involves ionic charge, hydration and ligand interactions; 'neon-like' electron count alone does not predict the details.

Case 7: Iron redox centers

Different formal oxidation states of iron often correspond to different d electron inventories. A protein's ligands modify the orbital energies and distribute charge; the free-ion Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ labels are useful starts, not the full spectrum.

Case 8: Copper redox proteins

Cu⁺ is conventionally d¹⁰ and Cu²⁺ d⁹ as free ions. The difference is relevant to redox reasoning, while coordination environment sets biological stability and potential; eleven neutral d+s electrons do not mean oxidation state +11.

Case 9: Gallium's filled 3d shell

Gallium is a main-group atom in period four. Treating every electron after [Ar] as valence would give 13, contradicting the accepted main-group three; including filled 3d¹⁰ with core resolves the problem.

Case 10: Zinc as a boundary case

Zn is a d-block element with a filled d¹⁰ configuration. Basic bonding discussions frequently treat its outer 4s² pair as valence; the choice shows why a single global valence rule is inadequate.

42. Twenty conceptual pitfalls: corrections 1–10

Each error is plausible because it mixes a useful simplified model with a different physical question. The correction makes the hidden assumption explicit.

Pitfall 1: 'Core electrons never affect bonding.'

Core states screen the nucleus and can respond to chemical environments; they simply are not usually the main electron-transfer states in ordinary bonding.

Pitfall 2: 'Core electrons are motionless.'

An orbital is a quantum state; electrons are not immobile particles frozen inside shell boundaries.

Pitfall 3: 'Valence means the farthest electron.'

A highest-n rule is convenient for main groups but insufficient for d-state participation; radial distributions also overlap.

Pitfall 4: 'The biggest shell is always the valence set.'

For neutral Fe, the highest n is 4; chemistry also involves n=3 d electrons.

Pitfall 5: 'All electrons after a noble gas are valence.'

Ga has a filled 3d¹⁰ group that conventionally belongs with core for its three-electron main-group count.

Pitfall 6: 'The main-group valence number equals the bond number.'

O has six valence electrons but commonly forms two bonds in water; bond count is a different molecular question.

Pitfall 7: 'Eight means an element must be unreactive.'

An octet is an electron pattern, not a universal kinetic or thermodynamic verdict about reactivity.

Pitfall 8: 'Helium has eight valence electrons.'

He has only 1s², two electrons; the first principal shell accommodates two.

Pitfall 9: 'Empty orbitals count as occupied valence electrons.'

Only actual electrons are counted; available orbitals contribute capacity, not occupancy.

Pitfall 10: 'The capacity 2n² is the observed valence count.'

It is a maximum number of shell quantum states, not the number of electrons present in a neutral atom.

43. Twenty conceptual pitfalls: corrections 11–20

Each error is plausible because it mixes a useful simplified model with a different physical question. The correction makes the hidden assumption explicit.

Pitfall 11: 'Zinc has twelve ordinary oxidation-state electrons.'

The 3d¹⁰+4s² inventory is twelve, but chemical valence often focuses on outer 4s² and Zn²⁺ is common.

Pitfall 12: 'Copper's eleven d+s electrons means Cu(+11).'

A bookkeeping count is not a formal oxidation-state assignment.

Pitfall 13: 'Neutral Cr is 3d⁴4s².'

Evaluated Cr ground state is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹; simple Aufbau guesses can fail.

Pitfall 14: 'Fe²⁺ has 4s².'

Standard free Fe²⁺ configuration is [Ar]3d⁶; the neutral 4s electrons are not retained.

Pitfall 15: 'A complex must have the free-ion orbital diagram.'

Ligand bonding, splitting and correlation alter the electronic states of complexes.

Pitfall 16: 'd-electron count is the spin count.'

d⁶ can have different unpaired counts depending on spin arrangements and environment.

Pitfall 17: 'Every transition metal always displays all d+s electrons in bonding.'

Which orbitals actually participate depends on element, oxidation state and chemical setting.

Pitfall 18: 'Oxidation state equals measured local charge.'

Oxidation states are formal assignments; real electron density is distributed.

Pitfall 19: 'Isoelectronic ions behave exactly alike.'

The same electron number with different nuclear charges can still yield different radii, energies and reactivity.

Pitfall 20: 'One can infer biological redox potentials from free-atom counts.'

Solvation and ligand/protein environment matter profoundly; orbital inventories are prerequisites, not predictions of potentials.

47. Assertion–reason reasoning 1–5

For each pair decide whether the assertion (A) and reason (R) are true and whether R correctly explains A.

Assertion–reason 1

Assertion: Na's neutral valence count is one. Reason: Na's ground-state outermost configuration is 3s¹.

Key: A is true; R is true; R does explain A. Both are true and the 3s¹ occupation directly explains the count.

Assertion–reason 2

Assertion: Gallium has thirteen standard main-group valence electrons. Reason: Gallium has 3d¹⁰ electrons after [Ar].

Key: A is false; R is true. The reason is true but filled 3d¹⁰ is normally placed with the core in the basic main-group convention.

Assertion–reason 3

Assertion: Neutral Fe's highest-n count is two. Reason: Neutral Fe is [Ar]3d⁶4s².

Key: A is true; R is true; R does explain A. The 4s pair has the maximum occupied n=4; the 3d six have n=3.

Assertion–reason 4

Assertion: Neutral Cu has eleven common oxidation-state electrons. Reason: Cu has [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹.

Key: A is false; R is true. The broad inventory is eleven; it is not an oxidation state.

Assertion–reason 5

Assertion: Helium has two valence electrons. Reason: The first shell contains just the 1s orbital.

Key: A is true; R is true; R does explain A. Its 1s² is a filled first shell with two spin states.

48. Assertion–reason reasoning 6–10

For each pair decide whether the assertion (A) and reason (R) are true and whether R correctly explains A.

Assertion–reason 6

Assertion: d electrons can matter in transition-metal bonding. Reason: All transition-metal d electrons always lie in the highest n shell.

Key: A is true; R is false. The assertion is broadly true; the reason is false (e.g. Fe 3d and 4s).

Assertion–reason 7

Assertion: Mg²⁺ and Ne can be called isoelectronic. Reason: Both contain ten electrons.

Key: A is true; R is true; R does explain A. Mg²⁺ has 12−2=10 and Ne has ten electrons.

Assertion–reason 8

Assertion: A core electron is impossible to eject. Reason: Binding energies depend on the occupied orbital.

Key: A is false; R is true. Core electrons can be photoejected with sufficient photon energy.

Assertion–reason 9

Assertion: Every Fe complex must exhibit the free Fe²⁺ ion orbital-box energy ordering. Reason: Ligands can split d-orbital energies.

Key: A is false; R is true. Ligand fields can change the energetic ordering and spin arrangements.

Assertion–reason 10

Assertion: Group-17 main-group atoms generally have seven ns/np valence electrons. Reason: Their common outer occupancy is ns²np⁵.

Key: A is true; R is true; R does explain A. The usual group pattern gives 2+5=7, with chemistry-specific exceptions to simple bonding inferences.

51. Five integrative challenges with guided solutions

Challenge 1 — Three different meanings of eight

Compare neon, Fe and oxygen: what does eight refer to in each relevant configuration?

Solution: Neon has eight in 2s²2p⁶; neutral Fe has eight in 3d⁶+4s² under the broad inventory but only two at highest n; oxygen has eight total electrons but six in n=2. The word “eight” requires its object.

Challenge 2 — Identifying a hidden d shell

A period-four main-group atom has Z=31. An answer sheet writes “13 valence electrons” because it sees [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p¹. Find the error and correct it.

Solution: In the conventional main-group shell count, highest n=4 holds 4s²4p¹ = 3. The 3d¹⁰ remains part of the 28-electron core. The total 18+10+3=31 is correct, but the partition was wrong.

Challenge 3 — Is oxidation state equal to orbital count?

A student argues that neutral Fe can attain +8 because [Ar]3d⁶4s² gives eight d+s electrons. Is the conclusion justified?

Solution: No. Orbital inventory alone says nothing about which very high oxidation states are chemically accessible or stable. Energetics, bonds and environment determine redox chemistry. Fe(II) and Fe(III) are common examples, but do not infer oxidation states by counting every orbital electron as removable.

Challenge 4 — Compare K⁺ and Fe²⁺

Give their electron counts, free-ion configurations and the question that needs extra context.

Solution: K⁺: 19−1=18, [Ar]. Fe²⁺: 26−2=24, [Ar]3d⁶. These are clear atomic inventories. Predicting a particular Fe²⁺ protein's detailed electron distribution, spin state or redox potential requires ligand/protein information.

Challenge 5 — Critique a claimed experiment

A photoelectron spectrum shows a high-binding-energy inner peak and a low-binding-energy outer feature. A report says “core electrons therefore have no influence on chemistry.” Identify the unsupported step.

Solution: Removal-energy separation supports a useful core/valence distinction, not total independence. Core charge contributes shielding; core binding energies can be chemically shifted; sufficiently energetic light ejects core electrons. The conclusion goes beyond the measured evidence.

52. Revision: an accurate one-minute decision tree

Start from the configuration of the precisely identified species. If it is a neutral main-group atom, find the greatest occupied n and count ns plus np electrons there; any remaining occupied inner states are the conventional core. If the species belongs to the d block, ask whether the problem demands outermost-n occupancy, 3d+4s (or more generally (n−1)d+ns) inventory, or a chemically active-electron assessment. For ions, apply electron-number conservation before discussing occupation.
