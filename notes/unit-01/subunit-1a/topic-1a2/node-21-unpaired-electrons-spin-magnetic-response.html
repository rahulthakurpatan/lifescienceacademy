Unpaired Electrons, Electron Spin and Magnetic Response

Discover why paired and unpaired electrons respond differently to magnetic fields, how spin-state models are tested, and where atomic counting must yield to molecular and biological evidence.

1. A magnetic question that electron counting alone cannot answer

In our previous chapter we distinguished the core of an atom from the electrons most involved in its chemistry. That inventory gives us a new question. If nitrogen and neon both contain electrons, why is an isolated ground-state nitrogen atom magnetically different from an isolated ground-state neon atom? The answer does not begin with atomic mass, total electron count, or whether an element is called a metal. It begins with how the occupied quantum states are paired.

Imagine bringing two samples into a nonuniform magnetic field. One experiences a tendency toward stronger field; another responds weakly in the opposite direction. We need to explain this contrast, distinguish the electron-scale model from the real measured specimen, and learn which predictions are genuinely justified by a configuration. The journey starts with the spin quantum number already introduced in Node 08 and the orbital boxes built in Nodes 14 and 15.

2. What experiments actually observe

Magnetic response is a property observed when matter is placed in an applied magnetic field. A specimen can become magnetized, and in a field gradient it may experience a measurable force. Such experiments were important because they showed that seemingly similar substances can behave very differently. Paramagnetic substances, in the ordinary introductory sense, acquire magnetization along the applied field; diamagnetic substances have a weak induced response opposing it. These labels describe responses under specified conditions, not a promise that every sample visibly jumps toward or away from a refrigerator magnet.

The electrical charges of moving electrons and their intrinsic spin provide microscopic sources of magnetic moments. We will first identify the simplest spin contributions, then see why real chemistry may require orbital motion, bonding and thermal effects as well. This order prevents a frequently repeated mistake: treating the number of unpaired atomic electrons as a universal formula for the behaviour of every molecule or solid.

3. The electron has intrinsic spin, not a literal spinning surface

An electron is a quantum particle with intrinsic angular momentum called spin. Its allowed projection on a chosen axis is commonly labelled ms = +½ or −½. We draw an upward or downward arrow to record one of these spin projections. Such arrows are bookkeeping representations; they are not miniature bar magnets physically rotating in a little orbital box, and they do not specify a permanently fixed arrow in ordinary three-dimensional space.

Its spin angular momentum magnitude has quantum number s = ½; the magnitude is √[s(s+1)]ℏ = (√3/2)ℏ. The electron also has a magnetic dipole moment related to spin. The direction of its spin magnetic moment is opposite to the spin angular momentum because its charge is negative. Students need this distinction when moving beyond simplified arrow diagrams, but counting paired and unpaired electrons does not require us to solve the full vector problem.

4. Why a pair of opposite spin projections cancels in a simple orbital picture

For two electrons occupying the same spatial orbital, the Pauli exclusion principle requires opposite spin projections. In an ordinary closed-shell configuration, the contributions of these two spin states combine to give zero total spin for that pair. A box written [↑↓] therefore has no net spin moment in this basic picture, while [↑] contains an unpaired spin contribution.

We must be precise about what cancels. A paired-electron atom may still show induced diamagnetism when a field perturbs its electron motion; saying 'zero permanent spin moment' does not mean that the electrons cease interacting with magnetic fields. Likewise, many-electron states can have subtler spin and orbital coupling than individual arrow boxes show.

5. Pauli and Hund: two different rules, one reliable starting procedure

Pauli's rule limits the occupancy of one spatial orbital to two electrons with different spin states. Hund's first rule tells us how to arrange electrons across a set of equal-energy orbitals in the familiar ground-state filling approximation: occupy each available orbital singly with parallel spin arrows before beginning to pair. These rules answer different questions. The first concerns allowed occupancy; the second concerns which of several allowed arrangements has lower energy under the stated model.

For p3, the three equivalent p orbital boxes are [↑] [↑] [↑]: three unpaired electrons. For p4, the schematic becomes [↑↓] [↑] [↑]: two unpaired electrons. One may choose all arrows reversed without changing the number of unpaired electrons. The absolute arrow orientation does not change the count.

6. How to count unpaired electrons without guessing from a symbol

First identify the actual species: neutral atom, positive ion or negative ion, and if relevant a molecule or coordination complex. Second obtain the ground-state electron configuration appropriate to that species; do not simply copy the neutral parent. Third separate any completely filled subshells, since their conventional paired electrons contribute no net spin. Finally distribute electrons over the degenerate orbitals of each partly filled subshell according to the relevant occupation model and count the singly occupied orbital boxes.

Counting protocol

Species → configuration → partially occupied subshell → orbital boxes → singly occupied boxes → qualified magnetic prediction. Never replace this logic with 'odd atomic number means paramagnetic': even-electron species can have several unpaired electrons.

7. The p-orbital pattern from p⁰ through p⁶

The p subshell contains three spatial orbitals and holds at most six electrons. With equal orbital energies and conventional Hund filling, the ground-state unpaired counts are symmetric around half filling: p0 gives 0, p1 gives 1, p2 gives 2, p3 gives 3, p4 gives 2, p5 gives 1, and p6 gives 0. The change from p3 to p4 is especially instructive: adding an electron decreases the number of unpaired electrons because it starts a pair.

8. An original orbital diagram: nitrogen, oxygen and neon

The neutral nitrogen ground state is 1s² 2s² 2p³; its three 2p electrons occupy different p orbitals, leaving three unpaired. Oxygen is 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ and has two unpaired. Neon is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ and has none. Both N and O have fewer electrons than Ne, but it is the distribution within open shells, not the total electron count alone, that gives the qualitative spin distinction.

9. Why the d subshell produces a wider range of spin counts

A d subshell has five spatial orbitals. In a degenerate five-orbital high-spin filling model, d0 through d5 have 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 unpaired electrons respectively; after half filling the counts fall 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 for d6 through d10. This is a powerful memory pattern because it follows from the orbital count rather than a separate ten-item rule.

Important condition: This sequence is not an unconditional prediction for d ions inside complexes. When surrounding ligands split the d orbitals enough, low-spin occupation can appear. The same d6 electron count may therefore be associated with four or zero unpaired electrons in two different octahedral environments.

10. A second original diagram: five d orbitals and the first pair

For an idealized high-spin d5 state, the five boxes each contain one arrow. In d6, the sixth electron must occupy an orbital already holding one electron in the common five-degenerate-orbital arrangement. The original five singles therefore become one pair plus four singles. The diagram below is a counting model; ligand-field splitting, multi-electron terms and spin–orbit interactions can modify a real system.

11. A neutral atom must be identified before counting spins

Consider a neutral ground-state iron atom, [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². The 4s electrons are paired and the d⁶ subshell gives four unpaired electrons in the conventional orbital picture. Manganese is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² and has five. Copper has [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹; its completely filled 3d shell contributes no net spin in this model, but the singly occupied 4s orbital gives one unpaired electron. Zinc is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² and has none.

These counts characterize isolated neutral-atom ground configurations under our elementary spin model. The bulk metals iron, manganese, copper and zinc are not simply enormous collections of independent neutral atomic boxes. Delocalized electrons, interactions between atoms and magnetic ordering determine their materials behaviour.

12. Ions change electron inventories before they change the question

Remove electrons to form a cation before counting unpaired states. For first-row transition metals in the elementary configuration scheme, neutral-atom 4s occupancy is generally removed before 3d when the positive ion is formed; after removal, write the resulting ionic configuration and distribute its d electrons appropriately. Thus Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, while Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵. The first gives four and the second gives five unpaired electrons in the free-ion high-spin picture. Mn²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵, also five. Cu²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁹, one, and Zn²⁺ is [Ar] 3d¹⁰, zero.

A common error is to claim Fe³⁺ has three 4s electrons or to remove a 3d electron from Fe before the 4s pair. Another error is to say that five unpaired electrons automatically remain on Fe³⁺ in every complex. We must keep the free-ion/weak-field counting premise visible whenever we use these numbers.

13. Magnetic properties in the simplest atom-and-ion classification

A species with a nonzero net ground-state magnetic moment ordinarily exhibits paramagnetic response at sufficiently low fields and without strong collective ordering; thermal populations and angular momentum coupling matter for detailed predictions. In the familiar introductory single-configuration test, one or more unpaired electrons indicate a likely paramagnetic atom or ion. A closed-shell species with no permanent electronic moment is instead usually classified as diamagnetic.

He, Ne, Mg²⁺ and Zn²⁺ are familiar simple closed-shell examples. Ground-state N, O, Fe³⁺ and Cu²⁺ are representative open-shell examples in the indicated free-species context. Notice that 'diamagnetic' is not synonymous with 'no magnetic response': diamagnetism itself is the field-induced response opposite the field.

15. Magnetization and susceptibility: what the symbols mean

Macroscopic magnetization M means magnetic dipole moment per unit volume, with SI unit ampere per metre (A m−1). Magnetic field strength H also has SI unit A m−1. In a sufficiently weak, linear, isotropic regime we write M = χH, with dimensionless volume susceptibility χ in SI. Positive χ corresponds to magnetization along H; negative χ corresponds to opposing magnetization.

Do not replace H by flux density B without noting the different units and constitutive relation: in SI, B = μ₀(H + M) for ordinary macroscopic media, with B measured in tesla. A real specimen's observed force also depends on the field gradient, sample shape and experimental geometry. Merely reading the sign of χ is not enough to calculate a force without further information.

16. From spin angular momentum to a magnetic moment

For spin angular momentum S, the elementary spin-moment operator has a magnitude scale gs μB S/ℏ, with gs approximately 2 for an electron. The Bohr magneton is μB = eℏ/(2me) ≈ 9.274 × 10−24 J T−1 (equivalently A m²). Here e is the positive magnitude of electron charge; me is electron mass; ℏ = h/(2π). The electron's magnetic moment vector points opposite its spin angular momentum because its charge is negative.

For several unpaired spins aligned by an applicable Hund high-spin ground-state model, their total spin quantum number is S = u/2, where u is their number. Quantum angular momentum has magnitude √[S(S+1)]ℏ, not simply Sℏ. That square root explains the nontrivial expression used in many introductory magnetic-moment calculations.

17. Deriving the spin-only magnetic-moment rule

Neglect orbital magnetic-moment contributions and use the electron spin g-factor approximation g ≈ 2. The effective spin-only moment is μso = g√[S(S+1)] μB ≈ 2√[S(S+1)] μB. Substitute S = u/2 for u parallel unpaired spins to obtain 2√[(u/2)(u/2+1)] μB, which simplifies algebraically to:

μso ≈ √[u(u + 2)] μB
u = number of unpaired electrons; μB is the Bohr magneton.

This is an effective spin-only moment, commonly used as an approximation for suitable first-row transition-metal complexes and simple textbook problems. It is not the measured moment of every atom, free ion, molecule or bulk metal. In particular, orbital angular momentum, spin–orbit coupling, low-spin configurations, magnetic exchange and temperature-dependent populations can change the observed value.

19. Worked calculation: Fe³⁺ free-ion count versus a moment estimate

Start from neutral Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶4s². Form Fe³⁺ by removing the two 4s electrons and one 3d electron, leaving [Ar]3d⁵. Five equal-energy d boxes singly occupied yield u = 5 and S = 5/2 in the elementary high-spin model. The spin-only expression gives μso = √[5(5+2)] μB = √35 μB ≈ 5.92 μB. Convert to SI if required: (5.916)(9.274 × 10−24 J T−1) ≈ 5.49 × 10−23 J T−1.

For a high-spin free 3d⁵ ion such as Fe³⁺, the ground term in the Russell–Saunders description is ⁶S (L = 0). Its first-order orbital moment therefore vanishes, making the 5.92 μB spin-only value a particularly useful reference. It should not automatically be reported as the measured moment of an arbitrary Fe³⁺ compound or protein: its actual environment, coupling, temperature and sample interactions still matter. A measured susceptibility also requires material-specific information.

20. Worked calculation: Fe²⁺ and a controlled comparison

Fe²⁺ has [Ar]3d⁶. In the high-spin five-degenerate-box treatment, its sixth d electron pairs with one of five existing singles; u = 4. Therefore S = 2 and μso ≈ √[4(4+2)] μB = √24 μB ≈ 4.90 μB. If an octahedral ligand environment instead stabilizes a low-spin d⁶ state with six electrons paired in the lower t2g set, u = 0 and the spin-only estimate is zero.

This comparison shows why a correct electron count is necessary but not sufficient to predict the magnetic response of a coordination compound. The chemical environment establishes the available orbital energies and possible spin states. The zero estimate also does not eliminate induced diamagnetism.

21. Why a ligand environment can change spin without changing the ion's d count

Six ligands around a transition-metal ion in an approximately octahedral arrangement split the five d orbitals into a lower-energy set of three (t2g) and a higher-energy set of two (eg). The energy cost of occupying the upper set competes with the energy cost of pairing electrons. When the octahedral splitting Δoct is relatively small, a high-spin filling is often favored for appropriate d counts; when Δoct is sufficiently large, a low-spin alternative can occur. For common d⁴ through d⁷ octahedral cases, this competition can alter the number of unpaired electrons.

Do not assume all octahedral complexes are low spin, or that weak-field/high-spin means electrons literally repel each other more strongly than in other species. The outcome depends on electronic structure and ligand field. Full orbital-symmetry derivations and bonding mechanisms belong in later chemistry topics; this node uses only the distinction necessary to make responsible magnetic predictions.

The next figure shows two possible octahedral occupations of d⁶. The high-spin case is t2g⁴eg² and has four unpaired electrons; the low-spin case is t2g⁶eg⁰ and has none. 23. A classic controlled comparison: two octahedral Fe²⁺ complexes

OpenStax Chemistry 2e contrasts the high-spin d⁶ ion in [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ with the low-spin d⁶ electronic state in [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻. Both contain iron in the +2 oxidation state and six d electrons, yet their experimentally supported magnetic behaviour differs because the ligand environment changes the energy splitting and the favored spin occupation. In the simplified ligand-field model the former has four unpaired electrons and the latter has none.

The chemical lesson is stronger than memorizing 'water weak, cyanide strong': same metal, same oxidation state and same d count do not guarantee the same spin state. The comparison concerns the electronic ground-state assignments of these specified complexes, not a promise that every complex containing those ligands behaves identically under all conditions.

24. Why a magnetic field can measure electronic structure indirectly

If an experiment establishes the magnitude and temperature variation of magnetic susceptibility, scientists can use theoretical models to test electronic spin-state hypotheses. The instrument does not literally count arrows inside orbital boxes. It measures bulk magnetic response; a researcher then accounts for the sample amount, field, diamagnetic contributions, temperature, impurities and possibly magnetic interactions before interpreting the signal.

In a laboratory, magnetic balances, Faraday- or Gouy-type force measurements and sensitive instruments such as SQUID magnetometers can provide magnetic data. Each has calibration requirements and its own limits. A classroom 'number of unpaired electrons' inference is therefore one part of a larger evidence chain, not a substitute for characterization of a real molecular sample.

25. Temperature matters: Curie's law and its limits

For sufficiently dilute, nearly independent magnetic moments in a suitable high-temperature, low-field regime, ideal paramagnetic susceptibility often follows Curie's law, χ = C/T, where T is thermodynamic temperature in kelvin and C is a material-specific constant when the chosen susceptibility convention is fixed. Doubling T from 300 K to 600 K then halves χ in this idealized model. This is a statement about thermal alignment: increasing thermal agitation makes field-induced alignment of moments less effective.

The law is not universal. Strong interactions may produce Curie–Weiss behaviour χ = C/(T − θ) over a limited regime, and ordering transitions, zero-field splitting or other physics can produce deviations. Many closed-shell diamagnetic materials show only weak temperature dependence. A spin-only estimate of μ does not itself specify χ without a model, number density and temperature.

26. Magnetic moment is not magnetic susceptibility

A Bohr magneton measures a magnetic-dipole-moment scale; susceptibility is a ratio characterizing how magnetization responds to a field. Confusing the two quantities leads to wrong units and false experimental conclusions. The spin-only formula μso = √[u(u+2)] μB provides a moment estimate for a specified electronic state. The law M = χH defines the linear response of a material. Neither equation alone supplies a specimen's attraction force.

For example, two substances containing species with the same unpaired-electron count can have different susceptibility because their particle concentration, temperatures, spin interactions and orbital contributions differ. The usual magnet-test question is a qualitative starting point; the measured material property needs a larger physical description.

27. Orbital angular momentum and spin–orbit coupling: the first major caveat

So far we have treated the spin as the main source of a paramagnetic moment. Electrons also carry orbital angular momentum, and in free atoms their spin and orbital angular momenta couple into total J. A real free-ion magnetic moment may therefore be better described by an appropriate angular-momentum term and a Landé factor than by the simple spin-only expression. In many first-row transition-metal complexes, ligand fields partly quench orbital contributions, making spin-only approximations useful; incomplete quenching and spin–orbit coupling can still create measurable deviations.

At higher levels, J, S and L are quantum numbers of total angular momentum rather than separate single-electron orbit labels. We do not derive term symbols here because the immediate exam skill is to distinguish counting a model's unpaired electrons from predicting a measured magnetic moment.

28. Closed shells are diamagnetic, not magnetically inert

When all occupied orbitals of a simple species form closed shells, the ground state generally lacks a permanent net electronic magnetic moment. Yet an external field changes electron motion and creates a small induced moment opposing that field. This is the physical origin of ordinary diamagnetism. It is present in matter more broadly than the simplistic paired/unpaired chart suggests; it is simply often overshadowed by stronger paramagnetic or ordered magnetic contributions.

Be careful with statements like 'paired electrons have no magnetic properties' or 'diamagnetic substances contain no electrons with spin.' Each electron has spin; paired states have canceling net spin in the elementary picture. Classification relies on the total state and material response rather than a count of electron arrows as the entire story.

29. O₂ is the molecule that exposes the limit of atomic orbital boxes

One oxygen atom is 1s²2s²2p⁴ and has two unpaired electrons in the simplified ground-state atomic picture. Molecular oxygen, O₂, contains two oxygen nuclei and sixteen electrons distributed among molecular orbitals extending across both atoms. It is not scientifically valid to take two isolated-atom spin counts, add them to get four, and declare that O₂ has four unpaired electrons.

Experiments show that ground-state O₂ is paramagnetic. Molecular-orbital theory accounts for two unpaired electrons in two degenerate π* antibonding orbitals. This provides a powerful model test because the simplest closed-shell Lewis structure alone does not predict that spin multiplicity. We use O₂ as an application and a limitation warning, not as an excuse to teach a complete molecular-orbital course before the Academy reaches bonding.

30. N₂, O₂ and the difference between atoms and molecules

An isolated ground-state nitrogen atom has three unpaired 2p electrons. However, ground-state N₂ is a closed-shell singlet molecule, typically classified as diamagnetic. Its magnetism cannot be obtained by adding 'three plus three' atomic unpaired counts. Likewise, ground-state O₂ is a triplet molecule with two unpaired electrons, not four. The assembly of atoms into a molecule changes the appropriate quantum states.

These two familiar gases form a valuable comparison: one should first decide whether the question concerns an atom, an ion, a molecule, or a solid. The same elemental symbol appearing in different species does not imply the same electron arrangement or magnetic state.

31. Why the full spin state sometimes matters more than an integer count

A system's total spin S is a quantum number of the combined state. In a simple high-spin open-shell orbital diagram, u parallel spins produce S = u/2. But in molecules or strongly interacting systems, spins can couple either to a larger resultant spin or partly cancel. Two spatially separated unpaired spins may form overall singlet-like or triplet-like states depending on interactions and coupling. We cannot automatically assign S from a visual tally of localized electrons unless the model specifies how they couple.

This is a key boundary when interpreting oxygen-binding proteins, copper centres, radical pairs and molecular magnetic materials. The count of singly occupied localized orbitals is an educational picture; a spectroscopic magnetic state depends on the total coupled wavefunction.

32. Biological case: deoxyhemoglobin versus oxyhemoglobin

Hemoglobin supplies a genuine biological example rather than a decorative one. In deoxygenated hemoglobin, the heme environment supports a paramagnetic high-spin iron-centred state, whereas oxygenated hemoglobin is diamagnetic overall. Magnetic differences between blood oxygenation states are relevant to understanding susceptibility-based MRI contrasts. A naive statement that 'iron has four unpaired electrons, therefore all hemoglobin is paramagnetic' would fail because oxygen binding changes the combined electronic structure.

The oxygen-bound heme system is not reliably represented by assigning every electron to a bare Fe²⁺ ion and treating O₂ as an independent gas molecule. Covalency, charge distribution and coupled spin states matter; models involving iron and bound dioxygen with antiferromagnetic coupling are used in research. This node uses the well-established magnetic contrast, while leaving the detailed biological mechanism and spectroscopy for later topics.

33. Biological case: Fe³⁺, Mn²⁺ and Cu²⁺ are not interchangeable labels

Under the free-ion high-spin picture Fe³⁺ is 3d⁵ (five unpaired), Mn²⁺ is also 3d⁵ (five), and Cu²⁺ is 3d⁹ (one). Yet these ions differ in oxidation state, ligand preference, chemistry and spectroscopy. In enzymes and metalloproteins, local coordination can alter electronic states and reactivity. Predicting the exact magnetic moment of an active site from elemental identity alone is not justified.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) can help probe paramagnetic metal centres, and magnetic susceptibility can constrain spin-state assignments. The presence of a paramagnetic centre should not be equated automatically with an enzymatic function; functional interpretations demand independent structural and biochemical evidence.

34. Biological case: why Zn²⁺ is a helpful contrast

Many zinc-binding proteins contain Zn²⁺. A simple Zn²⁺ ion has [Ar]3d¹⁰, with all d orbitals paired. It is ordinarily diamagnetic and lacks an unpaired-electron EPR signal under the simple ground-state electronic picture. Zinc-containing proteins can nevertheless perform important functions because coordination chemistry and electrostatic stabilization do not require unpaired electrons.

A Zn²⁺ cofactor in an enzyme is not a proof that every other part of that protein is diamagnetic; organic radicals or other metal centres may exist. This teaches an essential distinction: the magnetic behaviour of one metal centre is not automatically the complete magnetic behaviour of an entire biological macromolecule.

35. EPR and magnetic evidence: what an experiment can and cannot prove

Electron paramagnetic resonance is sensitive to transitions involving unpaired electronic spins in appropriate environments and under suitable conditions. A signal can support the presence of a paramagnetic centre and yield information about its interactions with nearby nuclei. Not observing an EPR signal does not prove the entire sample lacks unpaired electrons: relaxation, coupling, broad lines, instrument frequency and other selection or detection limitations can hide signals.

Similarly, magnetic susceptibility measurement indicates a bulk response. Interpretation can be complicated by sample impurities, temperature, multiple spin states and interactions. A good scientist uses two or more kinds of evidence where feasible rather than assigning a complete electronic structure from one observation alone.

36. A compact problem-solving decision tree

When a question asks 'paramagnetic or diamagnetic?', first underline the species name and charge. For a neutral isolated atom, write the correct ground-state configuration and orbital diagram. For a free ion, remove/add electrons before filling the valence orbitals. For a metal complex, check ligand geometry and whether a high-spin or low-spin state is supplied or inferable from reliable chemical context. For a molecule such as O₂, use molecular-orbital or experimentally supported total-state information rather than simply adding the free atoms' spins. For bulk material, do not infer ferromagnetism or measured susceptibility without material-level physics.

Safe exam language

“In the specified isolated-ion/high-spin/orbital-box model, the species has u unpaired electrons and is expected to exhibit a paramagnetic spin contribution.” This is more scientifically accurate than “an element is always magnetic because its atom has u unpaired electrons.”

37. Twelve short scientific cases, each with a model boundary

  1. Atomic N: 2p³ supplies three unpaired electrons; this is an isolated-atom result, not the spin of N₂ gas.
  2. Atomic O: 2p⁴ yields two unpaired; the O₂ molecule needs molecular orbitals.
  3. Atomic Ne: 2p⁶ is closed-shell and ordinarily diamagnetic; a field can still induce a weak response.
  4. Neutral Mn: [Ar]3d⁵4s² gives five unpaired in a neutral free-atom model; bulk manganese is more complex.
  5. Neutral Cu: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ gives one unpaired; this does not mean copper metal is a Curie-law local-moment material.
  6. Fe²⁺ free ion: 3d⁶ has a high-spin-model count of four, but bound coordination environments may differ.
  7. Fe³⁺ free ion: 3d⁵ has five unpaired in the simple model; orbital terms affect exact free-ion moments.
  8. Hexaaquairon(II): a commonly taught high-spin octahedral d⁶ complex with four unpaired electrons.
  9. Hexacyanoferrate(II): a low-spin octahedral d⁶ complex, with no unpaired electrons in the usual model.
  10. Oxygen gas: O₂ has two unpaired molecular electrons in its ground triplet state.
  11. Hemoglobin: deoxygenated and oxygenated forms have different magnetic responses because binding changes the electronic state.
  12. Zinc enzyme centre: Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰ and ordinarily diamagnetic, yet can assist catalysis or structural stabilization.

In every case the question is not merely “what is the configuration?” but “configuration of what physical system, under what assumptions?” That is the habit behind reliable scientific interpretation.

38. Twenty-five misconceptions and the reasoning that repairs them

  1. Every electron creates net paramagnetism. Electron moments can pair or cancel in the state.
  2. Paired electrons are unaffected by magnets. Closed shells respond diamagnetically to a field.
  3. More total electrons means more unpaired electrons. Neon has more electrons than nitrogen yet has none unpaired.
  4. All odd-electron atoms have exactly one unpaired electron. Neutral nitrogen has three.
  5. Even electron count guarantees diamagnetism. Oxygen atom has eight electrons but two unpaired.
  6. p⁴ has four unpaired electrons. Three p orbitals allow only two singles after one pair forms.
  7. d⁶ always has six unpaired electrons. Five d orbitals cannot provide six singly occupied boxes.
  8. d⁵ automatically means five unpaired in every compound. Strong ligand-field and coupling conditions change the state.
  9. 4s electrons are always retained in transition-metal cations. They are generally removed before 3d in the basic ion construction.
  10. Fe³⁺ equals the Fe atom plus three electrons. A +3 cation has lost three.
  11. All Fe²⁺ compounds have four unpaired electrons. Octahedral low-spin d⁶ can have zero.
  12. Same oxidation state means same magnetism. Ligands can change orbital splitting.
  13. Same d count always gives same observed moment. Spin state and orbital contribution matter.
  14. u = 0 means χ = 0. Diamagnetic response can be nonzero and negative.
  15. 1 μB is the unit of susceptibility. It is a magnetic moment scale.
  16. √[u(u+2)] always equals the measured moment. It is a spin-only approximation.
  17. Spin arrows are literal rotation directions. They label projections of intrinsic quantum spin.
  18. Attraction equals ferromagnetism. Ordinary paramagnetic samples can be weakly attracted.
  19. Fe is ferromagnetic because one isolated Fe atom has four unpaired electrons. Collective band and exchange interactions are required.
  20. O₂ has four unpaired because two O atoms have two each. The molecule has two in its ground-state molecular orbitals.
  21. N₂ is paramagnetic because nitrogen atoms are. Ground-state N₂ is closed-shell and diamagnetic.
  22. Every EPR-silent sample has no unpaired electrons. Experimental detection has limits.
  23. Curie's law holds for every magnetic material. Interactions and state changes can invalidate it.
  24. μ = χ. Moment and susceptibility have different physical meanings and units.
  25. Magnetic properties determine an enzyme's entire function. Function requires chemical and biological evidence.

40. Worked examples II: ten transition-metal atom and ion deductions

  1. Sc atom: [Ar]3d¹4s²; paired 4s plus one d electron → u=1.
  2. Ti atom: [Ar]3d²4s²; put both d electrons in separate boxes → u=2.
  3. V atom: [Ar]3d³4s²; three singly occupied d boxes → u=3.
  4. Cr atom: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹; five unpaired d plus one unpaired s → u=6 in the conventional free-atom configuration, not 5; the d-only table alone misses 4s¹.
  5. Mn atom: [Ar]3d⁵4s²; five singly occupied d boxes, paired s → u=5.
  6. Fe atom: [Ar]3d⁶4s²; d⁶ has four singles, paired 4s → u=4.
  7. Co atom: [Ar]3d⁷4s²; d⁷ has three singly occupied boxes → u=3.
  8. Ni atom: [Ar]3d⁸4s²; d⁸ has two unpaired → u=2.
  9. Cu atom: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹; filled d but singly occupied s → u=1.
  10. Zn atom: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²; filled d and s → u=0.

This list also exposes a trap: the simplified spin-only moment formula used for certain complexes should not be mechanically applied as an exact magnetic moment of a neutral free Cr atom based on its six unpaired spin labels. Neutral Cr is a many-electron atom with angular momentum coupling; use a full atomic term model for an actual free-atom moment.

41. Worked examples III: ions and ligand-field premises

  1. Fe²⁺: Fe loses 4s² → 3d⁶; high-spin model u=4.
  2. Fe³⁺: Fe loses 4s² and a 3d electron → 3d⁵; high-spin model u=5.
  3. Mn²⁺: [Ar]3d⁵ after 4s removal → u=5.
  4. Cu⁺: [Ar]3d¹⁰ after losing 4s¹ → u=0.
  5. Cu²⁺: [Ar]3d⁹ → u=1.
  6. Zn²⁺: [Ar]3d¹⁰ → u=0.
  7. Ti³⁺: [Ar]3d¹ → u=1.
  8. V³⁺: [Ar]3d² → u=2.
  9. High-spin octahedral d⁴: t₂g³e g¹ → four singles → u=4.
  10. Low-spin octahedral d⁴: t₂g⁴e g⁰ → one paired plus two singles → u=2.
  11. High-spin octahedral d⁵: t₂g³e g² → u=5.
  12. Low-spin octahedral d⁵: t₂g⁵e g⁰ → u=1.
  13. High-spin octahedral d⁶: t₂g⁴e g² → u=4.
  14. Low-spin octahedral d⁶: t₂g⁶e g⁰ → u=0.
  15. Low-spin octahedral d⁷: t₂g⁶e g¹ → u=1; compare the high-spin alternative t₂g⁵e g² with u=3.

The low-spin examples require the octahedral strong-field premise; they cannot be deduced from bare-ion configurations alone. d⁸ octahedral cases are also not universally 'spinless': the ordinary t₂g⁶e g² arrangement has two singly occupied e g orbitals in the conventional simple model.

42. Worked examples IV: dimensional and numerical checks

  1. One unpaired spin: √[1×3] μB = 1.732 μB. Multiplying by μB's SI value gives 1.61×10⁻²³ J T⁻¹.
  2. Two unpaired: √[2×4] μB = 2.828 μB; the factor 2.828 is dimensionless, unlike the moment.
  3. Three unpaired: √[3×5] μB = 3.873 μB; invert the square relation: u²+2u=15 gives u=3, not −5.
  4. Four unpaired: √[4×6] μB = √24 μB = 4.899 μB.
  5. Five unpaired: √[5×7] μB = √35 μB = 5.916 μB.
  6. Inverse exercise: A stated spin-only moment of approximately 2.83 μB gives u(u+2)≈8, hence u=2 for a nonnegative integer count.
  7. Curie ratio: If χ at 250 K is 4.0×10⁻³, χ at 500 K is 2.0×10⁻³ under the same ideal Curie-law conditions (χT constant).
  8. Magnetization: At H=1.0×10³ A/m and χ=3.0×10⁻⁴ (SI volume), M=χH=0.30 A/m, along the field.
  9. Diamagnetic response: χ=−1.0×10⁻⁵ at H=2.0×10³ A/m yields M=−0.020 A/m; minus means opposing field, not a negative amount of matter.
  10. Fe³⁺ estimate in SI: √35×9.274×10⁻²⁴≈5.49×10⁻²³ J/T; label as a spin-only model result, not an unconditional measured free-ion moment.

In calculations, write the model assumptions before substituting numbers. When a question provides an actual measured moment, orbital contributions, spin coupling and the chemical environment may prevent a unique inference from a simple integer table.

43. Original CSIR-NET / SET-style conceptual questions with explanations

All following questions are newly written practice, not claimed to be previous examination items. Each includes the crucial reasoning rather than a bare answer letter.

44. Original multiple-choice questions: 24 explained tests

Question 1 — Why is isolated ground-state nitrogen usually treated as paramagnetic?

  1. Its p³ occupancy contains three unpaired spins
  2. Its atomic mass is odd
  3. Its 1s shell is full
  4. Its atomic number is below ten

Answer A. 2p³ gives three separate singly occupied orbitals by Hund's rule; mass and atomic-number range do not establish magnetism.

Question 2 — Ground-state O atom has how many unpaired electrons in the basic orbital model?

  1. Zero
  2. Two
  3. One
  4. Four

Answer B. 2p⁴ occupies three p boxes with one pair and two singles, leaving two unpaired.

Question 3 — Which ground-state neutral atom is closed-shell?

  1. O
  2. P
  3. Ne
  4. Cu

Answer C. Ne ends in 2p⁶ and has all occupied orbitals paired; O, P and Cu have open shells.

Question 4 — Which of the following is the correct ground-state neutral Cu configuration?

  1. [Ar]3d⁹4s²
  2. [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²
  3. [Ar]3d⁸4s³
  4. [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹

Answer D. Copper is an established exception to the simplest filling guide; 3d¹⁰4s¹ has one unpaired electron.

Question 5 — For high-spin octahedral d⁶, what is u?

  1. 4
  2. 0
  3. 2
  4. 6

Answer A. Three t2g and two eg orbitals contain a t2g⁴eg² occupation with four single arrows.

Question 6 — For low-spin octahedral d⁶, what is u?

  1. 1
  2. 0
  3. 2
  4. 4

Answer B. Six electrons pair in the lower three t2g orbitals, giving u=0 in the simple strong-field model.

Question 7 — Which pair illustrates identical Fe oxidation states but different typical spin states?

  1. Fe and Fe³⁺
  2. Cu and Zn
  3. [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ and [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻
  4. N₂ and O₂

Answer C. Both iron complexes contain Fe²⁺ d⁶, but differing ligand fields favor high- versus low-spin states.

Question 8 — A closed-shell ion has which simple magnetic feature?

  1. No response to any field
  2. Always ferromagnetic
  3. Six unpaired electrons
  4. Usually a weak induced diamagnetic response

Answer D. No permanent unpaired-spin moment need not mean zero field response; induced diamagnetism remains.

Question 9 — Which physical quantity has SI unit A/m?

  1. Magnetization
  2. Bohr magneton
  3. Volume magnetic susceptibility
  4. Magnetic flux density

Answer A. Magnetization M is moment per volume in A/m; susceptibility is dimensionless, B is in tesla.

Question 10 — Which property is dimensionless in M = χH (SI volume form)?

  1. M
  2. χ
  3. H
  4. Magnetic moment

Answer B. M and H are both A/m, so χ=M/H is dimensionless.

Question 11 — Why must oxygen gas not be assigned four unpaired electrons from two O atoms?

  1. Its atoms have no electrons
  2. Molecules cannot be magnetic
  3. The molecule has new molecular orbitals
  4. O₂ is an ion

Answer C. The ground-state O2 molecular orbitals have two unpaired electrons; atom-box counts cannot simply be summed.

Question 12 — Which is the best statement about bulk iron ferromagnetism?

  1. It follows directly from counting a free Fe atom
  2. Every Fe²⁺ complex is ferromagnetic
  3. It means all spins are zero
  4. It requires collective electronic interactions in the metal

Answer D. Band electronic structure and interatomic magnetic exchange are necessary for bulk ordering.

Question 13 — Which has no unpaired d electrons in a simple ground-state isolated-ion model?

  1. Zn²⁺
  2. Fe³⁺
  3. Mn²⁺
  4. Cu²⁺

Answer A. Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰, completely filled; other listed ions have open d subshells.

Question 14 — What is the condition used to obtain μ≈√[u(u+2)] μB?

  1. All orbital contributions dominate
  2. Orbital contributions are neglected and S=u/2
  3. The sample has zero temperature
  4. Every electron spin is zero

Answer B. Spin-only moment uses g≈2 and coupled spin S=u/2, neglecting orbital contributions.

Question 15 — Which is true about electron spin arrows in an orbital box?

  1. They are drawings of real mechanical rotations
  2. They specify a fixed magnetic direction in every frame
  3. They label two spin projection states
  4. They show the electron's orbit radius

Answer C. Up/down are conventional spin projection symbols, not classical paths or mechanical rotation.

Question 16 — What occurs when the fourth electron enters three degenerate p orbitals?

  1. All four are unpaired
  2. The s shell empties
  3. An orbital holds three electrons
  4. One p orbital begins to pair

Answer D. p³ has three singles; p⁴ is [↑↓][↑][↑].

Question 17 — Why can an EPR-silent sample still contain spin centres?

  1. Signals can be undetected due to relaxation or coupling
  2. The centres have no nuclei
  3. All EPR systems are diamagnetic
  4. Spin can only occur at absolute zero

Answer A. Experimental absence of a detectable signal is not proof of the absence of magnetic centres.

Question 18 — Which relation is consistent with ideal Curie's law?

  1. χ∝T
  2. χ∝1/T
  3. χ is always zero
  4. μB∝1/T

Answer B. For suitable noninteracting paramagnets χ=C/T, with temperature in kelvin.

Question 19 — The low-spin octahedral d⁵ state has how many unpaired electrons?

  1. 0
  2. 3
  3. 1
  4. 5

Answer C. t2g⁵ gives two pairs and one single: u=1.

Question 20 — A neutral Cr atom's conventional orbital configuration has how many unpaired electrons?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 5
  4. 6

Answer D. 3d⁵ has five singly occupied d orbitals and 4s¹ adds a sixth unpaired s electron.

Question 21 — Which comparison is chemically sound?

  1. O₂ has two unpaired electrons in molecular π* orbitals
  2. N atom and N₂ must have identical spin
  3. O atom and O₂ have two unpaired electrons for the same reason
  4. O₂ is diamagnetic because a Lewis double bond exists

Answer A. Molecular-orbital theory predicts the triplet O2 ground state with two unpaired π* electrons.

Question 22 — In M=χH, a negative χ means the magnetization is:

  1. Guaranteed to be stronger than ferromagnetism
  2. Opposite to applied H in the linear model
  3. Undefined
  4. More than 100% spin

Answer B. A negative volume susceptibility indicates diamagnetic response opposing the applied field.

Question 23 — Which biological iron-containing sample is classically diamagnetic overall?

  1. Deoxyhemoglobin
  2. Every Fe³⁺ ion
  3. Oxyhemoglobin
  4. Every iron oxide

Answer C. Oxyhemoglobin is diamagnetic overall; oxygenation modifies the combined electronic structure of heme and dioxygen.

Question 24 — Which statement best identifies the limit of a free-ion configuration?

  1. It uniquely determines enzyme catalysis
  2. It directly measures an EPR spectrum
  3. It fixes a material's Curie temperature
  4. It can estimate unpaired counts but not every complex's spin state

Answer D. Coordination, bonding and coupling can modify actual spin states and magnetic response.

45. Numerical and short-answer practice with fully explained answers

Problem 25 — p-count pattern

Determine u for p², p³, p⁵ and p⁶ without writing all four atomic configurations.

Solution: Three p orbitals first fill singly, then pair. Hence u = 2, 3, 1 and 0. The pattern follows Hund and Pauli; it is not an oxidation-state rule.

Problem 26 — two ions

Compare free-ion Fe³⁺ and Zn²⁺ under a high-spin 3d-orbital counting model.

Solution: Fe³⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵ gives five single arrows; Zn²⁺ = [Ar]3d¹⁰ gives five pairs and zero singles. The qualitative spin response differs. Predicting the magnetic behaviour of actual salts needs the environment.

Problem 27 — inverted moment formula

An octahedral high-spin system is reported to have a spin-only estimated moment 3.87 μB. How many unpaired electrons?

Solution: Square the dimensionless moment ratio, giving u(u+2)≈15. Test u=3: 3×5=15. Thus u=3. A measured value merely near this number may not uniquely determine u without assumptions.

Problem 28 — low/high spin

An octahedral d⁶ compound changes from high-spin to low-spin. State both u values and the spin-only estimates.

Solution: High-spin t2g⁴eg² has u=4 → √24≈4.90 μB. Low-spin t2g⁶ has u=0 → 0 μB spin-only. The diamagnetic response of paired electrons remains possible.

Problem 29 — ideal temperature change

A dilute Curie-law paramagnet has χ=1.8×10⁻³ at T=300 K. Find χ at 450 K.

Solution: χT=C. Thus χ₂=χ₁T₁/T₂=1.8×10⁻³×300/450 =1.2×10⁻³ (dimensionless SI volume susceptibility). This assumes independent moments and no phase change.

Problem 30 — moment in SI

Calculate the spin-only μ for u=2 in joules per tesla, using μB=9.274×10⁻²⁴ J/T.

Solution: μ=√8 μB≈2.828×9.274×10⁻²⁴=2.62×10⁻²³ J/T. Keep the Bohr-magneton factor, or the result would have no physical unit.

Problem 31 — sign and unit

For χ=−4.0×10⁻⁶ and H=5.0×10³ A/m, determine M.

Solution: M=χH=−4.0×10⁻⁶×5.0×10³=−2.0×10⁻² A/m, directed against H. It is not a negative number of unpaired electrons.

Problem 32 — six orbital arrows?

Can d⁶ have six unpaired electrons in five d orbitals?

Solution: No. Five spatial orbitals can each host at most one singly occupied state. A sixth d electron must pair in an already used box in the degenerate model. High-spin d⁶ gives four unpaired, not six.

Problem 33 — ionization check

Neutral Co is [Ar]3d⁷4s². What is the conventional free-ion configuration of Co²⁺ and the high-spin d count?

Solution: Remove 4s² to get [Ar]3d⁷. In the five-degenerate-orbital model, the seven electrons leave three unpaired. Actual coordination complexes require a spin-state premise.

Problem 34 — identify a flawed biological inference

A student says, “Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic, so isolated Fe²⁺ alone completely describes oxygen binding.” What is missing?

Solution: Ligand binding changes the combined electronic structure and possible spin coupling; a bare ion does not reproduce the protein environment. Magnetism supplies evidence about electronic state, not a full binding mechanism.

Problem 35 — same d count, contrasting moments

Which of high-spin and low-spin d⁵ could yield approximately 5.92 and 1.73 μB, respectively, in a spin-only model?

Solution: High-spin d⁵ u=5 gives √35≈5.92 μB. Low-spin octahedral d⁵ has t2g⁵ and u=1 giving √3≈1.73 μB. Ligand-field premises are essential.

Problem 36 — EPR reasoning

A Zn²⁺ complex gives no ordinary Zn-centred EPR signal. Does this prove its entire protein is closed shell?

Solution: No. Zn²⁺ itself has closed d¹⁰ occupancy but other radical centres, cofactors or associated species could be paramagnetic, and EPR has sensitivity limits.

46. Assertion–reason questions: distinguish mechanism from coincidence

For each item judge the assertion (A) and reason (R). State whether both are correct and whether R explains A.

37. A: Isolated O atom has two unpaired electrons. R: Its 2p⁴ arrangement has one pair and two singly occupied p orbitals.

Both true; R correctly explains A. Apply Hund's rule to three degenerate p orbitals.

38. A: Ne is diamagnetic in its ground state. R: Its filled valence subshell has no permanent net spin moment.

Both true; R explains the simple closed-shell classification. Induced diamagnetism still exists.

39. A: Every octahedral Fe²⁺ complex has four unpaired electrons. R: All Fe²⁺ ions have six 3d electrons.

A false, R true under ordinary oxidation-state configuration counting. d⁶ can be high or low spin depending on the coordination environment.

40. A: Ground-state O₂ has two unpaired electrons. R: An O atom has two unpaired electrons.

Both statements true, but R does not explain A. The O₂ result comes from molecular orbitals and coupling, not directly from the atomic count.

41. A: Zn²⁺ is usually diamagnetic. R: Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰ in its simple ground configuration.

Both true; R explains the closed-shell ion classification. The complete material or complex still has to be specified for a bulk measurement.

42. A: μso and χ have identical units. R: Both describe magnetic phenomena.

A false, R true. A magnetic moment has J/T or A·m² dimensions, whereas SI volume susceptibility is dimensionless.

43. A: Paramagnetic susceptibility always follows Curie's law. R: Temperature can oppose field alignment of local moments.

A false, R true in relevant regimes. Collective interactions, orbital physics and ordering can invalidate the simple law.

44. A: Neutral ground-state Cr has six unpaired electrons in the simple orbital diagram. R: Its evaluated configuration is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹.

Both true; R explains A. There are five singly occupied d orbitals plus one singly occupied s orbital.

47. Integrative challenges that test evidence and assumptions

Challenge 1 — Disentangle two iron compounds

A report compares Fe²⁺ in an aqueous high-spin octahedral environment with low-spin hexacyanoferrate(II). Both species are d⁶, yet their magnetic moments differ. Write both one-electron occupations and explain why electron number alone fails.

Solution: High spin: t2g⁴eg², four singles. Low spin: t2g⁶eg⁰, no singles. The occupation energy depends on ligand splitting relative to pairing energy; iron oxidation state and total d count alone do not set the spin multiplicity.

Challenge 2 — Molecular oxygen as falsification test

A student predicts four unpaired electrons in O₂ by adding two atoms. Identify the broken assumption and the required independent evidence.

Solution: Combining two atoms creates delocalized molecular orbitals, so atomic unpaired counts do not add as conserved labels. Molecular-orbital theory predicts two unpaired π* electrons for the O₂ triplet ground state, consistent with magnetic experiments.

Challenge 3 — Estimate versus measurement

High-spin d⁵ suggests μso≈5.92 μB. A real complex has a measured moment that differs. List at least three physically meaningful explanations without changing its proton number.

Solution: Orbital contribution, spin–orbit coupling, thermal occupation or magnetic exchange between centres can change a measured effective moment; spin-state admixtures and errors/impurities are further possibilities. None requires changing the atomic number.

Challenge 4 — Mixed magnetic centres

A protein contains Zn²⁺ and an organic radical. Is 'Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰' enough to label the entire protein diamagnetic?

Solution: No. Zn²⁺ itself gives no unpaired d electrons, but an organic radical may have an unpaired spin. Include other centres and possible coupling before drawing a total-state conclusion.

Challenge 5 — Unit audit

A solution multiplies χ=2.0×10⁻³ by μB and reports magnetization. Explain the error and provide a consistent alternative if H=2.5×10³ A/m.

Solution: χμB has moment units, not magnetization units. In the SI linear response M=χH=5.0 A/m. To find an individual moment, one needs electronic-state information, not just χ.

Challenge 6 — Distinguish state and material

For a free isolated neutral Fe atom, derive the unpaired count. Then explain why that alone cannot establish the magnetic ordering temperature of iron metal.

Solution: [Ar]3d⁶4s² leaves four unpaired in a simple atomic box picture. Metallic Fe involves delocalized bands and interatomic exchange; collective ordering and transition temperature cannot be obtained from isolated orbital boxes.

48. Revision: a dependable examination workflow

Begin with the identity of the species, including charge and physical environment. Establish the correct electron configuration; use Hund's rule only within the assumptions where the relevant orbitals may be treated as degenerate. Count singly occupied orbitals, not total electrons, and distinguish the unpaired-spin count from an effective magnetic moment. Use μso≈√[u(u+2)] μB only when the spin-only premises apply; verify J/T or A·m² if converting to SI. For an octahedral complex, ask about high spin versus low spin before making a moment claim. For molecules such as O₂ and N₂, use molecular states. For real specimens, remember that susceptibility, temperature, exchange, impurities and orbital contributions matter.
