Atomic Number and the Modern Periodic Law

From mass-order contradictions to nuclear charge: follow the X-ray experiments and evidence that transformed the periodic table into the modern atomic-number system.

Learning outcomes: distinguish atomic number, mass number and relative atomic mass; explain the historical mass inversions and nuclear-charge hypothesis; reconstruct characteristic X-ray production, diffraction and Moseley's measurements; derive and apply the screened Kα law; interpret plots, uncertainties, isotope and ion identity; connect X-ray analysis to elemental studies in life sciences; solve original explained analytical and numerical problems.

1. The unanswered question left by a very successful periodic table

In the previous learning node, we followed chemists as they discovered a recurring pattern among the elements. Their work was remarkable because it came from actual compounds, reactions and measured relative atomic masses. Mendeleev even used the pattern to predict missing elements. Yet one awkward question remained: what numerical quantity gives every element its correct place when the order of relative atomic masses sometimes contradicts the chemical evidence? A theory that cannot answer that question is useful but incomplete.

Think of a library whose books are arranged approximately by physical weight. For most books, increasing weight might happen to track a more meaningful property, such as the number of pages. But a thin book printed on heavy paper can weigh more than a longer book. A librarian who wants a stable scientific numbering system needs a property intrinsic to each book's identity, not a coincidental measurement. Atomic mass helped reveal chemical order, but the exceptions made scientists suspect that it was not the fundamental atomic label.

This chapter tells how scientists turned that suspicion into an experimental test. We will begin with the mass-order paradox, move through evidence for the nuclear atom, learn why characteristic X-ray frequencies can distinguish elements, reconstruct Henry Moseley's measurements, and then derive the modern atomic-number principle. The mathematical steps will be explicit, but every equation will be connected to an observable and to its assumptions.

2. Three mass-order inversions that demanded an explanation

Consider argon and potassium. Standard relative atomic mass for argon is about 39.948, higher than potassium's 39.098, yet argon belongs before potassium in modern atomic-number order. Argon is a noble gas in the preceding family, while potassium starts a new alkali-metal sequence. If we sorted them by mass alone, the arrangement would contradict well-established chemical placement.

The same puzzle appears with cobalt and nickel. Cobalt's standard relative atomic mass is about 58.933 and nickel's about 58.693; nevertheless cobalt precedes nickel in the accepted chemical sequence. It appears again for tellurium, about 127.60, and iodine, about 126.904. Tellurium's chemically appropriate family and iodine's halogen properties called for an order opposite to a literal increasing-mass sort. These are historically important comparisons, not random isolated exceptions chosen after the problem was solved.

A good student should calculate the order under both proposed rules and state precisely what differs. For argon–potassium, increasing mass orders K before Ar; increasing atomic number orders Ar before K. For cobalt–nickel, mass puts Ni before Co; atomic number puts Co before Ni. For tellurium–iodine, mass puts I before Te; atomic number puts Te before I. The next question is not whether chemical observations can be ignored, but whether a measurable physical quantity can justify these consistent placements.

3. Atomic weight was a useful clue, not a causal law

Why did the historical weight-based table work as well as it did? For many naturally abundant elements, nuclei with more positive charge also tend to have more protons and more neutrons, and thus greater mass. The rough correlation makes a mass sequence similar to the eventual nuclear-charge sequence over large regions. That success was an empirical convenience rather than an identity of mass and nuclear charge.

Relative atomic mass is a dimensionless measure related to isotope masses and their abundance in a specified sample or standard atomic-weight convention. It is not an integer count of protons, and it need not be an integer even for a monoisotopic element because nuclear binding energy affects isotopic mass. In contrast, the nuclear charge of an element is quantized in whole positive elementary-charge units. The two quantities therefore contain different physical information.

Historically, pointing to the chemical order was not enough to establish the alternative. Scientists needed independent measurements that varied systematically with the proposed position number, including the awkward inverse-mass pairs. The opportunity arrived when research on the nuclear atom and high-frequency radiation converged. Instead of searching for another table drawn on paper, Moseley would measure a repeatable signature inside the atom.

4. A beginner's distinction: observation, classification and mechanism

An element such as potassium may be recognized by its chemical compounds or spectra. Once recognized, it receives a position in an ordered classification. A mechanism then explains why the ordering predicts chemical similarities. These three operations are distinct. Mendeleev made a major step in classification and prediction before the microscopic mechanism was established; Moseley's research connected a physical observable to an integer associated with the nuclear atom.

Suppose two colourless metal salts give distinct characteristic X-ray lines. Those lines provide a reproducible way to distinguish their elemental constituents, but a measurement by itself is not automatically an explanation of every compound they will form. Conversely, observing that related metals have similar typical ionic states is chemical evidence for a family, but it does not measure how many positive charges lie in each nucleus.

The chapter's goal is to connect each type of evidence without overstating any of them. We shall encounter an almost-linear graph, a compact approximate equation and the modern definition of atomic number. The graph is empirical; the equation uses a simplified model; the definition of element identity is exact in the modern theory. Confusing these levels is a common source of errors in high-level examination questions.

5. Why the discovery of the atomic nucleus mattered

Before the early twentieth century, chemical models treated atoms as fundamental constituents but did not have a reliable physical picture of where positive charge resided. In 1911, Ernest Rutherford interpreted alpha-particle scattering results as evidence for a very small, massive, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electrons. Most of an atom's volume is not occupied by a uniformly spread positive substance. This changed what a statement such as 'central charge' could mean.

The scattering evidence did not directly count the nucleus's individual positive charges for every element. It supplied the structural premise that made a nuclear-charge ordering plausible. The atom, in this picture, consists of a central positive charge and electrons whose number balances it in an electrically neutral atom. If the central charge differed systematically from one element to the next, spectroscopy might reveal that change through the binding energies of inner electrons.

For the present lesson, avoid revisiting the entire alpha-scattering apparatus or deriving Rutherford's cross section. Those belong to the atomic-structure history already covered. What we need is the causal bridge: a compact central charge creates an electrostatic potential; inner electrons respond strongly to that potential; transitions involving those electrons can emit photons that carry measurable energy information.

6. Atomic number first looked like an ordinal label

The word atomic number can mean a counting position in a historical arrangement or the modern number of protons in a nucleus. Those meanings did not become physically equivalent through mere naming. Earlier periodic tables used serial numbers to keep elements in a sequence, and their chemical order was persuasive, but the numeral did not initially come with an independently measured nuclear-charge interpretation.

Antonius van den Broek argued in 1913 that the ordering number could be related to the positive nuclear charge and the properties of the elements. As described by NIST, this was a promising hypothesis taken seriously by contemporary physicists, not a complete experimental demonstration. It supplied a question that could be tested by measuring something controlled by the atom's internal electrical structure.

The important chronological distinction is therefore: chemists recognized recurring chemical properties; the nuclear model gave a place for intrinsic positive charge; van den Broek proposed a connection between an element's place and that charge; Moseley tested whether spectroscopic data followed the proposed integer. If an examination attributes the modern experimental atomic-number law to Mendeleev alone, it collapses these distinct steps into a false history.

7. Why X-rays rather than visible colour were promising

Chemical colour depends on many features: oxidation state, molecular environment, solvents, ligands, electronic structure and illumination. The same element can give compounds of very different colour, and different elements can give similar-looking compounds. A physicist seeking an elemental fingerprint would prefer a measurement closely linked to nuclear charge and comparatively insensitive to whether a specimen was supplied as a metal or a particular chemical compound.

X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic radiation. Their photon energies are typically far above those of visible light, although ranges overlap with neighbouring bands and no sharp physical boundary is universal. Inner electronic levels in many atoms have much larger binding energies than the optical transitions of outer electrons. When an inner-shell vacancy is filled, the released photon can lie in the X-ray region.

Because the inner electronic states strongly sample nuclear attraction, the energy of a characteristic X-ray line responds systematically to nuclear charge. This does not mean all X-ray lines are identical for a given element in every chemical environment or that tiny chemical shifts never occur. It means that the underlying line pattern supplies a particularly powerful way of connecting elemental identity with a measurable physical spectrum.

8. What an X-ray photon carries: frequency, wavelength and energy

Electromagnetic radiation can be described by wavelength λ and frequency ν. In vacuum they satisfy c = λν, where c is the speed of light. A photon has energy E = hν, where h is Planck's constant. Combining the two gives E = hc/λ. A high-energy photon has high frequency and short wavelength. These are not separate phenomena but three mathematically connected descriptions of one radiative event.

X-ray energies are often reported in electronvolts or kiloelectronvolts. One electronvolt is 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ joule, and 1 keV = 1000 eV. Photon energy of 8.0 keV is therefore 8000 eV, not 8 eV. A useful conversion is hc ≈ 1239.84 eV nm, which permits quick calculation of wavelength from a photon energy expressed in electronvolts.

Suppose a detected line has E = 8.0 keV. Its wavelength is λ = 1239.84 / 8000 nm ≈0.1550 nm. The calculation describes a photon; the sample's molar mass or oxidation state does not directly enter this conversion. This basic dimensional competence is necessary before interpreting a Moseley graph; a wrong choice between nanometres and ångströms creates a tenfold error.

E = hν = hc/λ h = Planck constant; c = speed of light; ν in s⁻¹ (Hz); λ in m; E in J, with proper conversion to eV or keV.

9. The two very different components of an X-ray spectrum

When sufficiently energetic charged particles strike a metal target, they can undergo acceleration changes and produce a broad distribution of bremsstrahlung radiation. In addition, the atoms in the target may produce sharp characteristic X-ray lines. The broad continuum and the narrow line features have different physical origins. A graph showing only one of them should not be described as the entire spectrum.

Bremsstrahlung involves the motion of energetic electrons in the electric fields of target atoms and has a continuous energy distribution constrained by the incident-electron energy. Characteristic emission involves discrete changes among bound electronic states when inner vacancies are created. Moseley's work exploited the wavelengths of the characteristic lines rather than assigning each sample a single arbitrary continuous-radiation peak.

An experiment must separate these components. If a detector has weak energy resolution, the characteristic peaks may appear on a continuum or merge with nearby peaks. If the incident-electron energy is too low to create a particular shell vacancy, the corresponding line may be absent. Absence under insufficient excitation does not prove absence of the element; that distinction becomes important in modern X-ray fluorescence and spectroscopy.

10. How an inner-shell vacancy is created

Imagine a target atom whose innermost occupied shell is full. An incoming energetic electron transfers enough energy to eject one of the core electrons. The residual atom temporarily has a vacancy, sometimes called a core hole, in a strongly bound state. The configuration is excited and unstable with respect to several possible relaxation processes. The process requires a threshold related to the core-electron binding energy.

One route of relaxation occurs when an electron from a higher-energy occupied shell fills the vacancy. The difference between the initial and final atomic energies can be emitted as an X-ray photon. Another route is nonradiative emission of an Auger electron, so not every vacancy produces a characteristic photon. Moreover, the energies in a real many-electron atom depend on the whole configuration before and after the transition, not solely on an isolated Bohr orbit.

This picture corrects a common misunderstanding: the photon in the spectrum is not the incoming electron itself, nor does an X-ray necessarily remove a proton from the nucleus. The characteristic line records a change in atomic electronic energy. Nuclear charge influences those electronic energies and therefore influences the photon emitted during relaxation.

11. K, L and M: what the historical shell labels mean

The innermost principal shell is traditionally labelled K and corresponds broadly to n=1; the next is L, broadly n=2; the next is M, broadly n=3. This historical notation remains useful for identifying characteristic X-ray series. A transition ending in a K-shell vacancy produces a member of the K series; one ending in an L-shell vacancy produces an L-series line.

The commonly described Kα transition is primarily a transition from a 2p-like L-shell state into a 1s-like vacancy. Kβ commonly involves a higher state, typically 3p-like, filling the K vacancy. The simple schematic labels n=2→1 and n=3→1 are helpful for first calculations, but real atoms possess subshells, selection rules, multiplets, spin–orbit splitting and complicated final states. A single pair of perfectly sharp Bohr levels is a model, not the full measured structure.

The Greek α and β names identify different members of a series; they do not mean α-particles or β-particles are emitted. A Kα photon is electromagnetic radiation, not an alpha particle. Distinguishing historical naming systems avoids remarkably frequent exam traps in interdisciplinary courses.

12. Why the same element can produce several characteristic lines

A single core vacancy can be filled by electrons originating from different allowed electronic states, so the energy difference and emitted photon depend on the transition. Even the familiar Kα line often resolves into components such as Kα₁ and Kα₂ when measured with sufficient resolution. Their small energy difference is related to relativistic and spin–orbit structure, not to the sample containing two different elements.

Other strong features include Kβ lines and lines of the L series. Each series has its own approximate dependence on atomic number and screening. Consequently, a plot mixing unidentified K and L lines may fail to show a simple straight line. A careful researcher compares the same identified spectral line across a sequence of targets.

The inner-transition interpretation explains why the patterns are called characteristic. They encode the binding-energy structure of the element. It does not follow that a given elemental sample must produce every line at every excitation energy, nor that a line's measured intensity is independent of target thickness or detector geometry. These details matter for the use of spectra as physical evidence.

13. A spectroscopy experiment needs wavelengths, not merely visible glows

Visible spectroscopy can use prisms or gratings to separate light by wavelength. X-rays have much shorter wavelengths, so measuring their dispersion requires an appropriate optical method. At the beginning of the twentieth century, diffraction of X-rays by ordered crystal planes provided a practical route to quantitative wavelength measurements. The spacing of atomic planes in a crystal is comparable to the wavelength of many X-rays.

William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg helped establish the relevant diffraction method. Moseley could direct characteristic X-rays from a target toward a crystal and look for the angles at which selected wavelengths were strongly diffracted. If the crystal spacing was known, those angles could be translated into wavelengths rather than remaining qualitative markings on a photographic plate.

A measured peak angle is an observation; an inferred wavelength requires the diffraction equation and calibration; an inferred photon energy requires the photon relation. Working explicitly through those linked steps makes clear how a physicist could obtain data about the atomic interior without ever seeing a proton or an electron with an optical microscope.

14. Bragg's law and why a crystal works as an X-ray analyser

Consider two rays reflected or diffracted from successive parallel planes of a crystal separated by distance d. In the simple geometrical derivation, the ray associated with the lower plane travels an additional distance 2d sinθ, with θ defined relative to the diffracting lattice planes. Constructive interference occurs when this path difference equals an integer number m of wavelengths. This gives Bragg's law: mλ = 2d sinθ.

The integer m is the diffraction order; it is not the atomic mass number, electron principal quantum number or element's periodic-table position. For first-order diffraction m=1 and λ = 2d sinθ. A higher diffraction order can produce a peak at another angle for the same wavelength, so a measurement must identify the diffraction geometry before assigning a photon energy.

The elementary derivation assumes a coherent crystalline periodic structure and a suitable reflection geometry. Real diffractometers have finite instrumental resolution, mosaic spread, sample absorption and other effects. None of those complications invalidate the central calculation. They show why calibration and controlled measurement were essential to turning a photographic line into quantitative evidence for Moseley's law.

mλ = 2d sinθ m = positive diffraction order; d = crystal-plane spacing; θ = Bragg angle measured from the planes; λ = X-ray wavelength.

15. Reconstructing Moseley's instrument as a chain of evidence

The experiment can be summarized as a connected sequence of physical operations. An electron source accelerates electrons into a target. The target emits a spectrum containing characteristic lines. A crystal separates the radiation by diffraction, and a detector or photographic recording locates the angular positions of the strongest lines. Bragg's equation converts the angles to λ; ν=c/λ converts them to frequencies. Only then can the investigator compare different elemental targets quantitatively.

A meaningful comparison holds the line family and measurement method fixed. For example, one may compare Kα lines from consecutive metals while identifying their atomic ordering independently through accepted chemical behaviour. If each new element produces a line at systematically different frequency, the result can be plotted against a candidate element number. The graph reveals whether the numbering variable has a simple physical relationship to inner-electron energies.

Moseley's success did not depend on observing just one target or one isolated coincidence. It depended on repeated spectra across a range of elements, including a pair whose mass order conflicted with chemical order. A reproducible trend across several samples makes the new nuclear-charge hypothesis far more persuasive than a beautiful graph made from two arbitrarily chosen points.

16. An original apparatus diagram and what it measures

The beam path is not merely a picture of laboratory hardware. It is a chain of inferences. The excitation source creates a physical state in the specimen; the specimen emits photons of different energies; diffraction selects wavelengths; and the detector translates that selection into measurable marks. The computed frequency belongs to the characteristic photon, while the atomic-number interpretation is still a scientific hypothesis being tested against multiple elements.

When a sample changes from one element to the next, the energy of a corresponding K line changes in a repeatable manner. The crystal need not somehow identify the element chemically; it separates photons according to wavelength. It is the relationship between wavelength and element identity that gives the experiment its discriminating power.

Do not mistake the conceptual sketch for a complete blueprint. Historical equipment included additional arrangements for positioning samples, evacuating or protecting the beam path and recording lines. Those engineering choices matter to laboratory fidelity but do not alter the central measurement logic.

17. From angle to wavelength: a deliberately worked measurement

Suppose a teaching example uses crystal plane spacing d = 0.200 nm and an observed first-order diffraction angle θ = 29.0°. Then λ = 2(0.200 nm)sin29.0° ≈ 0.194 nm. Keeping nm throughout avoids an unnecessary intermediate metre conversion. Convert to photon energy with E = hc/λ ≈ 1239.84 eV nm / 0.194 nm ≈ 6.39 keV.

Every step must be interpreted carefully. The diffraction angle yields a wavelength only because d and the diffraction order were specified. The energy is that of a diffracted X-ray photon, not the voltage of the apparatus. It is close to a familiar iron Kα energy, but one should not identify an element uniquely from one rounded angle in an invented teaching example without considering calibration, line identity and competing peaks.

Changing diffraction order changes the relation: if m=2 at the same angle, λ doubles, and photon energy halves. It is mathematically incorrect to use the same wavelength formula for m=1 and m=2 without adjusting the order. This example shows why dimensional checks and an understanding of experimental geometry belong alongside memorized formulas.

18. What Moseley measured in 1913 and 1914

Henry Gwyn Jeffreys Moseley began measuring characteristic X-ray spectra of elements in 1913, working initially in Manchester and later at Oxford. He applied crystal-based wavelength analysis to systematic sequences of elements, comparing identifiable K and L lines. NIST and the University of Oxford document his work, and Moseley's original research was published in the Philosophical Magazine in 1913–1914.

His early measurements included elements in the calcium-to-zinc region. This was a clever range because many successive elements could be examined with related inner-shell lines, and it included the mass-order anomaly of cobalt and nickel. As his survey expanded, he could compare line frequencies across a much larger span of the periodic system. The instruments transformed a chemist's element sequence into a quantitative graph of inner atomic behaviour.

The outstanding achievement was not the invention of a new element list but the demonstration that a simple integer connected the characteristic X-ray frequency across elements. If an integer increasing by one at each step explained the spectroscopy while ignoring the mass-order inversions, that would be direct new evidence about the fundamental quantity distinguishing the elements.

19. The graph that turned a position number into physics

Imagine recording the same Kα line for several elements and computing its frequency ν. If we plot ν directly against an element sequence, the graph is curved because the energy scales approximately as a squared quantity. Moseley found that plotting the square root of frequency against the appropriate integer associated with an element gave an approximately straight line.

Straightness matters because it is a strong constraint. Many arbitrary numbers could be assigned to a few targets, but a common nearly linear relation over many targets is less easily explained as coincidence. The element number increased by one between successive accepted elements, including precisely where relative mass ordering gave the wrong sequence. The spectroscopic variable therefore supported a one-to-one integer ordering distinct from numerical mass.

The graph alone is not a complete many-electron quantum derivation. The empirical fact is approximate linearity for corresponding identified spectral series. The physical interpretation is that inner electron binding energies depend strongly on a positive central charge, and the relevant integer can be identified with its strength in elementary-charge units. That transition from plot to model is the heart of the modern periodic law.

20. Moseley's law in a precise and useful form

For a chosen characteristic X-ray line family, Moseley's empirical relationship can be written ν ≈ a(Z − σ)². Here ν is photon frequency, Z is atomic number, a is a line-dependent proportionality constant with frequency units and σ is an effective screening parameter. Taking square roots gives √ν ≈ √a (Z − σ). For a comparable series, this is a straight line in Z with slope √a and intercept −σ√a.

The sign convention is worth understanding rather than merely copying. Screening means the inner electron does not experience the full bare nuclear attraction in the simplest one-electron approximation. The quantity Z−σ is an effective nuclear-charge parameter for the selected transition, not the exact number of protons minus a physically removed fraction of a proton. The actual nucleus still has charge +Ze.

For the elementary Kα approximation, σ is often taken near 1.0. The numerical values for other series differ; NIST records a historical approximate Lα offset around 7.4, which should not be casually applied to K lines. The simple formula works impressively well in its suitable domain but is not exact for every element and every resolved spectral component.

ν ≈ a(Z − σ)²   ⇒   √ν ≈ √a(Z − σ)Z = proton number; σ = series-dependent effective screening approximation; a = positive constant determined by the transition. This is not one universal exact fit for all X-ray lines.

21. Why square-root plots are better than raw-frequency plots here

A mathematical plot is an analytical tool only when we understand the transformation. If ν = a(Z−σ)² and ν is positive, then taking its positive square root gives √ν = √a(Z−σ) for Z greater than σ, as in the intended core-transition context. The transformed relation is linear. Using the square root reduces the curved dependence to a line whose slope and intercept can be interpreted physically.

One could instead plot photon energy E because E = hν. Then √E = √(ha)(Z−σ), so a square-root-energy plot has the same linear form with a different slope and units. Plotting reciprocal square-root wavelength also works because ν=c/λ. These are mathematically equivalent transformations for a given identified line, not three unrelated periodic laws.

An exam might offer the statements 'frequency versus Z is linear', '√frequency versus Z is approximately linear' and 'wavelength versus Z is linear'. The second is the appropriate Moseley-form statement. The first omits the square dependence; the third ignores the reciprocal relation between frequency and wavelength. As usual, 'approximately' signals that real line energies have corrections and the simplified equation is not exact.

22. A bridge from the nuclear model to the Kα approximation

In a hydrogen-like model, the energy of an electron in principal state n can be written Eₙ = −13.6 eV × Z_eff²/n². This result comes from the Coulomb attraction in an idealized one-electron atom, with effective nuclear charge inserted as a simplifying adaptation to a multielectron environment. It is not a complete treatment of electron correlation or all atomic states.

After a K-shell electron is removed, one other electron generally remains in the K shell of an atom with an initially filled K shell. In a traditional first approximation, the transition electron experiences screening by roughly one elementary charge, so Z_eff≈Z−1. The energy difference between the n=2 and n=1 levels is then 13.6 eV(Z−1)²(1−1/4) = 10.2 eV(Z−1)².

The result displays the observed squared dependence on an integer nuclear charge with an offset. It gives an intuitive physical reason why √ν is nearly linear in Z. But it is essential to say what has been assumed: one-electron-like levels, a common effective screening factor, neglected fine structure and electron correlation, and a suitable Kα-like transition. We will quantify both the usefulness and the shortcomings of that model.

23. Deriving Kα photon energy without skipping the algebra

Write the approximate final energy E₁ = −13.6 eV(Z−1)² and the initial energy E₂ = −13.6 eV(Z−1)²/4. For emission, the photon carries the positive difference E_initial−E_final. Subtract carefully: E₂−E₁ = [−3.4 − (−13.6)] eV(Z−1)² = 10.2 eV(Z−1)². The positive result is physically required because emitted photons carry positive energy.

Now substitute E=hν. The approximate photon frequency becomes ν ≈ [10.2 eV/h](Z−1)², and √ν ≈ √(10.2 eV/h)(Z−1). This is the elementary version of Moseley's law with an effective Kα screening parameter near one. The coefficient has a frequency dimension because 10.2 eV divided by h (with consistent units) is measured in s⁻¹.

A common incorrect derivation subtracts 1/1²−1/2² in the wrong direction and reports a negative photon energy. That is a sign convention mistake, not evidence of negative-energy X-rays. The electronic states have negative binding energies relative to a chosen reference, but the difference released is positive. This explicit algebra is a useful template for later spectroscopy problems.

EKα ≈ (13.6 eV)(Z−1)²(1−¼) = (10.2 eV)(Z−1)²Approximates a Kα transition with fixed screening ≈1; valid as a teaching estimate, not a universal high-precision line database.

24. Why the screening number is not a proton subtraction

Students often read Z−1 and imagine that every Kα-emitting atom temporarily contains one fewer proton. That is false. The nuclear charge remains +Ze, and no proton is lost during ordinary atomic X-ray emission. The 'minus one' is a simplified statement about the electric-field shielding effect of another electron on the transitioning electron.

Effective nuclear charge is a model parameter that depends on electronic distribution and the transition being studied. For a multielectron atom, electrons can shield one another differently in different orbitals and states. The Kα approximation uses about one core electron's shielding; a different X-ray series can require a different empirical offset. Treating every σ as exactly one or as a literal count of nuclear particles would misread both the science and the mathematics.

This conceptual boundary matters for biology. A change from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ involves removal of an electron and can alter electronic energies and chemical function, but it does not change iron's Z=26. The identity of the nucleus is retained. One should distinguish 'the effective electronic potential experienced in a particular experiment' from 'the positive integer charge of the nucleus'.

25. The approximate formula earns credibility by predicting real energies

For iron, Z=26, so the simple screened model gives E_Kα≈10.2 eV ×25² = 6375 eV = 6.375 keV. A high-resolution reference gives Fe Kα₁ near 6.404 keV. The difference of about 0.029 keV is small on this scale, around 0.45% relative to the reference. The discrepancy is real and informative; it reflects the simplifications made in the model.

For copper, Z=29, and the predicted energy is 10.2×28² eV = 7996.8 eV, or about 7.997 keV. A reference Kα₁ line lies around 8.048 keV, again within about one percent of the simple result. The approximation captures the strong Z-dependence even though it cannot fully resolve electron interactions, relativistic splitting and other small contributions.

These comparisons provide the kind of theory–experiment feedback that makes an equation educational rather than decorative. Students should not silently copy a tabulated 8.048 keV value into a Bohr approximation and call it an exact prediction. Instead, state which is a model estimate, which is measured reference data, and what the difference tells us about the model's domain of validity.

26. Why Kα and Kβ cannot be treated as the same line

A Kα line involves an electron transition from a higher shell ordinarily described as n=2 into a K vacancy, whereas a common Kβ line is associated with a transition from a more distant state, often 3p-like, into the same K vacancy. The energy difference to n=1 is typically larger for the farther starting state, so a Kβ photon is usually more energetic and has shorter wavelength than the main Kα photon of the same element.

The simplified screened hydrogenic expression for an n=3→1 transition would contain (1−1/9), not (1−1/4). However, using the same screening σ for both lines would be an unjustified extra assumption: line-dependent interactions and screening complicate the estimate. Moseley observed separate straight-line families when corresponding lines were compared, not one universal line with interchangeable Greek labels.

If an examination provides two peaks and asks which is Kβ, it may be reasonable under stated conventional conditions to select the higher-energy characteristic K-series peak. If the spectrum contains mixed elements, detector artifacts or lines from different shells, that heuristic alone is insufficient. Correct identification requires attention to spectral context and calibrated energies.
