Periods, Groups and Electron Configurations

Build the table from quantum states and empirical chemistry: from hydrogen to the f block, with clear exceptions and original solved reasoning.

Learning outcomes: derive subshell and shell capacities; reconstruct actual period lengths, groups and blocks; use configurations to identify position; handle helium, hydrogen, d/f exceptions, and biology-related constraints; practise original NET/SET problems.

1. A chemical riddle that atomic number alone cannot solve

The previous learning nodes began with chemical evidence for recurring element families and then established the modern atomic-number order using nuclear-charge-related X-ray spectra. We now face the question those discoveries could not by themselves explain. Why should lithium and sodium behave in related ways even when they are separated by several elements? Why does an element with one more proton than neon, sodium, begin a very different chemical family rather than simply continuing neon's behaviour?

Imagine adding one proton and one electron every time we move to the next neutral element. The electron does not join a queue of identical particles crowded into one orbital. It enters a restricted set of quantum states, and those states may be arranged into shells and subshells. As a particular outer-electron pattern appears again, some chemical tendencies recur. A modern periodic table is therefore a compressed map of the sequence of electronically different atoms.

Our aim is to reconstruct that map from evidence and quantum-state arithmetic. We shall first derive why subshell regions have widths of two, six, ten and fourteen; then walk from hydrogen through the first full p and d periods; then confront the more complicated f region and the exceptions a simple diagram conceals. The result should be understanding rather than a memorized drawing.

2. Why the historical and quantum stories must remain separate

Döbereiner, Newlands, Meyer and Mendeleev recognized numerical or chemical patterns before quantum orbitals were known. Their early classifications were based on chemical compounds, relative atomic weights and comparisons of characteristic reactions. The recurring phenomenon was real long before its microscopic mechanism was understood. A student should not attribute a quantum explanation to an investigator who did not yet have its measurements or theoretical language.

Spectroscopy subsequently demonstrated discrete atomic energies; Rutherford's nuclear model identified a compact central positive charge; Bohr introduced quantized energy levels in hydrogen; wave mechanics described electron states by wavefunctions; Pauli established occupancy restrictions; and Hund's rules helped describe configurations with several comparable orbital energies. This is a chronological chain of discoveries, not one scientist suddenly drawing the final table.

The modern explanation uses these pieces together, but it is not a perfect mechanical algorithm for every atom. Interacting electrons have configuration-dependent energies; transition elements, lanthanoids and actinoids supply well-established counterexamples to a naive rigid filling sequence. Throughout the chapter we will distinguish an empirically observed ground-state configuration from a simplified prediction.

3. Protons define the element, electrons define its ordinary configuration

An atom has nuclear proton number Z. A neutral atom has exactly Z electrons, while a monatomic ion with signed charge number q has Z−q electrons. Sodium has Z=11 and eleven electrons when neutral; Na+ has ten. The element does not become neon merely because the cation happens to have the same number of electrons as a neutral neon atom. Its nucleus still contains eleven protons.

The total count is necessary but insufficient. Carbon has six electrons, yet writing the count does not tell us whether a 2s or a 2p orbital is occupied. We need to know the quantum states available to a many-electron atom, restrictions on those states and relative energetic preferences. Only then can we connect electron occupation to the row and column of the table.

This separation remains important in biology. Fe2+ and Fe3+ have different electron counts and can participate in different redox chemistry, while both remain iron nuclei with Z=26. Group classification concerns the element. A charge symbol describes a particular electronic species. A protein-bound metal may require an additional molecular electronic-state model.

4. An orbital is a state, not a little planet travelling on a circle

The Bohr model is a useful historical step, but a modern atomic orbital is not a literal circular electron track. It is a one-electron spatial state or wavefunction in a quantum model, associated with probability distributions and angular characteristics. A subshell contains a specified set of such states. The orbital-box diagrams familiar from chemistry are bookkeeping sketches of these quantum possibilities, not microscopic photographs.

In multi-electron atoms the independent-orbital approximation is useful but incomplete because electrons interact. We may write 3p4 to say that four electrons occupy a family of 3p orbitals in the chosen atomic configuration, but the label does not imply four separate p spatial orbitals. The p subshell has three; more than one electron can occupy a spatial orbital if the complete quantum states differ.

A good answer should preserve the domain of the model. Atomic configurations are essential for reconstructing periods and groups; they do not specify every molecular bond, ligand-field splitting or biochemical reaction. Later comparisons with biological ions will demonstrate exactly where additional evidence is required.

5. Principal quantum number: shells are allowed state families

The principal quantum number n takes values 1, 2, 3 and so on. In a hydrogen-like model it largely organizes energies; in a many-electron atom the relative energy depends on nuclear charge, shielding, electron-electron interactions and other corrections. Calling n a shell label is appropriate, but the shell must not be imagined as an absolutely rigid container with a fixed filling sequence.

For a chosen n, orbital angular momentum quantum number l may range from zero through n−1. At n=1 only l=0 is possible. At n=2, l=0 and 1 are possible. At n=3, l=0, 1 and 2 are possible. Thus 1p and 2d are forbidden orbital labels, whereas 3d is allowed and 4f is allowed because n=4 permits l=3.

The fact that 3d is mathematically permitted does not mean it must fill before 4s in the entire series of neutral atoms. Relative energies determine occupation. That distinction becomes the key to explaining why the third period contains eight elements even though its n=3 shell can in principle hold eighteen.

6. From l to s, p, d and f: the alphabet of the blocks

The spectroscopic letters s, p, d and f correspond respectively to l=0, 1, 2 and 3. A term such as 4p5 therefore means principal quantum number four, orbital angular momentum one and five electrons occupying the p subshell. A superscript does not count an element's protons or state its ionic charge. These are separate labels that must be interpreted in their correct contexts.

For each l, the magnetic quantum number m_l runs from −l to +l in integer steps. Thus the number of distinct spatial orbitals associated with the subshell is 2l+1: one for s, three for p, five for d and seven for f. This is a mathematical count of allowed angular-momentum states, not a claim that each orbital has a permanently fixed classical orientation in empty space.

To find a subshell's electron capacity we must combine these spatial orbitals with the two available spin projections. The resulting formula provides a direct bridge from quantum numbers to the familiar widths of parts of the periodic table.

7. Pauli supplies the factor of two

Electrons are spin-half fermions. In the conventional atomic-state description, spin projection m_s takes +1/2 or −1/2. The Pauli exclusion principle prohibits two identical electrons from occupying exactly the same complete one-electron quantum state. Each spatial orbital therefore accommodates at most two electrons with different spin projections.

Combine two spin states with 2l+1 spatial orbitals to obtain a maximum subshell occupancy of 2(2l+1). For s, l=0 gives two; for p, l=1 gives six; for d, l=2 gives ten; for f, l=3 gives fourteen. This calculation is a capacity limit, not a promise that every stable atom obeys the simplest Aufbau filling sequence.

Pauli tells us what is forbidden; energetics decides which allowed state is favoured. Hund's rules describe common arrangements among degenerate orbitals. Treating all three as the same rule leads to bad explanations of chromium and copper. In later examples, we will distinguish them explicitly.

8. Derive the block widths and distinguish them from period lengths

An s subshell accommodates 2 electrons, a p subshell 6, a d subshell 10 and an f subshell 14. In an idealized series of successive neutral elements, those capacities explain why regions of the familiar long-form table have two, six, ten and fourteen places. The pattern is not an unexplained choice of graphic designers: it is rooted in allowed quantum-state degeneracies.

These regions are not equivalent to four distinct period types. One period may involve an s and p region, producing 2+6=8 positions. A later period may also include ten d-region positions, producing 2+10+6=18. A still later period may incorporate fourteen f-region positions, making 2+14+10+6=32. Electron configurations of individual atoms may have exceptions while the broad block arrangement remains informative.

One must not claim that all s-type elements occupy the two leftmost boxes. Helium's ground configuration is 1s2 but it is placed with noble gases in group 18 because of its chemical behaviour. This is an early warning that subshell bookkeeping and chemical-family placement are related, not identical.

9. Why a complete shell has 2n² states

A complete principal shell n contains subshells with l values from zero through n−1. Summing their capacities gives Σ 2(2l+1) for those n allowed values. The sum of the first n odd integers is n squared, so multiplying by two yields 2n². We have thus derived the maximum electron count associated with a complete principal shell rather than importing the formula from a mnemonic.

For n=1 the capacity is 2, n=2 gives 8, n=3 gives 18 and n=4 gives 32. These numbers describe state capacities under the quantum-number model. They do not automatically equal the lengths of the first four periods of the actual long-form table, which are 2,8,8,18. The third shell's 3d states are populated after 4s begins the fourth-period sequence.

Consequently, when an examination asks for 'maximum electrons in the n=3 shell' the answer is 18; when it asks for 'number of elements in the third period' the answer is 8. Both are correct because they are answering different physical and classificatory questions.

10. Three labels describe three different dimensions of the table

A period is a horizontal row of elements. A group is a vertical column, numbered from 1 to 18 in IUPAC's recommended convention. A block is a region whose elements are broadly associated with building configurations involving s-, p-, d- or f-type subshells. These labels should never be used interchangeably. Potassium is in period four, group one and the s-block region; bromine is in period four, group seventeen and the p-block region.

A column often corresponds to repeated outer-electron patterns and similar broad chemistry, while a row tracks how accessible electronic states change as nuclear charge increases. The block hints at subshell participation. Yet each interpretation has exceptions, especially for d and f atoms and the placement of hydrogen and helium.

Older A and B Roman-numeral group labels differ among historical conventions and can introduce ambiguity. We will use numeric groups 1–18 and explicit family names. When a problem states only 'group VII' without defining its convention, a careful scientist asks which system is intended rather than inventing certainty.

11. Begin at hydrogen: why period one has only two elements

Neutral hydrogen has Z=1 and configuration 1s1. Its single electron occupies the only n=1 spatial orbital. Helium has Z=2 and ground configuration 1s2, filling that orbital with two opposite-spin electron states. Because no 1p orbital is allowed, the n=1 shell cannot accommodate a third electron in its 1s orbital. This explains the two-element first period.

Hydrogen is often placed in group one because it has a single outer s electron. Chemically it is not an ordinary alkali metal, however: hydrogen is a nonmetal with distinctive covalent bonding and acid–base chemistry. Helium, conversely, has an s2 configuration yet belongs with group-18 noble gases because its closed shell and low reactivity resemble their chemical family.

Both placements teach us to distinguish quantum orbital character from chemical classification. Helium's appearance at the far right does not create a fictitious 1p6 configuration. The science is stronger when we explicitly acknowledge these limits instead of forcing every family to follow a single printed rule.

12. Period two: building eight elements from two subshell capacities

After helium, lithium Z=3 has [He]2s1 and beryllium Z=4 has [He]2s2. Boron begins the 2p region with [He]2s2 2p1. Carbon is 2p2, nitrogen 2p3, oxygen 2p4, fluorine 2p5 and neon 2p6 with the same [He]2s2 core. This sequence contains two s-region positions followed by six p-region positions, hence eight elements in the second period.

Check the electron inventory rather than relying on the names. Nitrogen has Z=7 and configuration [He]2s2 2p3, so 2+2+3=7 electrons. Oxygen has 2+2+4=8. Neon ends the period at Z=10, with 2+2+6=10. If a solution writes neutral carbon as 2p3, its total is seven electrons and the species has been misidentified.

These configurations begin to reveal family logic. Lithium has one outer s electron, fluorine has an ns2np5 outer arrangement, and neon has the closed ns2np6 pattern. Their observable chemistry does not follow only from a superscript, but the recurring outer occupation makes important similarities and contrasts understandable.

13. A p subshell has three orbitals, but it can hold six electrons

The 2p region of the second period offers a chance to apply Hund's rule, not merely write total occupancies. A p subshell has three spatial orbitals corresponding to the three m_l values. When they are approximately degenerate in a free atom, electrons occupy them singly with parallel spin before pairing. Thus p1, p2 and p3 have respectively one, two and three singly occupied orbitals. At p4, pairing begins, giving two singly occupied boxes; p5 gives one and p6 none.

The sequence matters for magnetic behaviour, yet it is not a licence to transfer atomic arrows unchanged into molecules. A free neutral nitrogen atom is p3, while N2 is a molecule with very different bonding and electronic states. Oxygen's free atom has a p4 arrangement, but O2 requires molecular orbital theory for its observed paramagnetism. This is the first place the distinction between periodic atomic bookkeeping and chemical compounds becomes essential.

For table-position questions, the number of electrons occupying np determines the position within the p region. The exact choice of which of three equivalent orbital boxes first acquires an extra electron is not the basis of a different element or group.

14. Period three repeats eight places but does not fill every n=3 state

After neon, sodium begins period three with [Ne]3s1 and magnesium reaches [Ne]3s2. Aluminium starts the p part with [Ne]3s2 3p1. Silicon, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon successively have 3p2, 3p3, 3p4, 3p5 and 3p6. The chronological row is Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, yielding two s positions and six p positions.

At this point a serious learner asks why the 3d orbitals are absent even though quantum numbers allow them for n=3. The explanation is the many-electron ordering of atomic energies. Argon completes the 3s and 3p part of the arrangement. The next neutral element, potassium, occupies 4s instead of beginning a sustained 3d occupation. The broad observed sequence is therefore 3s, 3p, then 4s before the first 3d region.

The third-period length of eight must not be confused with the complete n=3 shell capacity of eighteen. Shell capacity counts allowed states; period length counts actual successive elements in a chemically meaningful ground-state sequence. They are connected but not equal quantities.

15. Why the phrase '4s fills before 3d' needs careful wording

In the conventional introduction, the next electron after the argon core is placed into 4s for potassium and a second into 4s for calcium. Scandium then begins the 3d occupation. For the specified isolated neutral ground states, this is an effective description of the observed sequence. It explains why the first central d region appears in period four rather than period three.

However, the phrase must not be promoted to an immutable inequality saying 4s is lower in energy than 3d for every atom, ion, configuration and molecule. Orbital energies depend on the identity and occupancy of the species. When many transition metals are ionized, electrons associated with the 4s orbital are normally removed before 3d electrons. The states also rearrange as the atom becomes charged.

For CSIR-NET reasoning, the safest chain is: identify the species, use the experimentally supported ground-state configuration, then apply an appropriate ionization or bonding model. Avoid copying a simple diagonal-arrow chart as though it were an exact many-electron theorem.

16. A first view of the fourth period: two, ten and six

Potassium [Ar]4s1 and calcium [Ar]4s2 occupy the first two positions in period four. Next come the ten elements scandium through zinc in the conventional d-region columns, broadly associated with the 3d subshell. Gallium through krypton then occupy the six p-region positions with outer 4p1 through 4p6 patterns. The period therefore contains 2+10+6=18 elements.

This order makes the geometry of the table intelligible. The d region is inserted between s and p because its subshell has five spatial orbitals capable of ten electron states. The p block resumes after that insertion rather than after an imagined full occupation of the entire n=4 shell.

Check the boundaries: K Z19 begins; Ca Z20 ends the s region; Sc Z21 starts the d region; Zn Z30 ends the conventional d region; Ga Z31 starts the p region; Kr Z36 closes the period. These exact atomic numbers make a useful independent check on any sketch.

17. Scandium, titanium and vanadium build the first 3d steps

Scandium Z21 has the neutral ground configuration [Ar]3d1 4s2, titanium Z22 has [Ar]3d2 4s2, and vanadium Z23 has [Ar]3d3 4s2. These three illustrate the simple expectation: after the argon core and occupied 4s region, the 3d count increases across the transition part of period four.

Yet one must distinguish an atom's total electron count from a rigid rule about which orbital receives the last electron. Scandium has 18+1+2=21 electrons; vanadium 18+3+2=23. These checks identify the right neutral species. They do not prove that every later 3d element must keep exactly two 4s electrons.

The five spatial d orbitals support up to ten electrons by Pauli's principle. Their filling under nearly comparable energies helps explain diverse magnetic and bonding possibilities, but metal ions in proteins experience ligand fields and cannot be read as simple isolated neutral atom boxes.

18. Chromium breaks the naive order without breaking the table

A simple filling algorithm might predict chromium Z24 as [Ar]3d4 4s2. The experimentally accepted isolated neutral ground configuration is instead [Ar]3d5 4s1. The electron inventory still totals 18+5+1=24; an electron is distributed differently between near-lying subshells because the full many-electron energy balance favours that arrangement.

It is tempting to claim the entire cause is that a half-filled d subshell is always more stable. Exchange interactions and correlated energies are relevant, but the phrase is too universal; later d-series exceptions demonstrate that no single half-full or full-full slogan predicts all configurations correctly. A reliable student learns the actual chromium case and understands why the simplistic prediction was an approximation.

Chromium remains in period four and group six. Its classification does not depend on pretending its ground configuration is the naive one. In a sophisticated answer, a pattern can be robust even when its simple one-electron implementation has exceptions.

19. Iron, cobalt and nickel: a connected series after the exceptions

Manganese Z25 has [Ar]3d5 4s2. Iron Z26 has [Ar]3d6 4s2, cobalt Z27 has [Ar]3d7 4s2 and nickel Z28 has [Ar]3d8 4s2. In this region the d occupancy progresses in a straightforward way, and a neutral-atom electron inventory can be verified by adding the argon core of eighteen electrons.

For iron, 18+6+2=26; for cobalt, 18+7+2=27. These counts are especially useful in life sciences because iron and cobalt appear in metalloproteins, but the ground configurations of isolated atoms should not be treated as a full description of their oxidation states in those proteins. A protein's ligands and redox environment may redistribute electron density and spin states.

Group eight for iron reflects the conventional table column and the broad sum of d and s electrons in this atom, not a claim that biologically stable iron always has a +8 oxidation state. Confusing group number, oxidation state and number of valence electrons is a frequent examination trap.

20. Copper and zinc finish the central region with another warning

Copper Z29 is [Ar]3d10 4s1 in its accepted neutral ground state rather than the naive [Ar]3d9 4s2. Zinc Z30 is [Ar]3d10 4s2. Both electron totals are correct, and the d subshell is filled in both neutral atoms. These cases show why the labels of periodic regions should not be read as mechanical instructions for the last electron of every element.

Copper frequently forms Cu+ and Cu2+ in chemical systems. Neutral Cu has filled 3d10, but Cu2+ has a 3d9 occupation in the usual monatomic ionic approximation, illustrating how ionization changes the electronic state without changing Z29. Zinc's common Zn2+ species has a filled 3d10 shell. Their biochemical roles can therefore differ sharply despite their neighbouring positions.

The d region has ten columns because of the capacity of d states. That remains meaningful even though individual ground-state configurations and common ionic chemistry demand careful exceptions. A periodic block is an organizational regularity, not a promise of identical oxidation or magnetic behaviour.

21. What does 'transition element' mean compared with d-block element?

The familiar central ten columns are usually called the d block, groups 3–12. Strict chemical definitions of transition elements refer to elements with an incomplete d subshell in the atom or in one of their characteristic cations. These two terms are closely connected but are not always identical classifications.

Copper is a useful example. Although the isolated neutral Cu atom has 3d10, Cu2+ has an incomplete 3d9 subshell, so copper satisfies the usual transition-element criterion through its cation. In contrast, zinc has d10 in the neutral atom and in its common Zn2+ species, and is often excluded from the strict transition-element category while remaining within the conventional d-block columns.

Scandium's neutral atom is 3d1 even though its common Sc3+ cation is d0. Therefore one should not decide 'transition element' only from the configuration of a single ion. Different textbooks sometimes use a broader conversational definition; when precision matters, state the criterion being used.

22. Gallium to krypton: the p region completes an eighteen-element period

After zinc, gallium Z31 has [Ar]3d10 4s2 4p1. Germanium Z32 adds 4p2, arsenic Z33 adds 4p3, selenium Z34 has 4p4, bromine Z35 has 4p5 and krypton Z36 completes 4p6. Each step raises total electron number by one and occupies one of the six positions of the period-four p block.

Gallium's [Ar]3d10 core must not be omitted from electron counting, but its simple main-group valence pattern is 4s2 4p1, not 'thirteen electrons all equally available for bonding'. Selenium's typical outer pattern is 4s2 4p4 and places it in group sixteen. The filled inner 3d subshell belongs to the electron inventory while its chemical role depends on the context.

Period four is now complete: K–Ca two s positions, Sc–Zn ten d positions and Ga–Kr six p positions. Unlike a memorized sequence, this construction also tells you what to do when a question gives an unfamiliar period-four configuration.

23. Modern group numbers emerge from repeating outer patterns

Lithium [He]2s1, sodium [Ne]3s1 and potassium [Ar]4s1 share the outer ns1 pattern and occupy group one. Beryllium 2s2, magnesium 3s2 and calcium 4s2 form the group-two pattern. Halogens fluorine, chlorine and bromine have ns2 np5 outer shells, while neon, argon and krypton have ns2 np6 valence shells.

This explains why families recur as atomic number increases without requiring every chemical property to be identical. Differences in nuclear attraction, shielding, orbital size and bonding conditions produce meaningful changes within families. The shared pattern supplies a starting point for prediction, not an exact numerical identity or a universal biological substitution rule.

The group number convention is 1–18. A chemical family is not merely a vertical line drawn on a page; it groups atoms whose electrons have broadly comparable valence-state patterns and whose characteristic compound chemistry often reflects that relationship.

24. Group 13–18 and the useful ns2 npk formula

For ordinary p-block main-group neutral atoms, the outer electron configuration is often ns2 npk, where k is one through six. The corresponding group number is 12+k. Thus group 13 corresponds broadly to np1, group 14 np2, group 15 np3, group 16 np4, group 17 np5 and group 18 np6. Counting the outer s2 and p electrons gives three through eight main-group valence electrons.

The formula has a very clear domain. Helium belongs to group eighteen chemically but has 1s2 rather than ns2 np6. Heavier atoms may also have occupied inner d or f subshells that must be included in the full electron inventory. In molecular and condensed matter chemistry, effective valence and oxidation-state behaviour is more complex than one superscript formula.

Consider selenium, period four group sixteen. Its shorthand ground state is [Ar]3d10 4s2 4p4. The total electrons are 18+10+2+4=34, matching Z34. Its np exponent four corresponds to group 16, while n=4 for the outer s/p shell matches its period. This is the kind of transparent inference students should learn to make.
