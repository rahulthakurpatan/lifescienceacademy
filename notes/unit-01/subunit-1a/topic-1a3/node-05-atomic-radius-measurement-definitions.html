What Is Atomic Radius? Measurement and Definitions

From a blurred quantum cloud to a measurable bond distance: the scientific history, operational definitions, experimental limits and biological meaning of atomic size.

Learning outcomes: explain why atoms lack hard edges; distinguish radial probability from an empirical radius; derive covalent, metallic and van der Waals radius conventions; understand what X-ray, electron and neutron diffraction actually measure; calculate distances with units and uncertainties; distinguish ions, oxidation and coordination states; evaluate crystallographic and biomolecular examples without confusing radius conventions.

1. The surprising question: how can an atom have a radius if electrons are clouds?

Our previous Node 1.A.3.04 explained why an electron's attraction to the nucleus changes with electronic screening and orbital penetration. That explanation already raises a new question. If an electron is not a tiny planet moving along a fixed circular orbit, where exactly does the atom end? Textbook tables often report a radius in picometres, but a wavefunction does not stop abruptly at the number printed in that table.

Imagine a carbon atom in diamond, a carbon atom within carbon dioxide, and an isolated neutral carbon atom in the gas phase. They share six protons but interact with very different surroundings. Their electrons occupy states affected by bonding, and a number inferred from one structure need not be identical to a number from another. A useful radius therefore must be defined together with the physical context and method of inference.

The learning goal is to turn the vague phrase 'size of an atom' into a scientific measurement question. We will follow the history of chemical distances, derive operational covalent, metallic and nonbonded radii, inspect diffraction and molecular spectroscopy, examine measurement uncertainty and apply the idea to authentic biomolecular structures. We will not simply present a periodic-trend arrow.

2. Early chemists saw combining proportions, not tiny atomic edges

Dalton's atomic theory used patterns in the masses of reacting substances to organize chemical composition. Relative atomic masses and stoichiometric formulas could be investigated long before individual atoms were imaged. Knowing that hydrogen and oxygen combine in fixed proportions does not reveal the distance between the centres of their nuclei in a water molecule. Atomic identity and geometric size are different experimental questions.

Later developments in gas laws, molecular chemistry and stereochemistry motivated geometrical models of molecules. Scientists could reason that bonds have lengths and angles without assuming atoms are perfectly rigid spheres. The familiar coloured ball-and-stick model is a teaching representation of relations between atoms, not a photograph of spherical surfaces.

A chronological teaching story should preserve this distinction. Chemical observations established patterns; physical and structural techniques later determined distances and electron distributions; empirical radius scales then summarized selected types of contact. A tabulated radius is a scientific inference from those observations under a chosen convention.

3. Why a nuclear radius and an atomic radius differ enormously

The positive nucleus is spatially compact compared with the electron distribution. A typical nuclear length scale is femtometres, whereas many chemical bond lengths and atom-radius conventions are in tens or hundreds of picometres. One femtometre is 10⁻¹⁵ metre; one picometre is 10⁻¹² metre. Therefore one picometre equals one thousand femtometres.

It is a serious mistake to call the nuclear radius the atomic radius merely because both are distances from a centre. The atom's chemical interactions involve electron distributions spread over a far larger region, while the nucleus determines charge and most of the mass. Different experiments address the different scales.

For example, 100 pm is 100,000 fm. This comparison is an order-of-magnitude exercise, not a claim that every atomic radius is 100 pm or that every nucleus has exactly the same radius. It helps students appreciate why a nucleus can be effectively point-like in introductory chemistry even though nuclear structure is real.

4. The Born probability interpretation removes the rigid shell boundary

Quantum mechanics describes a one-electron orbital with a wavefunction ψ. The probability density of observing the electron at a particular location is proportional to |ψ|² after normalization. For a bound state, the density typically becomes small far from the nucleus yet does not end at a perfectly sharp surface. There is no universal outer circle at which all electron probability suddenly becomes zero.

A sphere enclosing 90% of a selected orbital's probability could be assigned a mathematically defined radius. Another researcher could use 95% or 99%, producing a different number for the same wavefunction. Such quantiles can be rigorously defined when the probability distribution and convention are specified, but none is automatically identical to an experimental covalent or van der Waals radius.

The scientific habit is to ask 'radius of what distribution, enclosing what fraction, in what state?' rather than assuming a hard boundary. The absence of a sharp quantum edge is not a reason to abandon useful radius measurements; it is the reason we must give their operational definitions.

5. Radial probability is not a map of rigid electron shells

For an atomic orbital separated into radial and angular functions, radial probability may be written P(r)=r²|R_nl(r)|² when angular functions are normalized separately. Then P(r)dr gives the probability of finding an electron within a spherical shell of thickness dr around the nucleus. The word shell in this integral describes a volume element, not a solid planetary orbit.

For the ideal hydrogen 1s state, radial probability is proportional to r² exp(−2r/a₀). It begins at zero at r=0 because a spherical shell of zero radius has zero volume, reaches a maximum at r=a₀, then decays smoothly. The three-dimensional 1s probability density itself is nonzero at the nucleus, so radial probability and spatial density must not be confused.

The original diagram makes this contrast visible. The vertical probability curve peaks at one characteristic scale and has a long tail, while the shaded 'cloud' is a conceptual depiction. Its shaded circles are not measured boundaries. They help learners understand why radius conventions must be tied to a particular physical definition.

6. The Bohr radius is a length constant, not every atom's measured size

In the simple nonrelativistic hydrogenic model, the characteristic Bohr length a₀ is about 52.9177 picometres. The most probable radius of the hydrogen 1s radial distribution is a₀. This fact is historically important because it connects a quantum-mechanical state with an intuitively understandable length scale.

However, the average distance of an electron from the nucleus in hydrogen 1s is 3a₀/2, about 79.38 pm, and is not the same as the most probable radial distance. The root-mean-square radius is √3a₀, about 91.66 pm. All three are reasonable statistical descriptors of the same ideal state, and none should automatically be substituted for a covalent radius derived from molecular bond lengths.

To interpret a textbook number, ask what was calculated: most probable position, expectation of r, rms radius, a chosen charge-density contour or half a measured internuclear contact. All can be stated in picometres yet refer to different physical questions.

7. The radius of a probability contour depends on the chosen cutoff

Suppose we choose a spherical surface enclosing a specified fraction p of a hydrogen 1s electron's total probability. The mathematical task is to integrate the radial distribution from zero to radius R and set the resulting cumulative probability equal to p. One obtains a definite R for any specified p between zero and one. Changing p changes R.

For the normalized hydrogen 1s orbital, the cumulative probability inside R is F(R)=1−exp(−2R/a₀)[1+2R/a₀+2(R/a₀)²]. This is not the same as the simple exponential point density and is not a physical wall. At R=a₀, the enclosed probability is only approximately 32.3%, despite a₀ being the peak of the radial distribution.

The example is conceptually powerful because it shows that one ordinary atom can have several mathematically defensible radii. A published empirical radius usually instead summarizes interatomic distances in a stated bonding environment. The difference between statistical spatial extent and structural half-distance should stay explicit throughout the chapter.

8. From 'atom size' to an operational definition

An operational definition states how a quantity is obtained from an observation. Rather than saying 'carbon's radius is the distance to the last electron', we may say 'a covalent radius is inferred from internuclear distances in specified bonded structures, using a declared bond-order and calibration scheme'. The second statement can be tested and compared with measurements.

For identical atoms bonded together, assigning half the measured bond distance to each is natural because the two atoms have the same identity in a symmetric homonuclear bond. For unlike atoms, decomposing the total distance into two element-specific radii generally requires an empirical additivity model and calibration across many compounds. The measurement of bond distance does not directly reveal a unique boundary belonging to each atom.

The same principle applies to metallic and nonbonded contacts. We can infer a radius from a nearest-neighbour separation or a distribution of nonbonded distances, but the inferred number belongs to that convention. A learner should be able to state the method before reporting a value.

9. Three radius families answer three different structural questions

A covalent radius summarizes the typical separation of nuclear centres across a covalent bond, usually expressed through half a homonuclear bond length or an empirically fitted bond-radius scale. A metallic radius summarizes near-neighbour distances in a pure metal under specified conditions. A van der Waals radius summarizes a characteristic nonbonded contact scale in structures or gas-phase interactions.

Each is useful because chemical interactions produce characteristic geometric separations. Yet the resulting values are not interchangeable. A bonded chlorine pair may have a much shorter separation than two nonbonded chlorine atoms in a molecular packing arrangement. Using the van der Waals radius to predict a chlorine–chlorine single-bond length would confuse two different interaction types.

Throughout the chapter we will derive each rule step by step and identify what experiment constrains it. The equations look simple, but scientific care lies in selecting an appropriate type of contact and refusing to treat fitted radii as exact rigid spheres.

10. Why all three conventions are often displayed on one periodic table

Visual summaries of chemical data often place one radius number in each elemental box, because a single number is easy to scan. Such convenience can conceal the most important experimental qualifier. A table may contain covalent radii, empirical atomic radii, metallic radii or van der Waals radii, and the same element can have quite different entries depending on the source.

Students comparing numerical trends across different websites sometimes accidentally mix a covalent carbon value, a metallic sodium value and a van der Waals argon value into one line. The resulting graph is not a controlled comparison of one physical quantity. Different conventions cannot be assumed to measure the same boundary.

The solution is not to ban cross-classification comparisons. It is to label the property, source, units, state and conditions. A scientific graph with explicit mixed categories can illustrate differences among conventions, but it must not be misdescribed as an exact trend in a universal atomic radius.

11. The picometre and ångström make chemical distances readable

Atomic separations are inconveniently small in ordinary metres. One picometre (pm) equals 10⁻¹² m. One ångström (Å) equals 10⁻¹⁰ m, so 1 Å equals 100 pm and 1 pm equals 0.01 Å. A nanometre equals 1000 pm or 10 Å. All are length units, and a conversion is valid regardless of the chosen radius convention.

For example, a hypothetical internuclear distance of 1.54 Å equals 154 pm. Dividing by two gives 77 pm, if and only if the geometry supports the chosen half-distance radius interpretation. Converting units does not establish that this is a universal carbon radius, nor does the number alone identify bond order.

CSIR-NET questions often exploit confusing Å and nm or using 10 rather than 100 in a conversion. Write the unit factors before arithmetic: 1.54 Å ×100 pm/Å =154 pm; 0.154 nm is the same distance. Precision and definition must be checked separately.

12. The shortest distance between nuclei is not always a 'bond'

A crystallographic model may report that two atoms lie 3.4 Å apart. The numerical distance alone does not prove a covalent bond or identify a nonbonded interaction. Covalent bonding depends on electronic structure, connectivity and chemical environment, while weak contacts can occur in packed structures without a shared-electron bond.

A structural database contains both bonded and nonbonded atom pairs, and the categories require chemical rules plus geometric context. If one selects all shortest contacts without distinguishing interactions, a derived radius scale may mix incompatible phenomena. A nonbonded van der Waals contact and a covalent C–C link are not two measurements of the same physical radius definition.

This distinction also matters in proteins, where neighbouring residues may be close because of packing rather than covalent connectivity. A scientifically sound use of radii begins by identifying the type of interaction associated with the selected distance.

13. How two nuclei become coordinates in an experimental structure

Diffraction methods do not place little rulers between individual atoms. A structural experiment measures a pattern of scattered radiation as a sample is illuminated, and the pattern constrains the arrangement of scattering centres. Researchers refine a model of atomic positions that is consistent with the diffraction data and physical chemistry.

Once two atomic positions are defined in a coordinate system, their separation can be computed geometrically. For Cartesian coordinates in ångströms, the distance between positions (x₁,y₁,z₁) and (x₂,y₂,z₂) is the square root of (x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²+(z₂−z₁)². This is a Euclidean distance between modelled centres, not a radius by itself.

With fractional crystallographic coordinates, the unit-cell geometry must also be accounted for, especially in nonorthogonal cells. Periodic images and crystal symmetry can change which atomic positions count as nearest neighbours. Therefore the radius derivation follows structure solution and model interpretation, not a single detector peak.

14. The first lesson summary: a radius is a defined inference

We have moved from atomic theory to quantum probability and then to experimentally constrained geometry. The nucleus has an identifiable centre, but the electron distribution has a tail rather than a hard surface. The same atom can have different statistical length descriptors and different empirically inferred bonded or nonbonded radii.

The key scientific achievement is understanding what a radius table is actually summarizing. An atom is not a rubber ball whose exact outermost point is photographed. A radius is selected from a measurement or model under an explicitly stated convention, and the answer becomes meaningful only when that convention is known.

The next part of the story starts with the simplest structural case: a molecule containing two identical atoms linked by a covalent bond. From their measured centre-to-centre distance we will derive the covalent radius, then investigate why bond order, molecular environment and empirical datasets matter.
