1.A.1.01.1 Why atoms matter: start with familiar objects

A glass of water, a leaf, a pencil, and a human cell look very different. Yet all of them are made from matter, and matter is built from atoms. The same basic building blocks appear in both living and non-living things. Life does not need a special type of atom; it depends on the way ordinary atoms are arranged and connected.

Imagine cutting a pure piece of copper into smaller and smaller pieces. A large fragment and a tiny fragment are still copper. Eventually, at the scale of single particles, we reach individual copper atoms. One copper atom is still an atom of the element copper. Cutting it into its constituent particles would no longer leave an intact copper atom. The important idea is not that an atom cannot be divided: it can be divided by physical or nuclear processes. Rather, it is the smallest unit that still identifies a particular chemical element.

Central idea: Atoms are the basic chemical units from which molecules, cells, tissues, and organisms are built. Biology becomes much easier when this chemical foundation is understood.

1.A.1.01.1.1 Matter, a simple starting point

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Water, gases in the air, rocks, proteins, and DNA are examples. Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation, not matter in the ordinary chemical sense. In everyday conditions, matter occurs as solids, liquids, gases, and other states under suitable conditions. Changes of state do not automatically change the identity of the atoms present.

A block of ice melts into liquid water and then may evaporate into water vapor. Throughout these physical changes, the water molecules remain H₂O. This is different from a chemical reaction, in which atoms are rearranged into different substances.

1.A.1.01.1.2 Why this idea belongs in life science

A cell membrane, enzyme, chromosome, and muscle fiber all contain atoms of particular elements. Their biological functions arise from atomic properties, bonding patterns, molecular shapes, and interactions. Understanding atoms is therefore the first step toward understanding why water dissolves salts, how proteins fold, how DNA stores information, and how metabolic reactions release or store energy.

1.A.1.01.2 Clear definitions: atom, element, molecule, compound

An atom is the smallest particle that still characterizes a chemical element. A chemical element is defined by the number of protons in its atomic nucleus. All atoms with six protons are carbon atoms, regardless of how many neutrons they contain or whether they have gained or lost electrons.

A molecule is a group of two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds as an identifiable unit. A compound contains atoms of at least two different elements chemically combined in fixed proportions for a given pure compound. These terms overlap, but they are not identical.

1.A.1.01.2.1 Three quick comparisons

O versus O₂: O represents one oxygen atom; O₂ represents a molecule containing two oxygen atoms. Both contain only oxygen, so they are not compounds of different elements.

H₂O: Each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Water is both a molecular substance and a compound.

NaCl: Ordinary solid sodium chloride is an extended ionic lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, not a collection of separate NaCl molecules. The formula NaCl states the simplest 1:1 ratio of ions in the solid.

A frequent mistake: “Every compound consists of molecules.” This is false. Many ionic compounds form extended structures rather than individual molecules.

1.A.1.01.3 How small is an atom?

Atomic dimensions are usually measured in nanometers (nm) or picometers (pm). One nanometer is 10⁻⁹ meter; one picometer is 10⁻¹² meter. A typical atomic diameter is on the order of 0.1–0.3 nm, although the effective size depends on the element and on how the boundary is defined. Unlike a marble, an atom has no perfectly sharp outer surface.

For comparison, a typical animal cell is roughly 10–30 micrometers across, though real cells vary considerably. One micrometer is 10⁻⁶ meter. A 10-µm cell is approximately 100,000 times wider than a 0.1-nm atomic diameter. This ratio is only an order-of-magnitude illustration, not a fixed rule for all cells and atoms.

Unit ladder: 1 m = 10³ mm = 10⁶ µm = 10⁹ nm = 10¹² pm. To convert nm into pm, multiply by 1,000. To convert µm into nm, multiply by 1,000.

1.A.1.01.3.1 Worked size comparison

Question: If a cell is 20 µm across and an illustrative atom is 0.2 nm across, approximately how many atomic diameters fit across the cell?

Step 1. Convert 20 µm to nanometers: 20 × 1,000 = 20,000 nm.

Step 2. Divide by 0.2 nm: 20,000 ÷ 0.2 = 100,000.

Answer: About 10⁵ atomic diameters. This is a size comparison only: atoms do not pack into cells as a simple straight row, and most biological structures include substantial intervening space.

1.A.1.01.3.2 Atom versus nucleus

The nucleus is far smaller than the atom. Nuclear radii are typically on the order of femtometers (10⁻¹⁵ m), while atomic dimensions are on the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m. These lengths differ by roughly four to five orders of magnitude. That does not mean electrons inhabit fixed circular tracks between those boundaries. It means the mass-rich central nucleus occupies a tiny region compared with the broader electron probability distribution.

1.A.1.01.4 The basic architecture: nucleus and electron region

An atom has two main structural regions. At the center is a very small and dense nucleus, containing protons and, in most atoms, neutrons. Around it is the much larger spatial region associated with electrons. Nearly all of an ordinary atom’s mass is concentrated in its nucleus, because a proton or neutron is much heavier than an electron.

Protons have a positive charge, electrons have a negative charge, and neutrons have no net electric charge. In a neutral atom, the numbers of protons and electrons are equal, so their total charges cancel. The atom’s structure is held together by interactions governed by fundamental forces: the electrostatic attraction between electrons and the positively charged nucleus is central to atomic electronic structure, while nuclear forces help bind nucleons in the nucleus.

Figure 1.A.1.01-F2. Conceptual atom, not to scale. The shaded region suggests electron probability; it is not a solid shell or a defined boundary. Colored nuclear spheres are symbolic and do not represent actual particle shapes.

1.A.1.01.4.1 Why the nucleus is heavy but small

Protons and neutrons each have a mass close to one unified atomic mass unit (u), while an electron has a mass roughly 1/1836 of a proton. Even with many electrons present, the nuclear particles account for almost all the mass of an ordinary atom. The atom’s overall size, by contrast, is determined by the spatial extent of its electron distribution.

1.A.1.01.4.2 Why opposite charges matter

Unlike charges attract. That attraction contributes to keeping electrons associated with the nucleus. However, a modern atom cannot be understood as a tiny solar system in which electrons circle the nucleus along perfectly defined paths. The electron is a quantum object, described by states and probabilities. Detailed historical models are treated in Nodes 02–08.

1.A.1.01.5 The three familiar subatomic particles

For introductory chemistry and life science, the three particles that matter most are the proton (p⁺), neutron (n), and electron (e⁻). They have different charges, masses, and roles. Protons determine elemental identity. Neutrons influence the isotope and contribute to nuclear stability. Electrons are essential for chemical behavior, including bonding and many interactions within cells.

1.A.1.01.5.1 Does every atom contain a neutron?

No. The common hydrogen isotope protium (hydrogen-1) has one proton and no neutron in its nucleus. It usually has one electron when neutral. This is a useful exception to remember whenever a definition casually says that every atom contains all three particles.

1.A.1.01.5.2 Is an electron just a tiny negative ball?

“Tiny ball” is sometimes useful as an introductory picture, but it becomes misleading if treated as a literal model. Electrons exhibit quantum behavior, including wave-like effects. Their position cannot be specified as a stable track in the way we describe a planet’s orbit. For most chemical questions, we work with electron states, energy levels, and probabilities.

Remember: Proton number determines element; electron number determines net charge; neutron number helps distinguish isotopes. These three statements solve a surprisingly large number of introductory questions.

1.A.1.01.6 Elemental identity and the periodic table

Each chemical element has a unique atomic number, Z, equal to its number of protons. Hydrogen has Z = 1; carbon has Z = 6; nitrogen has Z = 7; oxygen has Z = 8; phosphorus has Z = 15; and sulfur has Z = 16. These values are not labels chosen at random. They directly describe the constitution of the nucleus.

If a nucleus has six protons, its element is carbon. Adding one neutron changes the isotope, but not the element. Removing one electron makes a carbon ion, but not a different element. Changing the number of protons changes elemental identity; such changes involve nuclear processes, not normal chemical bonding.

1.A.1.01.6.1 Why do elements behave differently?

Different nuclear charges affect the arrangement and energy of electrons. Their electron configurations, particularly their outer electrons, help explain differences in bonding and reactivity. For example, carbon can form multiple stable covalent bonds and is especially useful in constructing a diverse range of organic structures. Sodium more readily forms Na⁺ under common chemical conditions. The complete rules of electron configuration and periodic trends will be explored in later topics, not assumed here.

1.A.1.01.7 Atomic number, mass number, and a first calculation

An atom’s mass number, A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus. The fundamental counting relationship is:

A = Z + N
Number of neutrons: N = A − Z

Here Z is atomic number (proton count) and N is neutron count. A is a whole-number count, not a measured atomic mass in grams or atomic mass units. The mass of a specific atom is close to, but generally not exactly equal to, A unified atomic mass units because of particle masses and nuclear binding energy.

1.A.1.01.7.1 Worked example: oxygen-16

Oxygen has Z = 8. Oxygen-16 has A = 16. Its nucleus therefore contains 8 protons and 16 − 8 = 8 neutrons. A neutral oxygen-16 atom has 8 electrons. If it gains two electrons to become O²⁻, it still has 8 protons and 8 neutrons, but now has 10 electrons.

1.A.1.01.7.2 Worked example: carbon-14

Carbon has six protons. Carbon-14 has A = 14, so its nucleus contains 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons. Both are carbon because both have 6 protons. Their nuclear behavior differs; carbon-14 is radioactive, while carbon-12 is stable.

Exam habit: Always identify Z first. Do not try to deduce the element from A alone. Two different elements can have atoms with the same mass number.

1.A.1.01.8 Atoms, ions, and the meaning of electrical charge

An atom is electrically neutral if it has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Because the proton and electron charges are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign, the charges cancel. A particle with more protons than electrons has a positive net charge; one with more electrons has a negative net charge.

When an atom loses one or more electrons, it becomes a positively charged ion called a cation. When it gains electrons, it becomes a negatively charged ion called an anion. Ordinary chemical ion formation involves electron transfer or redistribution, not the creation or destruction of protons.

1.A.1.01.8.1 Simple charge-count rule

Net ionic charge (in e units) = number of protons − number of electrons

Example: 12 protons and 10 electrons give +2. The particle is Mg²⁺ if its proton number is 12. Example: 9 protons and 10 electrons give −1, corresponding to F⁻.

1.A.1.01.8.2 Biological significance

Ions are vital inside living systems. Na⁺ and K⁺ contribute to membrane potentials; Ca²⁺ participates in cell signaling and contraction; Cl⁻ helps control electrical and osmotic balance; Mg²⁺ is involved in many enzyme reactions and interactions with ATP. Their behavior depends on charge, hydration, size, binding partners, and cellular transport systems, not merely on having an electric charge.

1.A.1.01.9 Isotopes: the same element with different neutrons

Isotopes are atoms of the same element whose nuclei have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Because their proton number is unchanged, they occupy the same position in the periodic table. Different neutron counts produce different mass numbers and can affect nuclear stability and certain physical properties.

Consider hydrogen. Protium (hydrogen-1) has one proton and zero neutrons. Deuterium (hydrogen-2) has one proton and one neutron. Tritium (hydrogen-3) has one proton and two neutrons; tritium is radioactive. All three are isotopes of hydrogen. They are not three different elements.

Isotopes often share similar chemical behavior because their electronic structures are similar, but they are not always chemically identical. Differences in mass can alter reaction rates and physical properties. The hydrogen–deuterium isotope effect is an important example. This qualification matters in biochemical tracing experiments.

1.A.1.01.9.1 Stable and radioactive isotopes

A stable isotope does not undergo spontaneous radioactive decay on relevant observable timescales. A radioactive isotope has an unstable nucleus and can transform by radioactive decay. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable; carbon-14 is radioactive. Radioisotopes can be valuable tracers when used with appropriate safeguards and experimental controls.

1.A.1.01.9.2 Notation preview

Scientists often write an isotope as ¹⁴₆C: the upper-left number 14 is A, and the lower-left number 6 is Z. The full treatment of nuclide notation, atomic mass, abundance, and radioactive half-life belongs to Nodes 15–27. The purpose here is to make their basic meaning immediately clear.

1.A.1.01.10 Atomic mass is not the same as mass number

The mass number is a count of nucleons. The mass of an atom is a physical quantity. These are connected but distinct concepts. Atomic masses are conveniently expressed using the unified atomic mass unit (u), defined as one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom in its ground state at rest. One u is approximately 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

The atomic weight shown for an element in many periodic tables may be non-integer because it reflects a specified isotopic composition, often the naturally occurring mixture. For instance, chlorine has more than one stable isotope. A value around 35.45 is therefore not the mass number of a hypothetical chlorine atom with “35.45 nucleons.” Individual nuclei have whole-number proton and neutron counts.

1.A.1.01.10.1 Three numbers that should never be confused

Z: atomic number = proton count. A: mass number = proton count + neutron count. Relative atomic mass: compares an atom’s mass with one twelfth of carbon-12 mass; it is dimensionless. The standard atomic weight of an element reflects an isotopic composition and is also dimensionless.

Common error: “Carbon has atomic number 6 and a periodic-table mass near 12, so every carbon atom must have six neutrons.” Carbon-13 and carbon-14 show why this claim is false.

1.A.1.01.11 An atom is not a miniature solar system

A familiar picture places a nucleus at the center with electrons drawn on circular rings. This is historically useful because it introduces the idea of a central nucleus and quantized energy levels. But literal circular, planet-like electron paths are not the modern scientific description of electrons in atoms.

In quantum mechanics, an electron is described by a wavefunction. The square of its magnitude is related to a probability density for finding the electron in a region when its position is measured. An orbital is a quantum-mechanical state or spatial wavefunction, not a highway along which a small ball permanently travels. Different orbitals may have different shapes and nodal regions.

Why does this matter to biology? Chemical bonds, polarity, molecular shape, and the interaction of electron-rich and electron-poor regions depend on electronic structure. Incorrect pictures of fixed tracks can create confusion later when studying covalent bonds, molecular orbitals, spectroscopy, and enzyme mechanisms.

1.A.1.01.12 From atoms to molecules and chemical bonds

Atoms interact with one another through their electrons. Under suitable conditions, they form chemical bonds. Covalent bonds are commonly described by sharing electron density between atoms; ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, often arranged in extended structures. Metallic bonding has its own collective electronic behavior.

Water is a useful example. Its formula H₂O means that each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The O–H bonds involve shared electrons, but the electron distribution is unequal because oxygen attracts bonding electrons more strongly than hydrogen. This contributes to water’s molecular polarity and to hydrogen bonding between water molecules.

Molecular properties are not obtained by simply adding the properties of isolated atoms. Oxygen and hydrogen are gases under ordinary conditions, but liquid water has very different behavior. Bonding and molecular structure create new, emergent physical and chemical properties.

1.A.1.01.12.1 Formula-reading practice

CO₂ contains one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms per molecule. C₆H₁₂O₆ contains six carbon, twelve hydrogen, and six oxygen atoms per molecule. The chemical formula reports composition; it does not by itself tell the complete three-dimensional structure or all of the substance’s properties.

1.A.1.01.12.2 Why bonding needs its own topics

Atomic structure gives the foundation; accurate prediction of bonds also requires electron configurations, electronegativity, molecular geometry, and energetic considerations. These are important enough to be separate, deeply developed topics later in Unit 1.A.

1.A.1.01.13 The major elements of living matter: CHNOPS

Six elements frequently appear in biological macromolecules: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur. The mnemonic CHNOPS helps remember their symbols. These are not the only elements needed by life: calcium, potassium, sodium, magnesium, iron, chlorine, and many trace elements are also physiologically important. The contribution of each element depends on its chemistry and biological context.

1.A.1.01.13.1 Carbon: a framework for organic molecules

Carbon can form strong covalent bonds with itself and several other elements, allowing chains, branched structures, and rings. This structural diversity supports sugars, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and numerous small metabolites. Carbon’s capacity to make multiple bonds is a chemical feature, not a property limited to living organisms.

1.A.1.01.13.2 Hydrogen and oxygen: water and functional groups

Hydrogen and oxygen form water, a major component of cells. Oxygen also appears in alcohol, carbonyl, carboxyl, phosphate, and other groups. Hydrogen participates in covalent bonds and in the acid–base chemistry of biological systems. The transfer of H⁺ in solution is more accurately treated through hydrated proton species; a bare proton does not usually remain isolated in bulk liquid water.

1.A.1.01.13.3 Nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur

Nitrogen is part of the amino groups of amino acids and the nitrogenous bases of nucleic acids. Phosphorus is fundamental to DNA and RNA backbones, phospholipids, and ATP-associated chemistry. Sulfur occurs in amino acids such as cysteine and methionine; cysteine residues may form disulfide bonds that help stabilize some proteins.

1.A.1.01.14 Biological connections that become clear through atomic thinking

1.A.1.01.14.1 Water and its unusual behavior

The electron distribution within water gives each molecule a permanent dipole. Water molecules can form hydrogen bonds with one another and with suitable groups in other molecules. These interactions contribute to water’s high heat capacity, cohesion, and ability to dissolve many ionic and polar substances. Atom-level explanations therefore help account for temperature regulation and the transport of dissolved substances in organisms.

1.A.1.01.14.2 Electrolytes and nerve signals

Cells maintain different concentrations of ions inside and outside their membranes. Changes in the movement and permeability of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Cl⁻ help create electrical signals and regulate cellular activities. The ion remains the same element even when it crosses a membrane or interacts with a channel protein; what changes is location and the interaction environment.

1.A.1.01.14.3 Enzymes and metal atoms

Some enzymes rely on metal ions such as Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, or Fe³⁺. These ions can stabilize charges, bind substrates, participate in redox reactions, or influence structure. Their special roles arise from electronic properties and chemical interactions. Not every enzyme needs a metal, and not every metal ion plays the same role.

1.A.1.01.14.4 Isotopic labeling in biological research

Scientists can trace atoms through biochemical pathways using stable or radioactive isotopes. For example, a suitable ¹³C-labeled substrate can help researchers study where carbon atoms go during metabolism. The labeled compound must be chosen carefully, and interpretation requires attention to isotopic effects, exchange reactions, and instrument measurements. The foundations of these methods are covered in later nodes.

Connection map: Atomic identity → electron behavior → bonds and interactions → molecular shape and energy → protein, membrane, and nucleic-acid function → cell physiology.

1.A.1.01.16 Solved foundational problems

Problem 1 — Particle counting. An atom has Z = 7 and A = 15. How many protons, neutrons, and electrons does the neutral atom have? Solution: protons = 7, neutrons = 15 − 7 = 8, electrons = 7. The element is nitrogen.

Problem 2 — Recognizing an ion. A particle has 17 protons and 18 electrons. Identify its charge and element. Solution: Z = 17 means chlorine. Charge = 17 − 18 = −1, so it is Cl⁻. Its isotope cannot be identified without the neutron count.

Problem 3 — Same element or different? Particle X has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Particle Y has 6 protons and 7 neutrons. Solution: both are carbon isotopes; X is carbon-12, Y is carbon-13. They differ in A, not Z.

Problem 4 — Identify the mistake. A periodic table gives an element’s relative atomic mass as 24.3. A student says each of its atoms has 24.3 nucleons. Solution: nucleon counts must be integers. A noninteger standard atomic weight commonly reflects isotopic composition; it is not a fractional nucleon count in an individual nucleus.

Problem 5 — Unit conversion. Express 0.15 nm in pm and meters. Solution: 0.15 nm × 1,000 = 150 pm. Since 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m, 0.15 nm = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

Problem 6 — Biology connection. Why can two substances made of the same elements have different properties? Solution: Their atom ratios, arrangement, bonds, and three-dimensional structures may differ. Elemental composition alone is not a complete molecular description.

Problem-solving sequence: Determine the given quantities → write what each symbol means → apply a suitable relationship → check units and whether the answer is physically possible.

1.A.1.01.17 Exam practice with fully reasoned answers

1.A.1.01.17.1 MCQ — identity of an element

Which property must remain the same for two atoms to be atoms of the same element? (A) Neutron number (B) Proton number (C) Mass number (D) Electron number

Answer: B. Elemental identity is defined by nuclear proton number. Isotopes may have different neutron numbers, and ions may have different electron numbers.

1.A.1.01.17.2 MCQ — neutral atom

An atom with 12 protons and 12 electrons has a net charge of: (A) +12 (B) −12 (C) 0 (D) +24

Answer: C. Twelve positive elementary charges balance twelve negative elementary charges.

1.A.1.01.17.3 MCQ — nucleon count

A nuclide with A = 31 and Z = 15 has how many neutrons? (A) 15 (B) 16 (C) 31 (D) 46

Answer: B. N = A − Z = 31 − 15 = 16.

1.A.1.01.17.4 MCQ — an exception

Which common atom can have a nucleus containing no neutrons? (A) Carbon-12 (B) Oxygen-16 (C) Hydrogen-1 (D) Nitrogen-14

Answer: C. The protium nucleus has one proton and no neutron.

1.A.1.01.17.5 MCQ — model accuracy

Which statement best describes an atomic orbital? (A) A fixed circular rail for an electron (B) A quantum-mechanical electron state with a spatial probability description (C) A neutron’s path (D) A solid shell with a hard surface

Answer: B. An orbital is represented using wavefunctions and electron probability, not a permanent classical trajectory.

1.A.1.01.17.6 MCQ — isotope recognition

Two atoms have the same Z but different A. They are: (A) Ions of different elements (B) Isotopes of the same element (C) Always both radioactive (D) Identical nuclides

Answer: B. Their proton numbers agree, while their neutron counts differ.

1.A.1.01.17.7 MCQ — formula interpretation

One molecule of glucose has formula C₆H₁₂O₆. The total number of atoms is: (A) 6 (B) 12 (C) 18 (D) 24

Answer: D. 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms per molecule.

1.A.1.01.17.8 MCQ — substance types

Which statement is most accurate? (A) O₂ is a compound of different elements (B) Every ionic solid is made of separate molecules (C) H₂O is a molecular compound (D) NaCl is an element

Answer: C. Water molecules contain hydrogen and oxygen. O₂ has one element only; NaCl is an ionic compound.

1.A.1.01.17.9 Application — biological chemistry

A protein contains both carbon and nitrogen. Does this mean it has only two kinds of atoms? Explain.

Answer: No. Proteins typically also contain hydrogen and oxygen, often sulfur, and may include other components or associated ions. Knowing that carbon and nitrogen occur does not specify the complete composition.

1.A.1.01.17.10 Application — isotope tracing

What makes ¹³C useful in studying the fate of carbon during metabolism?

Answer: ¹³C has the same atomic number as other carbon isotopes, allowing labeled carbon to participate in carbon-containing substrates. Its distinct nuclear mass and detectable isotopic signature permit tracing with suitable analytical methods. Experimental design still matters because isotope effects can occur.

1.A.1.01.17.11 Reasoning — nuclear versus chemical change

If one proton in a carbon nucleus changes so the nucleus has seven protons, can the resulting atom still be carbon?

Answer: No. Z = 7 corresponds to nitrogen. Such a change is nuclear, not an ordinary chemical reaction.

1.A.1.01.17.12 Quantitative reasoning — powers of ten

Convert a 15-µm cell width into nanometers.

Answer: 15 µm × 10³ nm/µm = 15,000 nm = 1.5 × 10⁴ nm.

1.A.1.01.18 Quick revision, concept connections, glossary, and references

1.A.1.01.18.1 Ten facts to recall

  1. Matter has mass and occupies space.
  2. An atom is the smallest unit characterizing an element.
  3. Proton number Z identifies the element.
  4. Mass number A = protons + neutrons.
  5. A neutral atom has equal proton and electron counts.
  6. Ions form when electrons are gained or lost, or when a charged group is formed.
  7. Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron numbers.
  8. Protium has no neutron.
  9. Electrons are described by quantum states, not fixed planetary tracks.

1.A.1.01.18.3 Glossary

Atom
Smallest particle still characterizing a chemical element.
Element
Species of atoms identified by their proton number.
Nucleus
Small, dense central region of an atom containing protons and usually neutrons.
Atomic number (Z)
Number of protons in a nucleus.
Mass number (A)
Sum of protons and neutrons in one nucleus.
Ion
Atom or group carrying net electric charge.
Isotope
Atom of an element differing in neutron number from another isotope of that element.
Orbital
Quantum-mechanical electron state described by a wavefunction.
Unified atomic mass unit (u)
One twelfth of the mass of a free carbon-12 atom in its ground state at rest.
