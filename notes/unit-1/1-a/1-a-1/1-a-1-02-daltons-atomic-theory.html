Dalton's Atomic Theory

Postulates and Limitations · A clear, detailed historical and scientific foundation for CSIR-NET Life Sciences & Biotechnology and State SET examinations.

Learning outcomes. Explain Dalton's postulates, the three laws of chemical combination, simple mass-ratio calculations, historical significance and modern limitations. Apply quantitative atom accounting to biological reactions and distinguish historical claims from modern scientific theory.

Contents · 1.A.1.02

1.A.1.02.1 The question that changed chemistry

Everything in a cell, a drop of water, and the surrounding air is made of matter. Today we explain chemical reactions through atoms and molecules, but how did scientists first connect these invisible particles with substances that could be weighed? At the beginning of the nineteenth century, chemists were discovering striking patterns in the masses of substances that reacted together.

John Dalton proposed that chemical elements consist of characteristic tiny particles, and that compounds form when different kinds of these particles join in regular numbers. Dalton’s atomic theory connected the small, unseen world of atoms with the measurable world of laboratory chemistry. Its value lay in providing a microscopic explanation for repeated quantitative observations.

Dalton could not directly observe atoms or see nuclei and electrons. Instead, he reasoned from experimental patterns. His theory was revolutionary but not perfect. Later discoveries corrected the claims that atoms are indivisible and that all atoms of one element have identical masses.

Core idea: Characteristic atoms and whole-number combinations help explain why chemical reactions occur in reproducible proportions. Dalton's historical picture is not the same as the modern atom.

1.A.1.02.1.1 What this node will teach

The historical background; five classical postulates; conservation of mass; definite and multiple proportions; mass-ratio numericals; limitations concerning isotopes, nuclei and electrons; and the relevance of atom accounting to biological chemistry. The development of modern atomic models is reserved for later nodes.

1.A.1.02.2 Before Dalton: philosophical ideas versus measurement

1.A.1.02.2.1 The ancient atomist idea

Ancient Greek philosophers, including Leucippus and Democritus, discussed matter in terms of tiny particles. The Greek term commonly rendered atomos indicated something that could not be further cut. This was a philosophical proposal, not a modern experimentally verified atomic theory. Other traditions explained matter through continuous substances or elemental qualities.

1.A.1.02.2.2 Why experimental chemistry mattered

Centuries later, scientific investigations became increasingly quantitative. Chemists carefully measured reactants and products, compared the composition of pure substances, and checked whether observations were repeatable. A useful theory had to explain not only that substances reacted, but also why particular mass proportions recurred.

A scientific explanation should connect observations, allow reliable predictions, and remain open to correction. Dalton's proposal built on earlier chemistry rather than appearing without historical background.

Exam caution: Dalton did not invent the idea of atoms or discover every law of chemical combination. His distinctive achievement was an influential quantitative atomic explanation.

1.A.1.02.3.1 Antoine Lavoisier: quantitative conservation

Lavoisier's work helped establish that the total mass of materials involved in a closed ordinary chemical reaction remains constant. A microscopic account must explain why substances change while their total measured mass remains.

1.A.1.02.3.2 Joseph Proust: definite composition

Proust's studies supported the observation that pure samples of a specified compound contain the same elements in the same mass proportions. The amount collected may differ, but the defining composition stays consistent.

1.A.1.02.3.3 John Dalton: characteristic atoms

Dalton developed a chemical atomic framework around 1803, including ideas about relative atomic weights and combination ratios. His book A New System of Chemical Philosophy began appearing in 1808. The theory helped make the law of multiple proportions intelligible through numbers of atoms.

1.A.1.02.3.4 Later revisions

J. J. Thomson's 1897 electron investigations showed that atoms contain smaller constituents. Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) and Chadwick's neutron discovery (1932) advanced the picture. Dalton's particle accounting largely survived, but his internally structureless atoms did not.

1.A.1.02.4 Five postulates of Dalton's atomic theory

Textbooks sometimes list four, five, or six postulates because they divide the main ideas differently. The following five headings express the core of the classical theory. They are historical statements, not all literal statements of present scientific knowledge.

Table 1.A.1.02-T1. Historical postulates and their explanatory purpose
No.Dalton-era statementWhat it explained
1Matter consists of very small, indivisible atoms.The particulate nature of matter
2Atoms of one element are identical in mass and characteristic properties.Consistent elemental composition
3Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.Differences between elements
4Atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios.Fixed and multiple proportions
5Atoms are rearranged during chemical changes, not created or destroyed in those changes.Conservation during chemical reactions

1.A.1.02.4.1 A postulate is not automatically an eternal truth

A postulate is part of a proposed explanatory framework. Evidence may support it, require modification, or contradict it. Dalton's statements were strong enough to explain much observed chemistry, but later experiments identified electrons, nuclei and isotopes that the original model did not include.

Reading strategy: Separate “According to Dalton's original theory” from “According to the modern atomic model.” Examinations may deliberately switch between these two wordings.

1.A.1.02.5 Postulate 1: matter is made of atoms

Dalton treated all matter as made of discrete building blocks. A large sample of an element contains many atoms of that element. If compounds result from combining such building blocks, their composition can be understood through particle numbers.

1.A.1.02.5.1 Why it was a powerful idea

Imagine measuring two samples of a pure compound. A sample twice as large may contain twice as many formula units or molecules, but the relative numbers of different constituent atoms remain unchanged. That naturally leads to constant mass composition.

1.A.1.02.5.2 Why “indivisible” is incorrect today

An atom contains subatomic constituents. Its electrons are part of its electronic structure; its nucleus contains protons and usually neutrons. Nuclear processes can change elemental identity. Atoms are basic chemical units, not indivisible particles in modern physics.

Wrong conclusion: “Because Dalton's indivisibility claim failed, atoms do not exist.” Correct conclusion: Atoms exist and have internal structure; his simple model was incomplete.

1.A.1.02.6 Postulates 2 and 3: element identity and characteristic atoms

Dalton stated that the atoms of one element share a characteristic mass and properties, while atoms of different elements differ. This made elemental identity part of the theory and supplied a reason why different elements react differently.

1.A.1.02.6.1 What defines an element today?

The atomic number Z, the number of protons in the nucleus, defines the element. Carbon has six protons; oxygen has eight. Proton count is more fundamental for identification than Dalton's proposed identical atomic masses.

1.A.1.02.6.2 Isotopes correct the identical-mass claim

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 have six protons each, but different neutron numbers. Both are carbon, though their atomic masses differ. Hydrogen-1 and hydrogen-2 show an even larger relative mass difference. Isotopes generally share closely related chemistry when electronic states match, but some isotope-dependent physical properties and reaction rates differ.

1.A.1.02.6.3 Equal mass number does not mean equal element

Argon-40 has 18 protons; calcium-40 has 20. Both have mass number 40, yet they are different elements. Their precise atomic masses are not equal. The same mass number indicates equal total nucleon counts, not equal proton numbers.

Three questions: Same Z? Same element. Different neutron count but same Z? Different isotope. Different Z? Different element, regardless of any shared mass number.

1.A.1.02.7 Postulate 4: atoms combine in numerical ratios

Dalton argued that atoms of different elements can join in small whole-number combinations. A molecule of modern water, H₂O, contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; a molecule of carbon monoxide, CO, contains one carbon and one oxygen; carbon dioxide, CO₂, contains one carbon and two oxygen atoms.

Figure 1.A.1.02-F2. Symbolic particle counts, not representations of actual molecular geometry, bond order, or electronic structure.

1.A.1.02.7.1 Why fixed ratios are useful

A reproducible particle ratio gives a reproducible mass ratio when the relevant atomic masses are fixed. Thus, quantitative chemical laws have an understandable microscopic foundation.

1.A.1.02.7.2 Compounds do not always contain separate molecules

In an ordinary sodium chloride crystal, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ occur in an extended ionic arrangement rather than discrete NaCl molecules. The formula expresses the simplest stoichiometric ion ratio. Some real solids show nonstoichiometric compositions because of defects, so “every material follows a small exact molecular formula” is too broad.

1.A.1.02.8 Postulate 5: atoms are rearranged in chemical reactions

Normal chemical reactions break and form chemical bonds without changing the identity of the atomic nuclei. Atoms that were present before the reaction also occur in the complete set of products, although their partners and arrangements may differ.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

On the left are four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. On the right, two water molecules also contain four hydrogen and two oxygen atoms. This is why balancing chemical equations requires matching the number of atoms of each element.

1.A.1.02.8.1 What is conserved and what can change?

In ordinary chemistry, elemental atom counts are conserved in the complete balanced description. Chemical bonds, molecular shape, internal energy and physical state may change. A reaction may release heat or require energy even though its atom counts remain balanced.

1.A.1.02.8.2 Why experiments should use closed-system accounting

If gas escapes an open vessel, the remaining material may weigh less. That does not demonstrate matter destruction. The escaped gas has left the measured portion of the system. To test total mass conservation, all products must be included.

1.A.1.02.9 Law of conservation of mass

The law of conservation of mass states that the total mass of reactants in a closed ordinary chemical reaction equals the total mass of products to an extremely good practical approximation.

Mass of all reactants = mass of all products

1.A.1.02.9.1 Worked example: calcium carbonate decomposes

A sample of 10.0 g CaCO₃ decomposes into 5.6 g CaO and carbon dioxide. Find the mass of CO₂.

Step 1: Write 10.0 = 5.6 + mass of CO₂ (in grams). Step 2: Subtract: 10.0 − 5.6 = 4.4 g CO₂. Step 3: Confirm the reaction CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ conserves each element.

1.A.1.02.9.2 Why an open vessel can show a smaller residue mass

If only the remaining solid is weighed, the escaped gas has not been included. The apparent mass decrease comes from the boundary of the weighing experiment rather than a failure of atom conservation.

1.A.1.02.9.3 Modern mass–energy qualification

For routine chemistry, conservation of mass works extremely well. In nuclear transformations, mass can convert into energy at a detectable scale; modern physics uses conservation of total mass–energy. Do not apply Dalton's simplified chemical statement to nuclear reactions without qualification.

1.A.1.02.10 Law of definite proportions

The law of definite proportions states that pure samples of a specific fixed-composition compound contain the same elements in the same relative masses, regardless of amount or source. Joseph Proust is closely associated with its experimental establishment.

1.A.1.02.10.1 Why pure water has a fixed hydrogen–oxygen mass ratio

Modern H₂O molecules contain two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Using approximate atomic masses H ≈ 1 and O ≈ 16, hydrogen contributes about 2 mass units and oxygen contributes 16. Therefore the H:O mass ratio is 2:16 = 1:8.

1.A.1.02.10.2 Worked question

If 6 g hydrogen combines fully in water, approximately how much oxygen is required? Multiply hydrogen mass by eight: 6 × 8 = 48 g oxygen. Total water formed is 54 g.

1.A.1.02.10.3 Three qualifications

A mixture such as a sugar solution does not have one obligatory mass ratio. Different compounds or isomers may share overall elemental proportions. Isotopically enriched samples and nonstoichiometric solids may need more careful definitions than the simple introductory formulation.

Remember: Definite proportions compares different samples of the same pure compound.

1.A.1.02.11 Law of multiple proportions

When the same two elements form more than one fixed-composition compound, compare the masses of one element that combine with an equal mass of the other. These different masses often form a ratio of small whole numbers. That is Dalton’s law of multiple proportions.

1.A.1.02.11.1 Carbon monoxide versus carbon dioxide

Using C ≈ 12 and O ≈ 16, CO contains 12 mass units of C and 16 of O. CO₂ contains 12 of C and 32 of O. For the same 12 units of carbon, the oxygen masses are 16 and 32.

Oxygen masses: 16 : 32 = 1 : 2

1.A.1.02.11.2 Why atom counting predicts this

CO has one oxygen atom for each carbon atom; CO₂ has two. If the carbon amount is held fixed, CO₂ has twice as much oxygen. Dalton's hypothesis of discrete combinations explains the numerical law.

1.A.1.02.11.3 Another example: nitrogen oxides

NO combines about 14 mass units of N with 16 of O, whereas NO₂ combines 14 of N with 32 of O. With nitrogen fixed, the oxygen mass ratio is again 1:2.

1.A.1.02.11.4 Do not compare unequal reference masses

If two compounds contain different measured masses of carbon, first calculate oxygen mass per gram of carbon for both. Only then form the ratio. Otherwise the resulting comparison may be wrong.

Key contrast: Definite proportions: same compound, compare separate samples. Multiple proportions: different compounds of the same pair of elements, keep one element's mass fixed.
