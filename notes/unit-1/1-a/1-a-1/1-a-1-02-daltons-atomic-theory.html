1.A.1.02.1 The question that changed chemistry

Everything in a cell, a drop of water, and the surrounding air is made of matter. Today we explain chemical reactions through atoms and molecules, but how did scientists first connect these invisible particles with substances that could be weighed? At the beginning of the nineteenth century, chemists were discovering striking patterns in the masses of substances that reacted together.

John Dalton proposed that chemical elements consist of characteristic tiny particles, and that compounds form when different kinds of these particles join in regular numbers. Dalton’s atomic theory connected the small, unseen world of atoms with the measurable world of laboratory chemistry. Its value lay in providing a microscopic explanation for repeated quantitative observations.

Dalton could not directly observe atoms or see nuclei and electrons. Instead, he reasoned from experimental patterns. His theory was revolutionary but not perfect. Later discoveries corrected the claims that atoms are indivisible and that all atoms of one element have identical masses.

Core idea: Characteristic atoms and whole-number combinations help explain why chemical reactions occur in reproducible proportions. Dalton's historical picture is not the same as the modern atom.

1.A.1.02.1.1 What this node will teach

The historical background; five classical postulates; conservation of mass; definite and multiple proportions; mass-ratio numericals; limitations concerning isotopes, nuclei and electrons; and the relevance of atom accounting to biological chemistry. The development of modern atomic models is reserved for later nodes.

1.A.1.02.2 Before Dalton: philosophical ideas versus measurement

1.A.1.02.2.1 The ancient atomist idea

Ancient Greek philosophers, including Leucippus and Democritus, discussed matter in terms of tiny particles. The Greek term commonly rendered atomos indicated something that could not be further cut. This was a philosophical proposal, not a modern experimentally verified atomic theory. Other traditions explained matter through continuous substances or elemental qualities.

1.A.1.02.2.2 Why experimental chemistry mattered

Centuries later, scientific investigations became increasingly quantitative. Chemists carefully measured reactants and products, compared the composition of pure substances, and checked whether observations were repeatable. A useful theory had to explain not only that substances reacted, but also why particular mass proportions recurred.

A scientific explanation should connect observations, allow reliable predictions, and remain open to correction. Dalton's proposal built on earlier chemistry rather than appearing without historical background.

Exam caution: Dalton did not invent the idea of atoms or discover every law of chemical combination. His distinctive achievement was an influential quantitative atomic explanation.

1.A.1.02.3 Chronology: the evidence and the theory

The timeline below gives important milestones without suggesting that every discovery happened at a single precise moment.

Figure 1.A.1.02-F1. A simplified scientific timeline with several contributors.

1.A.1.02.3.1 Antoine Lavoisier: quantitative conservation

Lavoisier's work helped establish that the total mass of materials involved in a closed ordinary chemical reaction remains constant. A microscopic account must explain why substances change while their total measured mass remains.

1.A.1.02.3.2 Joseph Proust: definite composition

Proust's studies supported the observation that pure samples of a specified compound contain the same elements in the same mass proportions. The amount collected may differ, but the defining composition stays consistent.

1.A.1.02.3.3 John Dalton: characteristic atoms

Dalton developed a chemical atomic framework around 1803, including ideas about relative atomic weights and combination ratios. His book A New System of Chemical Philosophy began appearing in 1808. The theory helped make the law of multiple proportions intelligible through numbers of atoms.

1.A.1.02.3.4 Later revisions

J. J. Thomson's 1897 electron investigations showed that atoms contain smaller constituents. Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) and Chadwick's neutron discovery (1932) advanced the picture. Dalton's particle accounting largely survived, but his internally structureless atoms did not.

1.A.1.02.4 Five postulates of Dalton's atomic theory

Textbooks sometimes list four, five, or six postulates because they divide the main ideas differently. The following five headings express the core of the classical theory. They are historical statements, not all literal statements of present scientific knowledge.

Table 1.A.1.02-T1. Historical postulates and their explanatory purpose
No.Dalton-era statementWhat it explained
1Matter consists of very small, indivisible atoms.The particulate nature of matter
2Atoms of one element are identical in mass and characteristic properties.Consistent elemental composition
3Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.Differences between elements
4Atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios.Fixed and multiple proportions
5Atoms are rearranged during chemical changes, not created or destroyed in those changes.Conservation during chemical reactions

1.A.1.02.4.1 A postulate is not automatically an eternal truth

A postulate is part of a proposed explanatory framework. Evidence may support it, require modification, or contradict it. Dalton's statements were strong enough to explain much observed chemistry, but later experiments identified electrons, nuclei and isotopes that the original model did not include.

Reading strategy: Separate “According to Dalton's original theory” from “According to the modern atomic model.” Examinations may deliberately switch between these two wordings.

1.A.1.02.5 Postulate 1: matter is made of atoms

Dalton treated all matter as made of discrete building blocks. A large sample of an element contains many atoms of that element. If compounds result from combining such building blocks, their composition can be understood through particle numbers.

1.A.1.02.5.1 Why it was a powerful idea

Imagine measuring two samples of a pure compound. A sample twice as large may contain twice as many formula units or molecules, but the relative numbers of different constituent atoms remain unchanged. That naturally leads to constant mass composition.

1.A.1.02.5.2 Why “indivisible” is incorrect today

An atom contains subatomic constituents. Its electrons are part of its electronic structure; its nucleus contains protons and usually neutrons. Nuclear processes can change elemental identity. Atoms are basic chemical units, not indivisible particles in modern physics.

Wrong conclusion: “Because Dalton's indivisibility claim failed, atoms do not exist.” Correct conclusion: Atoms exist and have internal structure; his simple model was incomplete.

1.A.1.02.6 Postulates 2 and 3: element identity and characteristic atoms

Dalton stated that the atoms of one element share a characteristic mass and properties, while atoms of different elements differ. This made elemental identity part of the theory and supplied a reason why different elements react differently.

1.A.1.02.6.1 What defines an element today?

The atomic number Z, the number of protons in the nucleus, defines the element. Carbon has six protons; oxygen has eight. Proton count is more fundamental for identification than Dalton's proposed identical atomic masses.

1.A.1.02.6.2 Isotopes correct the identical-mass claim

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 have six protons each, but different neutron numbers. Both are carbon, though their atomic masses differ. Hydrogen-1 and hydrogen-2 show an even larger relative mass difference. Isotopes generally share closely related chemistry when electronic states match, but some isotope-dependent physical properties and reaction rates differ.

1.A.1.02.6.3 Equal mass number does not mean equal element

Argon-40 has 18 protons; calcium-40 has 20. Both have mass number 40, yet they are different elements. Their precise atomic masses are not equal. The same mass number indicates equal total nucleon counts, not equal proton numbers.

Three questions: Same Z? Same element. Different neutron count but same Z? Different isotope. Different Z? Different element, regardless of any shared mass number.

1.A.1.02.7 Postulate 4: atoms combine in numerical ratios

Dalton argued that atoms of different elements can join in small whole-number combinations. A molecule of modern water, H₂O, contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; a molecule of carbon monoxide, CO, contains one carbon and one oxygen; carbon dioxide, CO₂, contains one carbon and two oxygen atoms.

Figure 1.A.1.02-F2. Symbolic particle counts, not representations of actual molecular geometry, bond order, or electronic structure.

1.A.1.02.7.1 Why fixed ratios are useful

A reproducible particle ratio gives a reproducible mass ratio when the relevant atomic masses are fixed. Thus, quantitative chemical laws have an understandable microscopic foundation.

1.A.1.02.7.2 Compounds do not always contain separate molecules

In an ordinary sodium chloride crystal, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ occur in an extended ionic arrangement rather than discrete NaCl molecules. The formula expresses the simplest stoichiometric ion ratio. Some real solids show nonstoichiometric compositions because of defects, so “every material follows a small exact molecular formula” is too broad.

1.A.1.02.8 Postulate 5: atoms are rearranged in chemical reactions

Normal chemical reactions break and form chemical bonds without changing the identity of the atomic nuclei. Atoms that were present before the reaction also occur in the complete set of products, although their partners and arrangements may differ.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

On the left are four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. On the right, two water molecules also contain four hydrogen and two oxygen atoms. This is why balancing chemical equations requires matching the number of atoms of each element.

1.A.1.02.8.1 What is conserved and what can change?

In ordinary chemistry, elemental atom counts are conserved in the complete balanced description. Chemical bonds, molecular shape, internal energy and physical state may change. A reaction may release heat or require energy even though its atom counts remain balanced.

1.A.1.02.8.2 Why experiments should use closed-system accounting

If gas escapes an open vessel, the remaining material may weigh less. That does not demonstrate matter destruction. The escaped gas has left the measured portion of the system. To test total mass conservation, all products must be included.

1.A.1.02.9 Law of conservation of mass

The law of conservation of mass states that the total mass of reactants in a closed ordinary chemical reaction equals the total mass of products to an extremely good practical approximation.

Mass of all reactants = mass of all products

1.A.1.02.9.1 Worked example: calcium carbonate decomposes

A sample of 10.0 g CaCO₃ decomposes into 5.6 g CaO and carbon dioxide. Find the mass of CO₂.

Step 1: Write 10.0 = 5.6 + mass of CO₂ (in grams). Step 2: Subtract: 10.0 − 5.6 = 4.4 g CO₂. Step 3: Confirm the reaction CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ conserves each element.

1.A.1.02.9.2 Why an open vessel can show a smaller residue mass

If only the remaining solid is weighed, the escaped gas has not been included. The apparent mass decrease comes from the boundary of the weighing experiment rather than a failure of atom conservation.

1.A.1.02.9.3 Modern mass–energy qualification

For routine chemistry, conservation of mass works extremely well. In nuclear transformations, mass can convert into energy at a detectable scale; modern physics uses conservation of total mass–energy. Do not apply Dalton's simplified chemical statement to nuclear reactions without qualification.

1.A.1.02.10 Law of definite proportions

The law of definite proportions states that pure samples of a specific fixed-composition compound contain the same elements in the same relative masses, regardless of amount or source. Joseph Proust is closely associated with its experimental establishment.

1.A.1.02.10.1 Why pure water has a fixed hydrogen–oxygen mass ratio

Modern H₂O molecules contain two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Using approximate atomic masses H ≈ 1 and O ≈ 16, hydrogen contributes about 2 mass units and oxygen contributes 16. Therefore the H:O mass ratio is 2:16 = 1:8.

1.A.1.02.10.2 Worked question

If 6 g hydrogen combines fully in water, approximately how much oxygen is required? Multiply hydrogen mass by eight: 6 × 8 = 48 g oxygen. Total water formed is 54 g.

1.A.1.02.10.3 Three qualifications

A mixture such as a sugar solution does not have one obligatory mass ratio. Different compounds or isomers may share overall elemental proportions. Isotopically enriched samples and nonstoichiometric solids may need more careful definitions than the simple introductory formulation.

Remember: Definite proportions compares different samples of the same pure compound.

1.A.1.02.11 Law of multiple proportions

When the same two elements form more than one fixed-composition compound, compare the masses of one element that combine with an equal mass of the other. These different masses often form a ratio of small whole numbers. That is Dalton’s law of multiple proportions.

1.A.1.02.11.1 Carbon monoxide versus carbon dioxide

Using C ≈ 12 and O ≈ 16, CO contains 12 mass units of C and 16 of O. CO₂ contains 12 of C and 32 of O. For the same 12 units of carbon, the oxygen masses are 16 and 32.

Table 1.A.1.02-T3. A classic multiple-proportions comparison

CO₂12322
Oxygen masses: 16 : 32 = 1 : 2

1.A.1.02.11.2 Why atom counting predicts this

CO has one oxygen atom for each carbon atom; CO₂ has two. If the carbon amount is held fixed, CO₂ has twice as much oxygen. Dalton's hypothesis of discrete combinations explains the numerical law.

1.A.1.02.11.3 Another example: nitrogen oxides

NO combines about 14 mass units of N with 16 of O, whereas NO₂ combines 14 of N with 32 of O. With nitrogen fixed, the oxygen mass ratio is again 1:2.

1.A.1.02.11.4 Do not compare unequal reference masses

If two compounds contain different measured masses of carbon, first calculate oxygen mass per gram of carbon for both. Only then form the ratio. Otherwise the resulting comparison may be wrong.

Key contrast: Definite proportions: same compound, compare separate samples. Multiple proportions: different compounds of the same pair of elements, keep one element's mass fixed.

1.A.1.02.12 Solved numerical problems with detailed reasoning

1.A.1.02.12.1 Problem 1: compare compounds with unequal sample sizes

Compound X contains 6 g carbon and 8 g oxygen. Compound Y contains 9 g carbon and 24 g oxygen. Do the data support a simple multiple-proportion relationship?

Step 1: Normalize X to one gram of carbon: 8 ÷ 6 = 4/3 g O per g C.

Step 2: Normalize Y: 24 ÷ 9 = 8/3 g O per g C.

Step 3: Compare (4/3):(8/3) = 1:2. The data are consistent with different fixed-composition carbon oxides.

1.A.1.02.12.2 Problem 2: an unknown water component

A sample of pure water contains 56 g oxygen. Find its approximate hydrogen mass using H:O = 1:8. Let hydrogen mass be x. x/56 = 1/8, so x = 7 g. The water mass is about 63 g.

1.A.1.02.12.3 Problem 3: conservation in a closed reaction

Reactant A (12 g) combines with reactant B (18 g) in a closed vessel. The reaction yields 9 g of product P and one other product. The other product must have mass 12 + 18 − 9 = 21 g, if all reactants are completely converted to those products.

1.A.1.02.12.4 Problem 4: same compound or different?

Two pure samples of compound M contain A:B mass values of 2:6 and 5:15. These simplify to 1:3 in both cases. They illustrate definite proportions, not multiple proportions, because the sample is said to be the same compound.

1.A.1.02.12.5 Problem 5: empirical formula

A compound contains 24 g C and 64 g O. Using approximate molar masses 12 g/mol and 16 g/mol, the amounts are 24/12 = 2 mol C and 64/16 = 4 mol O. Divide by 2: C:O = 1:2. The empirical formula is CO₂ if the compound contains only those elements.

1.A.1.02.12.6 Problem 6: balanced particle count

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, count atoms on each side. Reactants: four H, two O. Products: four H, two O. The coefficient 2 multiplies the entire formula H₂O; it is not the same as the subscript 2 after H.

1.A.1.02.12.7 Problem 7: quantitative uncertainty

Suppose measured oxygen ratios are 1.01:1.99 instead of exactly 1:2. Do not immediately reject the law. Evaluate purity and measurement uncertainty; real experimental data are rounded, whereas ideal stoichiometric models give exact simple ratios.

1.A.1.02.13 Dalton's solid-sphere or billiard-ball representation

Dalton's model is often pictured as a collection of small solid spheres. Each type of sphere represents an element, and different spheres can join in recognizable combinations. The picture communicates the historical concept of discrete building blocks. It does not represent modern electron clouds, nuclei, charge distributions, or molecular geometry.

Figure 1.A.1.02-F4. Original conceptual diagram of Dalton-era solid spheres. The sphere colors and sizes do not encode actual atomic structure.

1.A.1.02.13.1 What the model could explain

That a pure element contains characteristic atoms; that different elements have different kinds of atoms; and that compounds can be described by regular particle ratios. This was enough to provide a conceptual explanation for important mass relationships.

1.A.1.02.13.2 What the model could not explain

It did not describe electrical charge, electrons, isotopes, atomic spectra, the nucleus, periodic trends, or why particular bonds form. A solid sphere is a historical starting point, not an accurate electronic model. This is why later models were needed.

1.A.1.02.14 Relative atomic masses and the water-formula problem

Dalton tried to calculate relative atomic masses from measured combining masses. To compare mass on an atomic scale, however, a chemist must know how many atoms of each element are present in the compound. The mass ratio itself does not uniquely determine an atomic formula.

1.A.1.02.14.1 Why a 1:8 mass ratio does not prove HO or H₂O

Pure water has a hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio close to 1:8 by mass. Knowing that alone does not establish whether a constituent unit contains one H and one O, or two H and one O. Different choices for atomic masses and atom counts can fit a measured mass ratio. Independent experimental information is needed.

1.A.1.02.14.2 Dalton's historical assumption

Dalton used a simplicity assumption and treated water as though its composition corresponded to HO rather than the established modern H₂O formula. His early oxygen weight estimates were therefore incorrect. Some historical Dalton tables used an oxygen atomic weight around seven to eight on a hydrogen-equals-one scale, reflecting the measurements and assumptions available to him, rather than the modern value near sixteen.

Later work, including Avogadro's ideas about gases and molecules, helped distinguish atom counts from molecular counts. The scientific lesson is that a correct mass measurement may still allow an incorrect structural explanation.

1.A.1.02.14.3 Three different modern quantities

Atomic number Z counts protons. Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. Relative atomic mass compares a measured atomic mass with a specified carbon-12 reference. These are not interchangeable, and Dalton's historical relative weights were not today's precise mass values.

Important distinction: Empirical composition is established from measurements and atomic masses; a full molecular formula may require a molecular mass or other evidence. Molecular structure requires still more information.

1.A.1.02.15 Dalton's contributions to quantitative science

1.A.1.02.15.1 Connecting numbers with particles

The main achievement was the idea that laboratory mass ratios could be explained in terms of countable atoms. An invisible atomic model became a practical tool for analyzing measured substances.

1.A.1.02.15.2 Supporting stoichiometry

Chemical calculations often begin with a balanced equation. Such equations depend on whole-number counts of atoms. Once atomic masses and particle ratios are known, we can calculate how much reactant is required or how much product might be formed.

1.A.1.02.15.3 Making chemical identity more precise

Dalton's framework treated elements as having characteristic particles. This opened the path to a more systematic view of compounds, even though modern identity is determined by proton number rather than identical atomic mass.

1.A.1.02.15.4 Providing a model that could be tested and revised

A successful scientific idea need not remain unchanged forever. Experiments can improve it. Dalton's model failed to describe subatomic structure but survived as a foundation for ordinary chemical atom accounting. Learning this history is a practical lesson in how science progresses.

1.A.1.02.16 Limitations of the original theory

Table 1.A.1.02-T4. Dalton-era statements and modern corrections
Historical statementWhat modern evidence shows
Atoms are indivisible solid units.Atoms have electrons and nuclei; nuclei contain protons and usually neutrons.
All atoms of an element have identical mass.Isotopes have the same Z but different neutron counts and masses.
Different element types are always distinguished by their masses.Element identity is proton number; distinct nuclides can share a mass number.
Atoms cannot become atoms of another element.Nuclear transformations can alter proton number and elemental identity.

A solid sphere represents the complete atom.Electrons and nuclear structure require more advanced models.

1.A.1.02.16.1 Subatomic particles

Thomson's investigations of electrons showed that the atom had components smaller than Dalton's indivisible units. Rutherford and Chadwick helped establish the nucleus and neutron. These findings did not eliminate atoms; they improved the description.

1.A.1.02.16.2 Isotopes

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are both carbon, yet they have different masses. Their proton counts agree, so their elemental identity agrees. The identical-mass postulate therefore needed correction.

1.A.1.02.16.3 Isobars and different elements

Argon-40 and calcium-40 have the same mass number but different atomic numbers. Their precise masses are not identical, and they are not the same element. This shows why equal mass number must not be confused with elemental identity.

1.A.1.02.16.4 Nuclear reactions

Ordinary chemistry conserves nuclei of each element in its balanced reactions. Nuclear processes may change proton numbers or transform nuclei into other nuclides. The simple chemical no-transmutation assumption cannot be extended to such reactions.

1.A.1.02.16.5 Nonstoichiometry and complicated materials

Many simple pure compounds have fixed formulas, but some real solids exhibit composition variations linked to defects or vacancies. Mixtures and biological polymers also need careful treatment. This does not negate the usefulness of definite composition for compounds to which that law applies.

1.A.1.02.17.1 What still works?

Elemental atom accounting in ordinary chemical reactions and fixed stoichiometric combinations remain extremely useful. Many calculations in chemistry and biochemistry rely on these principles.

1.A.1.02.17.2 What must not be repeated as modern truth?

Absolute indivisibility, identical masses for every isotope of one element, and fixed spherical electron-free atoms are incorrect as modern scientific descriptions. An exam may ask for these as limitations, not as accepted facts.

1.A.1.02.18 Biological relevance: atom accounting in living systems

1.A.1.02.18.1 Enzymes do not make atoms disappear

An enzyme can accelerate a reaction by lowering its activation-energy barrier. It does not, in an ordinary biochemical reaction, create or destroy carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, or other elemental nuclei. The products must account for the elements supplied by the complete reactant system.

1.A.1.02.18.2 Overall aerobic respiration

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

Check carbon: six atoms on each side. Check hydrogen: twelve on each side. Check oxygen: 6 + 12 = 18 on the left and 12 + 6 = 18 on the right. This simplified overall equation summarizes a multi-step metabolic process but still demonstrates precise elemental bookkeeping.

1.A.1.02.18.3 Photosynthesis

One simplified net equation is 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light input and a more complicated biological mechanism. The equation balances atomic totals, but it does not by itself show which reactant supplies the atoms in a particular product. Experimental work using isotope tracers shows that oxygen gas released during oxygenic photosynthesis originates from water, not directly from carbon dioxide.

1.A.1.02.18.4 Biological polymers and chemical composition

Proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and polysaccharides contain recurring elements, but their biological functions depend on structure as well as composition. A molecular formula tells the numbers of each atom; it does not fully describe spatial arrangement, interactions, or reaction mechanisms.

1.A.1.02.18.5 Isotope tracers connect Dalton's idea with modern biology

A stable isotope such as carbon-13 can be included in nutrients and tracked through metabolic transformations using appropriate analytical methods. Dalton did not know isotopes. Modern tracer methods combine elemental identity, atom conservation, isotope distinctions, and sensitive measurements.

Big connection: Dalton's concept → chemical stoichiometry → correctly balanced biochemical reactions → quantitative metabolism and tracer research.

A reliable exam answer identifies the exact claim being tested. Ask whether the question is about historical postulates, present-day observations, fixed compound composition, or nuclear changes. Most errors arise from mixing these categories.

1.A.1.02.20 Higher-order conceptual applications

1.A.1.02.20.1 Why a mass ratio alone may not determine a molecular formula

Suppose a compound contains elements A and B in a given mass ratio. Without their accurate atomic masses and independent information about particle counts or molar mass, several models may explain the same mass data. Dalton's historical water-formula error illustrates this.

1.A.1.02.20.2 Can two different substances share a molecular formula?

Yes. Ethanol and dimethyl ether have the same molecular formula C₂H₆O but different atom connectivity and physical behavior. The law of definite proportions alone is not a method for identifying structure.

1.A.1.02.20.3 Can a balanced equation tell us whether a reaction is fast?

No. Balancing requires correct conservation of each element, but neither reaction rates nor thermodynamic feasibility follow from atom counts alone. Enzymes, temperature, concentration and reaction mechanisms are separate topics.

1.A.1.02.20.4 Is a 1:2 experimental mass ratio sufficient to prove a particular compound?

No. Such a ratio may support a simple particle combination, but the compound's identity still requires chemical evidence. Impurities, uncollected gases and variable-composition phases can affect what is measured.

1.A.1.02.20.5 Why do chemical and nuclear reactions require different language?

Chemical reactions change bonding while nuclei generally keep their identities. Nuclear processes may alter nuclei and convert one element into another. A question about radioactivity cannot be answered by treating all changes as ordinary chemical rearrangements.

1.A.1.02.21 CSIR-NET and SET practice: explained questions

1.A.1.02.21.1 Question 01 — Main contribution

Dalton's theory helped explain (A) electron orbital shapes (B) fixed composition of compounds (C) nuclear fission (D) neutron spin.

Answer B. Characteristic atoms combining in definite numbers explain repeated mass proportions; the other topics were not part of his model.

1.A.1.02.21.2 Question 02 — Limitation

Which discovery disproves the same-element/same-mass postulate? (A) Isotopes (B) Isomers (C) Enzymes (D) Solubility.

Answer A. Isotopes can differ in atomic mass while sharing proton number.

1.A.1.02.21.3 Question 03 — Multiple proportions

Two oxides contain the same 10 g of element X but 12 g and 24 g oxygen respectively. The oxygen mass ratio is (A) 1:3 (B) 1:2 (C) 3:4 (D) 4:5.

Answer B. 12:24 reduces to 1:2 with X held constant.

1.A.1.02.21.4 Question 04 — Definite proportions

Two samples of the same pure compound have A:B mass ratios of 2:5 and 8:20. Which law? (A) Definite proportions (B) Multiple proportions (C) Boyle's law (D) Charles's law.

Answer A. Both ratios simplify to 2:5.

1.A.1.02.21.5 Question 05 — Conservation

In a closed system, 13 g of A reacts completely with 7 g of B. The mass of all products is (A) 6 g (B) 13 g (C) 20 g (D) 91 g.

Answer C. 13 + 7 = 20 g, including gases and all other products.

1.A.1.02.21.6 Question 06 — Isobars

Argon-40 and calcium-40 are (A) the same element (B) different elements with the same A (C) isotopes of one element (D) an identical nuclide.

Answer B. They have different proton counts despite sharing A = 40.

1.A.1.02.21.7 Question 07 — Model detail

Which feature is absent from Dalton's solid-sphere model? (A) Discrete atoms (B) Characteristic element types (C) Electron and nuclear structure (D) Numerical combinations.

Answer C. Internal atomic constituents were discovered and studied later.

1.A.1.02.21.8 Question 08 — Escaping gas

A solid decomposes in an open flask and its residue loses mass. The strongest explanation is (A) atoms disappeared (B) gaseous products escaped (C) gases have no mass (D) ordinary chemical reactions always turn mass into measurable radiation.

Answer B. Uncollected gases must be included for total system accounting.

1.A.1.02.21.9 Question 09 — Element definition

Which quantity defines an element today? (A) Neutron count (B) Electron speed (C) Proton count (D) Net charge.

Answer C. Atomic number Z equals the number of protons.

1.A.1.02.21.10 Question 10 — Formula identity

Do two substances with molecular formula C₂H₆O have to be identical? (A) Yes (B) No, different atom connections are possible (C) Only in gases (D) Only in liquids.

Answer B. Ethanol and dimethyl ether are an example of structural isomers.

1.A.1.02.21.11 Question 11 — Normalized ratio

One compound contains 4 g X and 6 g O; another contains 6 g X and 18 g O. Their O masses per fixed X are in which ratio? (A) 1:2 (B) 2:1 (C) 1:3 (D) 3:4.

Answer A. 6/4 = 1.5 and 18/6 = 3, so 1.5:3 = 1:2.

1.A.1.02.21.12 Question 12 — Chronology

Which discovery occurred after Dalton's early theory? (A) Ancient atomism (B) Lavoisier's mass studies (C) Thomson's electron evidence (D) Proust's fixed-composition observations.

Answer C. Thomson's electron evidence dates to 1897.

1.A.1.02.21.13 Question 13 — Indivisibility

Which observation most directly contradicts absolute atomic indivisibility? (A) Existence of electrons within atoms (B) Constant composition (C) Balanced chemical equations (D) 1:2 mass ratios.

Answer A. Electrons show internal atomic structure.

1.A.1.02.21.14 Question 14 — Metabolic conservation

Why are carbon atom counts equal on both sides of the simplified aerobic respiration equation?

Answer: In ordinary biochemical reactions, carbon nuclei are rearranged, not created or destroyed. Six glucose carbon atoms correspond to six carbon atoms within six CO₂ molecules.

1.A.1.02.21.15 Question 15 — Historical uncertainty

Why could Dalton not identify every correct molecular formula from measured elemental mass ratios alone?

Answer: Mass ratios do not uniquely determine numbers of atoms per molecule. Correct relative atomic masses and independent molecular evidence are needed.

1.A.1.02.21.16 Question 16 — Nuclear transformation

A radioactive process changes a nucleus into a different element. Which Dalton-era simplification fails?

Answer: The idea that elemental atoms cannot transform into atoms of other elements. Nuclear processes can change proton number; ordinary chemical reactions still preserve elemental identity.

1.A.1.02.22 Final revision, glossary, references and next connection

1.A.1.02.22.1 Key facts for revision

  1. Dalton developed the chemical atomic theory around 1803; a major book appeared in 1808.
  2. The five major postulates concern particles, characteristic atoms, different elements, combination ratios, and rearrangement.
  3. Conservation of mass requires all products, including gases, in a closed-system accounting.
  4. Definite proportions compares pure samples of the same compound.
  5. Multiple proportions compares different compounds of the same two elements while fixing the mass of one.
  6. Atoms are divisible, and isotopes of one element need not have identical masses.
  7. Proton number determines element identity in modern theory.
  8. Dalton's model did not explain electrons, nuclei, spectra or nuclear transformations.

1.A.1.02.22.3 Glossary

Atomic theory
A scientific description of matter and its changes in terms of atoms.
Postulate
A foundational statement of a proposed theory.
Definite proportions
Constant elemental mass composition in pure samples of a specific fixed-composition compound.
Multiple proportions
Simple ratio of second-element masses combining with a fixed mass of the first element to make distinct compounds.
Conservation of mass
Approximate equality of total reactant and product masses in ordinary closed chemical reactions.
Stoichiometry
Quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.
Isotope
Nuclide sharing proton number with another but differing in neutron number.
Nonstoichiometric solid
A solid phase allowing some variation in elemental ratio, often due to structural defects.
