1. The double helix is stable, but it must also open

Imagine two copies of the same bacterial plasmid, each containing precisely the same sequence of bases. Both copies have the same molecular mass. Yet one moves rapidly through an agarose gel and another moves much more slowly. If their chemical compositions are identical, why do they behave differently? The answer is that a DNA molecule is more than a string of nucleotides: it is a flexible, twisted, electrically charged object whose overall shape and internal molecular contacts matter.

In the preceding chapters we learnt why complementary strands form the double helix and how RNA can fold into structures other than a simple duplex. Here we ask two connected questions. First, when the two strands are twisted around each other, how does the cell control the winding of an entire DNA molecule without losing the sequence? Second, why do paired strands stay together at one temperature and separate at another, and how can separated strands recognise one another again? The first question introduces topology; the second introduces molecular stability and hybridisation.

Recall the distinction between covalent and noncovalent interactions. Each DNA strand contains a continuous sugar–phosphate backbone connected by covalent phosphodiester bonds. In an ordinary duplex, the association between the two strands relies on complementary base pairing, stacking, solvent effects and interactions with ions. Heating can disrupt the association without cutting the backbones. A topoisomerase, in contrast, deliberately and transiently interrupts a covalent backbone to change a constraint that cannot be removed merely by heating or bending. These two forms of molecular change must never be confused.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to predict the direction of supercoiling caused by a moving polymerase, calculate linking number and superhelical density, interpret plasmid gels, explain why melting is cooperative, calculate simple thermal and reassociation quantities, and design a logical comparison between two hybridisation conditions. Throughout, we will distinguish what an experiment actually measures from the interpretation imposed on it.

These ideas belong to the study of nucleic-acid conformation and stability in Unit 1. Replication, transcription and chromosome organisation are introduced only as contexts; their detailed machinery is studied in the corresponding later units. The final connection to protein stability will show how the same thermodynamic principles operate even when the chemical building blocks change.

2. Why closed DNA remembers its winding

To understand topology, begin with a linear double helix whose two ends are free. If you hold one end and rotate the other, the DNA can change its winding. If you release the rotated end, the molecule can often relax by turning around its long axis. In this case, there is no requirement that the total linking of the two strands remain fixed. A linear molecule attached firmly at both ends is different: when neither end can swivel, additional rotation produces torsional stress. Topological constraints therefore arise not only from circular molecules but also from suitably constrained linear segments.

Now imagine joining the two ends of each strand separately to form a covalently closed circular duplex (cccDNA). The two strands become closed curves in three-dimensional space. They cannot be separated into two independent rings just by stretching or folding the molecule, because each ring threads through the other. To change this interlinking, at least one strand must be interrupted or a strand must pass through another. In physical DNA the latter requires a transient backbone break. This is the origin of a topological invariant.

A nick is a break in the backbone of one strand. The opposite strand can remain chemically intact, and base pairs can keep the two strands associated. However, rotation around the remaining intact strand at the nick can release torsional stress. The DNA usually becomes a relaxed open circle. Cutting both strands at one position instead converts a circular duplex into a linear duplex. Neither of these events necessarily changes the nucleotide sequence; the difference is in backbone continuity and the ability of the DNA to rotate.

The contrast between a nicked circle and a covalently closed circle is central to experiments. A relaxed covalently closed plasmid still has a definite linking number. A nicked plasmid has lost that topological constraint even if it looks similarly circular. Therefore, “relaxed DNA” does not necessarily mean “nicked DNA”. It can describe the lowest-stress member of a family of covalently closed molecules, as well as an unconstrained molecule whose average winding is near its natural preference.

Classical studies of circular viral and plasmid DNA made these differences visible through physical separation and sedimentation. One need not identify every crossing visually to conclude that the conformations differ. A useful experimental comparison uses the same DNA before and after introducing a nick: if the compact closed form becomes an open circle without changing length, a topological explanation is much stronger than a hypothesis based on base composition. The structural and mathematical treatment of this problem is developed in the teaching review by Deweese and colleagues [1] and the textbook chapter on alternative DNA conformations [2].

3. Linking number, twist and writhe

There are two distinct ways in which a duplex can look wound. First, the individual strands wrap around the local helical axis. Second, the helical axis itself can coil through space. The first is called twist (Tw); the second is called writhe (Wr). Picture a striped ribbon: the rotation of the stripe around the ribbon's centre line corresponds to twist, whereas coiling the centre line into a spatial loop corresponds to writhe. A ribbon is an analogy, not a molecular model; the relevant curves for DNA are its two strands and the centre line between them.

Linking number (Lk) is the signed integer that measures the interlinking of the two closed strands. With the usual DNA convention, an ordinary right-handed duplex has positive twist and positive linking number. A left-handed segment can contribute negative local twist. When a duplex is deformed without breaking its strands, the local twisting and the global shape can exchange contributions, but their sum cannot change. The geometric relation is:

Lk = Tw + Wr

Lk is an integer for two oriented closed curves. Tw and Wr are real-valued quantities and may be fractional. They are expressed in turns (dimensionless), not in base pairs or degrees. The equation is an exact geometric relation for a closed ribbon under consistent definitions; it is not an approximation specific to B-DNA. A full mathematical proof is not required for NET, but the conservation law can be understood by considering an infinitesimal deformation: as the axis bends and coils, a compensating rotation of the local frame changes twist by the opposite amount. Thus for an intact closed duplex, ΔTw + ΔWr = 0 whenever ΔLk = 0.

For an unconstrained B-DNA segment containing N base pairs, with a preferred helical repeat h base pairs per turn, the reference winding is approximately N/h. Under common solution conditions h is often taken as 10.5 bp per turn, although it varies with sequence, salt, temperature and ligand binding. For a planar relaxed circle, writhe is approximately zero. The ideal reference linking number Lk₀ = N/h may not be an integer. The allowed actual linking numbers of the closed circle are integers; the lowest-energy topoisomer may be near the ideal reference. In textbook numericals, N is often selected to make the quotient integral.

Consider a 2,100-bp circle with h = 10.5 bp/turn. Its ideal relaxed winding is 200 turns. Suppose it is closed with Lk = 190. It might distribute the deficit between Tw = 196 and Wr = −6. It could also adopt Tw = 193.5 and Wr = −3.5. Both conformations have Lk = 190. Neither twist nor writhe is individually conserved. If a problem states only the linking number and length, it is not generally possible to find the separate values of Tw and Wr without another measurement or assumption.

Writhe is not obtained by merely counting visible crossings in an arbitrary photograph. A planar projection can change as the molecule is viewed from a different direction; signed crossings over all viewing directions contribute to the geometric quantity. At NET level it is enough to use the sign or the numerical value supplied in a problem and avoid assigning the sign from a superficial figure-eight drawing. A closed DNA molecule can also be knotted, and two different DNA circles can be linked to each other. These are distinct topological situations: interstrand Lk describes one duplex, whereas catenation describes linking between separate duplex molecules.

4. Supercoiling stores mechanical energy

The preferred winding is determined by molecular structure and environment. If a covalently closed circle has more or fewer links than its relaxed reference, it cannot simply turn its ends to correct the difference. Instead it stores mechanical strain. The signed difference is called the linking difference:

ΔLk = Lk − Lk₀   ;   σ = ΔLk/Lk₀

The second expression is the superhelical density, σ (sigma). It is a dimensionless normalisation that permits comparison of circles of different sizes. For negative supercoiling, ΔLk and σ are negative; positive supercoiling has positive values. A 2,100-bp plasmid with Lk = 190 and Lk₀ = 200 has ΔLk = −10 and σ = −0.05. A 4,200-bp plasmid with Lk = 390 and Lk₀ = 400 has the same linking deficit but σ = −0.025. Thus the first circle is more negatively supercoiled relative to its length. The number −0.05 means five percent fewer links relative to the ideal reference, not that five percent of the base pairs are unpaired.

Negative supercoiling favours unwinding because opening a short duplex region reduces its contribution to positive helical twist. Positive supercoiling generally opposes such opening and favours increased winding or positive axis coiling. Yet neither statement says that every base pair is uniformly underwound or overwound: substantial strain can reside in writhe, and special sequences can respond differently to stress. In a bacterial chromosome, negative supercoiling can provide some of the energy needed to expose bases at an origin or promoter. It is not sufficient by itself to initiate the entire biological reaction; proteins, sequence recognition and the local chemical conditions still matter.

Why does a torsionally strained DNA bend rather than store every extra turn as local twist? Both twist and bending cost energy. A long DNA molecule can often trade some twist strain for bending and writhe at a lower total energetic cost. A very short, stiff DNA minicircle pays a larger relative bending penalty and need not distribute strain in the same way. In a stretched molecule, making a loop requires work against the stretching force, so the distribution changes again. These examples explain why the sign and magnitude of ΔLk do not uniquely specify a visible conformation.

A useful conceptual energy expression near the relaxed state is E ≈ K(ΔLk)² at fixed length and conditions, with K a positive effective elastic coefficient. This simplified quadratic describes an energy minimum at the relaxed reference and increasing strain for either sign. It does not describe every DNA structure, and K is not a universal constant. Once bubbles, writhed domains or alternative helices form, their energetic costs must also be considered. For conceptual questions, the central point is that supercoiling is an energetic state, not a new covalent chemical composition.

Supercoils can appear as an interwound, plectonemic shape, in which distant portions of the axis wind around each other, or as a toroidal/solenoidal arrangement, where the axis winds around a central object or region. Histone-associated DNA is a familiar biological example of constrained winding. A diagram of two interwound segments must not be interpreted as two separate DNA molecules; it may be two segments of one continuous duplex. Likewise, compactness does not identify the sign of the supercoil.

Now consider changing the reference winding rather than the actual linking number. A DNA-binding molecule can alter the preferred twist per base pair. If a closed plasmid initially has Lk = 200 and a ligand increases h from 10.5 to 11.0, then for 2,100 bp Lk₀ changes from 200 to about 190.91. The actual Lk is still 200 unless a strand passage occurs. Relative to the new reference, ΔLk is about +9.09. The molecule has acquired positive torsional strain without creating extra links. This logic is crucial to understanding intercalator experiments, where the relaxed reference moves even though the backbone remains intact.

5. Topoisomerases solve the winding problem

DNA molecules cannot change a topological invariant by ordinary bending. Cells overcome this problem using topoisomerases. These enzymes make transient breaks, rearrange the DNA and reseal the broken phosphodiester bonds. The reaction must be reversible at the chemical level: leaving a break unrepaired would convert useful control of topology into DNA damage. Active-site tyrosine residues form temporary covalent phosphotyrosyl bonds with DNA, conserving the bond energy for later resealing. The enzyme holds the broken ends in a controlled geometry rather than simply acting like a nonspecific nuclease.

Type I enzymes interrupt one strand. Type IA enzymes pass another single strand through the transient gap; an elementary strand-passage event changes Lk by one. Bacterial topoisomerase I usually relaxes negative supercoils, since its strand-passage mechanism requires suitable exposed single-stranded DNA. Type IB enzymes, including eukaryotic nuclear topoisomerase I, produce a nick and permit controlled rotation of the broken strand around the intact strand. Several turns can be released during one cleavage–religation encounter, so an unqualified claim that every type I catalytic cycle changes Lk by exactly one is too broad. Most type I relaxation reactions do not require ATP, although reverse gyrase is a notable ATP-dependent type IA-related exception that introduces positive supercoils.

Type II enzymes interrupt both strands in a duplex segment, transport another duplex segment through that opening, and reseal the gate. This changes the linking number of an ordinary closed duplex by two per complete strand-passage event. ATP is used to coordinate and drive the cycle. It is not simply used to glue two DNA ends together; the transient enzyme–DNA covalent intermediates preserve chemical energy for resealing. ATP-dependent conformational transitions allow the enzyme to capture a transported segment, open the appropriate gate, move the segment through and reset. The textbook treatment of topoisomerase action during DNA replication provides the connection between this chemistry and chromosome mechanics [3].

It is useful to follow the chemistry of the reversible break in greater detail. The oxygen of the catalytic tyrosine attacks a backbone phosphate, transferring the phosphodiester linkage from a DNA sugar to the enzyme. A free DNA hydroxyl group is generated at the other side of the break. During resealing, the hydroxyl attacks the protein-linked phosphate, restoring the DNA phosphodiester bond and releasing the tyrosine. In type IA enzymes and type II enzymes the transient linkage is generally to the 5′ end; type IB enzymes form a 3′-phosphotyrosyl linkage. This distinction explains why these enzymes can hold specific DNA ends without randomly degrading the molecule. ATP in the type II cycle powers the mechanical conformational transitions rather than replacing the reversible transesterification reaction.

Bacterial DNA gyrase is a specialised type II enzyme capable of introducing negative supercoils into relaxed DNA at the expense of ATP. It wraps DNA in a configuration that favours a particular direction of strand passage. Gyrase can also relieve positive supercoils generated during replication and transcription. Bacterial topoisomerase IV is another type II enzyme, important for unlinking daughter DNA molecules and also able to relax supercoils. Eukaryotic topoisomerase II is particularly important for relieving entanglement and separating daughter chromosomes. It is not synonymous with bacterial gyrase; a type II enzyme need not introduce negative supercoils.

Imagine a relaxed 3,150-bp plasmid with Lk₀ = 300. If gyrase lowers Lk to 282, then ΔLk = −18 and σ = −0.06. In the idealised absence of other enzymes and assuming every productive gyrase passage lowers Lk by two, nine strand-passage events are needed. A type IA relaxing reaction would then require eighteen single-link increases to restore Lk = 300 if no other event occurred. This comparison is about the change per passage, not the number of ATP molecules consumed or the number of enzyme binding encounters.

Type II strand passage has an important consequence beyond supercoiling. Imagine two newly replicated circular DNA molecules linked like two rings of a chain. Rotation at a nick does not automatically transport one intact duplex through the other. The duplex-passage mechanism of a type II topoisomerase can separate them. This is why chromosome segregation can fail even when local DNA twist is not unusually high. The cell must manage linking between strands, knots within a duplex and links between different duplexes.

For examination purposes, distinguish an inhibitor that prevents enzyme catalysis from a poison that stabilises the normally transient cleaved DNA–enzyme complex. Both can interfere with topology control, but the second mechanism accumulates protein-linked breaks. Examples include camptothecin acting on eukaryotic topoisomerase I and fluoroquinolones acting on bacterial gyrase or topoisomerase IV. The relevant mechanistic point is persistence of the cleavage intermediate, not drug dosing or clinical use.

6. How moving enzymes generate supercoils

A polymerase follows the helical path of DNA. In an ideal freely rotating system, the enzyme or DNA can rotate as the polymerase progresses, avoiding sustained torsional stress. In a cell, rotation can be hindered by the attached machinery, nascent RNA, membrane association, protein barriers or chromosome organisation. If the polymerase travels along a helical template but cannot rotate freely, DNA ahead of the moving complex becomes overwound while DNA behind it becomes underwound. This is the twin-supercoiled-domain model of transcription.

The two domains can coexist in one molecule because their opposite linking deviations can compensate globally. If four turns of positive strain accumulate ahead and four turns of negative strain behind, the total linking number of an otherwise intact closed plasmid need not change. The distribution is local. To change the actual total linking number, a topoisomerase must act or a strand must be broken. Therefore a protein moving along DNA can create torsion without directly changing total Lk.

The replication fork faces a related problem. Separation of the parental strands reduces their helical linking in the opened region, while the unopened DNA ahead tends to accumulate positive torsional strain if rotation is restricted. Topoisomerases relieve this stress; if the fork rotates, some linking can instead be transferred into intertwining of newly formed daughter duplexes. Decatenation then becomes necessary. The detailed replication machinery belongs in Unit 3, but the geometric principle already explains why a helicase cannot efficiently unwind unlimited constrained DNA on its own.

In eukaryotic chromatin, DNA wrapping around histone octamers constrains negative superhelical turns. If a circular DNA molecule is allowed to assemble nucleosomes and topoisomerases relax compensating torsion in the remaining DNA, removing the histones afterward can reveal a negative linking difference. One must not equate every geometric wrap of the nucleosome to one unit of ΔLk, because local DNA twist and the path of entry and exit also contribute. The experimentally observed change is often near −1 per nucleosome under standard conditions, not a universal identity. This provides a bridge to chromatin organisation without requiring memorisation of specialised structural parameters.

Negative supercoiling can also favour locally melted DNA or alternative conformations such as left-handed Z-DNA in suitable alternating purine–pyrimidine sequences. Changing a segment from positive right-handed twist to negative left-handed twist can absorb part of a negative linking deficit. The surrounding DNA and protein environment determine whether this transition is favourable. A sequence capable of Z-DNA formation does not necessarily adopt it in every cell or every supercoiled molecule. Here topology connects directly to molecular stability.

7. Reading topology experiments

Electrophoresis separates DNA molecules partly according to how easily they move through a porous gel. For equal-length plasmids in a typical agarose gel without special intercalating conditions, a compact supercoiled circle often migrates faster than a relaxed nicked circle, while a linearised form often lies between them. These patterns arise because shape influences friction and the ability to pass through pores. They are not a general molecular-weight ladder for circular DNA. Gel concentration, voltage, DNA length, intercalating dyes and topology can alter the relative mobility, so a well-designed experiment includes known controls.

A single-nick treatment provides an open-circle control; a restriction enzyme that cuts the plasmid once provides a linear-size control. A relaxed covalently closed preparation may be a distribution of several topoisomers, each differing from the next by one in Lk. A gel with sufficient resolving power may separate such a family into individual bands. The existence of a band ladder shows discrete linking numbers, not a collection of plasmids with lengths differing by one nucleotide.

Intercalating agents insert between adjacent base pairs and usually decrease the preferred helical twist. At a fixed linking number a negatively supercoiled circle can become less negative relative to the new reference, pass through a relaxed state, and eventually become positively supercoiled. The resulting mobility pattern can be nonmonotonic. An ordinary one-dimensional gel may not distinguish equally compact positive and negative topoisomers. Running the same DNA in a second dimension under a different intercalator concentration can reveal the different trajectories; the additional condition supplies information absent in the first dimension. It would be incorrect to assign supercoil sign from a single fast band.

As a reasoning exercise, suppose two plasmid preparations show different bands before treatment but become the same-size linear band after a single restriction cut. The simplest hypothesis is different conformations of the same-length DNA, provided digestion is complete. It is not proof that their sequences are identical: two different sequences can have the same length and the same restriction site. If nicking also eliminates the difference, the evidence for torsional topology becomes stronger. This distinction between a physical measurement and sequence identity will reappear when we examine melting temperatures.

8. What makes a nucleic-acid duplex stable?

Each base pair contains polar groups that can form hydrogen bonds. A standard A–T pair has two and a G–C pair has three. These interactions help ensure the correct geometry and chemical specificity of recognition. However, isolated bases in water also form hydrogen bonds with water. Forming a duplex replaces some base–water interactions with base–base interactions, and the resulting free-energy change cannot be found simply by multiplying the number of hydrogen bonds by a fixed bond energy. Hydrogen bonding is essential to the pattern of pairing, but it is not an adequate explanation of the full stability of DNA.

Base stacking describes the association of neighbouring aromatic bases along the helix. Bases have relatively nonpolar surfaces and polar edges. Stacking reduces the exposure of the aromatic surfaces to water and creates favourable dispersion and other electronic interactions between neighbouring bases. The exact balance includes solvent reorganisation, van der Waals and electrostatic effects. Adjacent base-pair steps differ in geometry and chemical composition, so their stability contributions depend on which bases are next to which. The nearest-neighbour approach [4] treats those adjacent steps as the useful units of duplex thermodynamics.

Consider two duplexes of equal length and equal GC percentage. In one, GC pairs are clustered together; in the other, GC and AT pairs alternate. The total number of standard base-pair hydrogen bonds can be identical, but the types and frequencies of neighbouring steps differ. Their melting temperatures need not be equal. Even if their average melting temperatures happen to be similar, their local melting profiles can differ: a long AT-rich region may open at a lower temperature than a GC-rich block in the same molecule. Consequently, GC content is a useful first predictor, not a full description of sequence-dependent stability.

The negatively charged phosphate groups also matter. The backbones of the two strands approach each other in the duplex, creating an electrostatic penalty. Positive counterions in the solution screen the repulsion. An increase in monovalent salt concentration generally stabilises ordinary duplex DNA relative to separated strands in the usual experimental range. Magnesium ions can be effective at lower concentrations than monovalent ions, and are particularly important in compact RNA folds, but their effects cannot generally be calculated by replacing each Mg²⁺ with exactly two Na⁺ ions. Specific binding, ion competition and activity matter.

Water is an active participant, not an empty background. Duplex formation reorganises hydration around the grooves and backbone. The separated strands can also form transient stacked structures and hairpins. Thus “single stranded” does not mean “completely random coil”. Temperature, pH and chemical additives can change the balance of these competing states. Stability is a property of the molecule together with its environment.

These mechanisms distinguish chemical stability from conformational stability. DNA has a relatively resistant deoxyribose backbone under many ordinary conditions, whereas RNA possesses a 2′-hydroxyl group that can participate in alkaline cleavage. Nevertheless, an RNA duplex or a compact RNA fold can be thermodynamically very stable because of stacking, pairing and ion-mediated tertiary interactions. A chemically labile RNA backbone and a high thermal melting temperature are not contradictory observations.

9. Free energy and the temperature-dependent balance

The simplest model treats duplex formation as a reversible association: A + B ⇌ AB, where A and B are complementary strands and AB is the duplex. At constant temperature and pressure, the standard Gibbs free energy of formation is ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. Formation of a well-matched duplex is usually exothermic (negative ΔH°), owing to favourable interactions and solvent effects. Association and ordering generally reduce entropy (negative ΔS°), though the complete entropy change also includes solvent and ions. Because −TΔS° is positive when ΔS° is negative, raising temperature often makes association less favourable.

ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°   ;   ΔG° = −RT ln K

T is absolute temperature in kelvin, R is the gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), and K is the dimensionless equilibrium association constant defined using activities. A negative standard free energy corresponds to K > 1. The actual reaction free energy is ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, where Q is the reaction quotient. At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K. This is not the same as saying that ΔG° must be zero at every equilibrium or at every melting midpoint. Concentration enters through Q.

Suppose a simplified oligonucleotide duplex has ΔH° = −240 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −0.70 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, taken as constant over a limited temperature interval. At 298 K, ΔG° = −240 + 208.6 = −31.4 kJ mol⁻¹. At 350 K it is −240 + 245 = +5 kJ mol⁻¹. The zero of the standard free energy in this simplified model occurs at 342.86 K, or about 69.7°C. That temperature is not automatically the measured Tm for a dilute two-strand association, because the equilibrium fraction depends on strand concentration. This example illustrates the sign logic rather than providing parameters for a real sequence.

For a non-self-complementary two-state duplex, suppose the total analytical concentration of strands is Cₜ, the strands are present in equal amounts, and concentrations are expressed relative to the standard concentration c° = 1 mol L⁻¹. At half association, [AB] = Cₜ/4 and [A] = [B] = Cₜ/4. The ideal association constant K = ([AB]/c°)/(([A]/c°)([B]/c°)) therefore becomes 4c°/Cₜ. Substituting −RT ln K for ΔG° and rearranging gives:

Tₘ = ΔH° / [ΔS° + R ln(Cₜ/(4c°))]

Use consistent energy units and convert the resulting kelvin value to Celsius by subtracting 273.15. This expression assumes a two-state equilibrium, equal amounts of distinct complementary strands, negligible activity corrections beyond those already incorporated in parameters, and approximately temperature-independent ΔH° and ΔS°. Self-complementary duplexes have a different concentration/symmetry treatment; intramolecular hairpins do not have this bimolecular concentration dependence. Long genomic DNA may contain many independently melting domains and is not described well by one short-duplex two-state formula.

For the illustrative thermodynamic parameters above at Cₜ = 4 μM, Cₜ/(4c°) = 10⁻⁶. With R = 0.008314 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, the logarithmic term is −0.11487 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so the denominator is −0.81487 and Tm ≈ 294.53 K = 21.38°C. If Cₜ is increased to 40 μM, the denominator becomes −0.79572 and Tm ≈ 301.61 K = 28.46°C. This increase follows because higher strand concentration makes association statistically more favourable. The hypothetical parameters were chosen to illustrate the mathematics, not to represent a standard biological duplex.

For an actual short DNA duplex, nearest-neighbour enthalpies and entropies are summed across adjacent base-pair steps, with initiation and other applicable corrections, before a concentration and salt model is used [4]. This is why a numerical result from a sequence calculator depends on its model and input conditions. Do not mix enthalpies from one parameter set with a salt correction calibrated for another without justification. At exam level, understand why neighbour order and solution composition are required, rather than memorising a complete research parameter table.

10. Denaturation, melting curves and hyperchromicity

When DNA is heated, thermal fluctuations increasingly disrupt paired and stacked regions. The strands begin to separate in susceptible locations; base pairs often open in groups rather than as completely independent units. This cooperativity arises because opening one region removes neighbouring stacking contacts and creates a boundary from which further opening can proceed. A long molecule can contain several domains with different melting temperatures. Local breathing occurs below the overall melting midpoint and does not necessarily imply complete dissociation.

The melting transition can be followed by ultraviolet absorbance. Nucleic-acid bases absorb near 260 nm. In an ordered duplex the electronic interactions between stacked bases reduce absorbance relative to extensively disrupted strands. As stacking is lost, absorbance increases: this is the hyperchromic effect. Conversely, formation of a duplex generally decreases absorbance (hypochromicity). The exact percentage depends on sequence, temperature, baseline and measurement conditions; a textbook value of about 30–40% is a rough illustration, not a universal constant. The classic studies of Marmur and Doty established the use of thermal absorbance profiles to estimate base composition [5].

Melting temperature, Tm, is the midpoint of the monitored transition under specified conditions. In a simple two-state duplex model it corresponds to half of the strands being associated in duplexes. In a long heterogeneous molecule, an absorbance midpoint is an operational measure of average loss of ordered structure; it does not necessarily mean that half of all molecules are completely single stranded. In quantitative work, native and denatured baselines may themselves vary with temperature, so the baseline-corrected fraction is preferred.

fmelted ≈ (AT − Anative)/(Adenatured − Anative)

Assume that the baseline values are 0.80 for native DNA and 1.08 for fully denatured DNA at the temperature range of interest. The absorbance at the ideal midpoint is 0.94. A reading of 1.01 gives (1.01 − 0.80)/(1.08 − 0.80) = 0.75, or 75% of the monitored transition. The overall hyperchromic increase relative to native DNA is (1.08 − 0.80)/0.80 × 100 = 35%. Neither calculation measures the number of covalent backbone breaks.

A sharp transition often indicates strong cooperativity over the monitored domain. Multiple shoulders or peaks in a derivative plot (dA/dT against temperature) can indicate more than one melting domain or molecular population. Broadening can also arise from heterogeneous fragment sizes or nonequilibrium heating. A melting curve does not by itself identify which exact bases opened; that would require additional structural or sequence-specific information. If heating is too rapid, the curve may depend on rate and not represent equilibrium. On cooling, a curve that fails to retrace the heating curve can indicate slow reannealing or kinetic trapping.

Thermal denaturation must be distinguished from hydrolysis and chemical damage. Ordinary controlled heating disrupts mainly noncovalent organisation. Strong acid can cause depurination; high pH disrupts normal base pairing, and prolonged harsh treatments can damage DNA. Alkaline conditions especially favour RNA backbone cleavage via the 2′-OH. The disappearance of a duplex signal is therefore not sufficient evidence that an intact, reusable strand remains; the conditions and an independent integrity check matter.

11. Predicting melting temperature without overgeneralising

Under comparable conditions, a GC-rich long duplex usually has a higher melting temperature than an AT-rich duplex. This empirical trend reflects both pairing and sequence-dependent stacking. The word “comparable” is essential: if the GC-rich sample is measured in much lower salt, or has a destabilising mismatch, the order can reverse. GC percentage alone is insufficient to compare arbitrary duplexes.

The frequently taught Wallace approximation for short DNA oligomers is Tm (°C) ≈ 2(A + T) + 4(G + C), with the symbols representing the counts of bases in one strand. It is a rough classroom rule for short oligonucleotides under conventional conditions, not a universal melting equation for genomic DNA, highly unusual sequences, modified bases or arbitrary ionic concentrations. A short strand 5′-ATGCGTAC-3′ contains A = 2, T = 2, G = 2, C = 2; the estimate is 2 × 4 + 4 × 4 = 24°C. This calculation is deliberately illustrative. A more reliable prediction needs a nearest-neighbour model and assay conditions, particularly for very short duplexes.

For sufficiently long, ordinary DNA under suitable conditions, a traditional empirical relation is Tm ≈ 81.5 + 16.6 log₁₀[Na⁺] + 0.41(%GC) − 500/N, where sodium concentration is in mol/L and N is duplex length in base pairs. This is the zero-formamide, no-mismatch version of a commonly used empirical hybridisation estimate [10]. Published empirical formulas have different coefficients and length corrections depending on calibration; use the particular formula stated in an exam. The expression assumes an appropriate range of monovalent salt and ordinary matched DNA; it is not a general formula for RNA, mixed Mg²⁺ buffers or a strongly mismatched oligonucleotide.

As an example, for a 1,000-bp DNA at 0.10 M Na⁺ and 50% GC, the equation gives 81.5 − 16.6 + 20.5 − 0.5 = 84.9°C. Under the same model, lowering Na⁺ tenfold to 0.01 M lowers Tm by 16.6°C, giving 68.3°C. Increasing GC content from 50% to 60% at the original salt raises the estimate by 4.1°C to 89.0°C. This is a comparison within a specified empirical model; it is not a measured value. One must enter 50 for 50% GC, not 0.50, and use molar salt concentration, not millimolar.

Length matters because short duplexes have a large fraction of terminal bases, which can fray. Longer duplexes generally become more stable until the simple length effect saturates, but domain structure then becomes increasingly important. A mismatch usually destabilises a duplex by disrupting normal pairing and stacking; its impact depends on identity, neighbouring bases and position. A terminal mismatch can behave differently from a central mismatch. Some mismatches can form alternative hydrogen bonds, so no single temperature penalty applies to every mismatch.

Solvents such as formamide and urea generally reduce duplex stability under hybridisation conditions by altering the relative solvation and interactions of paired and separated states. A more destabilising solution may allow hybridisation at lower physical temperature, but the stringency still depends on temperature relative to the Tm in that solution. Extreme pH alters the protonation patterns needed for normal pairing. Counterions, ligands and topology modify the balance as discussed earlier; the variable of interest must be isolated in a fair comparison.

There is one especially useful distinction for RNA. A single RNA strand can fold into an intramolecular hairpin, so its opening does not require separation into two freely diffusing molecules. In an ideal dilute solution, the hairpin equilibrium therefore has no simple dependence on total RNA concentration. A two-strand RNA duplex does have a concentration dependence. RNA can also have several cooperative tertiary transitions that respond strongly to Mg²⁺; a single UV midpoint may describe only part of the folding process. This is how the secondary and tertiary structure concepts from Node 1.B.04 connect to stability.

12. How separated strands find each other again

DNA denaturation does not erase the sequence if the backbones remain intact. The separated strands retain complementary base arrangements. When the solution returns to conditions favourable for pairing, Brownian motion causes random collisions. Most collisions are unproductive because the strands meet in the wrong orientation or with unrelated sequence regions in contact. Occasionally, complementary segments align long enough to form a stable nucleus of a few base pairs. This nucleation event is often the slow step in reassociation. Once a stable nucleus forms, additional adjacent bases pair more readily, and the duplex can extend in a zipper-like process.

The zipper analogy has limits. The nucleation may occur internally, not at a strand end, and secondary structures or mismatches can interrupt propagation. A strand containing a self-complementary sequence may form a hairpin before finding the correct partner. Highly repeated sequences may pair in several registers and create imperfect products. Thus favourable equilibrium does not guarantee immediate or perfectly faithful renaturation.

Temperature has two opposing effects on reassociation. Near the melting temperature, many potential nuclei dissociate before they can extend. Far below the melting temperature, mismatched contacts and competing secondary structures can persist and trap strands. Under classical long-DNA reassociation conditions, an optimum often occurs roughly 20–25°C below the relevant Tm, although this is not a universal annealing prescription [6]. Salt helps shield backbone repulsion and supports stable nucleation. A cooling protocol that permits molecular sampling can yield a more complete native duplex than rapid chilling under otherwise similar conditions, but the result depends on strand length, concentration, sequence and the timescale.

In the early 1960s, studies of thermal renaturation established that separated DNA strands could recover double-helical properties when given appropriate conditions [6]. The finding supported the view that a strand's sequence contains the information needed for complementary recognition. Recovered UV absorbance and a melting profile similar to native DNA were useful evidence of re-formation, but exact sequence identity and complete recovery require appropriate controls. The physical principle became central to nucleic-acid probes, genome analysis and many molecular biology methods.

For covalently closed circular DNA, the distinction between denaturation and complete separation is especially important. Even if many hydrogen bonds are disrupted, closed complementary rings remain topologically linked. They cannot diffuse away as two independent rings unless their topology permits it or a backbone break occurs. Their reassociation can therefore differ from that of two unconstrained linear strands. A simple bimolecular kinetic model is intended for freely diffusing complementary molecules, not for every closed DNA conformation.

13. Reassociation kinetics and Cot analysis

In an ideal solution of equimolar complementary strands, reassociation starts when two suitable molecules collide. The rate is proportional to the concentration of each unpaired partner. Let C represent the concentration of unpaired strands in one of the equally abundant complementary populations, in mol/L, and let k be the second-order reassociation rate constant in L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹. The complementary population has the same concentration C. Each successful reaction removes one strand from each population, hence:

−dC/dt = kC²

Separating variables gives dC/C² = −k dt. Integrating from C₀ at t = 0 to C at time t gives −1/C + 1/C₀ = −kt. Rearranging yields 1/C − 1/C₀ = kt. Divide by 1/C₀ and invert to obtain the fraction remaining single stranded:

C/C₀ = 1/(1 + kC₀t)

The reassociated fraction in this ideal model is 1 − C/C₀ = kC₀t/(1 + kC₀t). At half reassociation C/C₀ = 1/2, so kC₀t½ = 1 and C₀t½ = 1/k. The product C₀t is written Cot (or C₀t). If C₀ is measured as molar nucleotide concentration rather than molar strand concentration, the numerical k is defined consistently with that convention and fragment length; classical genomic Cot curves commonly use molar nucleotide concentration. Never combine constants from different concentration conventions without conversion.

Suppose C₀ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ mol L⁻¹ and k = 5 × 10⁴ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ in the one-population concentration convention. The product kC₀ = 0.10 s⁻¹ and the half-time is 10 s. At 30 s, kC₀t = 3 and C/C₀ = 1/4: 75% of each strand population has paired. Doubling C₀ to 4 × 10⁻⁶ M halves the half-time to 5 s. The half-Cot remains 2 × 10⁻⁵ mol s L⁻¹ as long as k and the conditions stay the same.

A different situation occurs when one complementary strand is present in vast excess, as in some probe hybridisations. The abundant partner concentration changes very little during the reaction. Then −dC/dt ≈ kB₀C, with B₀ effectively constant, and the observed kinetics are pseudo-first-order: C/C₀ = e−kB₀t. Its half-time is ln 2/(kB₀), not 1/(kC₀). The same physical nucleation chemistry can therefore yield different apparent reaction orders under different concentration conditions. Do not apply a simple second-order Cot formula merely because DNA is involved.

Now consider two genomic DNA preparations of equal total nucleotide concentration, equal fragment lengths and identical solution conditions. In a small, low-complexity genome, a particular sequence forms a larger fraction of all DNA fragments. Complementary partners encounter one another relatively often. In a larger, more complex genome, the same total nucleotide concentration is spread among many distinct sequences, so the effective concentration of each particular complementary partner is lower. The more complex sequence population reassociates more slowly. This relationship was exploited in classic analyses of repetitive and unique DNA [7].

A repeated sequence present in many copies has a higher effective complementary-partner concentration than a unique sequence at the same total DNA concentration. Thus repetitive DNA usually reassociates at lower Cot, whereas unique or low-copy DNA reassociates at higher Cot. On a graph with log₁₀(Cot) on the horizontal axis and fraction reassociated on the vertical axis, highly repetitive DNA forms an early rise, moderately repetitive DNA an intermediate rise and unique DNA a late rise. If the vertical axis instead shows fraction remaining single stranded, the curve falls from one toward zero. The order of the kinetic components stays the same; only the direction of the plotted response changes.

Suppose an ideal graph reaches a first plateau at 20% reassociation, a second at 55% and eventually 100%. The fast component accounts for approximately 20% of the DNA; the middle component accounts for 55 − 20 = 35%; the slow component accounts for 100 − 55 = 45%. Plateau positions show the amount, while horizontal positions describe reassociation rate. If the fast component has half-Cot 0.02 mol s L⁻¹ and the unique component has half-Cot 20 mol s L⁻¹ in a common component-normalised comparison, the fast component has a rate constant 20/0.02 = 1,000 times larger. This does not automatically establish an exact copy number without considering component concentration, sequence complexity and the model used.

Cot experiments reveal kinetic sequence complexity rather than the exact sequence of a genome. Fragment size, base composition, reassociation temperature, salt and repeated sequences of imperfect identity can shift the curves. Under controlled comparisons, the approximate half-Cot for a homogeneous unique-sequence population is proportional to its sequence complexity. If sample X has half-Cot 2 and sample Y has half-Cot 8 under matched conditions, Y has about four times the kinetic complexity of X in that simplified comparison. This is not a direct measure of organismal complexity or gene number. Some large genomes contain many repeated elements that inflate total DNA without proportionally increasing unique sequence complexity.

14. Hybridisation, complementarity and stringency

Hybridisation is the association of complementary nucleic-acid strands, whether DNA–DNA, DNA–RNA or RNA–RNA. In a probe-based experiment, a labelled nucleic acid is provided to recognise a complementary target. A target containing the matching sequence allows a stable duplex to form under suitable conditions. The label reports the presence or location of the bound probe, rather than creating the molecular specificity itself. DNA–RNA hybrids generally adopt an A-like geometry and have stability rules that differ from DNA–DNA duplexes. A DNA-only melting formula should not be transferred to hybrids without checking its applicability.

Consider two targets: one perfectly complementary to a 20-base probe and another containing a central mismatch. Both may hybridise at a relatively low temperature if salt is high enough. The mismatch typically lowers the melting temperature of the imperfect duplex. If the temperature is increased to a range where the perfect duplex remains largely intact but the imperfect duplex dissociates, discrimination improves. This is called increased hybridisation stringency. Stringency describes how demanding the conditions are with respect to complementarity, not simply how hot the experiment is.

Increasing temperature, decreasing salt concentration, or adding a destabilising reagent at otherwise comparable conditions can increase stringency. Each intervention reduces the stability of duplexes; the aim is to select conditions where the correct duplex survives better than unwanted ones. Conditions that are too stringent can eliminate even a perfect match, producing a false negative. Conditions that are too permissive allow partial matches to remain and can produce false positives. The appropriate temperature depends on sequence, length, salt and chemical additives.

As a thought experiment, suppose under the same buffer conditions a perfectly matched probe–target duplex has Tm = 65°C and the mismatched duplex has Tm = 53°C. A comparison at 45°C is likely to retain both appreciably. At 59°C, the perfect match is below its midpoint whereas the mismatch is above its midpoint, so discrimination is expected to improve. At 75°C, both are above their midpoints and the desired signal may be lost. Exact fractions cannot be predicted from midpoints alone; curve slopes and equilibrium conditions are also needed.

Hybridisation is the recognition principle behind Southern blotting (DNA target), Northern blotting (RNA target), in situ hybridisation and primer binding in amplification methods. Those methods belong to the techniques unit, but the physical mechanism is common: sequences recognise complementary sequences according to their association free energies and kinetics. A probe may fail to bind a target not because the target sequence is absent, but because it is inaccessible in a folded structure, the concentration is too low, the time is too short, or the conditions are inappropriate. A negative result needs a positive control for the assay.

Two DNA samples can have the same base composition and melting temperature while differing in base order. Conversely, two complementary sequences can produce duplexes of different thermal stability in different buffers. Melting is a collective physical property, whereas hybridisation under controlled conditions can test sequence complementarity over a particular region. Even a positive hybridisation result need not establish full genomic identity. The probe length and the stringency define what level of similarity the experiment can distinguish. These principles also explain why comparing melting temperatures cannot be a substitute for sequence analysis.

15. Putting topology, stability and kinetics together

Return to the two plasmids from the opening question. A difference in mobility can be caused by linking number and shape without any difference in base composition. If both plasmids are nicked and become open circles, the topological constraint is lost. If the linearised plasmids have equal length, that rules out a simple size explanation for the original mobility difference but still does not prove sequence identity. Heating both to obtain melting profiles tests their thermal stability under the selected conditions. Hybridising with a particular probe tests complementarity to that probe. The three observations answer different questions.

Topology and stability nevertheless influence each other. If local opening decreases twist in a closed duplex, conservation of Lk requires compensatory changes elsewhere in twist or writhe. Negative torsional strain can be partially relieved by local opening; positive torsional strain tends to resist it. Changing salt, temperature or ligand binding can shift the preferred twist and the energy of base pairing without directly changing Lk. Topoisomerases can then adjust the actual linking number, changing the energy landscape available to the DNA. This coupling makes the helix responsive to the changing needs of a cell while preserving its covalent sequence.

Equilibrium and kinetics must likewise be distinguished. A duplex may be very stable once formed yet associate slowly because complementary strands rarely encounter one another or because secondary structures impede nucleation. Two sequence populations may have equal melting temperatures but very different reassociation rates because one contains more copies of the same sequence. Faster association is not by itself evidence of stronger bonds. A rate constant measures how quickly the reaction proceeds; an equilibrium constant measures the distribution of states when equilibrium has been established.

These distinctions transfer to proteins, the next subunit. Like nucleic acids, proteins contain a covalent backbone and form higher-order structures through many weak interactions. Their native states arise from a balance of enthalpic and entropic contributions and may unfold cooperatively. In many cases proteins can recover structure after denaturation, but kinetic traps, aggregation and incorrect interactions can prevent recovery. Protein folding does not generally involve the same two-strand linking number as circular DNA. The shared lesson is to reason from the molecular constraints and free-energy landscape rather than memorise an isolated definition of stability.

16. Part B — original conceptual questions

These questions are written at NET/SET practice level; they are not claimed as previous-year official questions. Choose the best answer and then open the explanation.

1. Which quantity remains invariant when a covalently closed DNA duplex is continuously deformed? A. Tw only; B. Wr only; C. Lk; D. σ in all solvents.

Answer and reasoning

C. Lk cannot change without strand interruption/passage. A and B can exchange at constant Lk. D is wrong because the relaxed reference Lk₀ can change with environment.

2. Which molecule can readily release torsional strain by swivelling without a topoisomerase? A. Intact closed plasmid; B. Nicked circular duplex; C. Constrained linear duplex; D. Covalently closed viral DNA.

Answer and reasoning

B. The nick permits rotation around the intact backbone. A and D have closed strands; C has restricted ends.

3. Which statement about type II topoisomerases is correct? A. All introduce negative supercoils; B. They pass a duplex through a transient double-strand gate; C. They cannot unlink circles; D. They consume ATP to form every final phosphodiester bond directly.

Answer and reasoning

B. Duplex passage is the defining mechanism. A is specific to gyrase, not all type II enzymes. C contradicts decatenation. D misstates the role of ATP; reversible phosphotyrosyl chemistry supports resealing.

4. A260 increases during ordinary thermal DNA denaturation because: A. New bases are synthesised; B. Phosphates are removed; C. Base stacking is disrupted; D. DNA concentration necessarily increases.

Answer and reasoning

C. The electronic environment of bases changes. No synthesis (A), dephosphorylation (B) or change in concentration (D) is required.

5. Which change generally increases hybridisation stringency at fixed probe and buffer composition except for the changed variable? A. Lower temperature; B. Higher salt; C. Higher temperature; D. Lower formamide.

Answer and reasoning

C. Raising temperature favours dissociation of weaker matches. A, B and D generally stabilise pairing and lower stringency, all else equal.

6. In a Cot experiment, which DNA population generally reassociates first under matched conditions? A. Highly repetitive; B. Unique sequence; C. Largest distinct sequence complexity; D. Every population simultaneously.

Answer and reasoning

A. More complementary copies increase productive encounter probability. B and C generally have fewer matching partners per total concentration. D ignores kinetic complexity.

7. Two matched duplexes have identical GC content and length. Which conclusion follows? A. Identical Tm is guaranteed; B. Identical sequence is guaranteed; C. Nearest-neighbour composition may differ; D. Identical hybridisation specificity is guaranteed.

Answer and reasoning

C. Base order determines neighbouring steps. A, B and D do not follow from counts of bases.

8. Assertion: Negative supercoiling may promote local DNA opening. Reason: Opening a right-handed segment reduces its twist contribution. A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Answer and reasoning

A. At fixed Lk local opening can relieve part of negative torsional stress. The effect is conditional, not proof that the entire duplex is denatured.

9. Which association lacks a simple bimolecular total-strand concentration dependence in the ideal dilute limit? A. Two distinct DNA strands; B. DNA–RNA hybrid; C. Intramolecular RNA hairpin; D. Two complementary RNA strands.

Answer and reasoning

C. Hairpin folding involves one molecule. The other choices involve two separately diffusing partners, so mass action affects the equilibrium.

10. A fast plasmid band in a standard agarose gel proves which claim? A. It is positively supercoiled; B. It is negatively supercoiled; C. It is shorter than all other bands; D. None of these conclusions follows without controls.

Answer and reasoning

D. Compactness, size, dye and gel conditions influence mobility. Sign is not established by one fast band; circular and linear size comparisons require controls.

17. Part C — original analytical and data-interpretation problems

Write the invariant, specify the concentration or reference convention, and work through the signs before opening each solution. The numerical datasets are illustrative and not presented as experimental measurements.

1. A 5,250-bp closed plasmid has h = 10.5 and Lk = 475. Find ΔLk and σ. If Tw = 489.5, find Wr.

Answer and reasoning

Lk₀ = 5250/10.5 = 500. ΔLk = −25; σ = −25/500 = −0.05. Wr = 475 − 489.5 = −14.5. The fractional value is valid because Wr is not an integer invariant.

2. An intact closed duplex changes Tw from 198 to 194. Initial Wr = −8. Predict final Wr. Can this change occur without an enzyme?

Answer and reasoning

Initial Lk = 190. Final Wr = 190 − 194 = −4. Yes, an intact molecule can exchange twist and writhe continuously at fixed Lk; an enzyme is needed for a linking-number change, not necessarily for this deformation.

3. A 4,200-bp circle with Lk₀ = 400 has Lk = 382. How many ideal negative-supercoiling gyrase passages would produce it from Lk = 400? How many ideal IA relaxation passages restore it?

Answer and reasoning

ΔLk = −18. Gyrase lowers Lk by two per passage: nine. Type IA increases Lk by one for relaxation: eighteen. These are productive strand passages, not ATP or binding-event counts.

4. A 2,100-bp closed molecule has Lk = 190. A ligand changes its preferred repeat from 10.5 to 11.0 bp/turn. Calculate the new reference and sign of strain.

Answer and reasoning

New Lk₀ = 2100/11 = 190.91. ΔLk ≈ −0.91, σ ≈ −0.00476. It remains slightly negatively strained relative to the new reference; actual Lk is unchanged. Rounding Lk₀ to 191 hides the distinction between an ideal real reference and actual integer Lk.

5. A sample has native A260 = 0.72 and denatured A260 = 0.96. At 80°C A260 is 0.90. Find the apparent fraction melted and the full hyperchromic percentage.

Answer and reasoning

f = (0.90 − 0.72)/(0.96 − 0.72) = 0.75. Full increase = 0.24/0.72 × 100 ≈ 33.3%. Baselines assumed constant. The calculation is not a count of intact strands.

6. Using the stated long-DNA empirical expression, compare 1,000-bp samples A (40% GC, 0.10 M Na⁺) and B (60% GC, 0.01 M Na⁺). Which has higher estimated Tm?

Answer and reasoning

A: 81.5 − 16.6 + 16.4 − 0.5 = 80.8°C. B: 81.5 − 33.2 + 24.6 − 0.5 = 72.4°C. A is estimated 8.4°C higher; the salt difference outweighs GC in this model.

7. A hypothetical two-state heteroduplex has ΔH° = −200 kJ/mol and ΔS° = −0.60 kJ/(mol K). Find standard ΔG° at 300 K; is the measured Tm necessarily 333.3 K?

Answer and reasoning

ΔG° = −200 + 180 = −20 kJ/mol. 333.3 K is the zero of the standard free energy under constant parameters. The measured bimolecular midpoint depends on strand concentration, so the claim is not justified.

8. A complementary DNA population obeys second-order reassociation with k = 2 × 10⁵ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, C₀ = 5 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L. Find half-time and fraction paired after 4 s.

Answer and reasoning

kC₀ = 1 s⁻¹. Half-time = 1 s. At 4 s unpaired fraction = 1/(1 + 4) = 0.20, hence 80% paired. The concentration convention is the same for k and C₀.

9. In a Cot curve plotted as fraction reassociated, plateaus are 0.15, 0.50 and 0.95 within the measured range. Estimate fractions of the resolved components; what can be said about the final 5%?

Answer and reasoning

Fast 15%, middle 35%, late 45%, and 5% has not reassociated within the measurement window. It might be very slow, noncomplementary, damaged or trapped. Calling it unique DNA with certainty is not warranted.

10. A probe binds a perfect target with Tm 68°C and a mismatched target with Tm 57°C under identical conditions. Choose the most informative screening temperature among 45, 62 and 75°C.

Answer and reasoning

62°C is between the two midpoints, favouring discrimination while retaining more desired duplex. 45°C may retain both; 75°C may lose both. Exact signal ratios depend on curve shape and kinetics.

11. Two plasmids migrate differently, but both become the same linear band after one complete restriction cut. What additional experiment distinguishes torsional topology from sequence differences?

Answer and reasoning

Introduce a single nick into each and compare open-circle mobility with controls; a topology-sensitive gel under altered intercalator conditions can provide additional evidence. Sequence identity requires a sequence-specific assay or sequencing. Equal linear size alone does not prove identical sequence.

12. During transcription of a topologically constrained DNA molecule, four turns of overwinding accumulate in front and four of underwinding behind. Has total Lk changed? What if a topoisomerase relaxes only the positive domain by removing four links?

Answer and reasoning

Polymerase movement alone redistributes strain with net ΔLk zero. If a topoisomerase removes four actual links from the intact domain and no other compensation occurs, whole-molecule Lk falls by four. Negative strain may then remain.

13. Two DNA samples with equal GC content melt at nearly the same Tm, but one renatures at 100-fold lower Cot. Explain.

Answer and reasoning

Thermal stability and sequence complexity are distinct. The fast sample may contain repeated sequence copies that raise effective partner concentration. Compare equal fragment sizes, salt and temperature before inferring repetition. Similar Tm does not establish sequence identity.

14. One strand of a duplex is provided in huge excess. A trace complementary strand disappears with an apparent half-time independent of its own initial concentration. Is this inconsistent with bimolecular nucleation?

Answer and reasoning

No. The excess partner concentration stays approximately constant, giving a pseudo-first-order rate kobs = kB₀ and t½ = ln 2/kobs. Changing the excess partner concentration changes the observed rate.

15. A closed plasmid shows a change in the UV signal during heating but remains topologically interlinked. Explain why that observation is possible.

Answer and reasoning

Heat disrupts pairing and stacking, increasing UV absorbance. The covalently closed curves cannot diffuse into independent rings merely by losing noncovalent contacts. Lk is conserved if backbones are intact. Hyperchromicity is therefore not evidence of topological unlinking.

18. Consolidation and references

When solving a topology problem, first establish whether both strands are closed or whether the ends are constrained. Identify the preferred helical repeat and distinguish actual Lk from the ideal relaxed reference. Compute ΔLk and σ; use Tw + Wr to solve the missing geometric term. Ask whether a proposed change requires strand passage. In a stability problem, identify whether the measurement is thermal absorbance, association equilibrium, or reassociation kinetics. Keep temperature, salt, concentration, sequence and topology separate rather than treating all of them as GC-content effects.
