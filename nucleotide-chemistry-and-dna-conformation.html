1. From a sugar molecule to hereditary information

Imagine opening a cell and searching for a molecule that can hold a message, survive years of storage, be copied with extraordinary precision, and yet become available to enzymes within seconds. A simple lipid membrane can separate compartments, but cannot provide a long, arbitrary sequence of letters. A polysaccharide can store energy or display molecular patterns, but its ordinary biological role is not a complementary information archive. DNA solves this problem using a small alphabet attached to a repetitive, water-compatible backbone. The alphabet can change without changing the fundamental chemical architecture of the chain.

The preceding carbohydrate node introduced pentoses and their stereochemistry; the lipid node introduced amphipathic molecules and self-assembly. Both ideas reappear here. DNA uses a specific pentose, while aromatic bases stack and charged phosphates remain exposed to water. The chemical interactions from sub-unit 1.A explain why the chain can be both robust and reversibly separable. We will build the molecule from its atoms, derive its pairing and directionality, then ask how environment, sequence and topology remodel the helix.

Learning outcomes

  • Distinguish bases, nucleosides and nucleotides by chemical connectivity and nomenclature.
  • Predict polymer polarity, products of bond formation and cleavage, and the consequences of modifying the sugar or base.
  • Infer complementarity, composition, length and hydrogen-bond counts, while recognising the limits of simple models.
  • Compare A-, B- and Z-DNA mechanistically; explain grooves, hydration, stacking, melting and reassociation.
  • Use linking number, twist and writhe to solve topological problems, and interpret common structural measurements.

We will introduce synthesis, repair and packaging only where they clarify structure. Complete pathways of nucleotide metabolism, replication, transcription, chromatin and RNA function belong in their respective later lessons.

2. How evidence led to the double helix

In 1869 Friedrich Miescher isolated a phosphorus-rich substance from cell nuclei, naming it nuclein. The material was unlike familiar proteins, but its role was not yet known. Chemical work eventually established its sugar, base and phosphate components. Levene's tetranucleotide hypothesis treated DNA as a repetitive collection of four equally represented bases; the simplicity of that proposal initially made DNA seem an unlikely carrier of diverse information.

Griffith's bacterial transformation experiments in 1928 demonstrated that a heritable property could pass between cells. Avery, MacLeod and McCarty identified DNA as the transforming principle in 1944, and the 1952 Hershey–Chase bacteriophage experiments provided further evidence of DNA's genetic role. These studies addressed what material carries information, not the exact three-dimensional structure.

Chargaff measured base compositions and found that the proportions of A and T were approximately equal, as were G and C, in ordinary double-stranded DNA; overall GC content varied among species. Franklin and Gosling's fiber diffraction work and Wilkins's related structural studies constrained helix geometry and hydration-dependent forms. Diffraction revealed periodicities and the characteristic cross-like pattern of a helix, not a literal photograph of individual bases. Watson and Crick combined chemical geometry, pairing and diffraction constraints to propose the double helix in 1953.

Notice the logic: chemical composition specifies possible building blocks, diffraction restricts geometry, and base ratios restrict pairing. No single result alone uniquely derives the entire structure. The complementarity of the final model also suggested a way to copy the molecule. Later Meselson–Stahl density-label experiments tested semiconservative replication, a distinct experimental question.

Exam distinction

Chargaff measured composition; X-ray diffraction constrained structure; transformation and phage experiments addressed genetic material; Meselson–Stahl tested the mode of replication.

3. Nitrogenous bases: a chemically readable alphabet

DNA bases are nitrogen-containing aromatic heterocycles, with conjugated electrons distributed across nearly planar rings. Adenine (A) and guanine (G) are purines: a fused six-membered and five-membered ring. Cytosine (C), thymine (T) and uracil (U) are pyrimidines with one six-membered ring. DNA commonly uses A, G, C and T; RNA ordinarily uses A, G, C and U. The different arrangements of carbonyl oxygens, ring nitrogens and amino groups provide different hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor patterns.

Purines attach to sugar through N9 and ordinary pyrimidines through N1. The attachment replaces a hydrogen at the bonding nitrogen; the ring atom numbering remains distinct from sugar numbering. The bases are weakly basic or acidic in different contexts, not strong bases in the everyday sense. At physiological pH their canonical Watson–Crick edges predominantly occur in neutral states, whereas backbone phosphates make DNA strongly anionic.

The aromatic rings absorb ultraviolet light, especially around 260 nm. Absorption depends on conjugation, stacking and the chemical environment; it is not a direct count of hydrogen bonds. Aromatic stacking also affects base solubility. A nucleotide has a highly hydrated phosphate even though isolated bases have relatively limited water solubility.

Tautomerism, protonation and unusual pairing

In the prevalent forms, A and C present amino groups and G, T and U have keto/lactam functionalities. Rare proton rearrangements can alter donor–acceptor patterns (amino–imino or keto–enol tautomerism). A transient unusual tautomer or ionised base may mimic a correct pairing geometry and allow misincorporation. If a mismatch escapes proofreading and repair, another replication cycle can fix a mutation. The rare state is not itself a permanent mutation. pH, local environment and neighbouring bases can alter the relative abundance of these states.

A transition exchanges purine for purine (A↔G) or pyrimidine for pyrimidine (C↔T); a transversion exchanges the ring classes. These terms describe base substitutions, not the chemical process that created them. The complementary base pair must ultimately change for a stable substitution in duplex DNA.

4. Pentose stereochemistry and the meaning of prime numbers

Ribose is an aldopentose. In nucleic acids, the sugar occurs mainly as a β-D-ribofuranosyl unit (RNA) or a β-D-2-deoxyribofuranosyl unit (DNA). The five-membered sugar ring contains four carbons and an oxygen; C5′ extends outside it. The prime mark differentiates sugar positions from base positions. C1′ binds the base, C2′ distinguishes ribose from deoxyribose, C3′ participates in elongation, and C5′ normally bears phosphate. C4′ connects the ring to C5′. Ring oxygen is O4′.

Deoxy does not mean the sugar contains no oxygen. Only the 2′ hydroxyl is replaced by hydrogen; the ring oxygen and other oxygen-containing groups remain. The β designation describes stereochemistry at the anomeric carbon relative to the reference substituent of D-ribose. It is not the same as saying a base is in an anti conformation; β/α describes configuration at the anomeric center, whereas syn/anti describes rotation about the glycosidic bond.

The furanose ring is not flat. Atoms move out of its mean plane to reduce eclipsing and optimise packing. C2′-endo and C3′-endo are shorthand for important puckers. B-DNA predominantly has a C2′-endo-like sugar geometry; A-form duplexes favor C3′-endo. The RNA 2′-OH changes hydration, steric restrictions and accessible conformations, helping explain why ordinary RNA duplexes favor the A family rather than B.

RNA's 2′ oxygen can attack the adjacent phosphate intramolecularly when deprotonated, producing a 2′,3′ cyclic phosphate intermediate and a 5′-OH end. Hydrolysis opens the cyclic phosphate to a mixture of terminal 2′- or 3′-phosphates under typical alkaline conditions. DNA lacks this neighbouring nucleophile and is much more resistant to ordinary alkaline backbone cleavage. Strong alkali can still denature DNA by disrupting base pairing. Backbone chemical stability and duplex stability are different concepts.

Predict the effect

Replacing a 3′-OH with H prevents normal chain extension at that end. Replacing a 2′-OH with H suppresses the classic RNA intramolecular cleavage mechanism without eliminating all chemical damage.

5. From base to nucleoside to nucleotide

Linking a nitrogenous base to C1′ of the pentose produces a nucleoside through a β-N-glycosidic bond. The name of a purine nucleoside generally ends in -osine and a pyrimidine nucleoside in -idine. Adding phosphate to a sugar hydroxyl gives a nucleotide. A nucleotide can have one or several phosphate groups, and the attachment position must be specified when it is not the customary 5′ position.

AMP means adenosine monophosphate, not adenine monophosphate in strict nomenclature: it contains the base and ribose. Adenosine itself contains no phosphate. dATP contains adenine, deoxyribose and three phosphates. ATP and dATP differ at the 2′ carbon, not in the number of phosphates. The base T is a chemical methylated U; the sugar determines whether a thymine-containing nucleoside is a ribo- or deoxyribonucleoside. Thus thymine is not chemically forbidden in RNA, nor uracil in DNA.

The β-N-glycosidic bond can hydrolyse under certain conditions, especially purine loss from DNA under acid-promoted conditions. Losing a base leaves an abasic sugar-phosphate site rather than immediately removing the entire nucleotide from the backbone. A notable exception to ordinary N linkage is pseudouridine, an RNA nucleoside in which the sugar is C-linked to C5 of uracil; its folding significance belongs in 1.B.04.

6. Phosphate chemistry, charge and activated precursors

A nucleoside 5′-monophosphate has a phosphate monoester (P–O–C), whereas ADP and ATP have phosphate–phosphate anhydride linkages in addition to the ester. In a triphosphate, α is closest to the sugar, β next, and γ most distant. The phosphate monoester typically carries close to two negative charges around neutral pH; each backbone phosphodiester carries approximately one. Free ATP is substantially anionic at physiological pH and often exists as a magnesium complex. Protonation and metal coordination modify effective charge, so an exact numerical charge requires a specified chemical species and pH.

Phosphoryl transfer is important because phosphate is kinetically persistent under ordinary conditions while enzymes can selectively catalyse transfer. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate is favourable under normal cellular conditions because of differences in hydration, charge distribution, resonance and concentration between reactants and products. Breaking a bond alone requires energy; the net reaction can release free energy because new bonds and interactions form. In nucleotide incorporation, the α phosphate is retained in the DNA chain, while the β and γ phosphates leave together as pyrophosphate (PPi).

Not every nucleotide is a nucleic acid precursor. ATP and GTP transfer energy; cAMP and cGMP serve as cyclic second messengers; NAD⁺ and FAD contain nucleotide-derived moieties in redox cofactors; CoA has an adenosine-containing group; UDP-glucose and CDP-diacylglycerol are activated intermediates that connect to carbohydrate and lipid chemistry. A 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate links a single phosphate to two hydroxyl positions on the same sugar. This intramolecular diester is not a three-phosphate molecule.

Cells regulate purine and pyrimidine pools and use ribonucleotide reductase to supply deoxyribonucleotide precursors. dUTPase limits dUTP accumulation and thymidylate synthase contributes to dTMP production. Pool balance influences misincorporation rates; these examples explain why nucleotides are chemically related but not freely interchangeable in vivo. Detailed metabolic reactions belong in the metabolism nodes.

7. The phosphodiester bond creates a directional chain

Now join two nucleotides. A phosphate bridges the 3′ oxygen of one sugar and the 5′ oxygen of the next. The result is a 3′–5′ phosphodiester, C3′–O–P–O–C5′. The bases are attached laterally to C1′ and are not covalently linked to their complementary partners. Repetition produces an alternating sugar–phosphate backbone whose sequence is determined by the side-chain bases.

By convention a sequence is written left to right from 5′ to 3′ unless clearly marked otherwise. The 5′ end may carry a phosphate, triphosphate or hydroxyl depending on synthesis and processing; the 3′ end may also be modified. The position-based terminology remains valid regardless of end chemistry. A closed circular strand has local polarity but no free termini.

How polymerases make the bond

In ordinary DNA polymerisation, the primer 3′-OH attacks the α phosphorus of an incoming dNTP. Enzyme-bound divalent ions (typically Mg²⁺) facilitate nucleophile activation and stabilise developing negative charge; pyrophosphate departs. Hydrolysis of PPi by cellular pyrophosphatases helps drive net synthesis. The new nucleotide now presents the next 3′-OH, so the nascent strand grows 5′→3′ and the template is read 3′→5′. The reaction does not attach an incoming nucleotide to the 5′ end of the growing strand in normal DNA polymerase catalysis.

For a single linear strand of n nucleotide residues, the number of internal internucleotide phosphodiesters is n−1. For a circular strand without breaks, there are n. In a linear duplex of N base pairs, there are 2N nucleotide residues and 2(N−1) internal phosphodiesters. Terminal phosphate monoesters are not internucleotide diesters. A nick breaks one backbone bond without necessarily breaking the partner strand; a gap removes one or more residues; a double-strand break interrupts both strands.

DNases and RNases differ in substrate specificity and reaction chemistry. Endonucleases cleave internal linkages, while exonucleases remove residues from ends with defined polarity or substrate preference. Cleavage can yield 3′-OH/5′-phosphate or other terminal chemistries depending on the enzyme. A ligase joins a suitable 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate, generally using ATP or NAD⁺ depending on organism and enzyme. Base-pair recognition can hold a nick together, but covalent continuity requires the ligation reaction.

8. Why complementary bases fit: geometry and hydrogen bonding

Place a purine and a pyrimidine opposite one another. The fused-ring base occupies more space, while its single-ring partner occupies less. Canonical pairs keep the separation between the sugar attachment sites broadly similar. Purine–purine pairs tend to be too wide and pyrimidine–pyrimidine too narrow for an undistorted standard Watson–Crick duplex; mismatches can still exist by using alternative geometries.

In the ordinary A–T pair, the adenine N6 amino donor contacts thymine O4, and thymine N3-H donates to adenine N1. In G–C, guanine N1-H donates to cytosine N3, guanine N2-H donates to cytosine O2, and cytosine N4-H donates to guanine O6. These are two and three canonical hydrogen bonds, respectively. The exact geometry determines pairing specificity; simply finding two donor and acceptor groups is not enough.

Complementarity forces an antiparallel arrangement in normal Watson–Crick DNA. If the top strand is 5′-AGTCC-3′, its paired strand directly underneath is 3′-TCAGG-5′. The partner written in conventional 5′→3′ notation is 5′-GGACT-3′ (the reverse complement). The complement and reverse complement are not identical operations. This is crucial when selecting primers or reading a template sequence.

Hoogsteen pairing uses a different edge of a purine, often with a syn glycosidic orientation, and can occur in transient duplex conformations or triplex arrangements. A G–T mismatch may adopt a wobble-like geometry. Some pairs occur in protonated states. The Watson–Crick model describes the dominant paired structure of standard DNA, not the only chemically possible interaction.

Three different questions

How many hydrogen bonds? Count canonical pairs. Which pair is more stable? Consider sequence, salt, temperature and stacking. Which strand is the template? Identify its orientation and the direction of synthesis.

9. Chargaff relationships and quantitative reasoning

For a fully complementary double-stranded molecule with only standard bases, A=T and G=C when all residues in both strands are counted. Therefore A+G=T+C, total purines equal total pyrimidines, and the percentage of each pair class adds to 100%. There is no requirement that A=G or that A+T=G+C. Individual strands may have unequal A and T; some long genomes show approximate within-strand parity for statistical reasons, but this is not the exact duplex rule. Single-stranded viral genomes and RNA do not obey the exact duplex composition constraints.

Suppose a duplex has 1,000 bp and 36% GC. It contains 2,000 nucleotides; G+C = 720 residues, so G=C=360 residues and A=T=640. There are 360 GC pairs and 640 AT pairs, not 720 GC pairs. Canonical hydrogen bonds = 3(360)+2(640)=2,360. Internal phosphodiesters for a linear molecule = 2(1,000−1)=1,998. Its approximate B-DNA contour length is 1,000×0.34 nm = 340 nm. The numbers answer different questions and must not be mixed.

If g is the GC fraction expressed between 0 and 1 and N is the number of base pairs, GC pairs = Ng and AT pairs = N(1−g). The canonical hydrogen-bond count H = N(2+g). Conversely, g = H/N−2. The molecular mass of long dsDNA is approximately 660 Da per bp (a common biochemical conversion, dependent on composition and salt convention). A 3,000-bp molecule therefore has an approximate molar mass of 1.98×10⁶ g/mol. For precise oligonucleotide masses, sum actual residue masses and account for terminal chemistry.

A single strand contains 30% A, 20% T, 10% G and 40% C. The complementary strand contains 20% A, 30% T, 40% G and 10% C. Averaged over both equal-length strands, A=T=25% and G=C=25%. The individual strands differ substantially, even though the duplex obeys Chargaff's rule. If a single-strand composition is given, its GC fraction equals the duplex GC fraction after complete complementary pairing, but A or G alone cannot usually determine that fraction.

10. B-DNA: from a twisted ladder to a three-dimensional molecule

A ladder helps visualise pairing, but a real double helix is more than a ladder rotated around its axis. Base pairs are stacked roughly perpendicular to the axis in B-DNA, and consecutive pairs rotate by an average of about 34.3° for a 10.5-bp turn. The axial rise is approximately 0.34 nm per base pair, giving a helical pitch of about 3.57 nm. Classic idealised textbook models often use 10 bp per turn and 3.4 nm pitch. These are consistent approximations under different conventions and conditions; use values supplied by an examination question.

B-DNA is right-handed and roughly 2 nm wide. The two phosphate-rich backbones spiral around stacked bases. The base attachment sites do not sit exactly opposite one another around the helix, so the backbones define a wider major groove and a narrower minor groove. Both grooves provide chemical surfaces exposed to solvent. A protein can read a sequence in a groove without physically pulling apart the base pairs.

The glycosidic bond rotation is commonly called χ (chi). Most ordinary B-DNA nucleotides are anti: the bulk of the base points away from the sugar rather than over it. The sugar pucker and several backbone torsion angles determine how the phosphates connect. Local sequences can alter twist, roll, tilt, slide and rise. Consequently a real sequence is not a perfect uniform cylinder. A-tracts, for example, can produce sequence-dependent bending and distinctive minor-groove shape.

Reading grooves

In the major groove, the exposed chemical patterns for AT, TA, GC and CG orientations differ. In the usual shorthand A=acceptor, D=donor, M=methyl, H=nonpolar hydrogen, the AT orientation is ADAM and TA is MADA; GC is AADH and CG HDAA. The minor groove has fewer distinguishable patterns: AT and TA are commonly described as AHA, while GC and CG are ADA. These symbols represent groups along a chosen direction, not the nucleotide sequence itself. Proteins also recognise shape, hydration and electrostatic potential, so minor-groove binding is not sequence-independent by definition.

Direct readout uses contacts with exposed chemical groups; indirect or shape readout exploits sequence-dependent deformability, groove width and electrostatics. The phosphate backbone contributes many interactions independent of exact base identity. A transcription factor can recognise a particular sequence without breaking every internal hydrogen bond.

11. Why the duplex forms: stacking, water, ions and entropy

The free energy of duplex formation is not simply the sum of isolated base-pair hydrogen-bond energies. Before association, bases can hydrogen-bond with water; forming a duplex replaces some base–water contacts with base–base contacts. Base stacking buries aromatic surfaces, improves dispersion and other electronic interactions, and reorganises surrounding water. Stacking and pairing together produce the characteristic stability and selectivity of the helix.

Two negatively charged backbones repel one another. Mobile counterions and hydrated metal ions screen this electrostatic repulsion; monovalent salt generally stabilises a normal duplex relative to low salt, while Mg²⁺ can have pronounced effects at lower concentrations. Ion effects are not adequately described by counting positive and negative charges: counterion atmosphere, activity, hydration, sequence and concentration matter. Very unusual ionic conditions can favor alternative conformations or condensation.

Association restricts relative translational, rotational and conformational freedom of the two strands, an entropic cost. Solvent and ion rearrangements may compensate some of that cost. At fixed conditions ΔG=ΔH−TΔS, where ΔH is enthalpy change, ΔS is entropy change and T absolute temperature. Duplex formation is typically enthalpically favorable and entropically opposed, while heating can favor the separated state. These signs are useful for canonical models but actual values depend on reference states and sequence.

Hydrogen bonds explain a great deal about specificity but not all of stability. GC-rich sequences often melt at higher temperatures than comparable AT-rich ones, yet two sequences of identical GC percentage may have different stabilities due to the arrangement of neighbouring pairs, end fraying, mismatches, concentration and salt. A GC pair does not contribute a fixed three-unit free energy. Nearest-neighbour models address this by assigning thermodynamic contributions to adjacent base-pair steps plus initiation, end and symmetry corrections.

A necessary distinction

The covalent backbone preserves sequence through moderate heating. Noncovalent interactions stabilise the duplex reversibly. DNA's capacity to store and copy information depends on separating these two kinds of stability.

12. A-, B- and Z-DNA: a conformational comparison

DNA is flexible enough to occupy different structural families. Early fiber diffraction distinguished hydrated B-DNA from dehydrated A-DNA. Later atomic structural studies demonstrated Z-DNA, a left-handed helix with a repeating dinucleotide geometry. A form is also typical for ordinary double-stranded RNA and much of the geometry of RNA–DNA hybrids. A helix name specifies conformation, not a different chemical alphabet.

A-DNA is wider and shorter per base pair because the base pairs are displaced from the axis and inclined relative to it. Its central region and grooves look different from B-DNA. Reduced water activity can make A-DNA energetically favourable. The ribose 2′-OH favors A-type geometry in RNA duplexes, so the structure is biologically important even though ordinary genomic dsDNA is predominantly B-like.

Z-DNA is narrower and extends farther per base pair. In a common alternating d(CG) sequence, guanine adopts syn while cytosine remains anti, and consecutive sugar puckers differ. The phosphate backbone follows a zig-zag course, giving Z its name. The repeating unit is a dinucleotide rather than a single equivalent nucleotide. Left-handedness reverses the sign of helical twist, but both strands still have their original 5′→3′ covalent direction and retain predominantly Watson–Crick pairing.

High salt can favor Z formation in appropriate experimental sequences; negative torsional stress can also supply a driving force under cellular conditions. Cytosine methylation and Z-binding proteins can favor Z in certain contexts. B–Z junctions cost energy, so a single CG pair does not automatically make a Z segment. At a junction, local geometry is disrupted, including possible base extrusion. Not all DNA becomes Z under stress, and an alternating sequence alone does not guarantee it.

Other fiber conformations such as C, D and E have been described under special hydration, salt or sequence conditions; for CSIR-NET, the key is to understand conformational plasticity rather than memorise every rare fiber state. The listed numbers are approximate family averages, not immutable dimensions of every local step.

Common trap

A-DNA is right-handed, not left-handed. A DNA–RNA hybrid is usually A-like, not automatically B-DNA. Z-DNA is left-handed but its nucleotides have not become mirror-image L-sugars.

13. Sequence-dependent alternatives beyond ordinary duplex DNA

Ordinary B-DNA is the reference state, but a sequence can encode a propensity to adopt local alternatives. Inverted repeats can form intramolecular hairpins when a strand becomes available; in double-stranded, torsionally stressed DNA, both strands can extrude complementary hairpins to form a cruciform. A DNA palindrome in molecular biology is a sequence equal to its reverse complement, such as 5′-GAATTC-3′. It is not necessarily identical when its letters are merely reversed.

G-rich sequences can form G-quadruplexes: four guanines interact in a planar G-quartet using Hoogsteen-type interactions; multiple quartets stack around a central channel often stabilised by K⁺. Parallel, antiparallel and hybrid strand arrangements are possible. An intramolecular quadruplex can use four G-rich tracts from one strand; it need not require four separate DNA molecules. Telomeric and selected regulatory sequences can have such propensity. Formation depends on competition with normal duplexes, ions, loop length and proteins.

C-rich sequences can form an i-motif through intercalated hemiprotonated C·C⁺ pairs, favored in acidic conditions and in certain cellular environments with additional stabilising factors. A triplex can arise when a third strand recognises a homopurine-rich duplex region in the major groove through Hoogsteen or reverse-Hoogsteen interactions. Intramolecular triplex H-DNA can occur in suitable mirror-repeat sequences under torsional and pH conditions. A mirror repeat is not the same as an inverted repeat: mirror repeats reverse sequence along the same strand; inverted repeats use reverse complementarity.

Slipped-strand DNA can form when repeated sequences misalign, leaving loops of unpaired residues. Such conformations provide a chemical explanation for the susceptibility of repeats to expansion or contraction, although actual mutation depends on replication and repair processes. An R-loop is a three-stranded arrangement consisting of an RNA–DNA hybrid and displaced single-stranded DNA; it is not a triple-helical Hoogsteen triplex. Local melting, bubbles, forks and junctions similarly expose different surfaces to proteins.

14. Denaturation, melting curves and ultraviolet absorbance

Heat can separate complementary strands without cleaving their phosphodiester backbones under normal melting-assay conditions. Increasing temperature promotes local opening, often starting at less stable sites or ends, and eventually separates the strands. Extreme pH disrupts base protonation patterns and may also cause chemical damage if exposure is sufficiently harsh. Urea and formamide can alter solvent interactions and destabilise duplexes. Denaturation is not the same as depurination or nuclease digestion.

At 260 nm, double-stranded DNA absorbs less strongly than the same residues in a less stacked state. Upon melting, absorption rises: the hyperchromic effect. Hypochromicity is the lower absorbance associated with ordered base stacking. The magnitude is commonly around 30–40% for dsDNA melting, but depends on sequence and measurement conditions. A plot of absorbance against temperature shows a transition region; heterogeneous long molecules may exhibit multiple melting domains rather than one ideal sigmoid.

For a simple two-state duplex at a specified strand concentration, Tm is the temperature at which half of the relevant strands are duplexed and half are single-stranded. In long heterogeneous DNA, it is an operational midpoint of the melting transition, often related to the fraction of base pairs melted. Lower and upper absorbance baselines can have temperature dependence; a midpoint should be determined after baseline correction rather than assuming the numerical average of two arbitrary measurements.

Factors that generally increase Tm for a comparable ordinary duplex include higher GC content, stronger nearest-neighbour stacking, increased stabilising salt and higher concentration of complementary strands for a bimolecular transition. Mismatches, destabilising modifications and denaturing solvents usually decrease Tm. A single mismatch near the center can have a larger effect than one at a fraying end, but sequence context matters. Short duplexes show concentration dependence; a unimolecular hairpin has a different concentration relation.

Estimating Tm and understanding the approximations

A rough Wallace rule for short oligonucleotides is Tm(°C) ≈ 2(A+T)+4(G+C), where the letters denote counts in one strand. A 12-mer with seven AT and five GC residues has estimated Tm ≈ 14+20=34°C. This is not a universal law or a suitable formula for long DNA, modified bases, extreme salt or detailed PCR design.

For long duplex DNA, a traditional empirical estimate under specified monovalent salt conditions is Tm(°C) ≈ 81.5 + 16.6 log₁₀[Na⁺] + 0.41(%GC) − 500/N, where concentration is in mol/L and N is duplex length in bp. Formula variants use different length corrections and ranges. Do not apply it to arbitrary Mg²⁺ buffers or short oligonucleotides. For N=1000 and 50% GC at 0.1 M Na⁺, the stated approximation gives 81.5−16.6+20.5−0.5=84.9°C; reducing salt tenfold gives about 68.3°C within the formula, not a universal experimentally measured shift.

For a simple non-self-complementary equimolar oligonucleotide duplex under a nearest-neighbour model, Tm(K) ≈ ΔH°/[ΔS°+R ln(Ct/4)] with appropriate salt correction; Ct is total strand concentration in mol/L (referenced to 1 M), R=1.987 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when ΔH is in cal/mol and ΔS in cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Subtract 273.15 for Celsius. Self-complementary sequences use a different concentration/symmetry convention. The equation follows from the association equilibrium at half duplexed strands; it assumes a two-state system and consistent parameter sets.

Example of an optical measurement: A260 changes from 0.60 to 0.81 after melting. Relative hyperchromicity = (0.81−0.60)/0.60×100=35%. If baselines are constant, the midpoint absorbance is 0.705. A rise does not imply additional nucleotides were created: the electronic environment changed.

15. Reassociation, hybridisation and the role of sequence complexity

When denatured strands are cooled under suitable conditions, complementary regions can find one another. The first successful short paired region is a nucleation event; additional adjacent pairs form through zipper-like propagation. Cooling too abruptly or using inappropriate salt may trap competing secondary structures or prevent efficient pairing. The same sequence specificity allows DNA–DNA hybridisation, RNA–DNA hybridisation, probe detection and primer annealing.

For a simplified second-order reassociation of complementary strands, write −dC/dt=kC² with C as the concentration of unpaired target strands under the chosen convention. Integration gives 1/C−1/C₀=kt, hence C/C₀=1/(1+kC₀t). At half reassociation, C₀t₁/₂=1/k. Actual Cot experiments usually specify nucleotide concentration and fragment length, and the effective rate is influenced by sequence complexity, salt, temperature and fragment size. The equation is an ideal model, not a universal rate for all genomic fractions.

Repeated sequences find partners quickly because many matching copies are available. Highly repetitive DNA therefore reassociates at lower Cot; moderately repetitive DNA follows, and unique or low-copy sequences reassociate later. Complexity is not simply total genome length: two equal-sized genomes with different repeat proportions can produce different reassociation profiles. Sheared fragments, temperature and ionic strength must be comparable for meaningful comparisons.

Stringency describes how demanding hybridisation conditions are for complementarity. Raising temperature, lowering salt or using denaturing solvents generally raises stringency by destabilising imperfect duplexes. At overly high stringency, even correct duplexes can be lost. Under lower stringency, mismatched hybrids can persist, which may be useful for finding related sequences but also increases nonspecific detection. A probe can be designed to bind a target region in antiparallel orientation.

16. DNA topology: linking number, twist and writhe

For a linear duplex with freely rotating ends, unwinding can be relieved by end rotation. A covalently closed circular duplex (or a constrained domain with effectively fixed ends) cannot freely change the number of strand linkages. Imagine drawing two closed loops that wind around one another: without strand passage or breakage, their topological linking is invariant. This distinguishes geometry, which can vary continuously, from topology, which has discrete constraints.

For a closed duplex, Lk = Tw + Wr. Lk is linking number, an integer for two closed strands; Tw is the integrated local winding of the strands about the duplex axis; Wr is the signed coiling of that axis in space. Tw and Wr need not be integers. Right-handed B-DNA local twist is positive under the usual convention. The relation applies to the entire closed ribbon or a properly defined constrained domain, not to an arbitrary open DNA fragment without specifying closure.

Relaxed linking number Lk₀≈N/h₀, where N is the number of base pairs and h₀ is the relaxed helical repeat (often 10.5 bp/turn for B-DNA). ΔLk=Lk−Lk₀; superhelical density σ=ΔLk/Lk₀. For many practical problems Lk₀ is treated as a real reference even when N/h₀ is not an integer; actual covalently closed topoisomers have integer Lk. Negative ΔLk indicates underlinking and positive ΔLk overlinking relative to that relaxed state.

Suppose a closed plasmid contains 2,100 bp with a relaxed repeat of 10.5 bp/turn. Lk₀=200. If Lk=188, ΔLk=−12 and σ=−0.06. One possible configuration is Tw=194 and Wr=−6, another Tw=197 and Wr=−9. Both have Lk=188. The actual partition depends on torsional and bending elasticity, solution conditions and bound proteins. If the question instead says Tw=192 and Wr=−4, their sum is also 188; no change in Lk has occurred.

Underwinding can promote local opening of the helix, making strand separation easier at some origins and promoters; overwinding generally opposes opening. A plectoneme is an interwound duplex axis; a toroidal arrangement wraps around a surface or axis, as in DNA wrapping around histones. The same sign of topological change can be accommodated through different geometries. Do not identify the handedness of a local DNA helix from the appearance of a supercoil: B-DNA can remain right-handed while its axis is supercoiled.

Negative torsion can also favor unwinding into a bubble or formation of Z-DNA or cruciforms when the sequence and energetics permit. Melting a local region lowers its contribution to positive twist; with total Lk fixed, the rest of the molecule must compensate by changing twist or writhe. A left-handed Z segment similarly absorbs negative linking difference. In a nicked circle, strand rotation can dissipate torsional stress, removing the invariant associated with two closed strands.

17. Topoisomerases and experimental signatures of DNA shape

Helicases separate strands but do not by themselves change the linking number of an intact closed molecule. A progressing replication fork or RNA polymerase can generate torsional stress ahead and behind it when rotation is constrained. Topoisomerases use controlled, transient phosphodiester cleavage and resealing to change linking number or disentangle molecules. A catalytic tyrosine forms a reversible covalent phosphotyrosyl intermediate, conserving bond energy and allowing resealing.

Type I enzymes generally do not require ATP for ordinary relaxation, but reverse gyrase is an ATP-dependent type IA-family exception that introduces positive supercoils and occurs in many hyperthermophiles. Type II enzymes can remove crossings between separate DNA molecules (decatenation), not just intramolecular supercoils. Some type IA enzymes can act on single-stranded interlinks. The important distinction is the geometry of strand passage and the number of strands cleaved, not merely whether a protein uses ATP.

In agarose electrophoresis, DNA migrates toward the positive electrode because its backbone is negatively charged. A covalently closed supercoiled plasmid is often more compact and runs faster than the same-sized relaxed linear or open circular forms under common gel conditions. Nicked open circular DNA often moves more slowly, linear DNA is intermediate. However this ordering is conditional on gel concentration, buffer, voltage, plasmid size, supercoiling and intercalating dye; never infer an exact topology solely from apparent mobility without controls.

Intercalators such as ethidium bromide insert between stacked pairs and locally unwind DNA, reducing Tw. In a closed circular molecule Lk remains fixed, so Wr and/or remaining Tw compensate. Starting with negatively supercoiled DNA, enough intercalator may relax it and further addition can induce positive supercoiling. Two-dimensional gels using different intercalator concentrations help distinguish positive and negative topoisomers. A set of neighboring topoisomer bands often differs by one unit of Lk; migration depends on the magnitude and sign of supercoiling under the assay conditions.

18. DNA mechanics, bending and molecular scale

Contour length is the length traced along the duplex axis, not the straight-line distance between endpoints. For B-DNA, L≈0.34N nm. A 4.6-Mbp bacterial chromosome has contour length about 1.56 mm, far larger than a micrometre-sized cell. Bending, supercoiling and protein organisation make packaging possible. The approximately 2-m DNA length often quoted for a diploid human nucleus follows from about 6.4 billion bp times 0.34 nm, and refers to the total extended contour length across chromosomes.

Persistence length describes the scale over which tangent directions stay correlated in a thermal polymer. Under common physiological-like ionic conditions, dsDNA persistence length is about 50 nm (roughly 150 bp), although it depends on sequence, salt, tension and model. For a worm-like chain, the tangent correlation is ⟨t(s)·t(0)⟩=exp(−s/Lp), with s contour distance and Lp persistence length. Thus at s=Lp the correlation is 1/e, not zero. dsDNA is locally stiff but flexible over long distances; ssDNA is generally much more flexible, though its own stacking and secondary structure matter.

DNA wrapping around a histone octamer illustrates the coupling of electrostatics and mechanics. Roughly 147 bp form about 1.65 left-handed superhelical turns around a nucleosome core. These are turns of the duplex axis, not conversion of its local double helix to Z-DNA. Histone contacts and DNA bending contribute to positioning. Detailed nucleosome structure, chromatin remodeling and higher-order genome organisation belong to later nodes.

Single-molecule force measurements can stretch DNA and reveal elastic and structural transitions. Atomic force microscopy can visualise surface-bound contours, optical or magnetic tweezers can probe force and torsional response, and diffraction or structural methods can determine local geometry. A measurement made on a stretched molecule is not automatically its relaxed cellular configuration. Linking number changes require strand passage or a break in a closed system even when force changes geometry.

19. Chemical modifications, lesions and structural consequences

DNA is durable, not chemically inert. Spontaneous depurination removes a purine base, leaving an apurinic site; spontaneous deamination converts cytosine to uracil, adenine to hypoxanthine and guanine to xanthine. Uracil can pair with A and thereby cause a C:G→T:A transition if the lesion is copied and escapes repair. 5-methylcytosine deaminates to thymine, creating a T:G mismatch. Because T is a normal DNA base, the damaged position is less readily recognised by simple discrimination against uracil. This helps explain mutation hotspots at methylated CpGs.

Thymine rather than uracil is a useful normal DNA letter because cytosine deamination generates U, making it possible for repair systems to recognise many uracil lesions in DNA. This is a biological rationale, not a claim that uracil can never occur in DNA. Some bacteriophages use modified base alphabets. In RNA, ribothymidine can occur as a modified nucleotide, notably in tRNA. Base identity and sugar chemistry must be kept separate.

Oxidative damage can generate 8-oxoguanine, which can mispair with adenine in a syn conformation and potentially yield a G:C→T:A transversion after fixation. UV light may produce cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6–4 photoproducts between neighboring pyrimidines on one strand, distorting the helix and impeding polymerases. Alkylation at O6 of guanine can favor pairing with T. Each lesion creates a distinct chemical recognition problem; a lesion is not necessarily already a heritable mutation.

5-methylcytosine and other regulated modifications can encode information beyond the standard sequence by changing protein recognition, groove chemistry and structural propensity. 5-methylcytosine retains normal pairing with G; its methyl group projects toward the major groove. Some bacterial restriction-modification systems also use base methylation to distinguish self DNA. These functions differ from random DNA damage, even if the chemistry can influence mutation risk.

Chain-terminating analogues, intercalators and groove-binding molecules show how small chemical changes alter nucleic acid behavior. A dideoxynucleotide lacks the 3′-OH required for continued extension, the principle exploited in Sanger sequencing. Intercalation alters stacking and helical dimensions; groove binding can occur without insertion between pairs. DNA-binding specificity does not imply all such interactions are covalent. Full repair pathways and sequencing procedures are covered separately.

20. How we measure nucleotide chemistry and DNA conformation

UV spectroscopy exploits the aromatic chromophores of bases. Beer–Lambert law is A=εcl, with A dimensionless, ε molar extinction coefficient, c molar concentration and l path length in cm when units are compatible. Common approximate conversions for A260=1 in a 1-cm path are 50 µg/mL dsDNA, 40 µg/mL RNA and 33 µg/mL ssDNA; sequence-specific extinction coefficients give more accurate oligonucleotide estimates. A 10-fold diluted dsDNA sample with A260=0.24 corresponds to about 0.24×50×10=120 µg/mL in the original sample. The conversion assumes reasonably pure DNA and a standard path length.

A260/A280 around 1.8 is often used as a crude dsDNA purity indicator; RNA commonly approaches 2.0. A260/A230 values near 2–2.2 are often expected for clean preparations. pH, buffer, low concentration, baseline errors and mixed contaminants alter ratios. A normal ratio does not prove identity, integrity or absence of RNA. Fluorometric dyes can offer more selective quantitation; electrophoresis examines size distribution and integrity. Neither method alone establishes full conformation.

In a circular dichroism measurement, left- and right-circularly polarised light are absorbed differently by a chiral structure. B-DNA often has a positive band near 275 nm and a negative band near 245 nm, while Z-DNA can show a substantially different, approximately inverted signature near those regions. Exact spectral positions and intensities depend on sequence and conditions; CD alone cannot uniquely identify every conformation. A combination of independent assays is stronger than a single diagnostic peak.

Consider an experiment adding salt to a CG repeat. A changed CD spectrum and increased Tm do not necessarily report the same physical transition: salt may stabilise duplexes and alter the conformational ensemble simultaneously. Compare an AT-rich control, monitor the change reversibly, and use an independent structural assay. When testing negative supercoiling, compare covalently closed and nicked samples of identical sequence. Good experiments distinguish changes in structure from changes in amount, purity or strand integrity.

Never confuse a base, a nucleotide, a base pair and a phosphodiester. A base pair is a noncovalently associated pair of residues; a nucleotide contains sugar, base and phosphate; one internucleotide phosphate bridges two sugars. A double-stranded molecule has two sequences, one written as the reverse complement of the other. Percent GC refers to residues or pairs with the same fraction in a complete canonical duplex, but the absolute counts differ by a factor of two.

Do not treat A, B and Z as different chemical polymers; they are conformations. Do not assume every purine is syn in every DNA; syn is characteristic of purines in ordinary alternating Z-DNA. Do not infer melting temperature solely from hydrogen-bond counts, or assume that denaturation destroys the backbone. Do not apply Lk invariance to a freely rotating linear molecule or a nicked circle. Do not confuse a right-handed local helix with the handedness of higher-order coiling.

The central narrative is now complete: sugar stereochemistry constrains local geometry; base functional groups determine recognition; phosphate establishes polarity and charge; noncovalent interactions produce a reversible information-bearing helix; environment and topology determine which conformations are occupied. Each concept follows from the chemical foundations established in 1.A rather than requiring separate memorisation.

  2. 400
  3. 397
  4. 398

Answer: C. 397

Originally 2(200−1)=398. A nick removes one internucleotide bond, giving 397.

C24. A third strand binds the major groove of a suitable duplex without displacing a strand. This is consistent with

  1. Ordinary B-DNA only
  2. Triplex DNA
  3. A nick
  4. An R-loop by definition
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Triplex DNA

Triplex binding uses Hoogsteen-type contacts; an R-loop instead displaces a DNA strand.

C25. A 40-bp duplex, GC-rich, melts below another 40-bp AT-rich duplex. What should be checked first?

  2. Whether all DNA is left-handed
  3. Whether phosphorus is absent
  4. Salt, mismatches, concentration and actual sequences

Answer: D. Salt, mismatches, concentration and actual sequences

Composition is only one determinant; compare experimental and sequence conditions.

23. Consolidation and bridge to RNA structure

Use a blank sheet to draw a base–sugar–phosphate unit; label C1′, C2′, C3′ and C5′. Draw two antiparallel strands and mark the covalent and noncovalent bonds. Explain why the same sequence can form a different helical geometry when water activity or torsional stress changes. Finally calculate GC fraction, length, H bonds and σ for a closed plasmid. If you can connect the answers rather than recite them, you have grasped the central structural logic.

DNA prioritises long-term sequence storage. RNA uses a related backbone but retains the 2′-OH, which expands chemical possibilities and changes preferred conformation. In Node 1.B.04 we will examine how a single strand can fold into stems, loops, pseudoknots and catalytic or regulatory structures. The concepts of pairing, stacking, ion-dependent stability and strand polarity developed here will become the starting point.

Review the earlier chemical principles in 1.A.02 and 1.A.05 whenever the thermodynamic or electrostatic explanation is unclear.
