1. Why a cell separates its genome from its cytoplasm

A cell must solve two problems that appear to contradict one another. Its genetic information needs to remain organized and accessible to enzymes that copy or read DNA. At the same time, the products of that information must reach the places where proteins are made and used. A membrane that completely isolated DNA would prevent communication; an unrestricted opening would destroy the advantage of maintaining a distinct environment. The eukaryotic nucleus resolves both problems by combining a boundary with controlled exchange. The central theme of this chapter is that nuclear identity depends not on isolation alone but on the continuous selective movement of molecules.

Recall the preceding chapter on vesicular transport. A secretory protein reaches the endoplasmic reticulum through a membrane translocation channel and subsequently travels in membrane-bounded carriers. Nuclear proteins take a different route. A DNA polymerase, a histone or a transcription factor is usually synthesized on a cytosolic ribosome, yet acts on the nuclear side of the envelope. A messenger RNA is synthesized and processed mainly in the nucleus but ordinarily meets a translating ribosome in the cytoplasm. These journeys occur through nuclear pores rather than through a sequence of budding and fusion events. This contrast is fundamental: different destinations require different solutions to the problem of intracellular sorting.

Several useful terms establish the vocabulary. The genome is the collection of genetic information, whereas chromatin is the complex of nuclear DNA with its associated proteins. A chromosome is an organized unit of chromatin, not a structure that exists only during mitosis. The nuclear envelope is the paired membrane boundary; the nucleoplasm is the internal aqueous environment in which chromatin, proteins, RNAs and nuclear bodies are organized. The nucleolus is a specialized domain within the nucleus, mainly concerned with ribosome production. The terms nucleus and nucleolus are therefore not interchangeable.

Separating transcription from translation allows a eukaryotic cell to process many RNA molecules before they encounter the cytoplasmic translation machinery. It also makes transport a point of regulation: a transcription factor can be present in a cell without having access to its DNA targets until it enters the nucleus. This provides a way to respond to a signal without having to synthesize a completely new regulatory protein. The envelope does not itself guarantee that DNA cannot be damaged, and a nuclear location does not mean a protein is permanently trapped there. Nuclear proteins and RNAs are continuously exchanged, retained and removed as their functions require.

A bacterial nucleoid contains a highly organized chromosome but normally lacks a surrounding nuclear envelope and nuclear pore complexes. Transcription and translation can consequently be coupled in bacteria. Eukaryotic mitochondria and plastids also contain genetic material, so the statement that all cellular DNA resides in the nucleus is incorrect. Nevertheless, in a typical nucleated eukaryotic cell the nucleus contains the main chromosome complement. We will first identify its parts, then investigate how its boundary establishes selective communication, and finally consider how the entire structure changes during cellular activity and division.

2. Recognizing the nucleus: morphology and cellular diversity

The nucleus often appears as a rounded region in a light micrograph because chromatin binds many commonly used nuclear stains. A nucleolus may appear as a distinct dense body within it. Electron microscopy reveals much finer features, including the two nuclear membranes and pores that cannot be resolved individually with ordinary light microscopy. These methods answer different questions: a nuclear stain can locate DNA-rich regions, but it does not directly establish that a specific protein forms a membrane pore. A fluorescent antibody against a nuclear pore protein identifies its location; a functional transport assay is still needed to establish its role.

Many interphase nuclei are roughly spherical or ovoid, often several micrometres across, but neither position nor shape is universal. A mammalian neutrophil has a lobed nucleus connected by narrow bridges, while a mature skeletal muscle fibre contains many nuclei in a shared cytoplasm. A large central vacuole often displaces a plant cell nucleus toward the edge. Multinucleation may result from cell fusion or from nuclear division without complete cytokinesis. Nuclear number is not automatically equivalent to chromosome number, and a lobed nucleus is not necessarily several independent nuclei.

Some mature cells are important exceptions to the usual nucleated eukaryotic pattern. Mammalian erythrocytes expel their nucleus during maturation; the mature red blood cell thus cannot carry out new nuclear transcription and has very limited capacity to replace its proteins. Many non-mammalian vertebrate erythrocytes retain a nucleus. Mature angiosperm sieve-tube elements lack a nucleus and depend on neighboring companion cells for substantial metabolic support. These exceptions illustrate specialization, rather than showing that a nucleus is unnecessary for the development of those cell types.

Size and staining intensity can provide useful clues but need careful interpretation. A conspicuous nucleolus often indicates a cell with substantial ribosome production, for example a rapidly growing cell. A small dark nucleus can reflect relatively condensed chromatin, but staining depends on the dye and preparation. A single optical section through a nucleus may miss a nucleolus completely or show apparent discontinuities in a curved envelope. Counting organelles from one section therefore does not necessarily give their true three-dimensional number.

There is a particularly important distinction between DNA content and nuclear size. A cell may enlarge its nucleus as it grows, and DNA replication increases DNA content before mitosis, but there is no universal rule that doubling DNA exactly doubles nuclear volume. Nuclear transport, chromatin packing, cell size and the mechanical properties of the envelope all contribute to dimensions. Consequently, nuclear shape and size should be interpreted in the context of the cell type and its physiological state.

As we proceed, use this practical rule for interpreting an image: first identify the cell type and stage, then distinguish the boundary from its contents, and finally decide which observations are directly supported by the technique. The architecture of a typical interphase animal nucleus is a useful reference model, not a mandatory description of every eukaryotic cell. This is particularly important when comparing plant cells, fungi and differentiated animal tissues.

3. The double membrane and the topology of the nuclear envelope

A nuclear envelope contains two separate lipid bilayers: the inner nuclear membrane (INM), facing the nucleoplasm, and the outer nuclear membrane (ONM), facing the cytoplasm. The narrow compartment between them is the perinuclear space, commonly of the order of a few tens of nanometres in conventional electron micrographs. Each membrane is itself a bilayer with two lipid leaflets. Thus two membranes means four leaflets, not a single membrane with unusually thick hydrophobic material. Their separation may vary locally, and no one numerical width defines all nuclei.

The outer nuclear membrane is physically continuous with the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum. Consequently, the perinuclear space is continuous with the ER lumen. Ribosomes can associate with the cytoplasmic surface of the outer membrane, just as they associate with rough ER. The membrane surface facing the perinuclear space, however, does not carry translating cytosolic ribosomes. This relationship explains why an electron micrograph may show ribosome-studded membranes close to the nucleus without implying that translation occurs inside the nucleus.

At each nuclear pore, the inner and outer membranes join in a curved ring. The resulting opening connects nucleoplasm and cytoplasm, not the perinuclear lumen and cytoplasm. It is occupied by a nuclear pore complex. This topology is easy to miss in a flat textbook drawing. Consider three distinct aqueous spaces: cytosol, nucleoplasm and the continuous ER/perinuclear lumen. The pore channel connects the first two; the ER lumen belongs to the third. A nuclear enzyme that travels through a pore never needs to enter the ER lumen or cross the hydrophobic core of either bilayer.

Membrane continuity does not mean identical molecular composition. The outer membrane shares many properties with the ER, whereas the inner membrane is enriched in proteins interacting with nuclear lamins and chromatin. A membrane protein synthesized into the ER may reach the outer membrane by lateral movement within the connected membrane network. To accumulate at the inner membrane it may diffuse through the peripheral membrane region of the pore and be retained by nuclear binding partners, or use other targeting pathways depending on the protein. Its domains must be compatible with the restricted geometry of that route. One must not imagine that all inner-membrane proteins are simply soluble importin cargos.

This organization creates both permeability and retention problems. Lipid continuity allows movement of many membrane components, but selective interactions concentrate different proteins where they function. The membrane prevents indiscriminate exchange of large hydrophilic solutes, while its pores provide aqueous routes with a selective barrier. An outer membrane ribosome may produce a protein destined for the ER lumen, but nuclear histones are normally made by cytosolic ribosomes and subsequently imported. The location of synthesis and the final destination must be distinguished.

A useful thought experiment is to place a freely diffusible fluorescent molecule inside the ER lumen. In an intact cell it can reach the perinuclear space without a vesicle. Its presence there does not predict entry into nucleoplasm, because the inner membrane and pore membrane form a distinct boundary around the lumen. In contrast, a small aqueous tracer placed in cytosol can enter nucleoplasm through pores but does not thereby enter ER lumen. The two experiments demonstrate different types of continuity. This membrane architecture is the foundation for understanding what the nuclear pore actually selects.

4. Nuclear lamina: a flexible mechanical framework

A lipid membrane alone would be a poor structure for maintaining the shape of a large organelle subject to cellular forces. On the nucleoplasmic side of the inner nuclear membrane, many animal cells have a protein network called the nuclear lamina. Its major constituents are lamins, which are related to the intermediate filament family. A-type lamins (especially lamin A and lamin C) and B-type lamins form interacting networks with different molecular attachments and developmental expression patterns. These networks lie underneath the envelope rather than within the perinuclear space.

A lamin molecule contains a central rod region that promotes association with other lamin molecules. Interactions between these proteins generate a network that distributes mechanical load instead of allowing all force to concentrate at one point of the membrane. The network is attached to the inner membrane through membrane-associated proteins and through interactions involving lamins themselves. Connections with chromatin help position some chromosome regions near the envelope. These associations are selective and dynamic: not every chromosome region touches the lamina, and contact with the periphery is not an absolute indicator that a particular gene is inactive.

In animal cells, proteins that span the nuclear envelope can connect the lamina and nuclear interior to the cytoskeleton. A widely used conceptual example is the LINC complex (linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton). SUN-domain proteins in the inner membrane interact across the perinuclear space with KASH-domain proteins in the outer membrane, including nesprins in animals. Their cytoplasmic partners can connect to actin, intermediate filament-associated proteins or motor systems. The result is a physical route by which forces from outside the nucleus can influence nuclear position and shape. The envelope remains a boundary even while force is transmitted across it.

This mechanical role is distinct from the exchange role of nuclear pores. If lamina organization fails, the nucleus can develop blebs, abnormal shapes or increased mechanical fragility, even if many pores remain functional. Conversely, disruption of transport receptors can change nuclear protein composition before an obvious shape defect appears. Some lamin or associated protein mutations cause laminopathies, including particular muscular dystrophies and disorders with premature-aging features. The relevant organelle principle is that a structural protein defect may affect mechanically stressed tissues and the organization of the nuclear interior; detailed disease genetics is not needed here.

In plants there is also a peripheral nuclear scaffold and proteins that support nuclear shape and positioning, but the canonical metazoan A- and B-type lamins are not universal. Plant-specific CRWN proteins provide important lamina-like functions. Budding yeast likewise lacks the conventional animal lamin network. Hence a drawing of lamin A/C should be read as an animal-cell example, not as a list of obligatory components of every eukaryotic nucleus.

The lamina also has to be reversible. A network that could never disassemble would impede the large structural rearrangements required by open mitosis. Lamin phosphorylation changes molecular interactions and helps the network disassemble as many animal cells enter mitosis. Later dephosphorylation contributes to its restoration. The distinction between a stable interphase scaffold and a permanently fixed shell is crucial: nuclear stability is the product of regulated molecular assemblies. We will return to this property after examining the pores and nuclear interior.

5. Nuclear pore complexes: structure and selectivity

Electron microscopy revealed that nuclear pores are not simple holes punched through two membranes. Each is occupied by a large assembly of proteins called a nuclear pore complex (NPC). These assemblies exhibit an approximately eightfold rotational organization when viewed along the pore axis. The complete diameter of a vertebrate complex is of the order of one hundred nanometres; its central transport passage is much narrower, and dimensions differ with species, preparation and structural state. The external diameter of the whole complex must not be confused with the effective size of the route available to a freely diffusing solute.

The proteins making up the NPC are nucleoporins, often abbreviated Nups. A typical vertebrate complex contains many copies of several dozen kinds of nucleoporins. Some form a supporting scaffold, others anchor it to the curved pore membrane, and others extend into the transport pathway or project into the surrounding compartments. Cytoplasmic filaments project toward the cytoplasm; on the nuclear side, filaments converge to form a basket-like arrangement. These projections contribute to interactions with transport factors and the organization of traffic. The scaffold supports the geometry of the pore rather than acting as a conveyor belt for every cargo.

A central challenge is how the same pore passes small metabolites efficiently while restricting the entry of many large proteins. Numerous nucleoporins contain intrinsically disordered regions rich in phenylalanine-glycine repeats (FG repeats). These flexible regions create a selective molecular environment. Transport receptors interact transiently with FG motifs, making passage favorable for appropriate receptor-cargo complexes. A large unrelated macromolecule that cannot make these interactions enters less readily. Different physical descriptions of the FG barrier emphasize different aspects of its dynamic organization; a fixed rigid mesh with one universally valid molecular-weight cutoff is an oversimplification.

Ions, nucleotides and many small soluble molecules can pass passively down their concentration gradients. Small proteins can also enter passively, at rates that depend on size, shape and interactions. Textbooks often use approximately 40–60 kDa as a rough range beyond which passive equilibration becomes inefficient on common experimental timescales. This is not an absolute threshold. A compact protein and an elongated protein of equal molecular mass do not necessarily have the same effective hydrodynamic size. Even smaller proteins may accumulate preferentially in one compartment if they bind to components there.

Large proteins, protein complexes and ribonucleoprotein particles usually require receptor-assisted movement. Their cargo need not pass as an unfolded chain in the manner characteristic of several other organelle translocation pathways. Many native folded proteins and assembled complexes can use NPCs when they have suitable targeting information and geometrical compatibility. Ribosomal subunit precursors are important examples of relatively large transported assemblies. Their exit is not evidence that the pore has ceased to be selective: the relevant variable is not mass alone, but productive interaction with the transport system.

The pore does not contain a separate one-way door for every type of cargo. Import and export receptors use the shared passage and can move in either physical direction. Directional accumulation emerges because cargo binding and release are biased on opposite sides of the envelope. To understand that asymmetry we must first identify the signals that allow a cargo to engage a receptor and then examine the Ran nucleotide cycle that maintains different chemical conditions in the two compartments.

6. How a protein acquires a nuclear address

A protein synthesized in the cytoplasm can reach a nucleus simply by diffusion if it is sufficiently permeable and not otherwise retained. However, a large nuclear enzyme needs a reproducible way to distinguish its destination from other cellular compartments. A nuclear localization signal (NLS) is a region or structural feature of a cargo that can be recognized by nuclear import machinery. Classical NLS sequences often contain clusters of positively charged lysine and arginine residues. The well-known SV40 large T-antigen signal PKKKRKV is a useful example, but it must not be treated as the only permitted NLS sequence.

A classical signal can be monopartite, with one principal basic cluster, or bipartite, with two clusters separated by a linker. Nucleoplasmin provides a classical bipartite example. In a common import pathway, importin-alpha recognizes a classical basic NLS and acts as an adapter for importin-beta, which interacts with the FG-rich pore environment. Other nuclear import receptors bind cargo directly without importin-alpha. This distinction matters because deletion of importin-alpha need not eliminate every nuclear import pathway. The transport receptor family is broader than one particular alpha-beta pair.

An NLS is normally not removed by cleavage during nuclear import, unlike the signal peptides that direct many secretory proteins into the ER. Retention of the signal permits a protein to be imported again after it returns to the cytoplasm or after nuclear envelope reassembly during open mitosis. The signal may lie within a polypeptide rather than only at its amino terminus. A protein can also enter through association with another protein that carries a signal; this is sometimes called piggyback import. Consequently, finding no obvious basic sequence in a protein does not prove that the protein can never enter the nucleus.

The location of a signal in the primary sequence is not the complete story. A functional signal must be physically accessible to the receptor. A protein whose NLS is buried in its folded structure or covered by a binding partner may remain cytoplasmic. A signaling-induced conformational change or release of that partner can expose the signal. Phosphorylation may change the affinity of a receptor for a cargo or alter accessibility, with either positive or negative consequences depending on the protein. Nuclear localization can thus be controlled without changing the DNA sequence that encodes the signal.

Nuclear export signals (NESs) provide a corresponding route out of the nucleus. A common class contains appropriately spaced hydrophobic residues, often leucines, and is recognized by exportin-1 (CRM1 or XPO1). Again, this is one major pathway, not a universal sequence rule for all nuclear exports. An individual protein may carry both an NLS and an NES. Such a protein can shuttle continuously, with its steady-state distribution reflecting the balance of entry, exit and binding within each compartment.

An important exam distinction is between necessity and sufficiency. Mutating a candidate NLS and observing failure of import suggests that the region is necessary for that cargo under the tested conditions. Attaching that sequence to an otherwise cytoplasmic large reporter and observing nuclear accumulation tests whether the sequence is sufficient to confer targeting in that context. Neither experiment alone excludes all indirect effects. The mutation might alter folding, and the fusion might introduce an additional binding surface. Well-designed control constructs make those interpretations much more secure.

7. Ran establishes the direction of nuclear transport

A receptor that binds a cargo and can pass through a pore does not automatically produce accumulation in one compartment. If receptor binding and release occurred with equal probabilities on both sides, movement would tend toward equilibrium. Directional nuclear transport therefore requires a chemical asymmetry. In most eukaryotes this is provided by Ran, a small GTP-binding protein that alternates between a GTP-bound and GDP-bound state. As in other GTPase cycles, the nucleotide state changes the proteins with which Ran interacts.

Two regulators occupy different cellular locations. RCC1, the major Ran guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (RanGEF), is associated with nuclear chromatin. It stimulates release of GDP from Ran and allows GTP binding. In the cytoplasm RanGAP accelerates GTP hydrolysis, converting Ran-GTP to Ran-GDP and inorganic phosphate. Ran-binding proteins assist parts of this process. Because exchange is concentrated in the nucleus and hydrolysis is promoted in cytoplasm, the usual interphase state has relatively high Ran-GTP in the nucleus and relatively high Ran-GDP in cytoplasm.

Ran itself must circulate to maintain this arrangement. Nuclear transport factor 2 (NTF2) binds Ran-GDP in the cytoplasm and assists its movement back into the nucleus. There RCC1 promotes nucleotide exchange, regenerating Ran-GTP. Ran-GTP can leave the nucleus in complexes with transport receptors, after which hydrolysis releases the receptor and produces Ran-GDP. The cycle can therefore continue only while guanine nucleotides are available and the spatial organization of the regulators is maintained. RanGEF does not catalyze hydrolysis, and RanGAP does not load GTP onto Ran.

The free energy used by the system is associated with the maintenance of a nonequilibrium nucleotide cycle. GTP hydrolysis in the cytoplasm helps make return steps favorable and prevents futile accumulation of Ran-GTP outside the nucleus. It is misleading to imagine a GTP molecule being hydrolyzed each time a cargo moves through an individual FG repeat or a pore pushing the cargo inward by a power stroke. Receptor movement through the NPC is driven by thermal motion and reversible interactions. The chemically biased loading and unloading reactions create net direction.

This explains an otherwise puzzling observation: the same Ran-GTP molecule promotes dissociation of an import receptor from its cargo but helps an export receptor bind its cargo. Ran does not have a universal instruction that means move inward or move outward. Its interaction with different classes of receptors has different consequences. Import complexes are favored on the cytoplasmic side where Ran-GTP is relatively scarce; many export complexes are favored on the nuclear side where Ran-GTP is abundant.

A loss of the gradient can be serious even if electron microscopy reveals morphologically normal NPCs. When Ran-GTP is unusually high in cytoplasm, many import receptors cannot efficiently load their cargo; when nuclear Ran-GTP falls, import complexes fail to release cargo efficiently and Ran-dependent export complexes fail to form normally. In either case, the physical pathway remains but directional transport is compromised. The distinction between a pore defect and a gradient defect will be central to interpreting experiments later in the chapter.

8. The complete receptor-mediated nuclear import cycle

Consider a newly synthesized DNA-binding protein with an accessible classical NLS. In the cytoplasm the concentration of Ran-GTP is low relative to the nucleus. Importin-alpha binds the basic signal on the cargo, while importin-beta binds importin-alpha and makes transient contacts with nucleoporins. The cargo, adapter and receptor constitute an import complex. This assembly is selective because a protein lacking a suitable signal is unlikely to be recruited by the same mechanism. The receptor may nevertheless bind other cargos through alternative adapters.

The loaded complex encounters the cytoplasmic surface of a nuclear pore. Interactions with FG nucleoporins permit it to enter and move through the selective channel. It can fluctuate in position and may return to the cytoplasm before successful passage. It is not necessary to assume that every encounter with a pore completes an import event. Productive passage places the receptor and its cargo in the nuclear environment, where the local Ran nucleotide state differs from that at the point of loading.

Nuclear Ran-GTP binds importin-beta and promotes disassembly of the classical import complex. Additional nuclear factors help release the NLS cargo from importin-alpha. Once released, the cargo may diffuse through the nucleoplasm, bind chromatin or assemble with other proteins. Binding to a nuclear partner can prolong residence, but even an unbound cargo with a functional NLS can be maintained at a higher nuclear concentration through repeated cycles of import. The final location depends on the balance of these processes rather than on the initial passage alone.

The transport factors must be reused. Importin-beta associated with Ran-GTP can pass back to the cytoplasm, where hydrolysis of Ran-bound GTP promotes complex dissociation. Importin-alpha has its own recycling route, mediated by the export receptor CAS together with Ran-GTP. Returning these molecules is an essential part of sustained nuclear import. A model that ends as soon as the cargo enters the nucleus cannot explain how a cell imports a continuous stream of newly synthesized proteins with a finite receptor pool.

The simplest direct beta-family import pathway omits importin-alpha: its receptor recognizes the cargo itself, interacts with FG nucleoporins, and releases cargo when nuclear Ran-GTP binds. Both pathways rely on the asymmetry of Ran. A mutation that prevents the cargo from binding importin-alpha may specifically interfere with classical NLS import but leave a direct receptor pathway available to other cargos. In contrast, severe disruption of the Ran gradient has consequences for many receptor pathways simultaneously.

Imagine an NLS-bearing reporter that binds importin normally but cannot be released because its receptor no longer responds to Ran-GTP. It may dock or even move through an NPC, yet productive accumulation of free cargo fails. A fluorescent spot near the nuclear envelope could therefore represent stalled complexes rather than successful delivery to the nuclear interior. In experimental reasoning, identify the precise step measured: receptor recognition, pore entry, passage, cargo release or recycling. A transport defect at one stage should not be automatically assigned to every other stage.

9. Export, RNA traffic and the distinction from import

An exportin solves the reverse cargo-loading problem. In a representative CRM1-mediated pathway, an NES-bearing protein binds exportin in the nucleus together with Ran-GTP. These interactions favor a ternary complex containing cargo, export receptor and Ran-GTP. The complex traverses the NPC by reversible interactions with FG nucleoporins. In cytoplasm RanGAP and associated factors promote GTP hydrolysis, destabilizing the complex and releasing the cargo. The free exportin can then return to the nucleus for another round.

Compare the loading steps carefully. In classical protein import, the low cytoplasmic Ran-GTP state allows an import receptor to bind cargo. In export, the high nuclear Ran-GTP state promotes formation of the export complex. Hydrolysis occurs mainly on the cytoplasmic side in both cycles, but import cargo is released in nucleus, whereas export cargo is released in cytoplasm. There is no contradiction: Ran-GTP has opposite effects on cargo affinity for the two classes of receptors. The pore remains physically bidirectional.

RNA export requires additional distinctions. Most mature messenger RNAs leave the nucleus as messenger ribonucleoprotein particles (mRNPs), not naked RNA strands. RNA-binding proteins help connect RNA processing to the recruitment of export machinery. For most bulk mRNA export in animals, NXF1 together with NXT1 is a major receptor system and is not directly driven by the classical Ran-GTP export cycle. At the cytoplasmic face, ATP-dependent remodeling involving DDX19 (Dbp5 in yeast) helps strip or rearrange export-associated proteins, supporting directionality. Thus the assertion that every nuclear export event uses Ran-GTP directly is false.

Other RNA cargos use different mechanisms. Many transfer RNAs are exported through specific exportins in a Ran-GTP-dependent manner. Pre-ribosomal particles recruit multiple export factors; in a common large-subunit pathway, an adapter bearing an NES helps CRM1 recognize the pre-60S particle. Export of ribosomal precursors does not involve budding through the inner nuclear membrane followed by ER trafficking. Under ordinary cellular conditions these assemblies pass through NPCs, and late maturation occurs in the cytoplasm. The detailed processing reactions of each RNA class will be studied in the fundamental-processes unit.

Nuclear export is tied to quality control. Many faulty or incompletely processed RNAs are retained or degraded rather than exported productively. This is not an infallible rule, nor is a single pore protein sufficient to judge all RNA processing events. Rather, assembly of appropriate RNA-binding proteins, export-factor recruitment and surveillance cooperate to bias traffic toward properly prepared cargos. A block in RNA export may produce accumulation of RNA in the nucleus despite normal transcription.

For a protein that carries both an NLS and an NES, nuclear concentration is a dynamic result. Inhibition of CRM1 can increase the nuclear concentration of its shuttling substrates while their import remains active. It does not necessarily increase the nuclear concentration of every protein, and it does not directly block NXF1-mediated bulk mRNA export. Leptomycin B is a classical inhibitor used to illustrate CRM1-dependent export. Interpretation requires a specific substrate and suitable controls because secondary effects of altered nuclear signaling can influence many other processes.

10. Experimental evidence: proving selective exchange

The nuclear pore model becomes convincing when structure, cargo targeting and molecular perturbation are examined together. A classical experimental starting point is to introduce fluorescently labeled proteins into the cytoplasm and observe where the signal appears. A small inert tracer may equilibrate between compartments; a large inert reporter may be excluded or enter slowly; an appropriately targeted large reporter can accumulate within the nucleus. When the same reporter backbone is used for the targeted and untargeted versions, differences in size and shape are minimized and the targeting information becomes a plausible cause of the changed localization.

In 1984, Kalderon and colleagues tested a short sequence from SV40 large T antigen. Mutation of a critical basic residue interfered with nuclear localization, and transfer of the signal-containing sequence to normally cytoplasmic proteins could direct their nuclear accumulation. The importance of these observations lies in their experimental logic rather than the viral background. Localization information was shown to be portable. The amino acid sequence was not merely a feature of a protein that happened to reside in the nucleus: changing or transferring the sequence altered where a protein accumulated. This is evidence for a receptor-readable address.

A second approach separates soluble transport factors from the nucleus. Selective permeabilization of the plasma membrane with an appropriate agent, such as digitonin under controlled conditions, can allow cytosolic components to be washed away while maintaining the nuclear envelope. If a fluorescent NLS cargo is then supplied without the required soluble factors, efficient nuclear accumulation is lost. Addition of suitable cytosol or reconstituted transport factors and an energy-supporting nucleotide system restores accumulation in standard assays. An intact nuclear envelope must be verified using an exclusion tracer; if it leaks, nonspecific entry can mimic successful transport.

A third approach perturbs the NPC. Certain lectins, such as wheat germ agglutinin, bind glycosylated nucleoporins in vertebrate systems and can interfere with receptor-mediated transport. Such an observation implicates the pore, but it does not prove that all solutes are affected identically or that the lectin identifies the only relevant nucleoporin. In a complementary experiment, altered Ran nucleotide cycling can disturb directional import and export even when gross NPC structure appears intact. Distinct experimental perturbations therefore separate the selective conduit from the energy-dependent machinery that biases cargo distribution.

Consider the illustrative data below. Fluorescence is measured as the mean concentration-like intensity within a nuclear region divided by that in cytoplasm. The same large fluorescent protein is used in the first three conditions; a fourth sample carries the functional NLS but lacks productive Ran cycling. The numbers are constructed for reasoning practice, not values reported in a historical paper. A ratio above one indicates enrichment, not necessarily irreversible trapping. The wild-type signal supports enrichment, whereas mutation reduces it. The Ran perturbation reduces enrichment even with the intact signal, showing that an address alone cannot maintain directionality.

A complete interpretation still requires checking expression level, reporter stability, nuclear membrane integrity, background fluorescence and the sampling time. If the reporter binds chromatin only after import, the nuclear ratio reflects both passage and retention. A kinetic experiment that follows initial nuclear entry and subsequent exit can distinguish these contributions more effectively than a single endpoint. This is why a localization picture is the beginning of a mechanistic argument, not its conclusion.

11. Nucleoplasm, chromatin and membrane-free nuclear organization

The nuclear envelope encloses a structured environment rather than an empty cavity. Nucleoplasm contains water, ions, metabolites, soluble proteins, RNAs, chromatin and assemblies that are enriched in particular molecules. Diffusion allows molecules to explore available space, but their movement is modified by crowding and interactions with other components. Some proteins exchange rapidly with the soluble pool, while others remain associated with chromatin for longer intervals. A nuclear protein may therefore have several different mobility states even though it never crosses the envelope during the measurement.

Chromatin occupies much of the nuclear volume. Regions that are relatively accessible and less compacted are traditionally described as euchromatin, whereas relatively compact chromatin is described as heterochromatin. In many interphase animal cells, heterochromatin is enriched near the nuclear envelope or nucleolus, but this pattern is not universal. The terms describe organizational states rather than two permanently distinct chemical types of DNA. The detailed nucleosome structure, histone modifications and chromosome organization belong to the next subunit; here the key point is that nuclear architecture influences the location and accessibility of genetic material.

Nuclear bodies are regions enriched in specific proteins and RNAs without being surrounded by a lipid bilayer. The nucleolus is the largest and most readily recognized example. Nuclear speckles are enriched in factors associated with RNA processing, and Cajal bodies participate in the maturation or organization of certain ribonucleoprotein complexes. These descriptions do not mean all transcription or splicing takes place inside a particular body. Many such factors exchange with surrounding nucleoplasm and act at numerous chromatin-associated sites. Nuclear organization is a way to concentrate and coordinate molecules, not a collection of permanently sealed miniature nuclei.

How can a body maintain an identity without a membrane? Multiple weak interactions among RNAs and proteins can favor their association locally. A component that interacts strongly enough with other molecules of the body becomes enriched there while still being able to exchange with its surroundings. Phase separation is a useful physical framework for understanding several nuclear bodies, particularly the nucleolus, although not every body or every aspect of its organization is explained by a single ideal liquid model. Chromatin attachment, ongoing RNA synthesis, molecular assembly and regulated turnover can contribute to its shape and persistence.

This creates a helpful contrast with the ER. A soluble ER lumenal protein generally cannot freely enter the cytoplasm because the membrane creates a physical boundary. A nucleolar protein can leave the nucleolus for the nucleoplasm without crossing a bilayer. Its distribution depends instead on binding and partitioning. Consequently, dissociation of the interactions that maintain a nuclear body may cause it to disperse without any membrane rupture. This distinction will become particularly clear when nucleoli disappear and reform during the cell cycle.

Some older accounts refer to a nuclear matrix inferred from relatively insoluble material remaining after extraction. The nucleus certainly has structural networks and many molecular attachments, but extraction can produce or stabilize assemblies that do not correspond to a universal rigid internal skeleton in living cells. The peripheral lamina has well-established molecular constituents; the existence of an equivalent permanent scaffold throughout all nucleoplasm should not be assumed. In exam questions, distinguish observed spatial organization from an overly literal model of a fixed matrix.

12. Nucleolus: how a ribosome-producing domain is organized

Cells that produce many proteins need a reliable supply of ribosomes. Each ribosome is a complex of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and numerous ribosomal proteins. The task is more demanding than making any one protein: large numbers of different molecular components must be synthesized, processed and assembled in appropriate ratios. The nucleolus concentrates much of the machinery for producing these complexes in a distinct region of the nucleus. Its existence is an example of functional organization without a surrounding lipid membrane.

Nucleoli form in association with nucleolar organizer regions (NORs), chromosomal loci containing repeated genes for the major precursor rRNA. In humans these major NORs lie on the short arms of five pairs of acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21 and 22). The number of visible nucleoli need not equal the number of these chromosomes or the number of rDNA copies. Several active NORs can contribute to a shared nucleolus, some loci may not be active, and nucleoli can fuse or separate as cells change. The defining feature is association with active ribosome biogenesis rather than an invariant count or position.

Electron microscopy of many actively growing vertebrate cells distinguishes three characteristic regions. Fibrillar centers (FCs) contain rDNA-associated components and transcription machinery. Active transcription of major rRNA genes is commonly associated with the boundary between the FC and dense fibrillar component (DFC). Newly produced precursor rRNA enters the DFC, where early processing and modification occur. The surrounding granular component (GC) contains later assembling preribosomal particles. These are functional enrichments, not sharply sealed chambers or an absolutely linear one-molecule-at-a-time assembly line.

Why does this arrangement emerge? The repeated rRNA genes provide localized sites of RNA synthesis. Nascent RNA recruits proteins and RNA-containing processing complexes. Their interactions create a region enriched in the molecules required for early steps; later particles associate with different sets of proteins as assembly progresses. Ongoing rRNA production and molecular partitioning therefore help establish and maintain the characteristic organization. When transcription is strongly inhibited, the normal organization can change markedly. This provides a direct link between a structure visible under a microscope and a biochemical process.

A nucleolus has DNA associated with it because the ribosomal DNA genes that organize it are chromosomal regions. It is not, however, an independent DNA-bearing organelle with a genome of its own like a mitochondrion. Nor does the absence of an enclosing membrane mean the nucleolus is a random collection of material. Its concentration of rRNA, ribosomal proteins, processing factors and assembling particles is highly organized. The major function of the nucleolus is ribosomal subunit biogenesis, rather than synthesis of all nuclear RNAs or translation of proteins.

Nucleolar size is often related to the demand for ribosome production. A cell growing rapidly may have a prominent nucleolus because substantial amounts of rRNA and ribosomal components are being assembled. A large nucleolus alone is not a quantitative measurement of ribosome output: altered processing can also cause material to accumulate. In stressed or nutrient-limited cells, ribosome production and nucleolar organization may change as growth slows. This responsiveness makes the nucleolus a useful readout of cellular state, but its appearance should be paired with functional measurements when the mechanism matters.

13. From nucleolar rRNA to cytoplasmic ribosomal subunits

Ribosome production requires coordinated contributions from the nucleus and cytoplasm. In a typical mammalian cell, RNA polymerase I transcribes repeated rDNA into a long primary precursor commonly called 47S pre-rRNA. Early processing produces intermediates including 45S pre-rRNA. Many introductory accounts refer to the major precursor as 45S; the terminology depends on the species and processing stage. The essential fact is that the long precursor contains the sequences that give rise to 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs, interspersed with spacer sequences that are removed during maturation.

The fourth major rRNA of the cytosolic ribosome, 5S rRNA, is usually transcribed separately by RNA polymerase III outside the nucleolus in mammalian cells, then joins the large-subunit assembly pathway. It does not arise by cleavage of the 47S precursor. In budding yeast the organization of 5S genes within the rDNA repeat differs from mammals; the polymerase distinction remains. This is a common examination trap: one cannot infer the enzyme that synthesizes a molecule merely from the fact that the molecule later participates in nucleolar assembly.

Ribosomal proteins follow a different route. Their genes are transcribed mainly by RNA polymerase II to generate mRNAs that are processed and exported. Cytoplasmic ribosomes translate these mRNAs. Newly made ribosomal proteins are then imported into the nucleus and many accumulate in the nucleolus, where they associate with rRNAs and developing particles. The nucleolus therefore depends on proteins that were produced outside it. A cell cannot maintain normal ribosome production simply by sustaining polymerase I activity if nuclear import of ribosomal proteins is disrupted.

During assembly, the pre-rRNA is cleaved and modified, aided by small nucleolar ribonucleoproteins (snoRNPs) and other factors. The modifications and processing help the RNAs acquire functional structures, while proteins join in a coordinated manner. Some factors are temporarily associated and are removed as particles mature. The small-subunit pathway produces a pre-40S particle containing 18S rRNA. The large-subunit pathway produces a pre-60S particle containing 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs in the conventional mammalian scheme. These particles undergo further maturation in the nucleoplasm and cytoplasm.

The two precursors leave the nucleus separately through NPCs, then undergo late maturation and participate in cytoplasmic translation. A functional eukaryotic cytosolic ribosome is called 80S, comprising a 40S small subunit and a 60S large subunit. The S unit is the Svedberg, a sedimentation coefficient equal to 10⁻¹³ seconds. It reflects sedimentation speed relative to centrifugal acceleration, which depends on mass, shape, buoyancy and friction. The numbers are consequently not additive: 40S + 60S does not imply 100S. The bacterial 70S ribosome with 30S and 50S subunits illustrates the same principle.

The pathway forms a biological feedback in a broad functional sense. Existing cytoplasmic ribosomes synthesize proteins needed to construct future ribosomes. Nuclear rRNA synthesis supplies a scaffold and catalytic RNA components, while cytoplasmic translation supplies proteins. Nuclear import and export connect these two production systems. An inhibitor that blocks rRNA synthesis, one that blocks ribosomal protein import, and one that blocks preribosomal export can all reduce future ribosome availability, yet produce different immediate distributions of intermediates. Understanding the sequence allows those mechanisms to be distinguished without memorizing an unrelated collection of inhibitor effects.

14. Nuclear dynamics during the cell cycle

An interphase nucleus is not a stationary container waiting for cell division. Its volume and shape can change as the cell grows; nuclear pores can be inserted into an intact envelope; nuclear proteins shuttle and chromatin reorganizes. Replication during S phase increases DNA content, but the nuclear boundary ordinarily persists throughout interphase. Chromosomes do not disappear when they become difficult to distinguish individually by conventional microscopy. They remain present in relatively extended forms suited to the ongoing work of the cell.

Many animal cells undergo open mitosis, during which the familiar membrane-bounded nucleus temporarily ceases to exist as a separate compartment. As a cell enters mitosis, cyclin-dependent kinase activity, particularly cyclin B–CDK1, promotes phosphorylation of lamins and other nuclear-envelope components. These changes weaken the interactions that maintain the interphase nuclear lamina and contribute to the disassembly of NPCs and envelope remodeling. Nuclear-envelope breakdown permits cytoplasmic spindle microtubules to gain access to chromosomes. The lamina is reorganized, not simply destroyed and resynthesized wholesale at each division.

Nuclear membranes are redistributed into the connected ER membrane system during open mitosis in many animal cells. Different membrane and lamin-associated components can have different behaviors: many A-type lamins disperse into a soluble pool, while B-type lamins retain substantial membrane association due to their modifications and interactions. The broad picture is a temporary loss of the normal barrier, not the conversion of all membrane lipids into free monomers. Once the compartment is open, the usual interphase distinction between nuclear and cytoplasmic soluble pools is also altered.

During late anaphase and telophase, mitotic kinase activity falls and phosphatases reverse many mitotic phosphorylations. Membrane sheets and other nuclear-envelope components associate with the separated chromosome masses; membranes become reorganized around them, NPCs assemble, and the lamina is rebuilt. As the envelope becomes functional, selective nuclear import again establishes distinct protein distributions. Reassembly therefore requires more than just placing two lipid membranes around DNA: the pores, selective transport system and mechanical scaffold must all recover.

Nucleolar reorganization accompanies these changes. In typical open mitosis, rRNA transcription is greatly reduced and the interphase nucleolus disassembles. Several nucleolar proteins and RNAs redistribute. As rRNA production resumes around active NORs, nucleolar material is recruited and reorganized into new nucleoli. The reappearance of a nucleolus follows the resumption of its underlying assembly processes; no vesicle carries an intact old nucleolus to each daughter nucleus.

Open mitosis is not the only strategy. Some fungi, notably budding yeast, undergo closed mitosis in which the nuclear envelope remains substantially intact and the spindle acts within the nuclear compartment. Other organisms exhibit intermediate or semi-open strategies. It would be incorrect to state that every eukaryotic mitosis requires complete nuclear-envelope breakdown. What is universal at the level relevant here is the requirement to distribute the genome and re-establish functional nuclear organization in the daughter cells, using mechanisms adapted to the species.

A mutation that prevents appropriate lamin phosphorylation can interfere with envelope disassembly in cells that rely on open mitosis. Conversely, a defect in envelope reassembly or pore incorporation can leave daughter nuclei improperly compartmentalized. These predictions connect molecular changes to observable structure. Full regulation of cell-cycle transitions, checkpoints and spindle mechanics is reserved for Subunit 2.D; the present purpose is to explain the reversible organization of the organelle itself.

15. Quantitative reasoning: geometry, flux and enrichment

Nuclear organization is often tested with simple quantitative reasoning rather than a formula unique to nuclei. Start with an idealized spherical nucleus of radius r. Its surface area is A = 4πr² and its volume V = (4/3)πr³. Dividing the two expressions cancels π and two powers of radius, giving A/V = 3/r. The ratio has units of inverse length. This matters because NPCs occupy the surface whereas many nuclear reactions occur in the volume. If pore density stays constant, a larger nucleus has fewer pores relative to its volume even though it has more pores in total.

Suppose a spherical nucleus has radius 4 μm and a hypothetical uniform pore density of 12 pores per μm². Its area is 4π × 16 = 201.06 μm², so the estimated number of pores is 201.06 × 12 ≈ 2,413. Its volume is (4/3)π × 64 ≈ 268.08 μm³. If radius doubles to 8 μm, surface area and pore number increase fourfold, while volume increases eightfold. The estimated pores per unit volume decrease by half. These values are illustrative, not a universal pore density or nuclear size. Real nuclei may be flattened, lobed or irregular and pore density can change with cellular state.

For a simplified passive transport model, let Cc and Cn be cytoplasmic and nuclear concentrations of an unbound tracer, in molecules per μm³. Define K as the total effective passive exchange coefficient of all pores, with units μm³ per second. Then the net inward rate in molecules per second is J = K(Cc − Cn). For a fixed nuclear volume Vn, the number of nuclear molecules is Nn = Vn Cn. Conservation gives dNn/dt = J and therefore Vn dCn/dt = K(Cc − Cn). We assume constant cytoplasmic concentration, constant nuclear volume, no binding, no synthesis or degradation, and a linear permeability regime.

Rearrange the equation by defining the concentration deficit Δ = Cc − Cn. Since Cc is constant, dΔ/dt = −(K/Vn)Δ. Integrating dΔ/Δ = −(K/Vn)dt gives Δ(t) = Δ(0)e^(−Kt/Vn). Thus the difference from equilibrium decays exponentially with time constant τ = Vn/K. The half-time for the deficit is ln(2) × Vn/K. For Vn = 200 μm³ and K = 20 μm³/s, τ = 10 s and half-time ≈ 6.93 s. Starting from zero nuclear tracer, after 10 s its nuclear concentration reaches approximately 63.2% of the fixed cytoplasmic value. A different rate of equilibration does not change the predicted final free concentration in this passive model.

For a shuttling reporter maintained by directional import and export, use effective rates kin and kout in s⁻¹. Assume the cytoplasmic concentration is held fixed, ignore binding and passive exchange, and define the flux into the nuclear volume in concentration units by dCn/dt = kin Cc − kout Cn. At steady state the derivative vanishes, so kin Cc = kout Cn and Cn/Cc = kin/kout. If kin = 0.12 s⁻¹ and kout = 0.03 s⁻¹, the ratio is four. If export is selectively reduced to 0.015 s⁻¹ with import unchanged, the predicted ratio becomes eight. The model does not say that receptors themselves possess these universal rate constants; they are effective parameters under the assumed conditions.

A measured ratio can be confused with an amount ratio. If Cn/Cc = 4 and the nuclear volume is 100 μm³ while the accessible cytoplasmic volume is 400 μm³, then Nn/Nc = (4 × 100)/400 = 1. Equal total amounts occupy the two regions despite a fourfold nuclear concentration. Mean corrected fluorescence may approximate concentration if brightness is comparable, whereas total integrated fluorescence is related to amount. An exam question may exploit exactly this difference.

In a capacity-limited hypothetical case, 2,000 pores each support an average of 30 completed import events per second under the given conditions. The aggregate rate is 60,000 events per second. If each event delivers one molecule and none leaves, accumulating 300,000 molecules would take five seconds. This is an upper-simplicity model: real traffic shares pores, binding can be saturable, and opposing fluxes or limited receptor pools alter the net rate. A count of pores alone does not specify transport capacity. These models are tools for reasoning about organelle organization, not precise descriptions of every living nucleus.

16. Connecting nuclear structure with functional defects

We can now use a unified model to interpret failures. The envelope establishes a boundary and preserves the relationship with the ER. NPCs provide selective passage. Import and export receptors recognize cargos, while Ran or other pathway-specific mechanisms establish directional bias. Lamins and associated proteins give mechanical support and connect the interior to cytoskeletal forces. Nucleoplasm organizes chromatin and other nuclear assemblies. The nucleolus concentrates machinery for making ribosomal precursors. These components cooperate, but each has a different primary function.

Suppose a mutant cell has a highly irregular nuclear outline, yet a large NLS reporter enters its nucleus almost as efficiently as in the control. This pattern points more directly to the mechanical organization of the envelope than to a total loss of pore function. A lamina or nuclear-envelope attachment defect is plausible. It is not proven solely by the shape, since changes in cytoskeletal forces or chromatin organization can also alter nuclear geometry. Labeling lamins and measuring mechanical sensitivity would distinguish among those explanations.

Now suppose a cell has a smooth envelope and normal pore staining but many normally nuclear proteins remain cytoplasmic. An import-factor defect, failure of Ran asymmetry or selective barrier alteration may explain the observation. Normal staining of a pore marker only demonstrates that the marker is present at its expected location. It does not establish that receptor docking, FG interactions, cargo release and receptor recycling are functional. Comparing small inert tracer diffusion with receptor-dependent import, and comparing import and export reporters, gives more specific evidence.

A third cell accumulates mature mRNA in its nucleus but shows relatively normal classical protein import. A defect in mRNA export factors, processing-dependent recruitment or cytoplasmic remodeling is more plausible than a complete loss of every NPC or of all nuclear transport. Because mRNAs and classical NLS proteins use different receptor systems, the result can be selective. One would compare RNA processing status and export-factor localization before concluding that the block occurs at the pore itself.

A fourth cell has normal nuclear import of a ribosomal protein but accumulates pre-rRNA and shows an abnormal nucleolus. This points toward rRNA processing or assembly rather than proving a general import defect. If instead mature preribosomal particles accumulate in the nucleus while earlier processing is intact, a late export step becomes a stronger possibility. Distinguishing where a substrate accumulates from where its product normally appears is a powerful method for locating a blocked pathway.

In each example, begin with the physical location of the lesion, follow the substrate through the normal sequence and predict the immediate consequence. Later consequences may be indirect: impaired ribosome production can slow cell growth, changed nuclear transport can alter gene expression, and mechanical damage can secondarily affect many nuclear processes. A single endpoint rarely proves causality. NET Part C questions reward a model that explains the whole pattern and acknowledges the limitations of each observation.

The next organelle, the ER, is not a separate unrelated topic. Its membrane is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane and its lumen with the perinuclear space. However, its protein entry mechanisms, ribosome attachment and internal biochemical specialization differ from nuclear transport. The transition from nucleus to ER is therefore a study in how a cell can connect physical structures while preserving different molecular environments and functions.

17. Part B — Conceptual and assertion–reason practice

Question 1

Which compartment is directly continuous with the perinuclear space?

A. Nucleoplasm; B. ER lumen; C. Cytosol; D. Mitochondrial matrix

Answer and explanation

B. The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with ER membrane, so their lumens connect. A and C meet through the NPC channel, not through the perinuclear lumen. D is a separate organelle compartment.

Question 2

A classical nuclear localization signal most commonly contains which residues?

A. Lysine and arginine; B. Exclusively leucine; C. Only glycine; D. A long hydrophobic signal peptide

Answer and explanation

A. Basic clusters are characteristic of classical NLS motifs. B resembles many CRM1 export signals, C is not a classical NLS rule, and D describes typical ER targeting rather than nuclear entry.

Question 3

Which pairing is correct?

A. RCC1 — cytoplasmic hydrolysis; B. RanGAP — nuclear exchange; C. NTF2 — Ran-GDP import; D. CRM1 — classical NLS adapter

Answer and explanation

C. NTF2 returns Ran-GDP to nucleus. RCC1 catalyzes nucleotide exchange predominantly in nucleus, RanGAP promotes hydrolysis mainly in cytoplasm, and CRM1 is an export receptor.

Question 4

What is the predominant role of FG nucleoporins?

A. Transcribe rRNA; B. Form a selective interaction environment for transport receptors; C. Hydrolyze all cargo-bound GTP; D. Polymerize nuclear lamins

Answer and explanation

B. FG repeats support selective receptor-assisted passage. A belongs to RNA polymerases, C misassigns the Ran cycle, and D concerns lamina assembly.

Question 5

Which rRNA is not derived from the major Pol I precursor in mammalian cells?

A. 18S; B. 28S; C. 5.8S; D. 5S

Answer and explanation

D. 5S is synthesized separately by Pol III. The other three are processed from the long Pol I precursor.

Question 6

What is the principal role of the nuclear lamina?

A. ATP production; B. Structural support and chromatin-associated organization; C. Translating nuclear mRNA; D. Creating the ER lumen

Answer and explanation

B. Lamins support nuclear architecture and interact with other nuclear components. A is chiefly mitochondrial in aerobic cells, C normally occurs on cytoplasmic ribosomes, and D is a membrane topology property.

Question 7

Which statement about NPC passage is most accurate?

A. Every cargo must unfold; B. Every protein over 40 kDa is absolutely excluded; C. Many folded receptor-bound proteins can pass; D. Export occurs through separate one-way NPCs

Answer and explanation

C. Receptor interactions allow many folded cargos to traverse NPCs. A confuses other translocation routes, B turns a kinetic guideline into an absolute cutoff, and D misrepresents the shared bidirectional pore.

Question 8

Which statement describes mature mammalian red blood cells?

A. They retain all nuclear transcription; B. They lack a nucleus after maturation; C. They have a large nucleolus; D. Their nucleoid resembles that of a bacterium

Answer and explanation

B. Enucleation occurs during erythroid maturation. A and C require a nucleus; D incorrectly treats the differentiated cell as a prokaryote.

Question 9

Assertion: Nuclear import can accumulate a cargo against a concentration difference. Reason: Ran nucleotide cycling maintains asymmetric receptor binding and release.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason does not explain; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Chemical asymmetry drives repeated directional receptor cycles, maintaining a nonequilibrium cargo distribution. Mere passive passage would not maintain a free concentration difference.

Question 10

Assertion: The nucleolus has no enclosing lipid bilayer. Reason: Its components can be enriched by interactions with rRNA and assembling particles.

A. Both true, reason explains the basis for membrane-free organization; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Molecular association and ongoing assembly can create a distinct compartment without a membrane. The reason explains how the nucleolus can maintain organization despite the absence of a bilayer.

Question 11

Which is a major pathway for bulk mammalian mRNA export?

A. NXF1/NXT1; B. Importin-alpha alone; C. Sec61; D. Pol I

Answer and explanation

A. Bulk mRNA export is largely NXF1/NXT1-dependent, with cytoplasmic remodeling. B concerns classical import, C ER translocation and D rRNA synthesis.

Question 12

Which statement about open mitosis is correct?

A. All eukaryotes undergo complete envelope breakdown; B. Lamins permanently disappear; C. Phosphorylation helps reorganize the lamina; D. Nucleoli are distributed as sealed vesicles

Answer and explanation

C. Mitotic phosphorylation contributes to disassembly; lamins reassemble later. A ignores closed mitosis, B ignores recycling and reassembly, and D confuses membrane-free bodies with vesicles.

18. Part C — Experimental and quantitative practice

Question 1

A large reporter without an NLS has a nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio of 0.3. A wild-type NLS fusion gives 6.0; a point-mutated NLS gives 0.4. Which conclusion is strongest?

A. The point mutation proves that all nuclear import uses this sequence; B. The tested sequence contributes to targeting of this reporter; C. All pores have become larger; D. The mutant necessarily cannot bind any protein

Answer and explanation

B. The controlled reporter series supports a specific contribution of the signal. A generalizes beyond the experiment, C is not supported by changing only cargo, and D makes an untested claim about every interaction. Folding and stability controls remain important.

Question 2

A nuclear RanGEF loses activity while cytoplasmic RanGAP remains active. Predict the first-order effect.

A. Increased nuclear Ran-GTP; B. Impaired import-cargo release and Ran-dependent export loading; C. Increased Pol III activity necessarily; D. Loss of ER-lumen continuity

Answer and explanation

B. Exchange is needed to sustain nuclear Ran-GTP. Its decline compromises both unloading of import complexes and assembly of export complexes. A is opposite, C is not a direct necessary result, and D concerns topology.

Question 3

A mutant importin-beta binds classical cargo via its adapter and FG repeats, but cannot bind Ran-GTP. Which step is most directly compromised?

A. Cargo release and receptor recycling; B. rDNA transcription; C. Nuclear membrane lipid synthesis; D. 5S rRNA gene expression

Answer and explanation

A. Ran-GTP interaction is critical to unloading and recycling of many import complexes. Passage or docking may still occur; B, C and D are not direct functions of that receptor.

Question 4

A membrane-impermeant fluorescent solute is restricted to the ER lumen. Which nuclear-associated region can it access by simple luminal diffusion?

A. Nucleoplasm through FG repeats; B. Perinuclear space; C. Nuclear basket; D. Cytosolic filaments

Answer and explanation

B. Physical luminal continuity allows this route. NPC passage and its projections lie on the cytosol–nucleoplasm axis, not in the continuous ER lumen.

Question 5

A CRM1 inhibitor is added to cells expressing a shuttling CRM1 substrate with an intact NLS. Which observation is expected before secondary effects dominate?

A. Nuclear enrichment of substrate; B. Direct block of all NXF1 mRNA export; C. Disappearance of all pores; D. Increased exit of substrate

Answer and explanation

A. Import continues while CRM1-mediated exit is inhibited. B confuses distinct export systems; C is not the inhibitor's direct effect, and D reverses the prediction.

Question 6

A spherical nucleus of radius 5 μm has 10 pores per μm². Approximate its total pores.

A. 314; B. 3,142; C. 5,236; D. 785

Answer and explanation

B. A = 4π × 25 ≈ 314.16 μm². Multiplying by 10 gives approximately 3,142 pores. A is the area alone; C corresponds to an unrelated volume-based operation; D uses an incorrect projected area.

Question 7

At fixed density, nuclear radius triples. How does pore count per unit volume change?

A. Triples; B. Ninefold increase; C. Falls to one-third; D. Remains unchanged

Answer and explanation

C. Area and pore count scale with r², volume with r³, so count/volume scales as 1/r. A and B have the wrong direction; D would require density to increase with radius.

Question 8

An unbound tracer equilibrates with a reservoir; Vn = 150 μm³ and K = 15 μm³/s. What is the half-time of the concentration deficit?

A. 10 s; B. 6.93 s; C. 15 s; D. 0.1 s

Answer and explanation

B. τ = Vn/K = 10 s. Half-time = ln(2)τ = 6.93 s. A is the time constant, C is the numerical value of K with the wrong units, D is the inverse time constant expressed incorrectly.

Question 9

A simplified reporter has kin = 0.06 s⁻¹ and kout = 0.02 s⁻¹. After a mutation kin halves and kout doubles. What happens to its steady nuclear/cytoplasmic concentration ratio?

A. 3 to 0.75; B. 3 to 3; C. 3 to 6; D. 0.75 to 3

Answer and explanation

A. Initially 0.06/0.02 = 3. After mutation 0.03/0.04 = 0.75, a fourfold reduction. B ignores both rates, C reverses their combined effect, and D reverses the order.

Question 10

A nucleus contains twice the corrected mean reporter fluorescence per unit volume as cytoplasm, but is one-quarter of the cytoplasmic volume. What is the nuclear/cytoplasmic reporter amount ratio?

A. 2; B. 8; C. 0.5; D. 1

Answer and explanation

C. The amount is concentration times volume, so Nn/Nc = 2 × 1/4 = 0.5. A confuses concentration with amount; B divides instead of multiplies; D does not follow the data.

Question 11

Pol I transcription is selectively inhibited while Pol III initially continues. Which new RNA production is most directly affected?

A. 18S, 5.8S and 28S precursors; B. Only 5S; C. All mRNA; D. All mitochondrial rRNA by the same enzyme

Answer and explanation

A. The long Pol I precursor contains the three major rRNAs. B belongs to Pol III; C mainly to Pol II; D confuses mitochondrial transcription with nuclear Pol I. Existing mature ribosomes need not vanish immediately.

Question 12

A mutant cell accumulates normally processed pre-60S particles in nucleus while early pre-rRNA processing remains intact. Which defect is best supported?

A. Initial Pol I transcription; B. Large-subunit export or its export-factor recruitment; C. Loss of all ribosomal protein import; D. Failure to form any nucleolus

Answer and explanation

B. The assembled particles exist but fail to reach cytoplasm. A, C and D predict more upstream defects inconsistent with the given normal processing and assembly.

Question 13

A cell displays normal small-solute passage, normal classical NLS import, but strong nuclear retention of mature mRNA. Which hypothesis should be tested first?

A. Complete NPC elimination; B. NXF1-dependent export or mRNP remodeling; C. Lack of all Ran in every compartment; D. Loss of all nuclear membranes

Answer and explanation

B. The selective RNA phenotype supports an RNA-specific pathway. A and D contradict intact differential transport, and C would likely compromise many classical import pathways as well.

Question 14

A lamin mutant produces blebs under mechanical stress, yet a fluorescent NLS reporter remains nuclear. Which experiment would best test the direct proposed defect?

A. Measure nuclear deformation and lamin organization under matched stress; B. Count cytoplasmic ribosomes only; C. Measure mitochondrial oxygen alone; D. Assume all nuclear transport is lost

Answer and explanation

A. The phenotype suggests structural fragility and should be tested by a mechanical and lamina-specific readout. B and C are indirect, and D is contradicted by the reporter observation.

Question 15

A treatment prevents dephosphorylation of mitotic lamins during exit from open mitosis. What prediction is most defensible?

A. Normal lamina reassembly may be impaired; B. Nuclear import must become infinite; C. Closed mitosis becomes compulsory; D. All DNA is converted to RNA

Answer and explanation

A. Reversal of mitotic phosphorylation supports lamina assembly. B is meaningless kinetically, C is not a necessary consequence, and D is unrelated to membrane reorganization.

Question 16

Two fluorescent proteins have the same mass, but one is elongated and the other compact. Their passive NPC entry rates differ. What follows?

A. The pore has exactly two kinds of permanent size gate; B. Hydrodynamic shape and interactions can affect passive passage; C. Both must use identical importins; D. The result disproves passive diffusion

Answer and explanation

B. Molecular mass alone is not the whole determinant of passage. A invents an architecture, C assumes receptor use, and D ignores diffusion constraints in a selective crowded environment.
