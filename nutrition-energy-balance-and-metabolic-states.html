From absorption to nutritional homeostasis

A vertebrate does not normally receive nutrients at the same rate at which its cells consume them. A meal supplies a comparatively brief wave of absorbable material, whereas the brain, kidneys, heart, intestinal wall and other tissues require chemical energy even during sleep. The physiological problem is therefore not simply to extract nutrients from food. It is to match a variable supply to a continuous and changing demand without allowing the concentration of important substrates in extracellular fluid to move outside tolerable limits. This is achieved by coordinating nutrient absorption, temporary storage, oxidation and mobilization. The term nutritional homeostasis describes the maintenance of adequate nutrient availability and physiological function across these changing conditions.

The earlier chapters followed dietary polymers through hydrolysis and across the intestinal epithelium. Here the starting point is the absorbed product. Monosaccharides and most amino acids reach the liver through the portal circulation, whereas most long-chain dietary lipids leave the intestine in chylomicrons through lymph before reaching systemic blood. This anatomical difference influences the timing and distribution of fuels. It does not mean that lipids never reach the liver or that all absorbed molecules are immediately consumed. An absorbed glucose molecule may be oxidized, retained in glycogen, incorporated into a biosynthetic product or converted into another circulating substrate. An absorbed amino acid may enter a newly synthesized protein or undergo nitrogen removal and carbon skeleton metabolism.

Metabolism refers to all chemical transformations occurring in a living organism. Catabolic processes break down molecules and make energy available for cellular work; anabolic processes build new molecules and typically consume energy. Neither process is simply switched off when the other dominates. During feeding, skeletal muscle synthesizes proteins while continuing to degrade damaged proteins. During fasting, liver cells construct glucose while oxidizing fatty acids. The physiological state determines the balance of rates, not the existence of mutually exclusive pathways. This distinction will become important when interpreting tracer experiments in which a tissue simultaneously takes up and releases a substrate.

A nutrient can serve several roles. Glucose can provide energy and carbon for biosynthesis; an amino acid can provide nitrogen, carbon and a building block for a protein. Calcium contributes to skeletal structure, intracellular signaling and muscle activation, yet it is not oxidized to supply ATP. Energy availability and nutrient adequacy are therefore related but different requirements. An animal can receive abundant chemical energy while lacking a molecule necessary for growth, repair or coenzyme function. Conversely, a diet rich in vitamins and minerals may still provide insufficient energy to sustain an animal with high metabolic demand.

A useful conceptual model is to separate three quantities: the rate at which fuel enters a pool, the rate at which it leaves, and the amount present. A pool can remain constant while material flows rapidly through it. The blood glucose pool illustrates this principle particularly well. A nearly constant concentration between meals does not imply that tissues have stopped consuming glucose. Instead, hepatic and renal glucose production compensate for consumption. During the absorptive period, intestinal appearance adds another input, and endogenous production normally falls. Thus a physiological concentration is the observable outcome of interacting fluxes and does not by itself reveal which flux has changed.

Energy conservation applies to the entire organism, but individual tissues can have different energy balances. A liver cell may release glucose while an adipocyte accumulates lipid; an active muscle may consume fuel while the whole animal is gaining energy. The distinction between a local metabolic change and whole-body energy storage prevents a common mistake in interpreting physiological experiments. Increased fatty acid oxidation in one tissue is not enough to establish that whole-body fat stores have decreased. To make that inference one must examine both total entry into the fat pool and total removal over an appropriate interval.

The need to maintain an internal supply of fuel can be understood by considering ATP turnover. ATP is a relatively small, continuously renewed cellular pool rather than a large long-term store. A muscle fiber uses ATP to drive cross-bridge cycling and ion transport; a neuron uses energy to restore ion gradients after electrical activity. Although their rates of use differ, both must regenerate ATP continually. Fuel molecules contain chemical energy that can be made available through controlled oxidation, while the ATP system couples that energy to work. The presence of large quantities of triglyceride in the body does not remove the immediate requirement to deliver usable fuel to a cell and to maintain its capacity for ATP production.

The same nutritional problem can be examined at three scales. At the molecular scale, oxidation, reduction and biosynthesis determine what can be done with particular molecules. At the organ scale, specialized tissues take up, transform, store or release these molecules. At the organism scale, the sum of intake and expenditure determines how much chemical energy is retained. An explanation at one scale may be necessary but insufficient at another. For instance, an enzyme that accelerates triglyceride hydrolysis may explain fatty acid release from adipocytes, but the ultimate change in fat mass depends on re-esterification, dietary fatty acid delivery and oxidation throughout the body. This is why it is useful to draw a system boundary before using an energy or mass balance.

Nutrients can also be classified by their immediate destination rather than by chemical identity. Some absorbed material becomes part of a rapidly circulating pool, some is temporarily stored, some is incorporated into functional tissue and some is oxidized. These destinations compete for the same substrate. A glucose molecule that becomes a residue in glycogen is not being oxidized at that instant, but it may be oxidized later. A fatty acid incorporated into a membrane is not part of the neutral triglyceride reserve, even though both molecules contain fatty acyl chains. An experimental label followed into a lipid fraction must therefore be interpreted in relation to the kind of lipid identified.

A further distinction concerns a substrate's availability and its utilization. Availability can refer to the amount of a substance in blood, to its delivery rate through perfusion or to its access across a cell membrane. Utilization concerns its subsequent metabolism. An organ with reduced blood flow may receive less substrate even if arterial concentration is unchanged. A tissue with defective transport may fail to take up a nutrient that is abundant outside the cell. A tissue with impaired oxidation may take it up without extracting the expected energy. Consequently, the statement that a nutrient is present is not the same as the statement that it is physiologically available or useful. This distinction connects the intestinal transport mechanisms of the preceding chapter with the nutritional states considered here.

Homeostasis is often depicted as a fixed horizontal line, but physiological variables fluctuate within ranges. Following a meal, glucose appearance changes rapidly and the regulatory system allows a temporary increase in concentration while acting to limit the excursion. Over a much longer interval, changes in energy stores may alter feeding behavior and expenditure. A feedback system that corrects every small fluctuation immediately would be unrealistic; absorption, transport, endocrine responses and metabolic conversion have finite rates. A physiological prediction should therefore specify the time scale of observation. A result measured minutes after feeding and a result measured after weeks of altered nutrition can be consistent even if their directions differ.

Nutrient classes and dietary essentiality

The macronutrients are conventionally grouped as carbohydrates, lipids and proteins because they are required in substantial amounts and their organic carbon skeletons can supply chemical energy. Water is also needed in large quantities and is sometimes classified as a macronutrient, although it supplies no metabolizable energy. Micronutrients, including vitamins and mineral elements, are required in smaller quantities. The classification refers to the amount required, not to importance. A severe lack of a micronutrient can impair metabolic reactions even when macronutrient intake is ample.

Dietary essentiality is a statement about biosynthetic capacity relative to physiological need. A molecule is nutritionally essential when the organism cannot make enough of it under the relevant conditions and must obtain it from its environment. The term does not mean that a nonessential nutrient lacks biological importance. Glucose, for example, is a crucial substrate for mature mammalian erythrocytes, but adult humans can produce glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. In contrast, linoleic acid must be supplied because mammalian enzymes cannot introduce a double bond at the appropriate position in the carbon chain. Essentiality can differ among species, developmental stages and physiological conditions.

Carbohydrates include sugars, starches and other polysaccharides. Digestible starch provides glucose after hydrolysis, whereas sugars contribute monosaccharides directly or after disaccharide cleavage. Some carbohydrate polymers resist host digestive enzymes. Their fate depends on chemical structure and on microbial fermentation. In humans, fermentable fibre can yield short-chain fatty acids absorbed from the colon; other material leaves in faeces. The absence of a conventional essential carbohydrate category should not be confused with the absence of a glucose requirement in particular cells, or with an assumption that all carbohydrate sources have the same physiological effects.

Dietary lipids serve as concentrated energy sources and provide fatty acids for membrane phospholipids. Triglycerides contain a glycerol backbone esterified to three fatty acids. Storage of reduced carbon in fatty acid chains is efficient because their oxidation transfers many electrons to cellular electron carriers. Neutral lipid also associates with much less water than glycogen. Phospholipids and cholesterol, on the other hand, have important structural functions that cannot be captured by a simple energy count. Dietary lipid can also assist the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, although absorption depends on additional factors such as bile and mucosal integrity.

Proteins supply amino acids for structural proteins, transporters, enzymes, receptors and other nitrogen-containing compounds. Unlike adipose triglyceride and glycogen, the body's functional protein mass is not a dedicated inert fuel store. Mobilizing this mass can reduce contractile capacity, immune competence or enzyme function. Dietary protein quality therefore depends not only on its total mass but also on its digestibility and the availability of indispensable amino acids in relation to physiological requirements. A protein source deficient in a required amino acid cannot sustain unrestricted protein synthesis merely because it provides many other amino acids.

The distinction between an essential nutrient and an essential dietary source becomes clearer when considering the variety of animal feeding strategies. Ruminant microorganisms can synthesize amino acids and certain vitamins using substrates available in the rumen. The host subsequently digests much of the microbial biomass and absorbs microbial fermentation products. This alters the relationship between dietary composition and absorbed nutrients but does not eliminate the biochemical requirements of the host's tissues. A species that relies on microbial provision may be sensitive to changes in its fermentation system that have little effect on a species obtaining the same molecules directly from food.

Nutritional requirements also change during growth, reproduction and recovery. A growing animal must retain amino acids and minerals to construct new tissue, whereas a mature animal at stable mass must principally replace losses and support turnover. A lactating mammal transfers energy, amino acids, water and minerals to offspring. Comparing the same absolute intake in these different circumstances would be misleading unless the relevant outputs are included. Requirement is a relationship between intake, absorption, endogenous synthesis and demand rather than a constant property of a nutrient molecule.

Carbohydrate classification in nutrition involves more than the presence of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Starch and cellulose are both glucose polymers, yet their glycosidic linkages differ and mammalian enzymes do not readily hydrolyze cellulose. A chemical analysis that reports the mass of carbohydrate does not necessarily indicate the mass of absorbable glucose. A portion of resistant starch can also escape small-intestinal digestion and enter the colon, where microbes may ferment it. The amount of energy available from a food thus reflects both its chemical constituents and the digestive and microbial capacities of the species consuming it. This is the physiological reason that dietary composition must be interpreted in conjunction with digestibility.

The same comparison applies to lipids. Triglyceride molecules differ in fatty acid chain length and degree of unsaturation, affecting their physical properties and some aspects of absorption and metabolism. Medium-chain fatty acids can reach portal blood more directly than many long-chain fatty acids, which are commonly incorporated into intestinal chylomicrons. Consequently, a similar mass of lipid does not necessarily create the same postprandial lipoprotein profile. Nevertheless, both groups can contribute to energy metabolism. The distinction is about delivery and molecular properties, not a rigid division between energy-bearing and non-energy-bearing lipids.

Proteins also differ in their physical accessibility to digestive enzymes. The nutritional value of a protein reflects its amino acid composition, digestibility and the amount consumed. A chemical assay of nitrogen may provide an estimate of total protein, but it cannot by itself identify which essential amino acid limits protein synthesis. Moreover, nitrogen may occur in nonprotein compounds. The general conversion of nitrogen to protein mass is thus useful in simple exercises while remaining an approximation. A complete evaluation of a diet cannot be obtained from its gross nitrogen content alone.

A physiological requirement can be expressed as a rate needed to maintain a functional pool or to support a new output. For example, the body loses some water through skin, lung, urine and faeces; the replacement requirement depends on those rates as well as metabolic water production. During heat stress, evaporation increases, altering water need without necessarily increasing energy intake by a proportional amount. During rapid growth, synthesis and deposition of tissue increase nutrient requirements; the growth-related need cannot be inferred from an adult maintenance requirement alone. These examples demonstrate that the word essential describes a dependency on an external supply, while the quantity required is an experimentally determined property of a physiological condition.

Another distinction is between the requirement for a nutrient and a recommendation for its intake. The physiological requirement concerns the amount needed for a defined function under defined conditions. Population dietary recommendations must also take account of variability in needs, measurement uncertainty and other practical factors. This chapter uses nutrient requirements to explain physiological mechanisms rather than to prescribe amounts for individuals. An exam question that provides a specific requirement is usually asking the student to compare it with the stated available intake. If no requirement or absorption estimate is supplied, one cannot determine sufficiency from the mass of a nutrient alone.

In a comparative problem, a herbivorous animal and a carnivorous animal can have different capacities for endogenous synthesis or access to microbial products. The general concept of essentiality remains applicable, but the list established for humans cannot automatically be imposed on every animal. Some mammals synthesize ascorbate whereas humans require a dietary source. Similarly, species differ in their ability to convert fatty acid precursors into particular long-chain products. A good answer names the organism and physiological stage rather than treating human nutritional classifications as universal biological laws.

Essential amino acids and fatty acids

Human nutrition conventionally recognizes nine indispensable amino acids: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Their carbon skeletons cannot be synthesized in adequate quantities for normal needs. Other amino acids can often be generated by pathways such as transamination, provided suitable substrates are present. The boundaries are conditional. Cysteine synthesis depends on sulfur supplied by methionine, and tyrosine can be formed from phenylalanine. An inability to carry out such conversions, or unusually high demand, changes the practical need for the product. In certain stages of development or physiological stress, endogenous synthesis may not match demand for some normally dispensable amino acids.

Protein synthesis requires all the amino acids specified by a polypeptide sequence. Imagine a cell that can construct a particular protein only when it has access to enough of twenty different building blocks. Excess of nineteen of them cannot replace the twentieth at the position specified by a codon. This illustrates the limiting amino acid principle, although real nutritional physiology also involves turnover, tissue pools, digestive availability and the ability to redistribute substrates between organs. A mixture of different dietary proteins can improve the combined amino acid profile if the deficits of one are compensated by the other. The correct comparison is not a simple count of how many amino acid names appear on a food label.

A nitrogen balance calculation gives one quantitative approach to whole-body protein status. Nitrogen input is compared with losses through urine, faeces and other routes. In a simplified problem protein is assumed to contain about sixteen percent nitrogen by mass, giving grams of nitrogen approximately equal to grams of protein divided by 6.25. The factor is an approximation because amino acid compositions vary. Net positive nitrogen balance may occur during growth or recovery, while negative balance indicates loss of body nitrogen. An animal can be in negative nitrogen balance even when energy balance is positive, for example when its available amino acid supply does not meet needs. Conversely, a growing animal may retain nitrogen despite simultaneous mobilization of some lipid.

The conventional essential fatty acids for adult human nutrition are linoleic acid, an omega-6 fatty acid, and alpha-linolenic acid, an omega-3 fatty acid (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements). Omega numbering counts from the methyl end of the chain. Mammals can elongate and further desaturate these starting molecules, but cannot generally create the defining omega-6 or omega-3 double bond arrangement de novo from oleic acid. Products include arachidonic acid from the omega-6 family and long-chain omega-3 fatty acids including EPA and DHA, although conversion efficiency varies. The relevant families are not interchangeable simply because they contain the same number of carbon atoms.

Fatty acid composition affects membrane physical properties and the availability of precursors for signaling molecules. This explains why a requirement for essential fatty acids is not equivalent to a requirement for a fixed energy fraction from fat. A diet could contain ample triglyceride composed predominantly of fatty acids that do not meet the essential requirement. The resulting deficiency is conceptually analogous to an amino acid limitation: supplying more total material does not repair a missing required chemical structure. Conversely, a small mass of an indispensable fatty acid can have consequences disproportionate to its contribution to total caloric supply.

In interpreting a controlled nutritional study, energy content, protein intake and essential fatty acid composition are separate experimental variables. If an experimental group grows more slowly after a change in diet, the cause cannot be assigned to fatty acid deficiency solely because the altered food contains less fat. The experiment should distinguish total energy, digestibility, intake, developmental state and the specific lipid composition. A rescue by replacement of the missing fatty acid, while other conditions remain comparable, would support a stronger causal interpretation than a simple correlation between dietary fat and growth.

The limiting amino acid concept can be explored quantitatively without confusing a simplified model with actual protein synthesis. Suppose a hypothetical animal needs ten available units of each of two indispensable amino acids to construct a defined amount of new protein. A diet supplies eighteen units of the first but only six of the second. The second supply can support six tenths of the stipulated synthetic target, even though the total number of units exceeds the combined requirement. If the first amino acid is further increased, the immediate modeled limit remains unchanged. If the second is supplied at ten units, the modeled target becomes possible, assuming no other constraint. The numbers are invented solely to demonstrate the principle; they do not represent amino acid requirements in any species.

The physiological model is more complicated because amino acids are continually released from existing proteins. In a short observation, a tissue may maintain synthesis despite reduced dietary supply by using endogenous amino acids. Such compensation does not demonstrate that the missing dietary component is unnecessary. The continued transfer of material from existing tissues can reduce net protein retention even while synthesis in a prioritized tissue remains temporarily normal. The appropriate whole-body outcome is therefore not simply the synthesis rate in one organ; one must also assess degradation, excretion and growth. This reasoning explains why duration and the choice of an endpoint matter in a nutritional experiment.

Consider a nitrogen-balance dataset explicitly constructed for teaching. An animal consumes 75 grams of protein during a defined interval; assume that protein contains sixteen percent nitrogen and that no other nitrogen source contributes. Nitrogen intake is 12 grams. The urine contains 9.0 grams of nitrogen, faecal output contains 1.5 grams, and measured other losses total 0.5 grams. Total losses are 11 grams and net nitrogen balance is positive by 1 gram. If the same animal loses an additional 2 grams of nitrogen through an unmeasured route, the true balance becomes negative by 1 gram. A positive apparent balance can thus result from incomplete measurement rather than genuine tissue retention.

The interpretation of nitrogen balance also requires appropriate temporal boundaries. Nitrogen consumed near the end of a measurement interval may remain in gastrointestinal contents, while urine nitrogen may reflect metabolism of food consumed earlier. A short interval therefore need not align input with output. In longer, controlled intervals, changes in tissue pools and the accuracy of collections still matter. A nitrogen balance of zero is compatible with substantial turnover of proteins and does not prove that every tissue is retaining its original protein mass. For example, amino acid redistribution can allow one compartment to gain protein while another loses it.

Essential fatty acid nomenclature deserves special attention because students often confuse the omega position with the delta position. In delta notation, the position of a double bond is counted from the carboxyl end; omega notation counts from the methyl end. Linoleic acid has eighteen carbon atoms and two double bonds and belongs to the omega-6 family. Alpha-linolenic acid has eighteen carbon atoms and three double bonds and belongs to the omega-3 family. Elongation adds carbon at the carboxyl end and can preserve the omega family classification. The inability of mammalian enzymes to establish the requisite distal double bond arrangement explains the dietary dependency.

For a controlled comparison, an experimental diet may contain the same total grams of fat as the control yet replace linoleic acid with a nonessential fatty acid. Similar energy intake does not imply equivalent capacity to produce membrane components and signaling precursors. Conversely, addition of linoleic acid at the expense of another fatty acid could improve a deficient phenotype with little change in total energy. This is a particularly clear example of how causal nutritional evidence can distinguish molecular necessity from caloric supply.

Vitamins, mineral elements and water

Vitamins are organic molecules needed in small amounts for normal function. Many are precursors of coenzymes that assist catalytic reactions. A coenzyme carries a chemical group or participates in electron transfer during an enzyme-catalyzed reaction. Thiamine contributes to thiamine pyrophosphate, which supports oxidative decarboxylation and other carbon transfer reactions. Niacin contributes to NAD and NADP, carriers central to oxidation-reduction reactions. Riboflavin supplies flavin derivatives such as FAD. These examples illustrate why inadequate vitamin availability can alter the ability to process energy-bearing nutrients without the vitamin itself serving as a major source of energy.

The fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K have diverse functions. Vitamin A derivatives support vision and gene regulation; vitamin D participates in regulation of calcium and phosphate homeostasis; vitamin E helps protect lipids against oxidative damage; vitamin K supports the modification of specific proteins, including clotting factors. Their intestinal handling is linked to lipid digestion and absorption (OpenStax, Nutrition and Diet). Deficient uptake can therefore follow impaired lipid assimilation despite adequate ingestion. Some fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate substantially in tissues, making their storage characteristics distinct from those of many water-soluble vitamins. The distinction is not absolute: vitamin B12, though water soluble, can be stored in the body for long periods.

Minerals are inorganic elements with structural, electrical or catalytic roles. Calcium and phosphate contribute to bone mineral, while sodium and potassium support extracellular and intracellular electrochemical gradients. Iron in haem proteins participates in oxygen transport and electron transfer. Iodine is required for thyroid hormone production, zinc supports numerous proteins and magnesium interacts with phosphate groups in ATP and other metabolites. Minerals cannot be created by metabolic pathways because chemical reactions rearrange existing atoms rather than transmute elements. Their adequacy depends on intake, absorption, distribution and conservation. A normal total body content does not always guarantee an appropriate functional supply to a particular tissue.

Water forms the solvent in which most cellular biochemical reactions occur and is necessary for transport, osmotic regulation and heat exchange. It is acquired from drinking, food and metabolic production during oxidation. Water is also lost in urine, faeces, respiratory vapour and evaporation. Water content is large relative to the mass of many nutrient stores and can change comparatively rapidly. This is why body weight is not a direct calorimeter. A change in water balance can alter total mass without a corresponding change in stored chemical energy. Understanding this separation is essential when using mass changes as evidence for energy balance.

Micronutrient deficiency can arise from inadequate intake, reduced absorption, altered metabolism, increased requirement or excessive loss. For example, failure of intrinsic-factor-dependent vitamin B12 absorption can reduce supply even when dietary vitamin content is adequate. The biochemical manifestation of deficiency may be delayed by pre-existing tissue stores. Different deficiencies can produce overlapping symptoms, so an observed functional change cannot always identify the missing nutrient without further evidence. Controlled nutrient replacement experiments are valuable when the relevant outcome improves specifically after the suspected limitation is corrected.

Nutritional sufficiency therefore cannot be described by a single energy number. Adequate energy must be accompanied by an appropriate supply of indispensable substrates, minerals and vitamins, plus the ability to absorb and utilize them. The physiological goal is maintenance of functional pools and appropriate growth or turnover, not maximization of any one intake. For examinations, separate the chemical identity of a nutrient, its role in physiology, its source and its contribution to oxidation. Those four features often differ.

Vitamin deficiency can limit metabolic throughput by affecting a particular reaction or set of reactions. In the case of thiamine-derived cofactor, reduced coenzyme availability can impair enzymes involved in converting pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and in other carbon transformations. An organism may therefore receive enough carbohydrate yet have difficulty processing part of the resulting carbon flux through oxidative pathways. The interpretation does not require claiming that the vitamin provides energy. Instead, the vitamin supports catalytic capacity, which enables energy-yielding reactions to proceed. The detailed reaction mechanisms belong to the biomolecular metabolism unit, but this physiological consequence is central to nutritional sufficiency.

The relationship between dietary mineral intake and tissue availability is similarly indirect. Iron exists in food in forms with different bioavailability. Absorptive and systemic regulatory mechanisms influence the amount ultimately incorporated into haem and other iron-containing proteins. A deficiency of iron may reduce oxygen-carrying capacity, which can restrict aerobic performance despite adequate carbohydrate and fat stores. A primary shortage of oxygen delivery differs mechanistically from a shortage of oxidizable substrate. Both may reduce the ability of tissues to sustain work, but increasing one variable does not necessarily compensate for the other.

Calcium offers an example of compartmental regulation. Its concentration in extracellular fluid is tightly regulated because of its roles in signaling, contraction and membrane excitability. Most body calcium is retained in mineralized tissue. A normal extracellular calcium concentration can therefore coexist with changes in skeletal calcium stores, provided regulatory mechanisms maintain the circulating pool. This reinforces the difference between a measured concentration and the total amount stored. When an exam provides a normal plasma measurement, it may not be sufficient evidence that long-term nutritional status is normal.

Water intake is likewise distinct from the amount of water retained. Renal conservation, evaporative loss and osmotic regulation contribute to the balance. Imagine a laboratory animal whose mass rises by 40 grams immediately after drinking 40 millilitres of water, assuming water density is approximately one gram per millilitre and no simultaneous losses. The increase in measured mass does not correspond to a 40-gram increase in tissue protein or lipid. Later excretion could reverse the change with negligible loss of body chemical energy. This simple scenario demonstrates why mass change cannot be translated directly into an energy change without information about material composition.

The fat-soluble vitamins illustrate the difference between ingestion and assimilation. A disturbance in bile salt delivery may impair formation of mixed micelles and thereby reduce the effective uptake of several fat-soluble nutrients. A water-soluble nutrient using a different transport mechanism may be less affected under the same conditions. Conversely, a defect involving a specific receptor for intrinsic factor-bound B12 may produce a selective problem despite normal lipid absorption. A carefully formulated experimental question can ask students to distinguish a generalized reduction in intake from a mechanistically selective failure of absorption. The correct prediction follows from tracing each nutrient along the absorptive pathway.

Nutritional status also depends on retention and utilization after absorption. A molecule can be absorbed normally but lost excessively through an excretory pathway; alternatively, a defect in conversion to a biologically active form can impair function despite an adequate precursor supply. These possibilities explain why the term deficiency can refer to inadequate functional availability rather than simply a measured dietary shortage. The strongest analysis identifies the affected step: ingestion, digestion, epithelial uptake, systemic transport, tissue conversion or retention. The next step is to predict what would happen to relevant blood, tissue or excretion measurements if that step were selectively impaired.

Gross energy, digestibility and metabolizable energy

The heat released when a dry sample is completely combusted in a bomb calorimeter measures gross energy. A bomb calorimeter encloses the sample in an oxygen-rich vessel and measures the temperature rise of a surrounding system with a known heat capacity. If the effective heat capacity is C and the corrected temperature increase is ΔT, the heat released has magnitude CΔT. The energy measured depends on chemical composition. The apparatus can oxidize compounds that are not fully digested or absorbed by an animal, so gross energy is not generally the physiological energy available to the organism.

A simplified sequence of energy accounting starts with gross energy intake. Subtracting the energy recovered in faeces gives apparent digestible energy. Further subtraction of energy in urine and combustible gases gives metabolizable energy. The term apparent reminds us that faecal material contains more than undigested dietary matter: endogenous secretions, sloughed epithelial cells and microbial products may contribute. Protein presents another distinction because nitrogen is often excreted in energy-containing compounds rather than fully oxidized to the products produced in a combustion chamber. Species differences in nitrogen waste and fermentation can alter these losses. The simplified animal-nutrition convention used in this chapter assigns combustible gases to the subsequent deduction; some food-energy accounting schemes, including the FAO conceptual diagram, deduct gaseous losses at the digestible-energy stage and include small surface losses in the metabolizable-energy calculation. State the convention explicitly; consistent bookkeeping gives the same final value for the same included losses.

The widely used general Atwater conversion factors for ordinary human mixed foods are approximately four kilocalories per gram for available carbohydrate, four for protein and nine for fat. A food kilocalorie is one thousand small calories, and one kilocalorie equals 4.184 kilojoules. The factors approximate physiological metabolizable energy rather than the exact gross combustion energy or the energy captured as ATP. They incorporate average assumptions about digestion and nitrogenous losses. They are not universal properties of every molecule in the nutrient class, and food-specific conversion factors, fibre fermentation and composition measurements can produce different estimates.

Consider a hypothetical meal containing 75 grams of available carbohydrate, 25 grams of protein and 20 grams of fat. Using the general factors, carbohydrate contributes 75 × 4 = 300 kilocalories, protein contributes 25 × 4 = 100 kilocalories, and fat contributes 20 × 9 = 180 kilocalories. Their sum is 580 kilocalories, equivalent to 2426.72 kilojoules. The contribution from fat is 180 divided by 580, approximately 31.0 percent of estimated energy, although fat accounts for only 20 out of the 120 grams of the three listed macronutrients. The example demonstrates why energy percentage and mass percentage are not interchangeable.

A second illustrative balance uses gross energy of 2500 kilocalories, faecal energy of 180 kilocalories, urinary energy of 70 kilocalories and combustible gas energy of 10 kilocalories. Apparent digestible energy is 2500 minus 180, or 2320 kilocalories. Metabolizable energy is 2320 minus 70 minus 10, or 2240 kilocalories. The apparent digestibility of energy is 2320 divided by 2500, or 92.8 percent. The metabolizable fraction is 2240 divided by 2500, or 89.6 percent. These are distinct quantities and the experiment must specify which it measures. Treating both as synonymous would overestimate energy retained by the physiological system.

The metabolic costs of processing a meal, including digestion, transport, synthesis and nitrogen disposal, contribute to energy expenditure. They should not be subtracted twice. If one uses metabolizable energy as intake and total expenditure already includes the thermic effect of food, the energy cost has already been counted on the expenditure side. An alternative accounting convention based on net available energy must adjust its expenditure definition accordingly. The physical result is independent of accounting convention only when boundaries and terms are consistent.

In the late nineteenth century, Wilbur Olin Atwater and colleagues combined chemical combustion measurements with physiological studies of dietary energy losses, laying the basis of the general conversion factors. FAO's 2003 technical report summarizes their history and assumptions. The practical outcome was a system connecting food composition to expected physiological energy availability. Such empirical conversion factors make large-scale food composition calculations possible without individually measuring every meal in a respiration chamber. Their scientific limitation is equally important: a calculated energy intake is an estimate. Variation in analytical methods, digestion, microbial fermentation and food structure means a precise-looking number does not necessarily represent a precisely measured individual flux.

The bomb calorimeter and the living animal answer different questions. The calorimeter measures combustion under conditions designed to maximize complete oxidation. In an animal, some compounds are not digested, some enter microbial pathways and some carbon or nitrogen leaves the body before complete oxidation. A measurement of heat from complete combustion thus establishes a chemical energy input but not the metabolic energy that remains after physiological losses. To compare two diets, one could measure the combustion energy of representative food samples and collect excreted material over a defined interval. The resulting estimate of available energy depends on the completeness of the measurements and on appropriate treatment of gaseous losses.

A simple calorimeter calculation illustrates the units. Suppose the calibrated effective heat capacity of the instrument is 10.0 kilojoules per kelvin. Combustion of a 2.00-gram hypothetical dried sample produces a corrected increase of 3.00 kelvin. The released energy is 30.0 kilojoules, equivalent to approximately 7.17 kilocalories. Per gram, the measured gross energy is 15.0 kilojoules, or approximately 3.59 kilocalories. This does not establish the metabolizable energy of the sample. If it contains substantial resistant organic matter, the physiological value could be lower. The distinction between measurement of an energy quantity and inference about its physiological fate is the key point.

The protein factor deserves special explanation. Complete combustion of protein involves oxidation of organic molecules under conditions different from the biochemical disposal of nitrogen. In mammals, amino nitrogen is processed into urea and other nitrogenous products, which retain some chemical energy and leave the body. Consequently, the average metabolizable value assigned to protein is lower than its gross heat of combustion. In other taxa, the principal form of nitrogen excretion differs; this can change the relation between gross and metabolizable energy. A single human food conversion convention should therefore not be applied uncritically to a ruminant or an aquatic animal.

The caloric value of a nutrient is also not its ATP yield. ATP yield depends on the metabolic route, oxygen availability, mitochondrial coupling and the accounting convention used for energetic costs. Two foods with the same metabolizable energy can produce different thermic responses after ingestion because their digestion and processing require different amounts of metabolic work. The energy values used in nutrition are measured or estimated on a whole-body basis. A calculation of ATP molecules per molecule of a specific substrate is a different problem that belongs to biochemical energy transduction.

A further worked example compares two hypothetical food portions of equal macronutrient mass. Portion A contains 50 grams of available carbohydrate and 10 grams of protein; portion B contains 30 grams of available carbohydrate, 10 grams of protein and 20 grams of fat. Both contain 60 grams of listed macronutrients. A provides an estimated 240 kilocalories, while B provides 120 plus 40 plus 180, or 340 kilocalories. The difference is 100 kilocalories. The substitution of 20 grams of carbohydrate by 20 grams of fat changes estimated energy by 20 times the difference between their factors, or 100 kilocalories. Water and other ingredients would alter total food mass but do not change this stated calculation.

There is an additional distinction between energy density on a dry mass basis and energy density of a food as eaten. Adding water increases wet mass while leaving the energy of the original dry components essentially unchanged. A food containing 400 kilocalories in 100 grams of dry material would have 200 kilocalories per 100 grams if an additional 100 grams of water were incorporated without other changes. This elementary dilution example helps prevent erroneous comparisons of dry food composition with prepared or hydrated foods. Any numerical question must specify the mass basis and whether the listed carbohydrate represents digestible material or total carbohydrate.

The energy-balance equation and its time scale

Energy is conserved. For a living animal treated as an open system, energy crosses the boundary in food and leaves as heat, external work and energy-containing products. If intake is expressed as metabolizable energy, the energy in faeces, urine and relevant gases has already been deducted. Let E in denote metabolizable energy entering the system over a specified interval and E out denote the corresponding energy expenditure. The change in stored energy over that interval is E in minus E out. If rates are measured in kilocalories per day, their difference is a rate; multiplying by the appropriate duration yields an energy quantity.

Positive energy balance means intake exceeds expenditure over the interval and stored energy increases. Negative energy balance means expenditure exceeds intake and stored energy decreases. Neutral energy balance means the net change is approximately zero. These statements describe an integrated outcome, not a particular endocrine signal or a specific tissue. A growing animal with positive balance may deposit protein and lipid; an adult with positive balance may primarily expand fat stores but may also refill glycogen or change other tissues. Neutral balance does not imply that catabolism and anabolism are absent. Both may proceed rapidly while their integrated effects cancel.

The expenditure side includes basal maintenance, physical activity and the thermic response to feeding; additional costs vary with physiological circumstances such as cold exposure, growth and reproduction. Basal metabolic rate is measured under a restrictive set of standardized conditions, not simply any period during which an animal is resting. Total daily expenditure includes activity and other energy-consuming processes not represented by a basal measurement. A numerical problem that compares dietary energy only with BMR will ordinarily overstate the energy surplus unless it explicitly assumes that no other expenditure contributes. Node 7.I.06 develops the measurement methods and respiratory gas calculations.

Suppose an illustrative subject receives 2300 kilocalories of metabolizable energy during a day. Maintenance and resting expenditure is estimated at 1500 kilocalories, activity expenditure at 450 kilocalories and meal-related thermogenesis at 200 kilocalories. If these are mutually exclusive and exhaustive components, expenditure equals 2150 kilocalories. The calculated daily balance is positive by 150 kilocalories. If the same balance were observed for ten days the total stored energy increase would be 1500 kilocalories, not necessarily a corresponding fixed increment of tissue mass. The example assumes a constant balance and says nothing about adaptation during those ten days.

The body can adjust both intake and expenditure when circumstances change. Hunger and satiety influence the likelihood of further feeding, while movement, tissue metabolism and heat generation alter energy output. A persistent imposed reduction in energy intake need not produce an indefinitely linear change in body mass, because mass, composition and expenditure may change with time. Conservation remains valid throughout. What changes is the numerical value of the input or output term. A distinction between a physical identity and a predictive physiological model is essential: the energy-balance equation is an accounting identity; predictions require a model for each changing flux.

Even short periods of positive and negative balance can occur within a single day. Between meals the body may draw on reserves; following a meal it may replenish them. A daily average can conceal these alternating periods. An individual experiment measuring plasma metabolites shortly after a meal cannot establish the energy balance of the day without additional data. Similarly, a stable body mass recorded across a few days cannot establish exact energy balance if changes in hydration and digestive contents obscure changes in tissue stores.

The energy balance of an animal can be written for a longer period by adding shorter intervals. If daily balance varies, the stored-energy change equals the sum of the individual balances, provided the same accounting boundary is used. Suppose a hypothetical animal has daily balances of positive 120, negative 80, positive 60 and negative 100 kilocalories over four days. Summing the signed quantities yields zero. The animal can therefore end the interval with the same total chemical energy as at the beginning even though it experienced four days with nonzero balance. Its exact tissue composition need not be identical, because different storage pools may have changed in opposite directions.

The relationship between stored energy and body mass has an additional mathematical uncertainty. A tissue's energy density is defined as the energy associated with a stated mass of that tissue. If a modeled storage change consists only of a compartment with constant energy density, dividing the energy change by that density gives a modeled mass change. However, the human body is not a single compartment of fixed energy density. Glycogen-associated water, protein, fat, mineral content and extracellular fluid can change together. Thus the familiar idea of applying a universal fixed energy-per-kilogram factor to every short-term mass change is not justified. The assumptions, not merely the arithmetic, determine the value of the estimate.

During growth, positive energy balance can be expected because new tissue contains chemical energy. The expenditure term includes the metabolic cost of constructing and maintaining tissue, while the retained energy belongs to the storage term. Confusing the energetic cost of synthesis with the energy retained in the synthesized tissue can lead to double counting. In a lactating animal, energy carried away in milk is an additional output if milk leaves the chosen system boundary. If the accounting model includes milk energy in expenditure, it should not be deducted separately. If it defines expenditure solely as heat and external work, the exported energy must be included as its own term.

Consider an animal with 1000 kilocalories of metabolizable intake, 700 kilocalories of heat and external work, and 200 kilocalories exported as milk in a day. The net retained chemical energy is 100 kilocalories if these are the only relevant terms. Another animal with the same intake and heat output but no milk export would retain 300 kilocalories under the same assumptions. The example is not a model of typical lactation requirements; it demonstrates how defining the system boundary changes the calculation. The same logic applies to energy deposited in eggs, reproductive material or other exported products.

Energy expenditure is often estimated rather than measured directly. Different methods have different uncertainty and temporal resolution. A recorded activity level can be converted to an approximate expenditure using a model, whereas calorimetry measures aspects of energy turnover more directly. If intake and expenditure are both estimated, the uncertainty in their difference can be relatively large. For example, an estimate of intake of 2000 with possible error of 100 kilocalories and expenditure of 1950 with possible error of 100 does not establish a precise positive balance of 50. A plausible range of differences can include both negative and positive values. Examination problems often provide exact input numbers, but experimental interpretation must consider the uncertainty.

Energy balance also differs from heat balance. An animal may maintain a nearly stable core temperature because heat production and heat loss are approximately matched, while its chemical energy stores decrease as it oxidizes stored fuels. Alternatively, the body may retain chemical energy from food while continuing to dissipate heat. The conservation law applies to both phenomena, but the stored variable and system boundaries differ. This distinction connects energy nutrition to thermoregulation without replacing the discussion of physical heat transfer in subunit 7.G.

Glycogen, triglyceride and functional protein

Animals store carbohydrate mainly as glycogen, a branched glucose polymer. Its branching architecture provides multiple ends at which enzymes can add or remove residues, permitting relatively rapid mobilization. Storing glucose in polymer form also avoids generating the enormous osmotic load that would arise if comparable numbers of glucose molecules were retained as free solute. Glycogen granules nevertheless associate with water, and changes in glycogen content may therefore coincide with changes in tissue hydration. The magnitude of associated water is context dependent and should not be treated as a universal fixed molecular ratio.

Liver glycogen and skeletal muscle glycogen are physiologically different pools. The liver can convert glycogen-derived glucose phosphate into free glucose using glucose-6-phosphatase and release it into blood. Skeletal muscle generally lacks the capacity for substantial export of free glucose from glycogen because it lacks the relevant glucose-6-phosphatase activity. Muscle glycogen therefore principally supports local energy metabolism. Muscle may contribute indirectly to circulating glucose production through release of lactate and alanine, which can serve as gluconeogenic precursors elsewhere. An examination question asking which tissue maintains blood glucose during early fasting should not be answered simply by comparing total glycogen masses.

Adipose tissue stores much of the body's reserve energy as triglyceride in intracellular lipid droplets. Triglyceride is highly reduced and hydrophobic, producing high energy yield per unit dry mass and requiring comparatively little associated water. When mobilized, its fatty acids can support oxidative tissues, and its glycerol can enter gluconeogenic pathways in organs equipped to use it. Fatty acid release is a regulated flux, not necessarily equivalent to net oxidation. Some released fatty acids are re-esterified. The circulating concentration depends on both release and removal, so a higher concentration does not uniquely establish a higher rate of fat oxidation.

There is no dedicated large pool of storage protein equivalent to adipose triglyceride. Tissue proteins serve physiological functions and undergo continuous turnover. Amino acid availability varies with dietary absorption, degradation and synthesis. During prolonged shortage, the use of amino acids as fuel or glucose precursors imposes costs on tissue integrity, explaining why starvation adaptation tends to reduce but cannot eliminate net protein loss. Growth and repair require that synthesis be supported even while proteins are continuously broken down.

The energetic consequences of losing equal masses of different tissue components are not equal. An illustrative loss of 100 grams of pure triglyceride represents roughly 900 kilocalories of chemical energy if the general lipid value is used as an approximation. A loss of 100 grams of hydrated tissue containing water, protein and minerals has a different energy equivalent. A loss of water alone can change scale mass without removing appreciable stored chemical energy. The examples distinguish material mass from energy content; they are not intended as a fixed conversion for human adipose tissue.

An experimental design investigating reserve mobilization might combine repeated body composition measurements with estimates of metabolizable intake and expenditure. Even then, precision is limited by measurement error, compartment assumptions and changing hydration. A single body mass reading cannot distinguish these possibilities. An independent estimate of glycogen or fat mass would improve interpretation, but the measurement method and its assumptions must still be understood.

To understand why glycogen and fat are complementary reserves, compare accessibility with density. Glycogen-derived glucose phosphate can enter glycolytic metabolism rapidly, including under conditions in which mitochondrial oxidative capacity is temporarily limiting. Fatty acid oxidation is primarily mitochondrial and depends on the aerobic metabolic system for sustained energy yield. A skeletal muscle preparing for rapid contraction thus benefits from retaining its own glycogen even if ample adipose triglyceride is available. The presence of a more energy-dense reserve does not remove the need for a readily accessible local reserve.

Liver glycogen additionally buffers the interval between gastrointestinal glucose absorption and endogenous glucose production. As a meal is absorbed, the liver can add glucose residues to glycogen; when absorption declines, part of this store can be mobilized. The capacity is finite. This finite capacity helps explain why other storage forms are needed for longer periods. The body can synthesize and retain substantial triglyceride in adipose tissue, which serves a different purpose from maintaining the rapidly adjustable blood glucose pool. An examination may contrast the amount of fuel stored with the rate at which a particular tissue can deliver it; these should not be assumed to vary in parallel.

A simple carbon accounting exercise demonstrates how glycogen storage differs from the storage of free glucose. A glycogen polymer contains many glucose-derived residues joined by glycosidic bonds. Counting a large polymer as one osmotic particle rather than counting every glucose residue separately is an idealized way to understand reduced osmotic burden. The actual physical state involves branching, granules, associated proteins and water, so the polymer cannot be treated as a perfect dilute solute in every quantitative respect. Nevertheless, polymerization helps avoid the extreme osmotic consequences that would accompany storing an equivalent number of independently dissolved glucose molecules.

Triglyceride mobilization also creates two types of fuel-related product. For each triglyceride molecule fully hydrolyzed, one glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules are released. The glycerol contains three carbon atoms and can enter pathways that generate glucose; the carbon in common even-chain fatty acids enters acetyl-CoA during oxidation. This molecular distinction is the reason that the statement that fat cannot be converted to glucose requires qualification. It is true for net glucose production from the acetyl-CoA of even-chain fatty acids in humans, but not for glycerol. It is the carbon pathway rather than the nutritional label fat that determines the answer.

The dynamic nature of the adipose compartment can be described using a simplified rate balance. Let the rate of triglyceride deposition be 12 grams per hour and the net loss through mobilization be 8 grams per hour, with the latter already adjusted for local re-esterification. The modeled reserve increases by 4 grams per hour. A change that raises both deposition and loss by 5 grams per hour leaves the net rate unchanged despite greater turnover. If an experiment measures only the mobilization flux, it cannot distinguish these cases without measuring deposition or total pool change. This is another example of why a measured metabolic rate is not necessarily equivalent to a change in the amount of tissue.

Finally, the reserve pools have different physiological consequences when depleted. Reduction in liver glycogen limits the capacity for glycogen-derived glucose production but can be compensated to an extent by gluconeogenesis. Reduction in muscle glycogen may constrain sustained or intense contractile activity depending on the task and available fuels. Depletion of triglyceride reserves reduces the available long-term energy buffer. Loss of functional protein compromises the machinery that performs metabolism and other physiological tasks. The severity of a deficit is therefore not determined solely by the remaining chemical energy; it also depends on which material has been consumed and whether critical functions can be maintained.

The absorptive state: distributing the meal

The absorptive state is the period during which appreciable nutrients are entering the body from the digestive tract. Its duration and intensity depend on meal size, composition, gastrointestinal motility and nutrient transport. A mixed meal supplies glucose and other monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids and other molecules over different time courses. The endocrine and nervous systems coordinate their distribution so that incoming substrates support current demand and replenish reserves. The simplest model of a carbohydrate-containing meal emphasizes an increase in insulin action relative to glucagon action, but actual hormonal responses depend on the composition of the meal.

Pancreatic beta cells respond to nutrient availability, and gut-derived incretin signals can augment insulin release in a glucose-dependent manner. Insulin favors nutrient storage and utilization in appropriate tissues. In skeletal muscle and adipose tissue it increases glucose uptake through recruitment of GLUT4 transporters to the plasma membrane. Insulin does not have to cause GLUT4 recruitment in every glucose-consuming cell: erythrocytes and many brain cells use other transport systems. The distinction between insulin-sensitive transport and insulin regulation of whole-body metabolism is particularly important. A cell can obtain glucose without acute insulin-stimulated transport while its substrate supply is indirectly influenced by insulin action elsewhere.

The liver receives portal monosaccharides and amino acids after intestinal absorption. Hepatic glucose transport is not described by the muscle GLUT4 recruitment model. Instead, the liver adjusts glucose phosphorylation, storage and production according to nutrient and hormonal conditions. Glycogen synthesis is favored when substrate is abundant and insulin signaling predominates. Suppression of hepatic glucose output helps limit the simultaneous appearance of glucose from food and endogenous sources. Some carbohydrate-derived carbon may enter lipid synthesis when conditions favor sustained storage, but not every excess glucose molecule is immediately converted into fat; oxidation and glycogen restoration are important competing fates.

Dietary long-chain triglycerides arrive in systemic circulation in chylomicrons. Lipoprotein lipase on capillary surfaces hydrolyzes their triglyceride cargo, making fatty acids available to tissues. Adipose tissue can take up these fatty acids and re-esterify them, while oxidative tissues can use them as fuel. The remnants of chylomicrons are ultimately cleared largely by the liver. Lipoprotein lipase acts on circulating lipoprotein triglyceride and should not be confused with the enzymes that mobilize triglyceride already stored within adipocytes. The two processes operate in different locations and have different physiological effects.

Amino acid absorption after a meal increases the availability of substrates for protein synthesis. Insulin and amino acid availability can together support a net anabolic response, although protein turnover continues. Amino acids in excess of current synthetic needs may be catabolized, with the nitrogen handled through excretory pathways and carbon skeletons entering energy metabolism. Since the body has no dedicated amino acid storage depot, a sustained imbalance between supply and use has different consequences from an equivalent carbohydrate or fat surplus. The liver plays an important role in nitrogen handling, but tissue-specific amino acid metabolism also occurs outside the liver.

A protein-rich meal illustrates why a simple statement that glucagon always falls after feeding is unsafe. Amino acids can stimulate glucagon as well as insulin; the resulting hepatic response helps preserve glucose availability while tissues handle the amino acid load. The hormonal balance reflects nutrient composition and the need to avoid inappropriate fluctuations in circulating substrates. In examinations, a mixed high-carbohydrate meal, an isolated amino acid stimulus and an exercise condition are not automatically equivalent experimental situations. Identify the actual perturbation before predicting a hormonal response.

The time course of nutrient delivery is an important part of absorptive physiology. Glucose arriving from the portal vein reaches the liver before the systemic circulation, while much of the triglyceride cargo in intestinal chylomicrons enters systemic blood via lymph. Thus a sample taken from a systemic vein immediately after a meal does not directly represent the rate of all nutrients crossing the intestinal epithelium. Gastric emptying, intestinal hydrolysis, transporter activity and the path into blood influence the observed pattern. In a controlled experiment, comparing identical meals requires attention to these upstream determinants of substrate appearance.

Insulin promotes an anabolic response partly by changing the balance between substrate deposition and substrate release. Suppression of hepatic glucose production is just as relevant to postprandial glucose control as stimulation of glucose uptake by some tissues. Imagine that the intestine introduces glucose into blood at a rate of three arbitrary units per minute. Endogenous production is one unit, and uptake is four units. The circulating pool remains constant under those conditions. If endogenous production instead remained at three units while uptake stayed at four, total input would be six and the pool would rise at two units per minute. This simplified example demonstrates why controlling production can limit a meal-related glucose excursion.

A distinction should be made between an endocrine effect and the direct metabolic effect of substrate availability. When glucose concentration increases, the driving forces and activity of metabolic pathways can change; insulin also alters transport and metabolism in target tissues. In an intact animal these influences occur together, making causation difficult to assign from a simple before-and-after comparison. An experiment in which glucose and insulin are manipulated separately, while relevant concentrations are monitored, can help identify the independent contributions. The principles of endocrine signaling are developed elsewhere in the curriculum; the important point here is that a physiological state consists of interacting causes rather than one hormone acting in isolation.

A common experimental approach to insulin-dependent glucose metabolism uses a controlled insulin infusion while glucose is supplied to maintain a chosen blood concentration. At steady state, and under assumptions about glucose production, the amount of glucose needed to maintain that concentration provides information about disposal. If endogenous glucose production is not fully suppressed, glucose infusion rate alone is not identical to total tissue glucose uptake; a tracer can help distinguish the contributions. This conceptual approach illustrates how researchers separate a hormone's effect from a change in substrate concentration. It is not necessary to master detailed clinical protocols to interpret the basic reasoning.

Protein handling after meals is likewise a balance of fluxes. A rise in circulating amino acids supplies substrates and can influence the regulation of protein synthesis, while insulin can suppress aspects of protein breakdown. A tissue may show a greater net protein gain without a proportionate increase in synthesis if degradation falls. Conversely, synthesis can rise while net gain remains small if degradation also rises. In a labeled amino acid experiment, measuring incorporation into protein is informative about synthesis but does not by itself provide the degradation rate. A separate measure of net exchange or turnover is needed.

The absorptive state is also not equivalent to positive energy balance over every possible interval. A small meal consumed by an animal with high ongoing expenditure may produce the hormonal and substrate patterns of absorption without offsetting the total energy consumed over the day. Likewise, a large meal can produce a transient storage response in some tissues while exercise causes fuel mobilization in others. Nutritional state describes the relation between recent nutrient delivery and the prevailing regulation of fuel use. Whole-body energy balance describes a quantitative net outcome over a specified interval. Examinations frequently test the difference between these two statements.

Postabsorptive metabolism and the maintenance of glucose

As intestinal nutrient appearance declines, the body transitions gradually toward a postabsorptive state. There is no universal instant at which absorption ends and fasting begins. A slowly digested mixed meal may continue to supply nutrients for longer than a smaller meal. Falling insulin action relative to counterregulatory signals reduces the tendency to store incoming substrates and permits mobilization of endogenous reserves. During an ordinary overnight interval, hepatic glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis both contribute to blood glucose. Their relative contributions change with the prior diet, activity, glycogen content and duration without food.

Glycogenolysis means release of glucose residues from glycogen, whereas gluconeogenesis means synthesis of glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. They are distinct mechanisms although both can contribute to hepatic glucose output. Gluconeogenic substrates include lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids. Lactate is produced continuously by cells relying on glycolytic metabolism, including mature erythrocytes. Returning lactate to glucose in the liver recycles carbon, but it requires energy. Thus the Cori cycle is not a source of free energy for the body; it shifts part of the energetic burden from lactate-producing tissues to organs able to oxidize other fuels.

Alanine released from skeletal muscle can carry both carbon and amino nitrogen to the liver. Its carbon skeleton can contribute to glucose production, while its nitrogen must be handled safely. Glycerol from adipose triglyceride hydrolysis supplies another gluconeogenic precursor. In contrast, the acetyl-CoA formed by degradation of even-chain fatty acids does not provide net glucose carbon in mammals. The pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is effectively irreversible under ordinary physiological conditions and the citric acid cycle does not yield a net gain of oxaloacetate from acetyl-CoA. Odd-chain fatty acid metabolism can make a limited gluconeogenic contribution through propionyl-CoA, so an absolute claim that no component of any lipid can contribute to glucose is also wrong.

As insulin action falls, intracellular triglyceride mobilization in adipose tissue tends to increase. Fatty acids circulate associated with albumin, whereas glycerol is water soluble. The availability of fatty acids allows tissues such as resting skeletal muscle, heart and liver to derive much of their energy from lipid oxidation. Liver oxidation of fatty acids also supplies energy needed for gluconeogenesis. This coordination spares circulating glucose for cells that depend more strongly on it. During activity, the pattern can differ because muscle contraction changes glucose uptake and fuel selection independently of the resting postabsorptive model.

Blood glucose homeostasis is achieved by changing appearance and disappearance rates. For a simplified plasma pool, if endogenous appearance is two units per minute and tissue uptake is two units per minute, its amount remains constant despite continuous turnover. If production decreases to one and a half units while use remains two, the pool falls at half a unit per minute until other responses intervene. The actual concentration also depends on distribution volume. This elementary flux reasoning explains why a normal measured glucose concentration cannot establish that hepatic output is normal; a matched change in tissue consumption could conceal an altered output.

The postabsorptive state must be distinguished from prolonged starvation. Short-term reliance on glycogen and gluconeogenesis does not imply that glycogen has already been completely exhausted or that ketone bodies have become the predominant fuel of the brain. The transition is gradual, and gluconeogenesis is already significant before hepatic glycogen is depleted. The next stage is best understood as an adjustment in the relative use of fuel sources rather than as a sudden succession of three non-overlapping pathways.

The early transition from a meal to reliance on endogenous fuel is governed by the declining rate of nutrient delivery rather than by the disappearance of glucose itself. Plasma glucose cannot simply be allowed to vanish when the digestive tract stops supplying it. The blood pool is small relative to continuous tissue use and must be replenished. Liver glycogen offers a readily mobilizable source, while gluconeogenesis permits glucose production from suitable precursors. Both can operate together because their control and substrate requirements differ. The term postabsorptive does not imply that all tissues switch simultaneously to fat oxidation.

Glycogen mobilization has a tissue-specific consequence because of the different fates of glucose phosphate. In liver, conversion to free glucose supports export; in skeletal muscle, the predominant fate is local metabolism. During glycolytic activity, muscle can export lactate, which carries three-carbon units to other tissues. The liver can synthesize glucose from two lactate molecules and return it to circulation. The energetic cost of this conversion must be paid by hepatic metabolism. If the liver oxidizes fatty acids to support the process, part of the organism's fuel expenditure is shifted from muscle to liver. This example illustrates the difference between redistribution of carbon and net generation of energy.

The Cori cycle can be represented by a simplified ATP accounting model. Glycolysis of one free glucose molecule to two lactate molecules yields a net two ATP in the glycolytic tissue. Gluconeogenesis from two lactate molecules to one glucose requires four ATP and two GTP, or six high-energy phosphate equivalents. The integrated cycle therefore has a net cost of four high-energy phosphate equivalents under this bookkeeping convention. Glycolysis beginning from glycogen residues has different initial ATP accounting, so the simple two-ATP assumption must be stated. The cycle is physiologically valuable because it permits lactate recycling and redistribution of the energetic burden, not because it provides a perpetual energy source.

A second carbon pathway uses alanine. Amino groups can be transferred to pyruvate, producing alanine that travels to the liver. There, the carbon skeleton can be converted back to pyruvate while nitrogen is directed toward disposal. This allows amino nitrogen to be transported in a form suitable for circulation while contributing carbon to glucose synthesis. In an extended nutritional deficit, however, the source of some amino acid carbon may be tissue protein. Reliance on this pathway can therefore impose a cost on tissue preservation. The physiological advantage of other gluconeogenic precursors and alternative oxidative fuels becomes apparent when considering that cost.

Glycerol differs from lactate and alanine because it originates from the backbone of stored triglyceride. During lipolysis, glycerol release accompanies fatty acid release, but the two products need not follow the same metabolic destination. Glycerol contributes to glucose carbon in organs with the relevant metabolic capacity, while fatty acid oxidation supplies energy and acetyl-CoA. This is a cooperative arrangement in which a single storage molecule supplies both a glucose precursor and oxidative fuel. It also helps explain why increasing mobilization of fat can support glucose homeostasis without making the general mammalian pathway from even-chain fatty acid acetyl-CoA to net glucose possible.

A useful experiment would compare the rates of glucose appearance under two conditions with similar plasma glucose concentration but different dietary histories. One subject or animal may enter the postabsorptive state with a large hepatic glycogen reserve, while another begins with less after previous activity. The first may have a larger glycogen-derived contribution to output, whereas the second relies relatively more on gluconeogenesis. Concentration measurements alone may not reveal the difference. A method capable of distinguishing glucose carbon derived from stored glycogen and from newly synthesized glucose would be more informative. The conclusion concerns relative fluxes and cannot be obtained from a universal number of hours since the last meal.

Prolonged fasting, ketone bodies and glucose conservation

When food deprivation continues, the contribution of liver glycogen generally diminishes and glucose production relies increasingly on gluconeogenesis. The liver is a major site, and the kidney can make an appreciable contribution, especially during prolonged fasting. Fatty acid mobilization and oxidation become important sources of energy for many tissues. At the same time, the liver converts a portion of fatty acid-derived acetyl-CoA into water-soluble ketone bodies, principally acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate, with acetone produced from acetoacetate. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is conventionally grouped with ketone bodies even though chemically it is a hydroxy acid rather than a ketone.

The formation of ketone bodies allows carbon derived from fatty acids to circulate in a form suitable for oxidation by several extrahepatic tissues. During sustained fasting, the brain increases its utilization of ketone bodies. The liver produces ketone bodies for export but does not significantly oxidize them because it lacks the mitochondrial succinyl-CoA:acetoacetate CoA transferase needed for the usual utilization pathway. Mature erythrocytes lack mitochondria entirely and therefore cannot oxidize either fatty acids or ketone bodies. Their ATP supply remains dependent on glycolysis. Consequently, glucose production does not cease when ketone utilization rises.

The use of ketones by the brain reduces, but does not eliminate, its glucose requirement. Less glucose demand can reduce the need to convert amino acid carbon into glucose, helping conserve functional protein relative to what would occur if the brain remained entirely dependent on glucose. This is an adaptation to fuel scarcity, not an elimination of the costs of starvation. Protein turnover and nitrogen losses continue, and prolonged deprivation can ultimately compromise essential functions. The extent and timing of these responses vary with previous nutrition, energy reserves, species and activity.

Owen and colleagues reported a landmark study in 1967 in which cerebral arterial and venous measurements in three individuals undergoing prolonged medically supervised fasting showed substantial cerebral uptake of ketone bodies. The study challenged the assumption that adult human brain metabolism invariably depends predominantly on glucose. Net uptake was inferred from differences in substrate concentration across the cerebral circulation, considered alongside blood flow. The inference is about net exchange across an organ; it is not a direct observation of the metabolism of every individual neuron. The small and selected study population also limits universal quantitative extrapolation. Its conceptual contribution remains important: tissue fuel preference can change with physiological state.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose arterial concentration of a substrate is 3.0 mmol per litre and venous concentration is 2.6 mmol per litre, with organ blood flow of 0.5 litres per minute. The net uptake is the flow multiplied by the concentration difference, or 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 mmol per minute. If venous concentration exceeds arterial concentration, the calculated uptake is negative, indicating net release under the stated sign convention. The calculation requires consistent sampling, appropriate blood or plasma flow and units. A positive value does not by itself reveal whether all extracted substrate was immediately oxidized or whether some entered other reactions.

Ketosis describes increased ketone body availability. It should not be equated automatically with ketoacidosis. In ordinary fasting, feedback, remaining insulin action, peripheral utilization and excretion constrain the extent of ketone accumulation, whereas severe insulin deficiency can generate a different and dangerous metabolic state. The distinction is a matter of regulatory context and acid-base balance rather than simply the presence or absence of ketone bodies. Detailed disease mechanisms belong to the subsequent metabolic homeostasis chapter.

Fasting adaptation illustrates a fundamental theme of physiology: maintaining a concentration in blood can require major changes in the composition of fuels used by tissues. A reduction in glucose consumption by one organ can be just as important for glucose conservation as increased glucose production by another. The most reliable examination reasoning tracks both sides of a balance and respects the metabolic capabilities of each tissue.

Ketone body formation resolves a problem of distributing fatty acid-derived carbon to tissues with different fuel preferences. Long-chain fatty acids circulate primarily bound to albumin and are used extensively by many oxidative tissues, but they are not the principal circulating alternative fuel of the brain during prolonged fasting. Ketone bodies are more suitable for transport in aqueous blood and can be taken up through monocarboxylate transport mechanisms. The ability to convert acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate into acetyl-CoA within extrahepatic mitochondria makes their oxidation possible. This pathway supplies ATP only if the recipient tissue has the necessary enzymes, mitochondria and oxidative capacity.

In hepatic mitochondria, fatty acid oxidation increases the availability of acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents. The regulation of ketogenesis is related to hormonal conditions, substrate supply and the fate of acetyl-CoA in the citric acid cycle. During fasting, glucose production and other metabolic demands influence the balance of intermediates available for oxidation. It is tempting to explain ketogenesis by saying that oxaloacetate simply disappears from the liver, but that wording is misleading. The liver continues to operate interconnected pathways, and concentrations and fluxes of intermediates are dynamically regulated. Ketone production reflects the balance of substrate delivery and metabolic capacity rather than an absolute absence of one intermediate.

The ratio of beta-hydroxybutyrate to acetoacetate is influenced by the mitochondrial redox state because interconversion of these compounds is linked to NADH and NAD. A shift in their ratio can therefore carry information about the chemical environment in which they are formed, but a blood ratio is not a perfect direct measurement of liver mitochondrial redox conditions. Transport, peripheral utilization and other processes can affect circulating concentrations. For general NET-level reasoning, it is sufficient to recognize that ketone bodies are chemically related yet not identical, and that their relative concentrations depend on metabolic conditions.

The gradual change in brain fuel selection has consequences for nitrogen economy. Before substantial ketone utilization, glucose-dependent organs contribute to a relatively high glucose demand, part of which may be met through gluconeogenesis using amino acid carbon. As ketones supply more of the brain's oxidative energy, the amount of glucose needed by the brain decreases. This reduces a major driver of amino acid use for glucose synthesis. The reduction in nitrogen loss is not due to the brain synthesizing protein directly from ketones, nor does it imply the complete cessation of protein degradation. It is an indirect consequence of altered substrate demand.

A hypothetical comparison makes the logic explicit. Suppose, purely for illustration, that a tissue initially consumes 100 units of energy exclusively from glucose, and later receives 60 units from ketone oxidation while its total expenditure remains 100 units. The glucose-derived contribution falls to 40 units. This does not mean whole-body glucose production falls by exactly 60 units: other tissues continue consuming glucose, and the conversion from energy units to substrate mass depends on energy yield. Nevertheless, the direction of the change in the modeled tissue is clear. A student can use this example to understand glucose conservation without memorizing a fixed percentage of brain energy supplied by ketones.

The liver's inability to oxidize ketones through the usual transferase-dependent pathway is an example of specialization that favors export. An organ need not consume every substrate it produces. Likewise, muscle may take up circulating ketones at one stage and rely more on fatty acids at another, depending on the prevailing physiological context. A measured change in net ketone uptake may therefore reflect adaptation in the recipient organ as well as production by the liver. Identifying which side of the exchange has changed requires measurements of more than one variable.

The return to feeding reverses many of these tendencies but is not an instantaneous restoration of every pool. Dietary substrates begin appearing, insulin action increases and mobilization of fat and hepatic ketone formation tend to decline. Glycogen can be replenished while tissues alter their oxidation rates. Following severe prolonged malnutrition, intracellular electrolyte shifts during renewed anabolic metabolism illustrate the importance of nutrient stores and regulatory context; detailed clinical management is outside this chapter. For physiological reasoning, the important distinction is that substrate delivery may recover more rapidly than tissue reserves or whole-body composition.

Nutritional deficiency, evidence and experimental reasoning

Energy deficiency and nutrient-specific deficiency can occur together or separately. Reduced energy intake tends to increase mobilization of reserves when expenditure is not reduced correspondingly. Inadequate protein or an indispensable amino acid limits the capacity for synthesis and tissue maintenance even if other fuels are sufficient. A lack of iron can impair haem-dependent processes, while inadequate iodine supply can alter thyroid hormone synthesis and hence metabolic regulation. The resulting physiological changes are not explained merely by a lack of food calories. Each deficiency changes a specific material or functional constraint.

The older descriptions of marasmus and kwashiorkor illustrate distinct patterns within severe undernutrition (NCBI Bookshelf review). Marasmus is characterized by marked wasting, whereas kwashiorkor includes nutritional oedema and other systemic abnormalities. These are not adequately explained by assigning one exclusively to calorie deficiency and the other exclusively to protein deficiency; infection, inflammation, dietary composition and metabolic responses can interact. Oedema also illustrates why tissue mass and measured body mass may diverge. Retained extracellular fluid can partially mask loss of functional tissues, so an apparently less dramatic fall on the scale is not evidence of nutritional sufficiency.

Controlled feeding studies can test whether changing one nutrient changes a physiological outcome. A pair-fed control receives a matched quantity of food, helping distinguish a specific nutrient effect from reduced intake caused by altered palatability. Isocaloric diets aim to provide comparable energy, while isonitrogenous diets aim to match nitrogen content. Neither design guarantees equivalence of absorbed energy or amino acid profiles; digestion and measurement errors must be considered. Inferences are stronger when the experimental groups are otherwise comparable, the outcome is defined in advance and replacing the missing nutrient reverses the specific defect.

Tracer experiments address a different question: not simply how much substrate is present, but how fast it appears, disappears or changes chemical form. A labeled glucose molecule can be tracked as it enters glycogen, undergoes oxidation or appears in another metabolite. In a simplified steady-state dilution experiment, the infusion of a labeled substrate and its measured enrichment can be related to the total appearance rate, subject to mixing and recycling assumptions. Plasma concentration alone cannot distinguish high production accompanied by high disposal from low production accompanied by low disposal. Tracer data and concentration data therefore complement each other.

Consider three hypothetical observations from the same healthy resting subject: following a carbohydrate-containing meal, insulin is relatively high and circulating fatty acid availability is suppressed; after an overnight interval without food, insulin is lower and endogenous glucose output supports plasma glucose; during more prolonged deprivation, ketone availability rises and glucose demand from the brain is partially reduced. These qualitative patterns represent expected tendencies, not a fixed set of concentrations or universal time thresholds. They illustrate how physiological reasoning connects hormone signals, substrate fluxes and the demands of different tissues.

An interpretation should also consider alternatives. If plasma ketone concentration rises, the increase could reflect greater production, reduced utilization or altered clearance; concentration alone cannot quantify the responsible flux. If urine nitrogen decreases, this may reflect reduced net protein loss, altered nitrogen handling or incomplete collection. If body mass falls abruptly, changing hydration and gut contents may explain much of the change. Selecting an additional measurement that distinguishes competing explanations is often the key to Part C problems.

The transition to the next chapter follows naturally. Having established that energy balance is a relation between intake, expenditure and storage, the next question is how expenditure is measured in an animal. Direct calorimetry quantifies heat output, whereas indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure from respiratory gas exchange under suitable conditions. BMR, resting metabolic rate and total expenditure require distinct definitions. The physiological fuel transitions described here provide the conceptual basis for interpreting changes in respiratory quotient and respiratory exchange ratio.

The experimental interpretation of a deficiency phenotype depends on the comparator. A nutrient-deficient diet may also differ in palatability, texture or digestibility. If animals offered that diet consume less food, a reduced growth rate could reflect reduced total energy rather than a specific shortage. A pair-fed control receiving a nutritionally complete diet in the same amount helps address the difference in intake. If the deficient group still grows more slowly, a nutrient-specific mechanism becomes more plausible, provided the available energy and other relevant conditions are comparable. A rescue group that receives the missing nutrient can strengthen the inference further.

A comparison of two hypothetical diets illustrates the logic. Diet A provides adequate indispensable amino acids and 1000 units of metabolizable energy over an interval. Diet B has the same estimated energy but lacks sufficient amounts of one indispensable amino acid. If diet B produces lower nitrogen retention and a specific replacement reverses the deficit without altering energy, the result supports the limiting amino acid explanation. It does not establish that the replaced amino acid is the only regulator of growth in all circumstances. The inference is specific to the conditions of the experiment.

Tracer dilution provides a quantitative way to distinguish concentrations from turnover. In a simplified steady-state system, suppose labeled glucose is supplied at a small known rate and is thoroughly mixed into a circulating pool. If the tracer has a constant known enrichment and its infusion does not materially disturb the system, the measured enrichment in the pool reflects dilution by unlabeled glucose appearance. Greater endogenous appearance at the same tracer infusion produces lower enrichment. Real experiments require corrections for the precise tracer convention, nonsteady conditions and recycling, so a numerical formula cannot be applied indiscriminately. The conceptual prediction remains useful: dilution can reveal altered turnover even when total glucose concentration is unchanged.

Organ exchange measurements also require attention to blood flow. If two organs have equal arterial-minus-venous differences for a substrate but different perfusion rates, their net uptake rates differ. Suppose organ X receives 0.8 litres of blood per minute and organ Y receives 0.2 litres per minute, with a concentration difference of 0.5 mmol per litre across both. Net uptake is 0.4 mmol per minute for X and 0.1 for Y, assuming appropriate measurements. The difference in total uptake is fourfold despite identical concentration differences. Conversely, uptake per unit tissue mass could differ depending on organ size, which is not given. The denominator must match the biological question.

A physiological experiment that measures plasma glucose, fatty acids and ketones before and after a meal provides a descriptive pattern. To identify causal relationships, an additional intervention may be needed. One could selectively alter a hormonal signal under controlled substrate conditions, compare organs with different enzyme capabilities, or trace the fate of labeled substrate. Controls should distinguish changes in absorption and delivery from changes in tissue metabolism. A single graph showing that insulin concentration and glucose disappearance rise together cannot by itself demonstrate that every observed glucose uptake event was caused directly by insulin.

Finally, a meaningful interpretation integrates multiple observations without assuming that one finding establishes the entire metabolic state. Low insulin together with increased fatty acid availability and ketone production is consistent with an extended postabsorptive or fasting condition, but exercise, illness or other physiological perturbations may influence similar variables. A question that supplies timing, nutritional history and tissue-specific measurements permits a stronger conclusion than one that supplies a single hormone value. The general skill is to construct a causal explanation that accounts for each observation and identify what remains uncertain. This skill will also be needed when interpreting the oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production measurements in the following node.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Essential means calorie-bearing

Correction: Essentiality is inability to meet demand by endogenous synthesis; iron and water are essential without fuel energy.

Why it is tempting: Dietary requirement and fuel value often appear in the same nutrient list.

Exam reasoning: Classify independently by requirement and energy contribution.

2. Nonessential means unnecessary

Correction: A nonessential amino acid can be biologically indispensable but normally synthesized in sufficient quantity.

Why it is tempting: Everyday use of essential differs from nutrition terminology.

Exam reasoning: Ask whether synthesis meets demand, not whether the molecule has a function.

3. All carbohydrates are digested equally

Correction: Digestibility depends on linkage, enzymes and microbial fermentation.

Why it is tempting: Starch and cellulose contain glucose residues.

Exam reasoning: Trace actual hydrolysis and absorption before assigning energy.

4. All calories are gross combustion values

Correction: General Atwater factors approximate metabolizable energy.

Why it is tempting: Both measures use kcal.

Exam reasoning: Identify whether faecal and urinary losses have already been deducted.

5. Fat supplies exactly twice carbohydrate energy

Correction: General factors are about 9 versus 4 kcal/g, a ratio of 2.25.

Why it is tempting: Rounded comparisons obscure the ratio.

Exam reasoning: Use the stated factors and units.

6. BMR equals daily energy expenditure

Correction: Daily expenditure includes activity and thermic responses in addition to resting maintenance.

Why it is tempting: BMR is commonly expressed per day.

Exam reasoning: Check the measurement conditions and components.

7. A fed state ensures daily positive balance

Correction: Absorptive physiology reflects recent nutrient appearance, not an entire day's integrated balance.

Why it is tempting: Insulin promotes storage locally.

Exam reasoning: Specify time interval and whole-body input/output.

8. Neutral balance means metabolism stops

Correction: Matched input and output permit high turnover.

Why it is tempting: Net zero is confused with zero gross flux.

Exam reasoning: Write both appearance and disappearance rates.

9. All glycogen supports blood glucose directly

Correction: Muscle glycogen primarily supplies local metabolism, unlike hepatic glycogen.

Why it is tempting: Both stores contain glucose residues.

Exam reasoning: Identify glucose-6-phosphatase availability.

10. Adipose lipolysis equals whole-body fat loss

Correction: Re-esterification and simultaneous deposition can alter the net reserve.

Why it is tempting: A release flux is confused with pool change.

Exam reasoning: Subtract net removal from deposition.

11. No lipid-derived carbon can become glucose

Correction: Glycerol and some odd-chain fatty acid carbon can contribute.

Why it is tempting: The acetyl-CoA limitation is generalized to all lipid components.

Exam reasoning: Track specific carbon skeletons.

12. Gluconeogenesis begins only when glycogen is exhausted

Correction: Both pathways can contribute early in fasting.

Why it is tempting: Textbook timelines are interpreted as switches.

Exam reasoning: Think changing proportions, not mutually exclusive phases.

13. The fasting brain never uses ketones

Correction: Prolonged fasting increases cerebral ketone utilization.

Why it is tempting: Normal fed-state fuel preference is overgeneralized.

Exam reasoning: Distinguish short from prolonged deprivation.

14. Ketones replace all glucose needs

Correction: Erythrocytes require glycolysis and brain retains some glucose demand.

Why it is tempting: A major alternative fuel is mistaken for total replacement.

Exam reasoning: Check tissue mitochondria and metabolic capabilities.

15. Liver oxidizes all ketones it produces

Correction: Liver exports ketones and lacks usual SCOT-mediated utilization.

Why it is tempting: Production is confused with consumption.

Exam reasoning: Differentiate enzyme capabilities of producer and recipient.

16. Ketosis is necessarily ketoacidosis

Correction: Acid-base consequences depend on production, utilization, clearance and regulation.

Why it is tempting: The terms share a root.

Exam reasoning: Interpret regulatory context rather than ketone presence alone.

17. Body mass directly measures stored energy

Correction: Water, gut contents, glycogen and tissue composition differ in energy density.

Why it is tempting: Scale readings are easily obtained.

Exam reasoning: Use a defined compartment model or composition data.

18. Protein synthesis alone proves net protein gain

Correction: Net gain depends on synthesis minus degradation.

Why it is tempting: Tracer incorporation measures one process.

Exam reasoning: Compare both rates or nitrogen retention.

19. Normal blood glucose proves normal glucose output

Correction: Matched changes in uptake can conceal changes in output.

Why it is tempting: Concentration is mistaken for flux.

Exam reasoning: Use tracer or appropriate balance data.

20. An isocaloric diet is necessarily nutritionally equivalent

Correction: Essential nutrient profile and bioavailability may differ.

Why it is tempting: Energy is treated as the sole requirement.

Exam reasoning: Use specific nutrient comparisons and rescue experiments.

Part B — Conceptual understanding

1. Which nutrient is essential in adult human nutrition but provides no metabolizable energy?

  1. Iron
  2. Glucose
  3. Linoleic acid
  4. Triglyceride
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Iron

Iron is a required element for haem and other proteins and cannot be oxidized as an organic fuel. Linoleate is essential but energy-yielding; glucose and triglyceride can also provide energy.

2. The general Atwater energy factor for available carbohydrate is approximately

  1. 9 kcal/g
  2. 4 kcal/g
  3. 4 kJ/g
  4. 0 kcal/g
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 4 kcal/g

The general factor is about 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g). Nine applies to fat; four kJ/g confuses the units.

3. Which is an indispensable amino acid in humans?

  1. Alanine
  2. Glutamate
  3. Lysine
  4. Aspartate
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Lysine

Lysine must be obtained in adequate amounts from the diet; the other listed amino acids can normally be synthesized.

4. Which pair comprises the two conventional essential fatty acids in human nutrition?

  1. Palmitate and stearate
  2. Oleate and palmitate
  3. Acetate and butyrate
  4. Linoleate and alpha-linolenate
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Linoleate and alpha-linolenate

Linoleate and alpha-linolenate supply the omega-6 and omega-3 families. Humans cannot introduce the required distal double bonds to create them de novo.

5. Why can liver glycogen support blood glucose directly whereas skeletal muscle glycogen generally cannot?

  1. Muscle lacks substantial glucose-6-phosphatase activity
  2. Liver lacks glycolysis
  3. Muscle lacks glycogen phosphorylase
  4. Liver cannot store triglyceride
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Muscle lacks substantial glucose-6-phosphatase activity

Hepatic glucose-6-phosphatase enables free glucose release. Muscle can mobilize glycogen but primarily uses its glucose phosphate locally.

6. The dominant long-term concentrated energy reserve is

  1. Plasma glucose
  2. Adipose triglyceride
  3. ATP
  4. Extracellular calcium
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Adipose triglyceride

Triglyceride is energy dense and hydrophobic. Plasma glucose and ATP are rapidly turning-over pools, not major long-term energy reserves.

7. In a carbohydrate-containing mixed meal, insulin generally favors

  1. Adipose intracellular lipolysis
  2. Hepatic glucose output
  3. Glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue
  4. Hepatic ketone export
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue

Insulin stimulates GLUT4 recruitment in muscle and adipose tissue and usually suppresses hepatic glucose output, lipolysis and ketogenesis.

8. Which cell cannot oxidize ketone bodies because it lacks mitochondria?

  1. Cardiac myocyte
  2. Hepatocyte
  3. Skeletal myocyte
  4. Mature mammalian erythrocyte
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Mature mammalian erythrocyte

Mature erythrocytes use glycolysis for ATP. Hepatocytes have mitochondria but lack the usual SCOT-dependent ketone utilization pathway; heart and muscle can oxidize ketones.

9. Which compound released by adipose lipolysis can supply net glucose carbon in humans?

  1. Glycerol
  2. Even-chain fatty acid acetyl-CoA
  3. Cholesterol ring
  4. Acetoacetate
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Glycerol

Glycerol can enter gluconeogenesis through triose phosphate. Acetyl-CoA from ordinary even-chain fatty acids and ketone bodies cannot yield net glucose carbon.

10. Under the simplified faecal-loss convention used in this chapter, apparent digestible energy is defined as

  1. Gross energy minus urinary energy
  2. Gross energy minus faecal energy
  3. Metabolizable energy minus heat
  4. Gross energy minus total expenditure
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Gross energy minus faecal energy

Faecal energy is subtracted to estimate apparent digestible energy. Urinary and combustible gas losses are additional deductions for metabolizable energy.

11. Which change characterizes prolonged fasting relative to an ordinary meal?

  1. Persistent suppression of fat mobilization
  2. Complete loss of erythrocyte glucose requirement
  3. Increased hepatic ketogenesis
  4. Increased intestinal nutrient appearance
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Increased hepatic ketogenesis

Falling insulin action and fatty acid delivery favor hepatic ketone formation. Erythrocytes still need glucose; intestinal delivery falls.

12. Which enzyme acts on triglyceride in circulating chylomicrons?

  1. Glycogen phosphorylase
  2. Glucose-6-phosphatase
  3. Pepsin
  4. Lipoprotein lipase
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Lipoprotein lipase

Lipoprotein lipase hydrolyzes triglyceride cargo at capillary surfaces. It is distinct from intracellular adipose triglyceride mobilization enzymes.

13. Neutral energy balance implies

  1. Approximately unchanged total stored chemical energy over the stated interval
  2. No ATP production
  3. No protein turnover
  4. Constant body water in all compartments
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Approximately unchanged total stored chemical energy over the stated interval

Equal integrated input and output imply no net chemical energy storage change, not an absence of metabolic turnover or hydration shifts.

14. Which vitamin is fat-soluble?

  1. Thiamine
  2. Vitamin K
  3. Riboflavin
  4. Vitamin C
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Vitamin K

Vitamins A, D, E and K are fat-soluble. B vitamins and vitamin C are water-soluble.

15. Nitrogen balance is most directly calculated from

  1. Energy intake minus BMR
  2. Fat intake minus faecal mass
  3. Nitrogen intake minus nitrogen loss
  4. Plasma glucose minus lactate
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Nitrogen intake minus nitrogen loss

Net nitrogen retention reflects input minus measured nitrogen losses, not energy balance or a single circulating concentration.

16. A key hepatic limitation on ketone oxidation is lack of

  1. Mitochondria
  2. Fatty acid oxidation
  3. Acetyl-CoA formation
  4. SCOT activity for usual ketone utilization
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. SCOT activity for usual ketone utilization

The liver has mitochondria and produces ketones, but lacks the usual succinyl-CoA:acetoacetate CoA transferase activity needed to use them.

17. Which factor is most important when defining an absorptive state?

  1. Appreciable intestinal nutrient appearance
  2. Exact clock time after a meal
  3. A mandatory positive daily energy balance
  4. Complete suppression of tissue catabolism
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Appreciable intestinal nutrient appearance

Absorptive state describes nutrient entry and associated regulation. Timing varies with digestion; whole-day energy balance and ongoing catabolism are separate.

18. Which of the following is a gluconeogenic precursor?

  1. Acetoacetate
  2. Lactate
  3. Palmitate-derived acetyl-CoA
  4. Molecular oxygen
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Lactate

Lactate yields pyruvate and contributes glucose carbon. The other choices cannot supply net glucose carbon through the ordinary mammalian pathways.

19. A normal blood concentration at steady state indicates

  1. No substrate turnover
  2. Every organ uses equal substrate amounts
  3. Appearance equals disappearance if pool volume is constant
  4. No endogenous production
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Appearance equals disappearance if pool volume is constant

A steady amount requires matched input and output, not zero flux. Different organs can have distinct net exchanges.

20. Which is correct about vitamin B12?

  1. It is a fat-soluble fuel
  2. It cannot be stored in the body
  3. It supplies 9 kcal/g in standard food accounting
  4. It is water-soluble but can be stored substantially
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. It is water-soluble but can be stored substantially

B12 is a water-soluble vitamin with appreciable body stores. It is a cofactor precursor, not a meaningful energy source.

Part C — Analytical reasoning

21. A meal supplies 65 g available carbohydrate, 30 g protein and 15 g fat. Its estimated metabolizable energy is

  1. 515 kcal
  2. 405 kcal
  3. 595 kcal
  4. 460 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. 515 kcal

65×4 + 30×4 + 15×9 = 260 + 120 + 135 = 515 kcal. Values using 9 for carbohydrate or summing mass rather than energy are incorrect.

22. A diet provides gross energy 2000 kcal, faecal energy 160, urinary energy 60 and combustible gases 20. Find metabolizable energy.

  1. 1840 kcal
  2. 1760 kcal
  3. 1780 kcal
  4. 2000 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 1760 kcal

Subtract all three energy losses: 2000−160−60−20=1760 kcal. 1840 is apparent digestible energy; 1780 omits gases.

23. An animal consumes 80 g protein (16% nitrogen) and loses 11.8 g nitrogen in all routes. Nitrogen balance is

  1. −1 g
  2. +12.8 g
  3. +1 g
  4. −11.8 g
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. +1 g

Nitrogen intake is 80×0.16=12.8 g. Net is 12.8−11.8=+1 g. Intake alone is not balance.

24. Four daily energy balances are +200, −100, +50 and −200 kcal. Which conclusion follows?

  1. +50 kcal stored
  2. −350 kcal stored
  3. Exactly 50 g fat lost
  4. −50 kcal stored
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. −50 kcal stored

The sum is −50 kcal. Tissue mass cannot be inferred without knowing the storage composition and energy density.

25. Two animals have equal arterial glucose concentration and equal distribution volume. Animal X has appearance and disappearance rates of 4 units/min; Y has rates of 2 units/min. Which is correct?

  1. Both pools can be steady despite different turnover
  2. X has twice the glucose concentration
  3. Y has no glucose turnover
  4. X must have positive energy balance
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Both pools can be steady despite different turnover

Both have zero net pool change because each appearance rate matches its disappearance rate. Concentration does not uniquely identify turnover or energy balance.

26. A skeletal muscle preparation mobilizes glycogen but cannot export substantial free glucose. Which observation is consistent?

  1. No lactate release is possible
  2. Increased local glycolysis and possible lactate export
  3. Immediate hepatic glycogen depletion in the preparation
  4. Glycogen mobilization must be absent
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Increased local glycolysis and possible lactate export

Muscle can use glycogen-derived glucose phosphate in glycolysis and release lactate. Lack of free glucose export does not imply lack of glycogen utilization.

27. An organ has arterial ketones 2.8 mmol/L, venous ketones 2.2 mmol/L and blood flow 0.4 L/min. Net uptake is

  1. 0.60 mmol/min
  2. 1.12 mmol/min
  3. 0.24 mmol/min
  4. −0.24 mmol/min
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 0.24 mmol/min

Flow times the arterial-minus-venous difference is 0.4×0.6=0.24 mmol/min. A positive value denotes net uptake, not proof of complete oxidation.

28. A diet lacking a single essential amino acid yields slower growth. Deficient animals also eat less. Which design best separates intake from the nutrient-specific effect?

  1. Compare only with unrestricted controls
  2. Measure mass once
  3. Increase all nutrient concentrations in the deficient group
  4. Use a complete pair-fed control and a nutrient-rescue group
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Use a complete pair-fed control and a nutrient-rescue group

Matching food intake and testing selective rescue help distinguish energy restriction and the missing amino acid. Unrestricted controls alone confound intake.

29. A glucose tracer infusion is identical in two steady states; enrichment is lower in state B. Under the simplified dilution assumptions, what is predicted?

  1. Higher unlabeled glucose appearance in B
  2. Lower unlabeled glucose appearance in B
  3. No glucose turnover in B
  4. Lower total glucose concentration is certain
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Higher unlabeled glucose appearance in B

More unlabeled glucose dilutes the same tracer infusion. Concentration and appearance rate are separate measurements.

30. A model meal adds 3 units/min of glucose to blood, hepatic output is 2 and uptake is 4. What is the net change in the glucose pool?

  1. −1 unit/min
  2. +1 unit/min
  3. +5 units/min
  4. 0 units/min
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. +1 unit/min

Total appearance is 5; disappearance is 4, giving +1 unit/min. A rate is not itself a concentration.

31. Insulin stimulates muscle glucose uptake but erythrocyte uptake persists without GLUT4 recruitment. Which inference is valid?

  1. Insulin has no whole-body metabolic effect
  2. All glucose uptake requires GLUT4
  3. Tissue-specific transport mechanisms differ
  4. Erythrocytes oxidize ketones instead
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tissue-specific transport mechanisms differ

Muscle GLUT4 is insulin responsive; erythrocytes use other transporters and depend on glycolysis. This does not negate systemic insulin regulation.

32. In the simplified Cori cycle starting with free glucose, peripheral glycolysis gains 2 ATP and hepatic gluconeogenesis costs 6 equivalents. Net whole-body accounting is

  1. +8 ATP equivalents
  2. +4 ATP equivalents
  3. 0
  4. −4 ATP equivalents
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. −4 ATP equivalents

Two gained minus six spent equals −4 high-energy phosphate equivalents. The cycle transfers carbon and energetic burden, not free energy.

33. An animal loses 0.5 kg over a day while body water falls markedly. Which inference is justified?

  1. The scale cannot establish the tissue energy loss
  2. Exactly 4500 kcal of fat were lost
  3. Exactly 0.5 kg of protein was lost
  4. Energy conservation has failed
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The scale cannot establish the tissue energy loss

Water has negligible chemical fuel value, and body mass includes multiple compartments. Composition measurements are needed.

34. In a prolonged fast, brain ketone uptake rises while erythrocyte glycolysis remains obligatory. Which prediction is most consistent?

  1. Endogenous glucose production ceases
  2. Glucose demand may decline but remains nonzero
  3. Hepatic ketogenesis stops
  4. Protein breakdown must increase without limit
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Glucose demand may decline but remains nonzero

Brain glucose use can fall, reducing gluconeogenic demand, but erythrocytes and some other requirements persist. Glucose production remains necessary.

35. Two foods have identical 400 kcal dry energy. Food A contains 100 g dry matter; food B adds 100 g water to the same dry portion. Energy per 100 g of B is

  1. 400 kcal
  2. 800 kcal
  3. 200 kcal
  4. 0 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 200 kcal

Total energy stays 400 kcal in 200 g wet mass. Per 100 g, energy is 200 kcal; dilution changes energy density, not original energy.

36. A lactating animal receives 1200 kcal metabolizable energy, expends 850 kcal as heat/work, and exports 250 kcal in milk. Retention is

  1. 350 kcal
  2. 600 kcal
  3. −100 kcal
  4. 100 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 100 kcal

The stated output terms are distinct: 1200−850−250=100 kcal. 350 ignores milk export.

37. Adipose deposition and net mobilization initially equal 10 and 7 g/h. Both increase by 4 g/h. The reserve change rate is

  1. +3 g/h, unchanged
  2. +7 g/h
  3. −1 g/h
  4. 0 g/h
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. +3 g/h, unchanged

Initially 10−7=3; subsequently 14−11=3 g/h. Greater turnover does not necessarily change net storage.

38. A nutrient-deficient diet has equal gross combustion energy to the control, but its faecal energy is higher. What is justified?

  1. Equal gross energy guarantees equal metabolizable energy
  2. Apparent digestible energy is lower for equal gross intake
  3. The deficient group necessarily gains fat
  4. Urinary energy is zero
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Apparent digestible energy is lower for equal gross intake

With equal gross input, greater faecal loss lowers apparent digestible energy. Metabolizable energy also depends on further losses.

39. A protein-only stimulus raises insulin and glucagon. Why is this not incompatible with the fed state?

  1. Glucagon is an intestinal enzyme
  2. Insulin cannot respond to amino acids
  3. Amino acids can stimulate both hormones, preserving glucose availability
  4. Fed state requires glucagon to be zero
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Amino acids can stimulate both hormones, preserving glucose availability

Amino acids can stimulate insulin and glucagon. Glucagon supports hepatic glucose availability; the hormonal response depends on meal composition.

40. A liver produces more ketones, but measured blood ketone concentration is unchanged. What additional change could explain this?

  1. Reduced peripheral utilization only
  2. Loss of all hepatic mitochondria
  3. Complete absence of ketone production
  4. Increased peripheral utilization or clearance
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Increased peripheral utilization or clearance

At a steady concentration, an increase in production can be matched by increased removal. Reduced utilization alone would tend to raise concentration.

41. In Figure 12, the solid curve declines while the dashed curve increases as nutrient deprivation continues. Which prediction follows from the physiological interpretation, rather than merely the curve shapes?

  1. Glucose production stops once the curves intersect.
  2. The crossing marks a universal fixed time for hepatic glycogen exhaustion.

42. A pair-fed experiment gives the following illustrative nitrogen retentions: complete diet +2 g/day, deficient diet −1 g/day, deficient diet plus the missing amino acid +1.8 g/day. The three groups have equal estimated metabolizable intake. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. The missing amino acid contributes to impaired nitrogen retention; the selective rescue supports causality under the stated conditions.
  2. All animals have equal protein status because their energy intake is equal.
  3. The deficient group loses exactly 1 g of muscle each day.
  4. The rescue proves that no other factor can affect nitrogen retention.
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. The missing amino acid contributes to impaired nitrogen retention; the selective rescue supports causality under the stated conditions.

The complete and rescued groups retain nitrogen while the deficient group loses it despite matched energy intake. Nitrogen mass is not equivalent to muscle tissue mass. Other determinants remain possible and the inference is limited to the controlled conditions.

SET, assertion–reason and integrated practice

43. Assertion: A person may have positive energy balance and negative nitrogen balance. Reason: Total energy adequacy does not guarantee sufficient indispensable amino acids.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Both true, reason explains assertion

Energy and nitrogen accounting concern different stored materials. An amino acid limitation can cause net nitrogen loss despite positive energy retention.

44. Assertion: During prolonged fasting, erythrocytes switch entirely to ketone bodies. Reason: Ketone oxidation requires mitochondrial metabolism.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Assertion false, reason true

Mature erythrocytes lack mitochondria and cannot oxidize ketones; the reason correctly explains why the assertion is false.

45. Assertion: Bomb calorimetry can overestimate food energy available physiologically. Reason: Combustion energy includes material that may leave the animal without complete oxidation.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Both true, reason explains assertion

The calorimeter oxidizes material that the animal may excrete or fail to digest; gross and metabolizable values differ.

46. An animal receives 1900 kcal and expends 1800 kcal per day. Intake uncertainty is ±100 kcal and expenditure uncertainty is ±100 kcal. What can be established from these bounds?

  1. A definite +100 kcal balance
  2. A definite negative balance
  3. The plausible balance includes negative and positive values
  4. Expenditure equals BMR
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. The plausible balance includes negative and positive values

The extreme differences are 1800−1900=−100 and 2000−1700=+300 kcal. The point estimate alone does not establish the sign.

47. A calorimeter with effective heat capacity 8 kJ/K rises by 2.5 K during combustion. Gross energy released is

  1. 3.2 kJ
  2. 20 kJ
  3. 10.5 kJ
  4. 20 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 20 kJ

Heat magnitude is CΔT=8×2.5=20 kJ, approximately 4.78 kcal. The result is gross combustion energy.

48. A 30 g fat portion is replaced by 30 g available carbohydrate, everything else unchanged. Estimated energy changes by

  1. −150 kcal
  2. +150 kcal
  3. −270 kcal
  4. 0 kcal
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. −150 kcal

The difference is 30×(4−9)=−150 kcal. Matching mass does not match energy.

49. Two organs have the same arterial-minus-venous difference of 0.4 mmol/L. Flow is 0.25 L/min in X and 1.0 L/min in Y. Which is correct?

  1. X uptake is fourfold higher
  2. Y uptake is fourfold higher
  3. Uptake is equal
  4. Y must be fourfold larger in mass
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Y uptake is fourfold higher

X uptake is 0.10 and Y uptake 0.40 mmol/min. Net exchange depends on flow and concentration difference; organ mass is unknown.

50. Which combination of statements is correct? I: Glycogen polymerization reduces the osmotic burden of storing glucose. II: Fat stores generally associate with less water than glycogen. III: Functional tissue protein is a dedicated inert energy depot.

  1. I only
  2. II and III
  3. I and II
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. I and II

I and II describe storage trade-offs. III is false because tissue proteins perform functions and their mobilization can compromise physiology.

51. Which combination is correct? I: Glycerol can contribute to gluconeogenesis. II: Even-chain fatty acid acetyl-CoA yields net glucose in mammals. III: Odd-chain fatty acid carbon can contribute via propionyl-CoA.

  1. I and III
  2. I and II
  3. II and III
  4. All three
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. I and III

I and III are correct. Even-chain acetyl-CoA cannot yield net glucose carbon via normal mammalian pathways.

52. A deficient diet causes poor growth; selective nutrient replacement restores growth at unchanged metabolizable energy. Which inference is strongest?

  1. All growth is controlled by that nutrient alone
  2. The replaced nutrient likely contributed causally to the original growth limitation under the test conditions
  3. Energy is never required for growth
  4. The result proves universal dietary requirements for all species
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. The replaced nutrient likely contributed causally to the original growth limitation under the test conditions

Selective rescue supports a causal nutrient limitation in the tested context. It does not eliminate other determinants or justify universal extrapolation.

Revision and mastery

Nutritional homeostasis requires adequate energy, essential nutrients and the ability to distribute and utilize them. Gross combustion energy differs from metabolizable energy. The energy balance equation concerns stored chemical energy and requires a specified time interval and system boundary. Tissue-specific fuel capabilities explain the gradual transition from absorption through postabsorptive metabolism to prolonged fasting. Brain ketone uptake can conserve glucose and reduce the demand for amino acid-derived gluconeogenesis, but erythrocytes remain dependent on glycolysis.

High-yield equations and terms

  • General food energy (kcal) ≈ 4 × available carbohydrate (g) + 4 × protein (g) + 9 × fat (g).
  • Under the stated simplified convention, apparent digestible energy = gross intake energy − faecal energy.
  • Metabolizable energy = apparent digestible energy − urinary energy − combustible gas energy.
  • Stored-energy change = metabolizable intake − all corresponding energy outputs.
  • Nitrogen balance = nitrogen intake − total nitrogen losses.
  • Net organ uptake = appropriate flow × (arterial concentration − venous concentration).
  • Essentiality, energy density, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis, substrate turnover, nitrogen balance and nutrient rescue.
