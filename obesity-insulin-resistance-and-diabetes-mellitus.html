1. From energy storage to metabolic disease

A meal transfers nutrients from the external environment to the internal environment, but the immediate demands of the body do not necessarily coincide with the arrival of those nutrients. An individual may absorb glucose rapidly during the hour after eating while skeletal muscle has little requirement for contraction. Conversely, the brain continues to require metabolic fuel during the night when the intestine is not absorbing significant amounts of carbohydrate. The central problem of metabolic physiology is therefore one of coordination across time and across organs. Storage of energy during abundance and release during scarcity are essential solutions to this problem, rather than pathological processes in themselves.

White adipose tissue provides an efficient store of chemical energy in triacylglycerol. Three fatty acyl chains esterified to glycerol form a highly reduced molecule that can be stored in a relatively anhydrous lipid droplet. The ability to store fat protects other tissues from sustained exposure to excessive incoming fuel. After eating, insulin promotes nutrient storage and restrains the release of fatty acids from adipose tissue. During fasting, reduced insulin action and appropriate counterregulatory signals permit the release of fatty acids and glycerol. The liver and other organs can then use or redistribute these substrates. The same tissue is thus beneficial when its response is correctly matched to physiological circumstances and potentially harmful when regulation is disrupted.

The first law of thermodynamics requires that change in stored energy equals metabolizable energy intake minus energy expenditure over a specified interval. In symbols, ΔE = Eᵢ − Eₒ, with each term expressed in an energy unit such as kilojoules. A positive difference must be stored somewhere in the body, but this equation alone says nothing about the composition of storage, the precise tissue in which it occurs or the feedback mechanisms that establish intake and expenditure. Short-term variation in glycogen-associated water and extracellular fluid can alter body mass without a proportional change in stored energy. Furthermore, expenditure and appetite are regulated responses; they are not fixed independent variables that remain unchanged when one component of the system is perturbed.

Obesity refers to excess adiposity that can impair health. Body mass index is mass in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. It is a convenient indirect measure rather than a direct measurement of adipose tissue mass or distribution. A tall muscular person and another person with greater body fat could have the same BMI. A person with relatively low total body fat may also possess limited capacity to store triglyceride safely in subcutaneous adipose tissue. These distinctions explain why insulin resistance cannot be identified merely by comparing body weights. Distribution, cellular properties, genetics, physical activity, sleep, age, dietary exposures and endocrine state all influence the physiological consequences of stored energy.

The useful question is not whether all fat is harmful, but whether adipose tissue is able to buffer substrate delivery without chronically disturbing the metabolic requirements of other organs. Insulin resistance describes a reduced response to insulin at a specified concentration, while diabetes mellitus describes a disorder characterized by hyperglycaemia. Although these states frequently coexist, neither is synonymous with obesity. The distinction between a regulatory defect and an observed concentration is essential to the rest of this chapter.

A concentration and a quantity are not the same variable. If a simplified blood compartment contains five litres and its glucose concentration is one gram per litre, the amount in that compartment is five grams. The body contains glucose outside the blood as well, so the example is not an estimate of total glucose stores. Nevertheless, it reveals the scale of the control problem: a carbohydrate-rich meal may deliver a much greater quantity than the glucose currently in blood. The quantity in the circulation need not increase by the full meal amount because absorption extends over time and tissues simultaneously remove glucose. An explanation of postprandial glycaemia must therefore account for the rate of input as well as the rate of output.

A simplified glucose pool can be described by a balance equation. Write dQ/dt = Rₐ − Rᵈ − U, where Q is the amount of glucose in the defined accessible pool, Rₐ is the rate of appearance from intestinal and endogenous sources, Rᵈ is metabolic removal and U is urinary loss. Each rate must have the same units, such as milligrams per minute. When glucose is stable, the derivative is approximately zero, but the fluxes may remain large. When glucose increases, its net appearance exceeds its net removal. This equation does not identify which process changed; it tells the investigator what classes of explanation are physically possible. If fluid distribution changes, a separate relationship between amount and concentration is needed.

A feedback controller behaves differently from an isolated enzyme reaction. If a tissue becomes less responsive to insulin, a small rise in glucose can increase beta-cell secretion, increasing the driving signal to that tissue. The feedback adjustment can hide the defect in a measurement of the controlled variable. A useful analogy is the change in effort required to maintain a constant load: an unchanged output does not show that the required input was unchanged. The analogy is limited because physiological insulin acts on several tissues simultaneously and its concentration depends on clearance as well as secretion. The key inference is that a normal glucose concentration can coexist with substantial changes in regulatory effort.

Changes in the physiological state must also be distinguished from changes in the time of measurement. An overnight fast decreases circulating insulin relative to the fed state and permits the mobilization of stored nutrients. That normal adaptation cannot be called insulin resistance solely because glucose uptake is lower than immediately after a meal. Resistance is defined relative to a comparable hormone exposure, tissue and physiological endpoint. During prolonged fasting, changes in muscle fuel preference can spare glucose for other tissues; during exercise, local energy demand may override the usual resting pattern. A valid comparison uses the relevant physiological conditions rather than treating all departures from a fed-state response as disease.

The regulation of body fat itself includes long-term and short-term signals. Gut peptides and gastric distension influence meal termination, while signals reflecting accumulated stores influence longer-term feeding and expenditure. Environmental availability of food, learning, stress, sleep and habitual activity can change these responses. A biological explanation therefore avoids assuming that every individual has identical control parameters. Energy conservation remains valid in all cases, while the physiological mechanisms determining the inputs, outputs and storage compartments vary. This is particularly relevant when comparing individuals who experience similar external nutritional environments yet develop different metabolic outcomes.

There is a distinction between relative energy excess and an absolute amount of dietary fat. Dietary lipid can be stored, oxidized or redistributed, while carbohydrate can supply immediate energy or participate in synthesis of lipid. The fate of a molecule depends on the metabolic setting in which it arrives. An experiment comparing two diets should therefore distinguish energy intake, macronutrient composition and changes in spontaneous activity. If one group consumes more energy while also eating a different nutrient mixture, an observed difference cannot automatically be attributed to the mixture itself. Pair-feeding is one approach to separating these factors, although it does not necessarily equalize the pattern of feeding or energy expenditure.

2. The normal insulin signal and its tissue-specific consequences

Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to nutrients and accompanying neural and hormonal signals. The hormone reaches liver through the portal circulation before reaching peripheral tissues at a concentration modified by hepatic extraction. A target cell receives the signal through the insulin receptor, a membrane receptor tyrosine kinase. Binding promotes activation of the intracellular kinase and phosphorylation of receptor and substrate proteins. Insulin receptor substrate proteins provide docking sites for signalling molecules including phosphoinositide 3-kinase, abbreviated PI3K. PI3K changes the composition of membrane phosphoinositides by producing PIP3. PIP3 recruits proteins that participate in activation of Akt, also called protein kinase B. PDK1 and mTOR complex 2 contribute to full Akt activation.

In skeletal muscle and adipocytes, Akt-dependent phosphorylation of regulators including TBC1D4 facilitates changes in Rab GTPase activity that permit GLUT4-containing vesicles to reach and fuse with the plasma membrane. This raises the number of glucose transporters available at the cell surface. Glucose enters by facilitated diffusion, not by direct coupling of glucose transport to ATP hydrolysis. Intracellular phosphorylation of glucose helps maintain a gradient of free glucose favouring entry. Glucose-6-phosphate can subsequently contribute to glycolysis or glycogen synthesis. Insulin also influences glycogen synthesis through regulatory kinases; inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3 is one component of this response.

Liver cells do not use GLUT4 translocation as their principal mechanism of glucose uptake. Hepatic GLUT2 supports bidirectional transport in relation to concentration gradients. Insulin regulates the fate of glucose inside hepatocytes and restrains hepatic glucose production. Akt-dependent phosphorylation of FOXO1 contributes to control of transcription of gluconeogenic genes, but acute control of net hepatic glucose output also depends on glycogen metabolism, substrate supply and extrahepatic effects. One should not conclude that every rapid change in glucose production requires new transcription. The rate of gluconeogenesis can respond to changing availability of substrates and signals long before new enzyme protein accumulates.

Adipose tissue displays another important response: insulin suppresses breakdown of stored triglyceride. One part of the mechanism involves activation of pathways that reduce cyclic AMP signalling and protein kinase A activity, including phosphodiesterase 3B. During stimulated lipolysis, protein kinase A promotes changes involving perilipin and hormone-sensitive lipase; adipose triglyceride lipase and associated regulatory proteins participate in the initial mobilization of stored triglyceride. The products, non-esterified fatty acids and glycerol, enter the circulation or are reused locally. Therefore suppression of lipolysis can reduce the delivery of gluconeogenic substrate to the liver even if a hepatic intracellular signalling pathway has not been directly altered.

The physiological actions of a hormone are best specified with a tissue, a process, a time scale and a concentration. Saying that insulin causes glucose uptake is correct for many skeletal muscle and adipose responses but cannot describe every organ. Erythrocytes, for example, use insulin-independent glucose transport. Tissue-specific insulin resistance does not imply that every cell is unable to obtain glucose. This observation also explains why high extracellular glucose can coexist with altered fuel utilization in selected tissues.

The insulin receptor is synthesized as a precursor and processed into a disulphide-linked complex containing extracellular alpha subunits and transmembrane beta subunits. Unlike the simplified picture of ligand-induced dimerization used for several receptor tyrosine kinases, the mature insulin receptor already exists as a linked complex. Insulin binding changes its conformation and promotes kinase activation. Phosphorylation of intracellular tyrosines then establishes sites for recognition by signalling proteins. This structure helps explain why a mutation in the intracellular kinase can alter signalling even when insulin binding is preserved. A ligand-binding assay and a downstream signalling assay ask different questions.

The lipid product PIP3 provides a local membrane platform rather than diffusing through the cell as a conventional soluble second messenger. Enzymes that remove phosphate groups from phosphoinositides counteract PI3K action. For example, PTEN converts PIP3 back towards PIP2 and thereby limits recruitment of proteins that recognize the activated lipid signal. A cell with normal insulin receptor phosphorylation but increased activity of a PIP3 phosphatase could display a reduced downstream Akt response. This possibility illustrates why the detection of normal receptor activation is insufficient to prove that an entire signalling pathway functions normally.

Signalling strength has several dimensions. The fraction of receptors occupied depends on ligand concentration and receptor affinity, but the magnitude of the physiological response also depends on receptor abundance, coupling efficiency, signal amplification, negative feedback and the capacity of the downstream effector machinery. In a muscle cell with severe depletion of GLUT4, increasing the upstream signal cannot restore unlimited transport because the available transporter pool imposes a ceiling. In a cell with a partially impaired upstream signal, increasing insulin concentration may recruit enough transporters to approach a normal maximum. The two defects may be distinguished by measuring responses across a sufficiently broad dose range.

A transport step and an enzyme step can be separately rate limiting. When GLUT4 is recruited to the membrane, glucose entry increases if there is an appropriate concentration gradient. If hexokinase activity or downstream substrate handling becomes limiting, further increases in transporter number do not necessarily produce a proportional rise in sustained glucose flux. Moreover, an assay using a trapped phosphorylated glucose analogue reports aspects of transport and phosphorylation rather than a complete accounting of glucose oxidation and glycogen synthesis. An experiment designed to determine the final metabolic fate of glucose needs additional measures such as labelled glycogen or labelled carbon dioxide.

Insulin influences the use of glycogen through coordinated regulation of synthesis and breakdown. Glycogen synthase adds glucose residues from UDP-glucose to the glycogen molecule, whereas glycogen phosphorylase participates in mobilization. The two processes are regulated by phosphorylation and allosteric effects of metabolites. Increasing synthesis while simultaneously suppressing breakdown makes net storage efficient. In a simplified pulse of labelled glucose, increased label incorporation into glycogen may reflect a change in entry into glycogen even when the net change in glycogen mass is small because unlabelled glycogen is also being mobilized. This is why isotope incorporation and change in total pool size need not be numerically identical.

Insulin signalling also influences protein synthesis and growth through Akt-dependent control of mTORC1, among other pathways. mTORC1 integrates nutrient availability with growth-related signals. Feedback from downstream nutrient and growth signalling can influence insulin receptor substrates. Students should not infer that the normal activation of mTORC1 by nutrients is itself pathological. Problems arise when feedback and metabolic demand remain mismatched over time or when a particular branch becomes dysregulated. The pathway is thus part of a regulatory architecture containing both feed-forward and feedback controls. Detailed regulation of energy sensing belongs in the preceding node, but the principle is necessary for interpreting selective insulin responses.

The liver receives hormonal information and substrate information together. In the fed state, portal glucose delivery and insulin action promote storage, while suppression of glucagon and changes in adipose lipolysis reduce the drive for endogenous glucose production. During fasting, the liver responds to altered hormonal ratios and substrate supply to preserve systemic glucose availability. A comparison of two isolated hepatocyte cultures at the same insulin concentration removes many extrahepatic influences. The same comparison in two intact animals may include differences in circulating glycerol, fatty acids, glucagon and neural input. Disagreement between the culture and the animal does not necessarily mean either experiment is wrong; they examine different parts of the network.

3. Adipose expansion, storage capacity and interorgan signals

Adipose tissue expands by hypertrophy, an increase in the size of existing adipocytes, and by hyperplasia, an increase in adipocyte number. Expansion requires coordination with extracellular matrix remodelling, blood supply and differentiation of precursor cells. When these processes are mismatched to the demand for storage, enlarged cells may experience local stress. Oxygen delivery can become inadequate relative to demand in some settings, matrix stiffness may change and damaged cells may attract immune cells. These features vary with depot and experimental model and should not be treated as inevitable consequences of every increase in adipocyte size.

Adipose tissue is also an endocrine organ. Leptin, whose circulating concentration generally relates to adiposity, informs neural circuits about long-term energy stores. A decline during energy restriction can signal relative energy insufficiency. In common obesity, leptin is often elevated, yet the expected reduction of food intake may be attenuated. This is not equivalent to congenital absence of leptin. Adiponectin, another adipocyte-derived protein, is often lower in insulin-resistant states and has actions linked to fatty acid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. However, circulating adipokine concentration is influenced by production, secretion and clearance; its correlation with a phenotype does not by itself establish a causal pathway.

Subcutaneous and visceral depots differ in anatomical drainage and metabolic characteristics. Products released by some visceral depots can enter the portal circulation and thus influence hepatic exposure. Yet a large subcutaneous depot can also supply substantial fatty acids to systemic blood because its contribution depends on both mass and release rate per unit mass. It is misleading to assign all circulating fatty acids to visceral fat. In a simplified example, a depot containing 10 kg of tissue with a release rate of two arbitrary units per kilogram per hour supplies twenty units per hour. A second depot of two kilograms releasing five units per kilogram per hour supplies ten units per hour. The smaller depot has greater specific activity, but the larger depot has greater total release.

Lipodystrophy illustrates the distinction between fat mass and storage capacity. In severe forms of lipodystrophy, absence or loss of functional adipose storage can accompany profound insulin resistance and accumulation of lipid in other organs. This observation contradicts the claim that insulin resistance must increase monotonically with total adipose mass. A safe buffer for fatty acids is protective when appropriately regulated. Conversely, excessive enlargement of a depot, loss of its storage function or abnormal substrate mobilization can redistribute metabolic stress to liver and muscle. The biological objective is effective substrate partitioning, not the elimination of adipose tissue.

Adipose tissue contains more than mature lipid-filled cells. The stromal vascular fraction contains vascular cells, immune cells, fibroblasts and adipocyte precursors, among other populations. The cellular composition affects how measurements of gene expression in a whole-tissue sample should be interpreted. A higher concentration of a macrophage-associated transcript may result from increased macrophage number, increased transcription within existing macrophages, or both. To distinguish these alternatives, researchers can compare whole tissue with sorted cell fractions and histological estimates of cell abundance. Without this distinction, a result might mistakenly be attributed to adipocytes themselves.

Healthy expansion of adipose tissue requires formation of new cells and appropriate access to substrates. Precursor differentiation changes the expression of genes involved in triglyceride storage and hormonal response. PPAR gamma is an important regulator of adipocyte differentiation and lipid-handling genes. When an adipocyte population can expand while preserving appropriate insulin-regulated substrate release, it can buffer fluctuations in nutrient delivery to other organs. A defect in differentiation or an abnormal distribution of storage capacity could compromise that buffering even without a particularly large increase in total fat mass. Cell number, cell size and depot location provide complementary rather than interchangeable information.

The rate of lipid entry into adipose tissue involves transport and hydrolysis processes in addition to uptake of fatty acids from circulating albumin. Lipoprotein lipase associated with capillary surfaces hydrolyses triglycerides in circulating lipoproteins, making fatty acids available for tissue uptake. Intracellular lipolytic enzymes, by contrast, mobilize the triglycerides already stored within adipocytes. Confusing these locations reverses the interpretation of a change in enzyme activity. Increased extracellular lipoprotein triglyceride hydrolysis can increase substrate availability for storage, whereas increased intracellular lipolysis can increase release from storage. Their net effects depend on subsequent re-esterification, oxidation and transport.

The measured plasma fatty acid concentration is the result of the balance between release and uptake. It need not increase if release rises but clearance increases proportionately. Conversely, the same release rate can support a higher concentration if tissue clearance decreases. Researchers can use a labelled fatty acid tracer to estimate turnover under appropriate steady-state assumptions. At a conceptual level, the dilution of a continuously infused tracer by endogenous unlabelled substrate gives information about entry of substrate into the measured pool. Knowledge of concentration alone cannot provide the same information. This distinction is particularly important when evaluating adipose insulin resistance because a high rate of lipolysis may be partly obscured by changes in fatty acid utilization.

Re-esterification complicates the simple picture of stored fat being either completely retained or completely released. Some fatty acids liberated inside an adipocyte can be esterified again rather than entering the circulation. Glycerol and fatty acid release are therefore not always in the simple three-to-one molar ratio expected from complete hydrolysis of triglyceride. The relationship depends on intracellular recycling and subsequent metabolism. Measuring both products can help distinguish net release from total mobilization, but neither alone is an unqualified measurement of all enzyme activity.

The association between low adiponectin and insulin resistance has a plausible physiological interpretation, because signalling downstream of adiponectin can influence fatty acid oxidation and inflammatory responses. Still, adiponectin is present in different oligomeric forms, and assays measuring total circulating abundance do not necessarily reflect the abundance or biological activity of each form. For an examination problem, the critical distinction is whether the experiment measured a circulating marker or perturbed the signal in a way that demonstrates a functional role. A result that is useful as a biomarker may remain informative without proving that raising the marker would restore normal physiology.

Leptin has a contrasting relationship to adiposity. Congenital loss of functional leptin can cause severe disruption of energy balance, providing evidence that the signal matters. Common obesity usually does not involve a simple absence of leptin. A high measured concentration may be accompanied by an attenuated response at relevant neural circuits. Transport into the central nervous system, receptor signalling and other network influences can alter responsiveness. Comparing leptin-deficient and leptin-resistant states illustrates the broader endocrine distinction between insufficient hormone and inadequate effect of the available hormone. The same distinction later becomes central to comparing insulin deficiency with insulin resistance.

An important distinction emerges when two experiments address different levels of the same hypothesis. Adipose tissue expression profiling can reveal that macrophage-associated transcripts rise with adiposity, whereas separation of macrophage and nonmacrophage cell fractions helps identify cellular sources of inflammatory mediators. Neither measurement alone establishes that a particular mediator causes reduced insulin action. To move from localization to causality, a researcher must selectively alter that mediator or its signalling pathway, measure the metabolic response and account for changes in body mass, food consumption, tissue composition and other competing explanations. A selective improvement in insulin action after a targeted perturbation supports a causal contribution under the tested conditions; it does not imply that every case of human insulin resistance has the same origin.

4. Inflammatory signalling and adipose dysfunction

In 2003, independent studies by Weisberg and colleagues and Xu and colleagues examined adipose tissue in obesity and reported evidence for increased macrophage accumulation and inflammatory gene expression. The importance of these observations was not the discovery of an entirely new cell type, but the demonstration that a metabolic organ changes its immune composition under conditions of excess nutrient storage. Histological examination, cell separation and expression analysis provided complementary evidence. Observing macrophages in adipose tissue alone does not prove that they initiated insulin resistance, since recruitment may occur after adipocyte injury. The causal question requires selective perturbations and carefully matched comparisons.

Cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor and pathways involving JNK or IKK can alter insulin signalling and cellular metabolism. Stress-responsive kinases can modify insulin receptor substrates and other signalling proteins. It is an oversimplification to claim that phosphorylation at any serine residue always inhibits insulin signalling: sites, timing and molecular context differ. A signalling protein can participate in normal negative feedback as well as pathological dysregulation. Experimental evidence should specify the modification, the condition under which it was measured and a functional endpoint such as glucose uptake or suppression of lipolysis.

Macrophages participate in clearance of dying cells, remodelling and tissue defence. Their phenotypes vary continuously with environment; simple M1 and M2 designations may be useful for introductory comparisons but do not capture the diversity of adipose immune states. Inflammation can influence insulin action indirectly by promoting fatty acid release, in addition to effects within insulin target cells. Increased substrate delivery may subsequently drive hepatic glucose production or lipid deposition. Thus a local inflammatory process can alter whole-body metabolic regulation without the inflammatory signal acting identically in every tissue.

A convincing experiment distinguishes association, necessity and sufficiency. If obese mice have elevated inflammatory markers and decreased glucose disposal, the two observations support association. If a tissue-specific intervention prevents a particular inflammatory pathway and insulin action improves while relevant confounders are controlled, the result supports a causal contribution of that pathway in that model. If activation of the pathway in an otherwise appropriate control reproduces aspects of the phenotype, it supports sufficiency under the tested conditions. Neither result proves that all human cases of diabetes arise by the same route. Diet composition, food consumption, body composition and activity can change as secondary consequences of genetic interventions and must be considered when interpreting a metabolic phenotype.

A crown-like structure in an adipose tissue section is an arrangement of immune cells around a damaged or dying adipocyte. Its appearance can provide evidence of local tissue remodelling and cellular injury. It is not by itself a measurement of systemic insulin action. Histology tells the researcher where cells are located; biochemical measurements identify signals or products; physiological tests establish consequences for substrate regulation. Combining the three can support a stronger mechanistic account than any one alone. An image of immune cell accumulation should therefore be interpreted as spatial evidence and not as a complete demonstration of a disease mechanism.

Innate immune receptors recognize diverse molecular cues. Certain nutrient-related cellular stresses can activate inflammatory pathways directly or indirectly, and gut-derived signals may contribute in some models. However, a direct interaction between a dietary molecule and a particular innate immune receptor cannot be assumed merely because receptor deletion changes the response to the molecule. The deletion may alter the cellular environment, downstream cytokines or microbiome. A convincing molecular claim requires appropriate biochemical evidence and controls for contaminants. Such details matter at NET level because an experimental phenotype can have multiple plausible causes even when it is highly reproducible.

Inflammatory signalling has a time dimension. A brief signal during normal host defence or tissue repair can be useful, while persistent low-grade activation may alter tissue function. Interleukin-6 illustrates this contextual dependence: its physiological effects depend on source, duration, target tissue and accompanying signals. It is inappropriate to treat every increase in a cytokine as proof of detrimental chronic inflammation. Exercise itself produces transient changes in signalling molecules. A comparison between exercise-induced and chronic adipose-associated signals must account for differences in time course and biological context rather than rely only on the molecule's name.

A simple experimental series can separate cytokine production from target-cell sensitivity. First measure cytokine concentration in conditioned medium from control and stressed adipose cells. Apply comparable media to a target cell system and measure insulin-stimulated Akt phosphorylation and glucose uptake. If stressed-cell medium reduces the response, add a selective neutralizing reagent with an appropriate matched control. Recovery would support a contribution of the neutralized mediator. Failure to recover might indicate other mediators, irreversible target-cell changes or inadequate neutralization. It would not prove that the cytokine is irrelevant in every organism. Measuring a functional endpoint together with a signalling endpoint is essential because a molecular change may not be large enough to alter flux.

An important distinction in such studies is between a model of disease and a mechanism shared across diseases. A high-fat feeding model in mice may produce obesity, changes in adipose immune populations and altered glucose tolerance. A genetic model with reduced leptin signalling may produce some similar outcomes through different initial disturbances. When a specific inflammatory pathway contributes in both models, the evidence for a common component strengthens, but translation to humans still requires independent observations. The goal is not to identify one animal model as a miniature copy of every human case; it is to test well-defined hypotheses about conserved physiological processes.

5. Ectopic lipid and impaired insulin action

Ectopic lipid is lipid stored in sites that are not primarily specialized for long-term bulk energy storage, such as skeletal muscle and liver. The accumulation of triglyceride in a tissue can indicate that lipid delivery exceeds oxidation and export over time. However, the triglyceride droplet itself is not necessarily the molecular agent responsible for insulin resistance. Certain lipid intermediates can influence cellular signalling, and the location of those intermediates within a cell matters.

Diacylglycerol is an intermediate in glycerolipid metabolism and a signalling lipid. In several experimental models, particular pools of diacylglycerol are associated with activation of novel protein kinase C isoforms. Hepatic PKC epsilon has been linked to impaired insulin receptor kinase activity, while muscle PKC theta has been implicated in altered downstream signalling in certain settings. Ceramides are sphingolipids that can inhibit components of Akt signalling through mechanisms that depend on cell type and molecular species. These mechanisms are examples of links between substrate flux and hormonal responsiveness; they should not be represented as one universally established sequence operating identically in all subjects.

An important exception is the high intramuscular triglyceride observed in some endurance-trained athletes who nevertheless display strong insulin sensitivity. The athlete's paradox directs attention to turnover, storage organization, oxidation and lipid intermediates rather than total triglyceride alone. Two muscles can contain similar amounts of stored triglyceride while differing in rates of fatty acid oxidation and accessibility of signalling lipid pools. In an experimental design, measuring bulk muscle lipid and glucose disposal is not enough to identify the causal species. Subcellular measurements, tracer studies and targeted manipulations can test competing explanations.

Lipid accumulation also requires consideration of balance. In a simplified hepatic fatty acid accounting model, the change in stored lipid over time depends on uptake from circulating substrates and synthesis of new fatty acids, minus oxidation and export. All terms must refer to comparable lipid or carbon units over the same interval. Suppose input and synthesis contribute 60 and 20 arbitrary fatty acid units per hour, oxidation consumes 35 units and export removes 30 units. Net accumulation is 15 units per hour. Increasing oxidation to 45 units while holding the other rates fixed reduces net accumulation to five units per hour. In an intact animal, however, rates may respond to the intervention, so the calculation is an accounting illustration rather than a prediction of an actual treatment response.

Mitochondria are central to fuel oxidation, but reduced oxidative capacity need not be the sole initiating lesion. If lipid delivery increases more rapidly than oxidative capacity, intermediates may accumulate even when oxidation itself is increased. Conversely, an observed mitochondrial defect can develop after chronic nutrient stress. To determine the sequence, researchers need longitudinal or perturbation evidence. A single cross-sectional comparison of mitochondrial abundance and insulin sensitivity cannot distinguish cause from consequence.

The phrase lipid overload is useful only if the relevant compartment and time scale are specified. Free fatty acids can enter muscle cells from the circulation, arise from hydrolysis of stored triglyceride or be redistributed between lipid pools. Fatty acyl-CoA molecules can then participate in oxidation, esterification or synthesis of complex lipids. Because these pathways share intermediates, altering one pathway may change the substrates available to another. An increase in fatty acid supply does not establish whether more fatty acids are oxidized or retained. The answer depends on the capacity and regulatory state of the receiving tissue.

The classic glucose-fatty acid cycle provides an early framework for thinking about competition between metabolic fuels. Randle and colleagues proposed that increasing fatty acid availability and oxidation could suppress glucose utilization through metabolic feedback. In a simplified version, oxidation of fatty acids raises the availability of acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents, which can restrict pyruvate oxidation. Changes in citrate and other intermediates can influence glycolytic flux. This framework concerns competition in metabolism downstream of glucose entry. Later experiments showing defects in insulin-stimulated glucose transport and glycogen synthesis helped distinguish regulation at the entry step from regulation of downstream oxidation. These models need not be mutually exclusive; they describe different processes and may contribute in different circumstances.

Consider an experiment in which a muscle preparation is exposed to an elevated fatty acid concentration. The investigators measure oxygen consumption and glucose oxidation but do not measure glucose transport. Glucose oxidation decreases while oxygen consumption remains similar. One interpretation is that fatty acid oxidation substitutes for glucose oxidation. It is not possible to conclude directly that insulin-dependent glucose transport has been inhibited, because glucose could still enter the cell and be directed towards glycogen or lactate. If a subsequent experiment shows reduced glucose transport at the same insulin concentration, the evidence supports an additional defect in uptake. Separating these measurements prevents the term insulin resistance from being used too broadly.

An acyl-CoA molecule is an activated metabolic substrate rather than a freely dissolved fatty acid. The energy required for activation and the enzymatic control of its subsequent fate create branch points in the lipid network. Esterification can package fatty acids in triglyceride, reducing their availability for other reactions. Under some conditions increased triglyceride synthesis therefore protects the cell from exposure to reactive lipid intermediates. A manipulation that decreases triglyceride accumulation could be beneficial if it lowers input or increases safe oxidation; it could instead be harmful if it prevents storage while leaving input unchanged. A lower triglyceride concentration is consequently not sufficient to prove an improved cellular state.

Diacylglycerol can be produced during triglyceride synthesis or breakdown, and it also arises in other lipid signalling processes. Different molecular species and membrane locations have different access to signalling proteins. A whole-cell measurement combines these pools. Suppose an intervention increases the amount of diacylglycerol stored in a relatively inert lipid-associated compartment while reducing membrane-associated diacylglycerol. Total abundance may rise even though access to a particular protein kinase C isoform declines. Measuring only total abundance could lead to an incorrect conclusion about kinase activation. A localization measurement and a functional kinase assay help test the model.

Ceramides likewise form a family of molecules rather than a single uniform toxin. Their biological effects depend on molecular structure, tissue and pathway context. Some ceramide-dependent mechanisms can attenuate Akt activity, but it is inappropriate to infer that every instance of insulin resistance is mediated by ceramides or that any increase in a sphingolipid measurement is sufficient evidence of that mechanism. If experimental inhibition of ceramide synthesis improves glucose transport, one should consider whether the intervention also changes other lipid pools or cellular stress. Rescue and specificity controls are valuable when assigning causal roles to metabolic intermediates.

The formation of lipid droplets is an active organizational process. Droplet-associated proteins regulate access of enzymes to stored lipid, while interactions with mitochondria and other organelles influence substrate trafficking. A trained muscle repeatedly encounters demand for oxidation and can mobilize local substrate stores during exercise. Its high intramyocellular triglyceride content may represent a readily available fuel reservoir rather than evidence of defective insulin response. In contrast, a sedentary muscle with altered turnover can accumulate a similar quantity of triglyceride while displaying quite different signalling and oxidation. The comparison reinforces the general rule that a pool size is a snapshot of the difference between past inputs and outputs, not a direct measurement of either rate.

Oxidative stress refers to disruption of the balance between oxidant production and the systems that regulate or remove reactive species. Reactive oxygen species also participate in physiological signalling. Mitochondrial electron transport, antioxidant capacity, nutrient flux and cellular workload all influence the effect of a change in oxidant production. In the setting of chronic substrate stress, oxidants can contribute to damage or altered signalling, but the relationship is not captured by a claim that more respiration always produces harmful oxidative stress. To establish functional significance, a researcher must measure an appropriate marker, alter the proposed pathway and determine whether the metabolic endpoint changes. Broad fluorescent probes and antioxidant interventions may have limitations in specificity.

6. Tissue-selective insulin resistance and glucose flux

A rise in blood glucose can result from increased entry into the circulation, reduced removal, or both. During fasting, hepatic glucose production is a major source of glucose; the kidney also contributes. During feeding, absorption from the intestine becomes an important source. Skeletal muscle is a major insulin-responsive site of glucose disposal under stimulated conditions. Adipose tissue contributes a smaller direct glucose disposal capacity than muscle but can strongly influence liver metabolism through release of fatty acids and glycerol. This is why changes in adipose insulin action can have consequences disproportionate to the direct glucose uptake by adipocytes.

In insulin-resistant skeletal muscle, a given insulin stimulus may produce less glucose transport and glycogen synthesis. The impaired response leaves a larger fraction of meal-derived glucose to be handled by other pathways. The effect on plasma concentration depends on compensatory insulin secretion, glucose effectiveness and other organs. If the pancreas secretes more insulin in response to the same load, blood glucose can remain within the usual range even when peripheral insulin sensitivity is reduced.

In adipose insulin resistance, lipolysis may remain relatively high despite insulin stimulation. Glycerol can serve as a gluconeogenic precursor; fatty acid oxidation in liver provides ATP and reducing equivalents and generates acetyl-CoA, which activates pyruvate carboxylase. The carbon skeleton of an even-chain fatty acid cannot make a net contribution to glucose via acetyl-CoA in humans. This distinction between providing energy or allosteric activation and providing glucose carbon is frequently tested. Glycerol released from triglyceride, in contrast, can contribute carbon to glucose production.

The liver may fail to restrain glucose production normally in response to insulin while lipogenesis remains active. This apparently selective response is compatible with differences in signalling, substrate availability, chronic hyperinsulinaemia and regulation by glucose-responsive transcription factors. It does not require the conclusion that all hepatic insulin pathways have shut down. An investigator should measure both glucose production and lipid synthesis rather than infer one from the other. The detailed transport consequences of abnormal hepatic lipid synthesis and export belong to the following curriculum node on dyslipidaemia and fatty liver.

Hyperglucagonaemia or failure to suppress glucagon appropriately after a meal can further stimulate hepatic glucose output. Glucagon acts through a receptor coupled to cyclic AMP signalling, promoting processes that support hepatic glucose release. Insulin and glucagon therefore cannot be interpreted independently of the nutritional state. A high fasting glucose concentration alone cannot establish whether the principal cause is excessive hepatic production, deficient beta-cell function, altered glucagon regulation or another factor; it is an integrated outcome.

Whole-body glucose uptake reflects uptake across many tissues. A muscle biopsy provides information about the sampled muscle, but different muscle fibre populations have distinct capacities for oxidation and contraction. Tissue perfusion also influences delivery of glucose and insulin. Thus impaired uptake in vivo can arise partly from altered delivery to cells even when an isolated cell retains a signalling response under standardized conditions. Conversely, an intracellular signalling defect may become evident in culture independently of blood flow. Integrating vascular and cellular measurements is useful when localizing the mechanisms underlying a whole-body phenotype.

Glucose uptake from blood and glucose production into blood are distinct directions of flux. The liver can simultaneously synthesize glucose through gluconeogenesis and use glucose for other pathways. Net glucose output is the difference between these processes. An intervention that lowers net output does not necessarily abolish gluconeogenesis; it may increase glucose utilization or storage at the same time. Similarly, a tracer estimate of gluconeogenic contribution depends on the definitions and assumptions of the method. In exam questions, it is important to identify whether the variable is net balance, absolute pathway flux or fractional contribution to a total flux.

The fasting liver produces glucose from glycogen and from non-carbohydrate precursors. Glycogenolysis releases previously stored glucose residues, while gluconeogenesis constructs glucose from precursors such as lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids. During a prolonged fast, liver glycogen becomes depleted and the relative importance of gluconeogenesis increases. Insulin can suppress hepatic glucose production through several coordinated processes. A short intervention may rapidly change glycogen breakdown or substrate supply, whereas a longer intervention can alter expression of enzymes. Observing a change in fasting glucose after an intervention is not enough to determine which source changed.

The Cori cycle provides a useful example of organ-specific roles. Lactate formed during glycolysis in muscle or other tissues can enter blood, be used by liver to produce glucose and return to peripheral tissues. The energetic cost of glucose synthesis is paid by the liver, and oxidation of fatty acids can contribute to that energy supply. If adipose lipolysis is insufficiently suppressed, hepatic energy availability for gluconeogenesis may rise. This is an indirect connection between fat mobilization and glucose production. The existence of the cycle does not imply that lactate is merely a waste product; it is a substrate for oxidation and interorgan exchange. The relative directions and magnitude of flux depend on the physiological state.

Alanine is another important gluconeogenic precursor. In muscle, transamination can transfer an amino group to pyruvate, forming alanine. The liver can recover the carbon skeleton for glucose synthesis while handling nitrogen through pathways that ultimately include the urea cycle. During insulin resistance or insulin deficiency, altered protein metabolism can change amino acid availability as well as glucose and lipid handling. These connections demonstrate why glucose homeostasis cannot be understood by considering carbohydrate reactions in isolation. The full normal feeding and fasting cycles are treated in the preceding node; here they are used to identify the substrates through which insulin resistance in one organ can affect another.

Adipose tissue can alter hepatic metabolism even without a large increase in circulating glycerol concentration if the rate of glycerol appearance rises and hepatic extraction increases at the same time. The concentration reflects a dynamic balance. Tracer methods permit estimation of appearance and disappearance, while arteriovenous measurements combined with blood flow can estimate net exchange across a tissue bed. If the arterial glucose concentration is 5.5 mmol/L and the venous concentration leaving a limb is 5.0 mmol/L at a plasma flow of 0.4 L/min, the calculated net plasma glucose extraction is 0.2 mmol/min under the simplified assumptions. The concentration difference alone is not enough: doubling flow at the same difference would double net exchange.

The kidney contributes to glucose homeostasis by filtering glucose and normally reabsorbing most filtered glucose through transporters in the proximal tubule. Sodium-glucose cotransport uses the sodium electrochemical gradient generated indirectly by ATP-dependent pumping. If filtered glucose exceeds effective reabsorptive capacity, glucose can appear in urine. The renal contribution to glucose production also matters, especially during fasting. Glycosuria changes the whole-body glucose mass balance because glucose leaves the accessible pool without being metabolized by a target tissue. In a balance problem that explicitly includes urinary glucose loss, that term must be retained rather than attributing all loss to tissue uptake.

Glucagon secretion from pancreatic alpha cells responds to nutrients and intra-islet, neural and hormonal signals. After carbohydrate ingestion, appropriate changes in glucagon help limit unnecessary hepatic glucose output. In type 2 diabetes, suppression of glucagon can be impaired in some settings, contributing to hyperglycaemia. Glucagon is not simply a mirror image of insulin for every tissue; its major metabolic target includes the liver, whereas insulin has important direct actions in muscle and adipose tissue as well. The ratio of hormones may be a useful descriptive variable but does not substitute for knowledge of absolute concentrations, receptor responses and substrate supply.

A tissue-selective knockout experiment can illustrate indirect insulin action. Imagine that loss of an insulin signalling component in adipose tissue reduces the suppression of fatty acid release, while the hepatic insulin receptor remains intact. In the whole animal, liver glucose production may be less suppressed during insulin stimulation because of altered substrate delivery. In isolated hepatocytes from the same animal, the response to insulin under matched conditions may be substantially preserved. These observations would support an extrahepatic contribution. They would not prove that direct hepatic insulin signalling is dispensable. To establish that stronger claim would require an independent manipulation of hepatic signalling and careful comparison of both interventions.

7. Pancreatic beta-cell compensation and failure

Beta cells detect changes in nutrient availability and convert metabolic information into an electrical and secretory response. Glucose enters the cell through facilitative transporters; transporter usage differs among species, and human beta cells prominently express GLUT1 and also GLUT3 rather than relying exclusively on GLUT2 as commonly described in rodents. Glucokinase catalyses glucose phosphorylation and helps set the relation between extracellular glucose and metabolism. Increased glucose oxidation increases the ATP-to-ADP ratio, favouring closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. Reduced potassium conductance depolarizes the membrane, opens voltage-gated calcium channels and increases cytosolic calcium. Calcium then promotes fusion of insulin-containing secretory granules with the plasma membrane.

An insulin response to a sustained glucose stimulus commonly has a rapid initial component and a slower sustained component. The initial component is associated with readily releasable granules; continued secretion involves additional mobilization and metabolic amplification. Gut incretins such as GLP-1 and GIP amplify glucose-dependent secretion. Comparing oral and intravenous glucose without matching the glucose excursion can confound evaluation of the incretin effect, because the beta cell is responding to glucose as well as gut-derived signals.

When insulin sensitivity declines, beta cells can compensate by increasing secretion. Reduced hepatic insulin clearance can also increase systemic insulin concentrations. Therefore an elevated insulin concentration is not a direct measurement of secretion. Insulin and C-peptide arise from cleavage of proinsulin and are released in equimolar quantities. C-peptide has different clearance kinetics and is not extracted by liver in the same manner as insulin; interpretation requires consideration of renal function and sampling conditions. Combined measurements can help distinguish changes in secretion from changes in clearance.

In a simplified hyperbolic model, the disposition index is the product of an insulin sensitivity index and an insulin secretory response. Kahn and colleagues reported evidence for such a relationship in humans in 1993. The concept is that an amount of secretion adequate for an insulin-sensitive subject may be inadequate for an insulin-resistant subject. Suppose two illustrative individuals have sensitivity values of 4 and 2 in consistent index units. If the first secretes 50 response units, a compensating response of 100 in the second would maintain the same product of 200. A response of 75 in the second would produce an index of 150, a 25 percent reduction despite a larger absolute secretory response than the first individual. The precise index depends on the method and the appropriateness of the underlying relationship; this example is not a clinical threshold.

Beta-cell dysfunction can arise from interacting genetic predisposition, chronic metabolic stress, inappropriate nutrient exposure, oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress, altered gene expression and other mechanisms. A reduction in effective beta-cell function does not automatically establish cell death, since impaired secretion and changes in cellular identity can also contribute. Prolonged hyperglycaemia can itself worsen function, creating feedback in which impaired regulation generates further metabolic stress. Progression is not uniform; individuals differ in initial reserve, insulin sensitivity, duration of exposure and capacity for recovery.

The pancreatic islet contains multiple endocrine cell types, including insulin-secreting beta cells, glucagon-secreting alpha cells and somatostatin-secreting delta cells. These cells communicate locally as well as with distant tissues through their hormones. Local secretion can alter neighbouring cell function before the hormone is diluted in the circulation. Consequently, isolated beta-cell responses need not reproduce all aspects of an intact islet response. An isolated-cell study may provide a particularly clean test of glucose sensing, whereas an intact islet study can reveal interactions between cell types. Both levels of organization are important for interpreting changes in insulin and glucagon in diabetes.

Glucokinase provides a link between extracellular glucose and the rate of beta-cell metabolism. Its kinetic properties permit glucose phosphorylation to increase across a physiologically relevant concentration range. In a beta cell with a reduced capacity to phosphorylate glucose, the ATP-to-ADP ratio may rise less at a given extracellular glucose concentration, making closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels less likely. Conversely, increased glucose sensitivity at this step can shift the secretory response towards lower glucose concentrations. Such changes illustrate how a defect in a metabolic enzyme can alter endocrine regulation without directly affecting the structure of an insulin molecule.

ATP-sensitive potassium channels couple nucleotide concentrations to membrane conductance. Their pore-forming and regulatory subunits determine channel function and sensitivity to metabolic changes. In an idealized experiment, a channel that remains open despite elevated ATP would maintain potassium conductance and oppose depolarization. Glucose oxidation could proceed normally while calcium-dependent insulin release is impaired. If direct depolarization restored secretion in that cell, the result would support a defect in the coupling between metabolism and electrical excitation rather than a complete inability of secretory granules to fuse. This kind of bypass experiment is a powerful way to locate a defective step in a pathway.

Calcium entry is a triggering signal for granule fusion, but the magnitude of secretion also depends on the availability and readiness of granules. Granules must be produced, processed, transported and made competent for fusion. A cell that experiences a normal increase in intracellular calcium yet secretes less insulin could have a defect downstream of calcium entry. Measuring calcium concentration alongside released insulin would help distinguish this possibility from defective membrane excitation. In a complementary test, a stimulus that directly increases intracellular calcium may bypass the upstream metabolic and electrical steps, but it cannot necessarily restore a depleted or dysfunctional secretory apparatus.

Proinsulin is synthesized and folded in the secretory pathway before cleavage into insulin and C-peptide. If processing is inefficient or demand for new hormone increases beyond the capacity of the cell, the relation between proinsulin and mature insulin can change. A high proinsulin-to-insulin ratio can therefore provide information about beta-cell function in an appropriate setting. It does not uniquely measure one molecular defect, because secretion and clearance influence circulating concentrations of both forms. An experimental analysis should also consider the actual glucose stimulus, the duration of exposure and the baseline secretory demand.

The distinction between secretion and clearance can be represented by a simple balance. Let I denote the amount of insulin in a defined compartment, S the rate of delivery to that compartment, and k a fractional removal rate per unit time. In a single-compartment approximation, dI/dt = S − kI. At steady state I = S/k. Doubling the amount of insulin could result from doubling delivery while k remains constant, halving k while delivery remains constant, or a combination. Physiological insulin distribution is more complex, and hepatic extraction occurs before much of the secreted hormone reaches systemic circulation. Nevertheless the simple equation explains why a concentration cannot be interpreted as secretion without information about removal.

The disposition index places beta-cell performance in the context of insulin sensitivity. The original concept describes a compensatory relationship in which a decrease in sensitivity can be accompanied by an increase in secretion. In practice the measurement method matters: an intravenous glucose test, an oral glucose test and a clamp can generate different indices reflecting different aspects of the system. An oral response includes gastrointestinal and gastric emptying influences absent in a matched intravenous test. To compare values across groups, one should use a validated index derived under comparable conditions. The product of any arbitrary two measurements is not automatically an accurate disposition index.

Chronic increases in demand can initially stimulate adaptive changes in beta-cell function. A larger secretory response might result from changes in the glucose responsiveness of individual cells, an increase in functional cell mass, changes in granule pools or altered neural and hormonal influences. Over time, susceptibility to metabolic stress may compromise these adaptations. In human diabetes, direct measurement of beta-cell mass and longitudinal measurement of function are difficult, and changes in circulating insulin cannot independently reveal the contribution of each. Distinguish the established observation of inadequate effective insulin response from a proposed cellular explanation for that inadequacy.

The term glucotoxicity refers to detrimental effects of sustained excessive glucose exposure on cellular function. Lipotoxicity describes detrimental effects associated with lipid exposure or lipid intermediates. These processes can interact, but neither term should be used to imply that glucose and fatty acids are intrinsically poisonous at every concentration. Both are normal fuels and signals. The effect depends on duration, concentration, metabolic context and the ability of the cell to adapt. A laboratory experiment testing chronic high glucose should include a control for osmotic effects if the added glucose substantially changes medium osmolality. A fatty acid exposure experiment should control vehicle, albumin binding and relevant concentrations to distinguish exposure from nonspecific injury.

An important question is whether beta-cell dysfunction can improve after removal of metabolic stress. If an isolated islet shows a reduced glucose response after prolonged exposure and partial recovery after restoration of appropriate conditions, the result supports a reversible functional component. If viable cell number is reduced, recovery of the remaining cells does not demonstrate restoration of the original cell population. In an intact individual, improved sensitivity also lowers the secretion required to maintain glucose control. Improved glucose regulation may thus reflect recovery of secretory function, reduced demand, or both. Physiological evidence of improvement should not be presented as proof that every underlying cellular defect has disappeared.

8. Distinguishing the forms of diabetes mellitus

Type 1 diabetes typically involves autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, causing severe insulin deficiency. Insulin resistance may coexist but is not the defining initiating mechanism. Type 2 diabetes typically involves insulin resistance together with inadequate compensatory beta-cell function, although the relative contributions vary among patients. Gestational diabetes involves diabetes diagnosed in the second or third trimester that was not clearly overt diabetes before gestation, with physiological pregnancy-associated insulin resistance challenging beta-cell reserve. Monogenic forms may affect beta-cell glucose sensing or other components of the regulatory system. The broad label diabetes therefore does not specify a unique molecular lesion.

Severe insulin deficiency removes restraint on adipose lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis. Fatty acids enter liver, undergo oxidation and supply acetyl-CoA for ketone body formation. When ketone production and associated acid load exceed utilization and buffering, metabolic acidosis can develop. This risk is characteristic of severe insulin deficiency but is not restricted absolutely to type 1 diabetes. In contrast, uncomplicated physiological fasting also increases ketone production, yet feedback and residual insulin ordinarily limit the rise. Distinguishing regulated adaptation from loss of control is the underlying physiological principle.

Chronic hyperglycaemia can increase nonenzymatic glycation of proteins. Haemoglobin A1c reflects glycation of haemoglobin over the lifetime distribution of circulating erythrocytes and is influenced by recent glucose exposure. Conditions that alter erythrocyte survival or haemoglobin properties can affect the relation between HbA1c and average glucose. It is therefore an integrated marker rather than a direct measure of insulin sensitivity or a real-time glucose measurement. Persistent hyperglycaemia can also influence vascular and neural function through multiple pathways, but detailed clinical complications and therapy are beyond the central scope of this node.

The word mellitus distinguishes diabetes associated with glucose from diabetes insipidus, in which excessive dilute urine results from disturbances in water regulation. Both conditions may produce increased urine volume, but their underlying mechanisms differ. In uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, glucose in the tubular fluid can retain water osmotically when reabsorption is insufficient. In diabetes insipidus, the primary difficulty is inadequate water conservation in relation to vasopressin secretion or action. This comparison connects the current chapter with renal physiology and illustrates why a shared symptom does not establish a shared disease mechanism. A student should trace the relevant solute and hormone rather than reason from the symptom alone.

The identification of insulin as an effective regulator of glucose changed the interpretation of diabetes from an observed disorder of urinary and blood glucose to a disease that could be understood partly through endocrine physiology. The observation that insulin administration could restore glucose regulation in severe insulin deficiency supported the importance of the pancreatic signal. Later, measurements of endogenous insulin and responses to insulin showed that diabetes could not always be understood as absolute lack of the hormone. Some individuals displayed elevated insulin concentrations alongside hyperglycaemia. This apparent contradiction helped establish the importance of tissue responsiveness and relative insulin deficiency. The modern physiological model therefore separates hormone availability from hormone effect.

In typical autoimmune type 1 diabetes, immune processes reduce functional beta-cell mass and insulin secretory capacity. Autoantibodies against islet-associated antigens can provide evidence of an autoimmune process, but antibodies are not the only mediators of cell destruction. Cellular immune responses play a central role. Early in the disease some endogenous secretion can persist; its decline varies among individuals. The absence of obesity is not a diagnostic requirement for type 1 diabetes, and the presence of obesity does not exclude autoimmune beta-cell loss. These details matter because the same individual can possess features associated with more than one mechanism. Classification should reflect biological evidence rather than body size alone.

Typical type 2 diabetes differs in that available insulin action is insufficient relative to prevailing metabolic demand. Early in progression, systemic insulin may be elevated and glucose tolerance may be preserved or only moderately impaired. Later, beta-cell response may fail to meet the requirement. Absolute insulin secretion need not be absent, but the amount or timing can be inadequate. Because the defect is relative to demand, a decrease in insulin sensitivity can reveal a previously compensated secretory limitation. Conversely, an improvement in sensitivity may improve glucose regulation without restoring every element of beta-cell function. This relationship accounts for heterogeneity in disease presentation and progression.

Pregnancy changes energy and substrate partitioning to support fetal development. As pregnancy progresses, placental and maternal signals can contribute to reduced maternal insulin sensitivity. Beta-cell compensation normally helps maintain appropriate glucose regulation in the presence of increased demand. Gestational diabetes occurs when that compensation is inadequate for the physiological context. The disturbance may improve after delivery because the hormonal environment changes, but it can reveal an underlying susceptibility to later dysregulation. It is not necessary to memorize a separate molecular pathway for every pregnancy-related hormone to understand the core principle: a physiological change in sensitivity challenges a feedback system with finite compensatory capacity.

Monogenic diabetes illustrates the power of identifying a specific defect in a component of glucose regulation. A variant affecting glucokinase can alter the glucose concentration at which the beta cell increases secretion. Other variants can affect transcriptional control of beta-cell function or ion channels involved in stimulus-secretion coupling. These conditions differ from common polygenic type 2 diabetes, in which numerous genetic and environmental influences combine. A family history may be informative in both cases, but a family history by itself does not establish a monogenic mechanism. Pedigree patterns, biochemical phenotype and genetic evidence must be considered together. The current node needs only the physiological implications, not a specialist classification of every subtype.

The distinction between insulin resistance and insulin deficiency becomes particularly evident when tracing fatty acid release. In severe deficiency, the restraint on lipolysis is greatly reduced and the liver receives increased fatty acid substrate. Low effective insulin signalling together with counterregulatory signals favours fatty acid oxidation and ketone production. Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate can serve as circulating energy substrates, while acetone can arise from decarboxylation of acetoacetate. The acids can contribute to an increased anion gap when production exceeds utilization and removal. In normal fasting, however, ketones provide an adaptive substrate for extrahepatic tissues and their rise is generally limited by feedback. Ketone formation itself is not evidence of pathological acidosis.

Hyperglycaemia can also alter body water independently of ketoacidosis. If urinary glucose loss produces osmotic diuresis, water and electrolyte losses can follow. Extracellular glucose influences osmotic gradients between compartments, and water shifts can alter measured concentrations of other solutes. This is one reason that concentration changes in a physiological experiment cannot always be interpreted as changes in total body content. The acid-base and fluid consequences of severe metabolic decompensation are extensions of principles already taught in renal physiology. The purpose here is to understand why disrupted regulation of one metabolic substrate can affect apparently distant physiological variables.

Long-term glucose exposure can modify proteins through nonenzymatic glycation. Unlike an enzyme-regulated addition of a carbohydrate chain to a protein, glycation can occur through chemical interactions of reducing sugars with available amino groups. The abundance of a glycated product depends on the duration and magnitude of exposure and on the turnover of the target protein. Haemoglobin is useful in this respect because erythrocytes circulate for a prolonged period. However, a shortened erythrocyte lifetime can lower the opportunity for glycation even when glucose exposure is elevated. A biochemical marker should therefore be interpreted through the mechanism that generates it rather than treated as an infallible substitute for the regulated variable.

Hyperglycaemia-related tissue damage can involve multiple processes, including changes in oxidative state, altered protein modification and changes in cellular signalling. Exposure of a tissue depends partly on the way it handles glucose, which differs across tissues. The fact that skeletal muscle insulin-stimulated glucose uptake is impaired does not mean that every tissue is protected against high glucose exposure. Some tissues receive glucose through mechanisms that do not require insulin-dependent GLUT4 recruitment. This distinction helps resolve the apparent paradox of extracellular glucose excess occurring together with reduced insulin-responsive storage. Detailed clinical pathology of individual organs is unnecessary to understand this general principle.

9. Experimental measures and quantitative interpretation

A glucose tolerance test administers a defined glucose challenge and measures the resulting glucose profile. In an oral test, absorption, gastric emptying, insulin and glucagon secretion, incretin responses, tissue uptake and endogenous glucose production all contribute. A prolonged elevation in glucose can signal impaired regulation but does not uniquely localize a defect. An intravenous test changes several of these influences by bypassing the gastrointestinal tract. Selecting an experimental method depends on the question rather than simply which test is easiest to perform.

The hyperinsulinaemic euglycaemic clamp isolates insulin action more directly by maintaining an elevated insulin concentration and varying glucose infusion to hold plasma glucose near a chosen normal concentration. During steady state, ignoring urinary loss and net changes in the glucose pool, total glucose disposal equals the sum of endogenous glucose production and exogenous glucose infusion. If insulin suppresses endogenous production nearly completely, the glucose infusion rate approximates disposal. If residual production persists, the approximation underestimates disposal. Isotopic tracers can help estimate residual endogenous production and thereby separate hepatic and peripheral components.

Consider two hypothetical 70 kg participants at the same achieved insulin and glucose concentrations. Participant A receives 420 mg glucose per minute to maintain the clamp target; participant B receives 210 mg per minute. Normalizing to body mass gives six and three mg per kilogram per minute respectively. If residual endogenous production is negligible in both, B has approximately half A's mass-normalized disposal under those particular clamp conditions. Now suppose B has residual endogenous production of 70 mg per minute. Total disposal becomes 280 mg per minute, or four mg per kilogram per minute. The conclusion changes: the lower infusion requirement partly reflects incomplete suppression of endogenous glucose production. A single whole-body clamp number cannot locate all defects without further information.

HOMA-IR is a fasting surrogate based on glucose and insulin concentrations. The conventional simple equation uses fasting glucose in mmol/L multiplied by fasting insulin in μU/mL, divided by 22.5. If glucose is 5 mmol/L and insulin is 18 μU/mL, the value is 4.0. If glucose is reported in mg/dL, an equivalent formula uses divisor 405, based on the approximate conversion of 18 mg/dL per mmol/L. This index reflects assumptions about basal feedback and is not interchangeable with directly measured stimulated muscle uptake. Assay differences, altered secretion and exogenous insulin complicate interpretation. It is most useful when the assumptions and population are specified, not as a universal diagnostic cutoff.

Finally, insulin sensitivity is not a binary property. A dose-response experiment may show a higher concentration required to produce a given effect, a lower maximal effect, or both. A single concentration on the steep part of the curve can identify a response difference without distinguishing changes in potency and maximal capacity. Measuring multiple doses and relevant functional endpoints helps determine which property has changed. This reasoning applies equally to isolated adipocytes, skeletal muscle preparations and whole-animal studies, although delivery, clearance and feedback add complexity in intact organisms.

A well-designed tolerance test controls the starting conditions. Prior diet, physical activity, sleep, duration of fasting and time of day can affect glucose regulation. In a laboratory comparison, the protocol should define relevant conditions in both groups, then interpret any remaining differences in light of the known variability of the biological system. If one group exercises shortly before the test while another remains inactive, the result may reflect the acute effect of contraction rather than a stable difference in insulin action. Standardization improves interpretability but does not eliminate biological variation, so suitable replication and reporting of uncertainty remain necessary.

The area under a glucose concentration-time curve summarizes exposure over the observation interval. For measurements at times t₀, t₁ and so on, the trapezoidal approximation adds the area of each interval using the average of its two endpoint concentrations multiplied by its duration. Consider an illustrative series measured at zero, thirty, sixty and one hundred twenty minutes, with concentrations of 5, 9, 8 and 6 mmol/L. The interval contributions are 30 × 7 = 210, 30 × 8.5 = 255 and 60 × 7 = 420 mmol·min/L, giving total area 885 mmol·min/L. Relative to a constant baseline of 5 mmol/L across 120 minutes, the incremental area in this example is 285 mmol·min/L. An incremental area below baseline requires a stated convention. Neither the total nor incremental area alone locates the impaired organ.

Two curves with identical area can have different shapes. A rapid, high early excursion followed by rapid recovery may yield the same area as a smaller but prolonged elevation. The first pattern might reflect different absorption kinetics or timing of secretion; the second might reflect prolonged glucose appearance or slower removal. Neither interpretation is uniquely established by shape. Measuring insulin, C-peptide and relevant substrate fluxes can help distinguish mechanisms. A time series is more informative than one endpoint, but even a complete concentration curve is still the integrated output of multiple physiological processes.

A comparison of oral and intravenous glucose administration is especially instructive. Intestinal exposure to glucose stimulates gut-derived signals, and oral delivery also introduces variability in absorption. If an intravenous infusion is adjusted to reproduce the plasma glucose profile obtained during an oral test, any systematic difference in insulin secretion under the matched glucose conditions can provide evidence of an additional gastrointestinal contribution. C-peptide measurements can help account for differences in insulin clearance. Matching the glucose excursion is important: a larger insulin response to oral glucose is not evidence of an incretin effect if the oral route also produces a substantially higher glucose stimulus.

The clamp method can be understood as an experiment that uses feedback to control a variable while measuring the input required to maintain it. The insulin infusion creates the hormonal stimulus and glucose infusion prevents a fall in glucose concentration. Frequent monitoring allows adjustment of the glucose infusion. After equilibration, a stable concentration and relatively stable infusion rate support application of a steady-state balance. The term euglycaemic indicates that glucose is held near a chosen normal concentration, not that all metabolic processes have become normal. The term hyperinsulinaemic describes the imposed elevation of insulin, not a spontaneous disease diagnosis.

The same insulin infusion rate does not guarantee the same plasma insulin concentration in different subjects. Clearance and distribution may differ. Comparing glucose infusion rates without comparing achieved insulin concentrations can confound the analysis. Normalization to body mass or lean mass likewise answers different questions. If two people have similar muscle mass but one has substantially more adipose tissue, normalizing to total mass may yield a lower apparent disposal per kilogram even if the absolute muscle disposal is similar. Neither denominator is inherently correct in every setting; the experimental question determines which normalization is informative and should be reported.

The steady-state assumption is essential. If glucose concentration is falling rapidly, a portion of ongoing disposal is supplied from the existing glucose pool rather than from infusion and endogenous production alone. If it is rising, some incoming glucose is accumulating in the pool. Rearranging the glucose balance yields disposal as total appearance minus urinary loss minus the rate of accumulation in the defined pool. Measuring only infusion during an unstable period cannot establish disposal. The clamp interpretation is strongest when both hormone and glucose concentrations are sufficiently stable and relevant sources of endogenous appearance are considered.

Tracer dilution adds information about sources of glucose. Conceptually, a labelled glucose tracer is infused at a known rate and its relative abundance in the sampled pool is measured. At isotopic steady state, entry of unlabelled glucose dilutes the tracer. Under a simplified model with negligible tracer recycling and properly defined enrichment, the relationship between infusion and dilution permits estimation of appearance. Real calculations require attention to enrichment definitions, non-steady-state conditions and any label entering through infused glucose. The purpose at examination level is to understand that identical concentrations can arise with different turnover rates and that tracing molecules helps identify those rates.

A low-dose and a high-dose insulin clamp can provide complementary information because suppression of endogenous glucose production and stimulation of peripheral glucose disposal have different dose dependencies. At a lower insulin concentration, differences in hepatic or adipose responses may be particularly informative. At a higher concentration, peripheral disposal can become a prominent endpoint. However, dose ranges are not perfectly tissue specific, and the response depends on experimental conditions. A conclusion about a particular tissue is stronger when a tracer estimate, tissue exchange measurement or direct tissue endpoint supports it. Students should avoid treating the dose label as a direct measurement of an organ.

An insulin tolerance test in experimental animals administers insulin and follows the decline in glucose. A more rapid decline under standardized conditions can be consistent with greater insulin responsiveness, but the result is influenced by counterregulatory responses and starting glucose. As glucose falls, glucagon and other counterregulatory signals can stimulate endogenous glucose production. A later recovery in the glucose curve therefore does not necessarily mean insulin has ceased acting on every target tissue. The experiment probes an integrated dynamic response rather than isolating one tissue. Comparison with a glucose tolerance test and direct measurements may help separate secretory and sensitivity-related contributions.

Cultured cells and isolated tissue preparations allow more controlled testing of signalling mechanisms. In a muscle cell experiment, investigators might expose cells to a defined lipid treatment, add insulin and then measure receptor phosphorylation, Akt phosphorylation, surface GLUT4 and glucose transport. If receptor phosphorylation is preserved but Akt activation and transport are reduced, the data suggest a defect downstream of the receptor or in a parallel regulatory pathway. If Akt activation is preserved but surface GLUT4 remains low, attention shifts towards trafficking or fusion. A molecular measurement should be normalized appropriately to protein abundance and accompanied by controls for loading, viability and the specificity of the assay. A result obtained in culture should not automatically be treated as a quantitative prediction of whole-body physiology.

A rescue experiment tests whether restoring a component can improve a defect. Suppose a selective manipulation restores membrane recruitment of GLUT4 in an insulin-resistant cell and glucose uptake also improves. This observation supports the relevance of transporter trafficking to the phenotype. It does not establish that trafficking was the sole initial cause: a rescue can bypass an earlier defect. Combining rescue with a perturbation that reproduces the phenotype in a control system can strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. Proper controls are crucial because a nonspecific increase in cellular metabolism or a change in cell number can produce a misleading apparent improvement in glucose uptake.

Experimental evidence must distinguish biological replicates from technical replicates. Repeated measurements of a sample can improve precision of that sample estimate, but they do not provide the same information as independent organisms or independent cell preparations. If three assay wells contain material from one animal, they do not represent three independent animals. A meaningful comparison reports variability among the relevant independent units and avoids inflating sample size through technical repetition. This principle is especially important in metabolic studies, where individual differences in body composition and physiological state can be substantial. A small apparent difference between group averages may not justify a strong causal claim without evidence about variability and design.

When studying the effect of a genetic variant, a whole-body knockout can cause developmental adaptations and changes in several organs. Tissue-specific and inducible perturbations can help distinguish direct effects in a chosen tissue from secondary effects elsewhere or from long-term developmental compensation. An adipocyte-specific deletion that alters liver glucose output can support interorgan communication, but it is necessary to establish that the deletion occurred in the intended cell population and to consider changes in substrate supply. The appropriate control includes relevant genetic background and treatment conditions. An unexpected finding is often an invitation to distinguish levels of regulation rather than to discard the underlying physiological model.

10. Causal integration, heterogeneity and reversibility

A useful mechanistic model links excess substrate delivery and impaired adipose buffering to tissue-specific changes in lipid intermediates, inflammation and insulin responses. Pancreatic compensation initially limits glucose excursions. In susceptible individuals, compensation becomes inadequate and glucose rises; chronic hyperglycaemia can then worsen regulatory dysfunction. This is a feedback network rather than a rigid sequence. Genetic beta-cell defects can precede substantial insulin resistance, and reduced adipose storage capacity can produce insulin resistance in individuals without obesity. Any proposed sequence must be evaluated against these exceptions.

Exercise provides an example of how separate control mechanisms can converge on a shared endpoint. Muscle contraction stimulates glucose transport through pathways partly distinct from proximal insulin signalling, involving changes in cellular energy state, calcium-related signals and trafficking regulators. An insulin-resistant muscle can retain some contraction-stimulated glucose uptake. Chronic training may also change mitochondrial capacity, substrate turnover and subsequent insulin sensitivity. Demonstration of increased glucose uptake during exercise does not necessarily establish immediate normalization of every insulin signalling component.

A reduction in excess energy intake can alter the balance between substrate delivery and tissue oxidation or export. Improvements in glucose regulation can precede large changes in body mass in some settings, consistent with changes in hepatic substrate flux and storage. Such a response does not prove that only liver fat matters, nor does it mean that every form of diabetes is reversible. Remission of type 2 diabetes is distinguished from permanent cure because metabolic vulnerability may persist. The scope here is the physiology of changing energy flux rather than individual treatment recommendations.

The strongest examination answers trace mechanisms at the correct level and distinguish the observation from its explanation. Increased fasting glucose is an outcome; impaired suppression of hepatic glucose production is a possible mechanism; a tracer clamp provides a way to test that mechanism. Increased muscle triglyceride is an observation; accumulation of specific signalling lipids is a hypothesis about impaired insulin action; measurement of localization and a selective perturbation can test the hypothesis. Keeping these distinctions explicit allows the student to interpret unfamiliar experiments rather than memorize a single list of molecular names.

A network model is most useful when it makes predictions that can be contradicted by data. Suppose impaired adipose insulin action increases fatty acid delivery to the liver, and this increased delivery contributes to excessive hepatic glucose production. The model predicts that reducing fatty acid delivery while holding other relevant signals constant should reduce the proposed substrate-driven component of glucose output. If hepatic production remains unchanged despite confirmed reduction of delivery, other mechanisms must be considered. If it decreases, the result is consistent with a substrate-mediated contribution but still does not establish that all hepatic insulin resistance originates in adipose tissue. Such conditional predictions transform a diagram from a list of associations into a testable explanation.

The interorgan model also explains why different interventions can produce similar glucose outcomes. Increasing muscle glucose disposal can lower circulating glucose by increasing removal. Reducing hepatic glucose production can lower it by reducing appearance. Improving beta-cell glucose-dependent secretion can change both processes through endocrine feedback. Reducing intestinal glucose appearance can affect the timing and magnitude of the challenge. These possibilities do not make the underlying interventions physiologically equivalent. A result that shows improved glucose tolerance but does not measure the relevant fluxes leaves several possible explanations. This is a central skill in Part C questions: infer only what the measurement supports.

One of the difficulties in studying progression is that many candidate causes change together. In a population comparison, greater visceral adiposity, lower activity, altered circulating lipids and lower insulin sensitivity may coexist. Statistical adjustment can help account for measured variables, but unmeasured influences and reverse causality remain possible. For example, a reduction in activity may contribute to insulin resistance, while metabolic illness may itself reduce activity. A longitudinal study improves information about temporal order, and an experimental perturbation can test a particular causal mechanism. Neither design removes every limitation, so conclusions should identify the strength and scope of the evidence.

Variation in risk reflects the interaction of biological susceptibility with exposures. Genetic variants can alter secretion, adipose differentiation, hormone signalling or other processes; age-related changes can influence tissue composition and reserve; dietary and activity patterns can affect fuel delivery and demand. These factors do not produce the same outcome in every individual because the feedback network has variable capacity. The concept of reserve explains why a system can remain apparently stable until an additional challenge occurs. A pregnancy, change in body composition or a period of altered activity can reveal limited compensation without necessarily constituting the original cause of that limitation.

Metabolic flexibility refers to the ability to adjust substrate use in response to changes in fuel availability and demand. In healthy physiology, the transition from fasting to feeding generally involves a shift towards carbohydrate utilization, while fasting increases reliance on fat in many tissues. A blunted shift can accompany insulin resistance, but interpretation depends on the starting fuel mixture, energy demand and conditions of measurement. Respiratory exchange ratio offers information about whole-body substrate oxidation under appropriate steady-state conditions. It is not identical to a direct measure of glucose transport or insulin receptor activity. A muscle may take up glucose and store it without immediately oxidizing it. This distinction connects the preceding curriculum node to the disease mechanisms examined here.

The respiratory exchange ratio is the ratio of carbon dioxide production to oxygen consumption. For idealized complete oxidation of glucose, six oxygen molecules are consumed for six carbon dioxide molecules produced, giving a ratio of one. Oxidation of palmitate consumes twenty-three oxygen molecules for sixteen carbon dioxide molecules, giving approximately 0.70. A mixed substrate pattern can produce an intermediate value. During non-steady-state exercise, changes in ventilation and bicarbonate buffering can cause measured gas exchange to differ from cellular substrate oxidation; protein oxidation and other metabolic processes also complicate the interpretation. The ratio is therefore most useful when the assumptions of the measurement are satisfied.

An illustrative comparison helps connect substrate use and glucose control. Imagine two subjects with comparable resting energy expenditure and fasting respiratory exchange ratio. After a standardized insulin stimulus, the first shows increased glucose disposal and a rise in the ratio, while the second shows a smaller rise in glucose disposal and little change in the ratio. These observations are consistent with reduced ability to shift towards carbohydrate utilization in the second subject. But the ratio alone would not distinguish less glucose entry from greater storage of incoming glucose instead of oxidation. Combining measurements of uptake, oxidation and glycogen synthesis helps separate these possibilities. A change in fuel preference is a physiological finding whose molecular basis needs further evidence.

The term metabolic syndrome describes clustering of metabolic and cardiovascular risk-related traits rather than a single molecular disease. Abdominal adiposity, altered glucose regulation and abnormalities in lipids or blood pressure can arise through overlapping mechanisms, but they need not appear together in every case. A person may exhibit insulin resistance without meeting a particular syndrome definition. A syndrome criterion is a classification tool and should not be confused with a mechanistic requirement. The next node extends the current framework towards lipoprotein metabolism, fatty liver and cardiovascular consequences, so these topics are introduced here only to explain why interorgan dysfunction rarely affects glucose in isolation.

An effective integrative explanation returns to the normal physiological problem. Storage protects against excessive substrate exposure during a meal, mobilization supplies fuel during fasting, and hormonal feedback coordinates these functions across organs. Insulin resistance disrupts the relation between signal and response; beta-cell adaptation may compensate for a time; failure of adequate compensation allows the regulated glucose concentration to rise. Adipose tissue, liver, muscle, pancreas, gut, kidney and nervous system contribute different components of that outcome. At every stage, pool sizes, fluxes, concentrations, cellular signals and functional endpoints describe different aspects of the system. The ability to connect them without confusing them is the central conceptual achievement of this chapter.

Revision and mastery

Chapter summary and high-yield concepts

Obesity can change the capacity and regulation of adipose storage. In susceptible circumstances, altered substrate flux, lipid signalling and inflammatory pathways contribute to reduced insulin responses in muscle, liver and adipose tissue. Normal glucose can persist while beta cells compensate; type 2 diabetes develops when effective compensation becomes inadequate. The defining physiological distinction from typical autoimmune type 1 diabetes is the relative contribution of impaired action and secretory failure.

Prioritize receptor–IRS–PI3K–Akt signalling, GLUT4 trafficking, adipose lipolysis, hepatic glucose output, glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, disposition index and the steady-state glucose balance. Remember that concentrations, pool sizes and rates of flux answer different questions.

Key terminology

Insulin sensitivity
Response to a specified insulin exposure, endpoint and condition.
Ectopic lipid
Lipid storage in tissues not principally specialized for bulk energy storage.
Adipocyte hyperplasia
Increased adipocyte number, contrasted with hypertrophy.
Compensation
Adaptive increase in effective insulin delivery in response to reduced sensitivity.
Disposition index
Product of appropriate sensitivity and secretory-response indices in a model supporting that relationship.
Glucotoxicity and lipotoxicity
Context-dependent adverse effects of excessive glucose or lipid-related exposure.

Formula review

  • Energy balance: ΔE = intake − expenditure, over a specified interval.
  • Glucose pool: dQ/dt = appearance − metabolic disposal − urinary loss.
  • Steady-state clamp: disposal = glucose infusion + residual endogenous production (when urinary loss is negligible).
  • Disposition index = sensitivity × secretory response, with compatible index units.
  • Conventional HOMA-IR = fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μU/mL) / 22.5.
  • Net tissue exchange = flow × arteriovenous concentration difference, with compatible compartment and units.

Macrophages are always harmfulThey also clear debris and participate in repair; phenotype and context matter.Inflammation is often described only as a damaging state.Compare macrophage-mediated repair with persistent inflammatory signalling; distinguish context and duration.
Inflammatory association proves causalityPerturbations and matched controls are needed.Observational results often contain a plausible causal narrative.A knockout, rescue or pair-fed control is more informative about a causal contribution than correlation alone.
Fasting glucose measures muscle resistanceIt integrates production, disposal and endocrine responses.The glucose concentration is an integrated, accessible measurement.Use matched conditions and tissue-specific tracer endpoints to distinguish production and disposal.
Clamp infusion always equals disposalResidual endogenous production must be considered.The familiar clamp formula assumes near-complete suppression of endogenous production.When a question gives residual production, include it in the steady-state balance.
