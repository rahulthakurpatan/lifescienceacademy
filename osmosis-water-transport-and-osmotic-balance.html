1. Why cells must control water

A red blood cell placed in a sufficiently dilute salt solution may become spherical and rupture, while a plant cell in the same kind of environment often becomes firm. Neither cell has synthesized a new membrane in response to the solution. Both are surrounded by a membrane that allows water to cross, and both contain dissolved substances that influence water movement. The striking difference is that the plant cell has an additional mechanical boundary. Its wall can support pressure as water enters. The animal cell has no comparable rigid wall, so the same thermodynamic tendency can produce a very different outcome.

Water is the solvent in which many of the reactions of life occur. Losing water concentrates ions, metabolites and proteins, changes the distance between macromolecules and can disturb their interactions. Excess water dilutes these components and may mechanically stress the membrane. A change of cell volume is therefore not merely a change in appearance. It can change the conditions under which enzymes, transporters and structural proteins function. To understand how cells maintain an internal environment, one must distinguish the motion of water from the movement of substances dissolved in it.

Recall that thermal motion causes molecules to move in many directions. A membrane does not make molecules stop moving; it changes the probability of their crossing. In simple diffusion a permeable solute redistributes until its chemical potential is balanced. Water also moves spontaneously, but its chemical potential depends on the composition and pressure of the solution. The key observation is that a selectively permeable membrane permits the solvent to redistribute even when some of the solutes cannot redistribute. A solute gradient is thus converted into a water flux and, in suitable circumstances, a mechanical force.

Three quantities must be kept distinct throughout this chapter. The amount of dissolved particles describes an osmotic property of a solution. The permeability of a particular membrane determines which of those particles can produce a sustained osmotic imbalance. The mechanical properties of the enclosing cell determine how a change in water content is converted into pressure or volume. An examination problem may give information about only one of these quantities and ask the student to recognize that the others are still required.

Our starting assumption will be two aqueous compartments at a common temperature. A membrane permits water passage and may exclude or admit various solutes. We will first establish the equilibrium condition, then calculate the magnitude of the forces, and finally investigate what happens in a real cell that has transport proteins, walls or the ability to regulate its solute content.

2. Chemical potential: the thermodynamic reason for osmosis

Concentration is an amount per volume, but it is not itself a complete measure of the tendency of a substance to move. A molecule in a pressurized compartment, for example, can have a different thermodynamic tendency from a molecule at the same concentration in an unpressurized compartment. The relevant quantity is the chemical potential, μ, which is the change in Gibbs free energy per mole of a component added to a system at constant temperature, pressure and amounts of the other components. It has units of joules per mole.

Imagine transferring a small amount dn of water from compartment A to compartment B while the temperature is fixed. To first order the total free-energy change is dG = μw,Bdn − μw,Adn. If μw,A is higher, this transfer lowers G. Passive net water movement therefore has a definite direction even though individual water molecules cross randomly both ways. At equilibrium the two water chemical potentials are equal. It is unnecessary for the two solute concentrations to become equal if a solute cannot cross.

At a reference pressure P0 and temperature T, the chemical potential of pure liquid water is μw*. For a solution with water activity aw, an appropriate expression is

μw(T,P,aw) = μw*(T,P0) + RT ln aw + ∫P₀P V̄w dP.

R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature and V̄w is the partial molar volume of water. For modest pressure differences the volume can be treated as constant, making the pressure term approximately V̄w(P − P0). The activity term accounts for composition, including the effects of interactions in a nonideal solution. Pure water at the reference state has activity one; in ordinary aqueous solutions containing dissolved nonvolatile solutes its activity is below one. Because ln of a number below one is negative, mixing solute with water lowers the water chemical potential at the same pressure.

The lowering is not adequately explained by saying that solute ties up all the nearby water. Hydration certainly changes molecular interactions, but even an ideal mixture with no special solute–water attraction has a lower solvent chemical potential. Mixing increases the number of possible arrangements. In an ideal solution the activity of water is its mole fraction. In an actual cell, hydration, electrostatic interactions and crowding can change the quantitative relation between mole fraction and activity. The activity formulation retains the underlying principle without requiring a literal division into free and bound water molecules.

Pressure has the opposite effect. Adding liquid to a compartment at higher pressure requires mechanical work; positive pressure raises μw. This explains why a solution containing more dissolved particles can nevertheless lose water to a dilute solution if it is sufficiently pressurized. The familiar rule that water moves toward the side with more solute is valid only when pressure differences are negligible and solute permeability is appropriately restricted.

Consider two sides with water activities 0.995 and 0.990 at the same temperature and pressure. The logarithm of the ratio 0.990/0.995 is negative, so moving water from the first to the second reduces its chemical potential. Now raise the pressure on the second side. At some pressure the positive pressure term cancels the activity difference. Net flow ceases. Increase pressure further and the direction reverses. This single argument predicts ordinary osmosis, osmotic equilibrium and reverse osmosis without requiring three unrelated mechanisms.

3. Semipermeability and the classical osmometer

A useful experimental system is a U-shaped tube divided by a membrane through which water can pass but sucrose cannot. Initially the liquid levels are equal. Place pure water in the left arm and a sucrose solution in the right. Water molecules cross in both directions, but net transfer is toward the sucrose side because the water chemical potential is lower there at equal pressure. The sucrose molecules remain largely confined to their compartment. In a real experiment the membrane must retain the solute over the period of observation; leakage would gradually weaken the difference.

As water enters the right compartment, its liquid level rises and its sucrose solution becomes more dilute. The opposite arm loses water. The height difference creates a hydrostatic pressure difference across the membrane. Both dilution of the solution and increasing pressure oppose further transfer. Ultimately the chemical-potential difference becomes zero, although sucrose remains on one side. In the equilibrium state molecular exchange continues in both directions. A stationary liquid column is evidence for balanced flux, not evidence that the membrane has become impermeable.

Early quantitative studies of osmosis used artificial semipermeable barriers, including the precipitated membranes associated with Wilhelm Pfeffer's osmotic measurements. The important experimental advance was the ability to measure the pressure required to balance a solute difference rather than infer water movement solely from swelling. Van't Hoff subsequently related dilute-solution osmotic pressure to particle concentration and absolute temperature. The modern molecular interpretation accounts for this relationship through the chemical potential of the solvent. Historical and physical context.

Osmotic pressure π is conventionally defined as the excess pressure that must be applied to a solution to prevent net water entry from pure solvent across an ideal semipermeable membrane at the specified temperature. It is a positive quantity. If two solutions are separated rather than pure water and a solution, it is their osmotic-pressure difference that is relevant under dilute ideal conditions. If a piston applies exactly the balancing pressure to the more concentrated solution, there is no net flow. A greater pressure forces water toward the dilute compartment, leaving the rejected solute behind. This is the principle of reverse osmosis, but the pressure actually required in a real device depends on the concentration, membrane rejection and flow resistance.

The ideal osmometer is different from a living cell. An animal cell cannot generally sustain a large hydrostatic pressure across its flexible plasma membrane without deformation or rupture. A plant cell wall can oppose expansion, allowing pressure to build. A laboratory membrane may also reject a particular solute much more completely than a plasma membrane. Before transferring a conclusion from the osmometer to the cell, decide whether the barrier, pressure constraint and timescale are comparable.

4. Counting particles: molarity, molality, osmolarity and osmolality

Osmotic properties are colligative in the ideal dilute limit: the number of independent dissolved particles matters, rather than their mass or identity alone. One mole of glucose molecules yields approximately one mole of osmotically active particles. One mole of fully dissociated sodium chloride yields one mole of sodium ions and one mole of chloride ions. Although the solution is electrically neutral overall, both ion species contribute to the particle count. One mole of calcium chloride yields approximately three moles of particles if dissociation is complete.

Molarity M counts moles of a specified solute per litre of solution. Molality m counts moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. Osmolarity counts osmoles of dissolved particles per litre of solution, while osmolality counts osmoles per kilogram of solvent. A milliosmole is one thousandth of an osmole. The distinction between litre of solution and kilogram of solvent is important: addition of solute can change solution volume, and volume changes with temperature, whereas the mass of solvent in a closed sample is invariant. Osmolality is often measured in physiology using freezing-point depression; osmolarity is convenient for transport equations that use volume concentrations.

In dilute aqueous solutions, one litre of solution contains approximately one kilogram of water, so the numerical values of osmolarity in Osm/L and osmolality in Osm/kg are often close. This approximation is not a universal conversion factor. If a sample has 0.200 osmoles of particles in 0.800 kg of water and its measured volume is 0.900 L, its osmolality is 0.250 Osm/kg whereas its osmolarity is 0.222 Osm/L. No change of nomenclature can make the denominators equal.

The ideal van't Hoff factor i accounts for the number of independent particles per formula unit. For a mixture, ideal osmolarity Cosm = Σ ijCj. Thus 100 mM NaCl and 50 mM glucose yield approximately 200 + 50 = 250 mOsm/L, assuming complete salt dissociation. At appreciable concentration, ionic interactions make the effective osmotic contribution depart from the ideal count. An osmotic coefficient φ may be introduced to describe this departure. It is not correct to assign the integer factor two to a measured NaCl osmolality with exact precision under every condition.

Particle counting also explains why a large macromolecule produces less ideal osmotic pressure per gram than a small molecule of equal total mass. One gram of a 100,000 g/mol protein represents 0.00001 mol, whereas a gram of a 100 g/mol solute represents 0.01 mol. This does not make intracellular macromolecules biologically irrelevant: their exclusion from the membrane and their charge influence cellular osmotic balance, including the distribution of small counterions. Nevertheless the direct ideal particle contribution must be calculated from molecule number rather than mass.

For a practical calculation, prepare 5.844 g of NaCl in a final volume of one litre. With a molar mass of 58.44 g/mol, this is 0.100 M NaCl. Its ideal osmolarity is 0.200 Osm/L. Adding 18.016 g glucose and adjusting the final volume back to one litre adds another 0.100 Osm/L. The resulting ideal total is 0.300 Osm/L. If the final volume instead became larger, both concentrations would need to be recalculated. This is why exam questions distinguish final solution volume from solvent volume.

5. Deriving the van't Hoff equation and calculating osmotic pressure

Take pure water at pressure P0 on one side of an ideal membrane and a solution at P0 + π on the other. At osmotic equilibrium, the chemical potentials of water must be identical. Using the constant partial molar volume approximation, the pure-water reference terms cancel, leaving 0 = RT ln aw + V̄wπ. Rearranging gives π = −(RT/V̄w) ln aw. The sign is positive for a solution with water activity below one. This activity-based expression is more general than the dilute concentration formula, within its pressure approximation.

For an ideal solution, aw = xw. Write xw = 1 − xs, where xs denotes the total mole fraction of independent solute particles. When the solution is dilute, ln(1 − xs) ≈ −xs. Also xs ≈ ns/nw, since the number of water molecules is much larger than that of solute particles. Finally, the liquid volume V ≈ nwV̄w. Combining these three steps yields π ≈ RT ns/V = RT Cosm.

π = iCRT   (single solute);   π ≈ RT Σ ijCj   (ideal dilute mixture).

Here C is formula-unit molarity, i is the ideal or specified effective particle factor and T must be in kelvin. The derivation shows why the equation resembles the ideal gas equation: both express an ideal dilute particle-number dependence. Dissolved molecules are not a gas inside the liquid, and the origin of the osmotic pressure is the free-energy difference of water. The formula assumes a dilute solution, approximate ideal behavior and a membrane retaining the counted solutes for the pressure measurement. A partially permeable barrier requires a distinction between osmotic pressure of the solution and effective osmotic driving pressure across that barrier.

Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ with concentration in mol/m³ to obtain pascals, because a joule per cubic metre is a pascal. A solution containing 0.100 M sucrose at 25 °C contains 100 mol of osmotic particles per cubic metre. Then π = 8.314 × 298.15 × 100 = 247,882 Pa ≈ 0.248 MPa. Alternatively, use R = 0.082057 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, concentration in mol/L and obtain 2.45 atm. The two values agree because 1 atm = 101,325 Pa. Substitution of 0.100 directly into the SI version without converting litres to cubic metres would be wrong by a factor of one thousand.

Now compare 0.150 M NaCl with 0.300 M sucrose at 37 °C. The ideal NaCl particle concentration is 0.300 Osm/L, equal to the sucrose value. The ideal osmotic pressure of either against pure water is 8.314 × 310.15 × 300 = 773,576 Pa, about 0.774 MPa. The pressure difference between them is zero in this ideal calculation when both solutes are retained. However, an actual membrane may distinguish ions and sugars; a conclusion about cell volume requires permeability information. Nonideal electrolyte behavior can also make their measured osmotic pressures unequal.

Temperature matters quantitatively even without changing concentration. At fixed ideal C, π is proportional to absolute T. Raising temperature from 300 K to 330 K increases π by ten percent, not by a factor calculated from the Celsius temperature. In contrast, membrane permeability may change differently with temperature, so a ten percent increase in equilibrium balancing pressure does not predict a ten percent increase in cellular swelling rate. Thermodynamic driving force and transport kinetics are distinct quantities.

Suppose that two large reservoirs at 300 K contain retained solutes at 0.20 and 0.30 Osm/L. Their osmotic-pressure difference is RT(100 mol/m³) ≈ 0.249 MPa. If the more concentrated side is held at an excess pressure of 0.150 MPa, its pressure is insufficient to compensate, and net water enters that side. If the applied excess pressure is 0.300 MPa, the concentrated side has the higher water chemical potential and net water leaves. These results presume the reservoir concentrations remain fixed; in finite compartments dilution alters the difference during the experiment.

6. Tonicity is a property of a solution relative to a membrane

One of the most frequent mistakes in cell biology is to use isotonic and iso-osmotic as interchangeable words. Iso-osmotic solutions have equal total osmotic particle concentration (or equal osmotic pressure under the specified ideal comparison). Isotonic means that the external solution does not cause a sustained osmotic change in the volume of the particular cell being considered under the stated conditions. The former compares solutions; the latter requires knowledge of which solutes are effectively retained and the cell's mechanical and regulatory properties.

Consider a model animal cell initially containing 300 mOsm/L of retained solutes. If it is transferred to a very large reservoir containing 300 mOsm/L sucrose and the membrane retains sucrose as well as the internal particles, the solution is both iso-osmotic and isotonic in the simplified model. At equal pressure there is no sustained change in volume. Water crosses in both directions. If the reservoir instead contains 200 mOsm/L of retained particles, water enters; if it contains 400 mOsm/L, water leaves. Such solutions are described as hypotonic and hypertonic, respectively, relative to the original cell.

Change the external solute to urea and suppose that the membrane is permeable to urea but retains the original intracellular material. A 300 mOsm/L urea solution is initially iso-osmotic with the cell, but it is not isotonic in the long-term model. Urea enters down its own chemical-potential gradient. As the internal urea concentration increases, intracellular water activity falls relative to the outside. Water enters and the cell expands. Eventually the urea concentrations on both sides would be equal if the cell survived and pressure differences were negligible. At that stage urea could not balance the contribution of the retained intracellular solutes because it would contribute equally on both sides. With no external retained solutes there is no finite ideal volume equilibrium. A red blood cell may lyse before reaching such a hypothetical state.

There are two reasons for specifying the experimental time. First, urea does not instantly equilibrate in all cells; transport proteins and membrane composition determine how rapidly it crosses. Second, living cells can change the intracellular amount of solute over time. A solute that behaves as impermeant during a brief measurement may enter slowly during a prolonged incubation. Thus a statement about tonicity must specify the relevant membrane and timescale, rather than assign a permanent label to every solution containing a given chemical.

It is possible for a solution with greater total osmolarity than the cell to be hypotonic in the long run. For instance, a mixture containing 150 mOsm/L retained sucrose and 250 mOsm/L permeating urea has total osmolarity 400 mOsm/L. If water moves much faster than urea initially, the cell may first shrink because the external total concentration is greater. Later urea enters, the transient difference due to urea diminishes and water enters. The eventual equilibrium volume is determined by the 150 mOsm/L retained external solute, so the cell tends to expand beyond its starting volume. A single observation immediately after transfer would not predict the final behavior.

Conversely an external solution with 300 mOsm/L retained solute plus 100 mOsm/L permeating urea is hyperosmotic but, after urea equilibration, isotonic in the ideal fixed-retained-solute model. It can cause an initial shrinkage followed by recovery. A solution containing only 150 mOsm/L retained solutes cannot become hypertonic merely because a rapidly equilibrating solute adds a large osmolar contribution. To reason correctly, first count all particles for osmolarity, then evaluate their effectiveness in maintaining an osmotic gradient across the particular barrier.

One should also distinguish solute entry from solvent entry. When a penetrating substance enters, its own concentration tends to equalize, but that does not necessarily lead to equal total particle counts at unchanged cell volume. The retained intracellular molecules remain present and are diluted as water enters. Conservation of these retained particles is the basis for the quantitative volume model introduced next.

7. Predicting cell volume: conservation of retained solute

Suppose an animal cell is in a large bath and contains N osmoles of retained intracellular solute. Assume the cell is idealized as a flexible compartment at negligible pressure difference, there is no solute regulation or lysis, and its entire starting volume V0 is osmotically accessible water volume. Let its initial concentration be C0 = N/V0. If it is placed in a bath containing only retained solutes at concentration Ce, water enters or leaves until N/Vf = Ce. The final water volume follows directly from conservation.

Vf/V0 = C0/Ce.

Thus a cell initially at 300 mOsm/L in a 250 mOsm/L nonpenetrating solution would reach 300/250 = 1.20 times its initial volume under the model. In 450 mOsm/L it would reach 0.667 of its initial volume. The prediction is inverse, not linear with extracellular osmolarity: lowering the external concentration from 300 to 200 produces a 50% volume increase, whereas raising it from 300 to 400 produces a 25% decrease. Equal changes of concentration in opposite directions are not equal changes of volume.

Real cells contain a volume occupied by solids and water that does not behave as a freely exchanging osmotic solvent. Denote an idealized osmotically inactive volume by b. The accessible volume initially is V0 − b. If the retained solute concentration in this accessible volume is C0, then N = C0(V0 − b), and at equilibrium Vf = b + (V0 − b)C0/Ce. This is a simplified Boyle–van't Hoff volume relation. Its graphical form predicts a straight line when final total cell volume is plotted against reciprocal external concentration, with intercept b. The line is useful only in a range where solute amount and mechanical conditions remain approximately constant.

For example, a cell with total initial volume 100 fL and inactive volume 30 fL contains 70 fL of accessible water at 300 mOsm/L. In 200 mOsm/L retained external solute, the water volume becomes 70 × 300/200 = 105 fL, and total volume becomes 135 fL. Without the correction one would predict 150 fL. A measurement below that simple prediction does not necessarily prove water cannot cross; inactive volume and mechanical constraints must be considered.

A finite external bath introduces another change. In a large reservoir it is legitimate to treat the external concentration as constant, but in a small closed chamber water entering the cell removes volume from the bath and concentrates its retained solutes. If the two compartments have retained particle amounts Ni and Ne, total accessible water volume VT, and negligible pressure difference, equilibrium requires Ni/Vi = Ne/(VT − Vi). Multiplying and collecting terms gives Vi = VTNi/(Ni + Ne). For equal starting volumes of 1 arbitrary unit with 300 and 100 mOsm/L respectively, the cell ends with 2 × 300/400 = 1.5 volume units, rather than the threefold expansion predicted for an infinite 100 mOsm/L bath.

All such calculations are predictions of a specified physical model, not guarantees of biological survival. If the calculated final volume exceeds the maximum volume that the membrane can contain, the cell may rupture before reaching it. If regulatory transport alters N, the model must be updated. If the wall supports pressure, concentrations need not be equal at equilibrium. The value of a simple equation lies as much in its assumptions as in its numerical result.

8. Animal cells: swelling, crenation and hemolysis

Animal plasma membranes form a flexible boundary with limited capacity for expansion of the lipid bilayer itself. The red blood cell is a particularly useful illustration. Its normal biconcave form provides a relatively large surface area for its volume. On water entry, the disc becomes less concave and approaches a sphere. This shape change can accommodate some increase of volume without a comparable increase of membrane area. After the shape reserve is exhausted, continued water influx raises membrane tension and may rupture the cell. Rupture of erythrocytes releases hemoglobin into the surrounding solution and is called hemolysis.

In a hypertonic solution of effectively nonpenetrating solutes, water leaves the red cell and the cell shrinks. Its membrane and underlying cytoskeleton reorganize into irregular shapes, often described as crenation. Crenation is not equivalent to hemolysis. The former is a loss of water and change in shape; the latter is disruption of membrane integrity. In a hypotonic solution a cell can swell without lysing if the osmotic challenge remains within its mechanical tolerance or if regulation restores its volume in time.

A red-cell suspension offers a simple connection between thermodynamics and observation. A dilute retained salt solution lowers the osmotic driving pressure outside relative to inside. Water influx changes the cell geometry, and sufficiently severe swelling releases hemoglobin. The amount of hemoglobin detected in the extracellular fluid can therefore serve as a measure of hemolysis. However, the measured amount is not identical to the water permeability of the intact membrane. A fragile cell can release hemoglobin earlier than a mechanically stronger one even if their initial water permeability is similar.

The familiar physiological saline example is a useful approximate calculation: 0.9% mass/volume NaCl corresponds to 9 g per litre of solution. Dividing by 58.44 gives approximately 0.154 mol/L, and ideal full dissociation gives 0.308 Osm/L. Its measured osmotic behavior differs from that ideal value owing to ionic interactions. Its broadly isotonic behavior toward human erythrocytes is related to the effective retention of extracellular salt across the relevant short-term membrane conditions, not simply to its numerical molarity. This is an educational example, not a prescription for treating fluid disorders.

Animal cells also differ from one another. Cell size, membrane reserve, cytoskeletal support and water channels change the speed and degree of swelling. Nucleated cells frequently possess mechanisms for regulatory volume decrease or increase; mature erythrocytes have their own restricted transport capacities. Consequently one cannot assume that all mammalian cells behave quantitatively like a simple lipid vesicle or like erythrocytes, even when exposed to the same bath. The physical principles remain shared, while the parameters of the system vary.

9. Plant water potential: expressing chemical potential in pressure units

A plant cell is a different mechanical system because the plasma membrane is surrounded by a relatively strong wall. Its central vacuole often occupies a large fraction of its volume, with water crossing both the plasma membrane and the tonoplast. Solute in the cytosol and vacuole lowers water activity. Water entering these compartments expands the protoplast, which presses against the wall. The wall resists expansion, raising hydrostatic pressure. This makes it especially useful to express chemical potential in pressure units, as water potential Ψw.

Define Ψw = (μw − μw*)/V̄w at a specified common temperature and reference state, with pure water at reference pressure and elevation assigned zero. For ordinary cell-scale comparisons, the constant-volume approximation gives Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. The solute potential Ψs = (RT/V̄w)ln aw is zero for pure water and negative for typical aqueous solute-containing solutions. In the ideal dilute approximation Ψs = −iCRT. Pressure potential Ψp = P − P0 can be positive for turgid cells, zero at atmospheric gauge pressure, or negative in a liquid under tension. Thus osmotic pressure π is positive whereas ideal solute potential is its negative. They must not be added with the same sign.

At broader spatial scales, water potential can also include gravitational potential Ψg = ρgh relative to a reference height, and a matric term Ψm describing effects of adsorption and capillarity in suitable systems. With ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³ and g ≈ 9.81 m/s², one metre of elevation contributes approximately 0.00981 MPa. Across a single cell this is normally negligible. Matrix interactions may be significant in dry cell walls, seeds or soil, but in a simple hydrated cell problem they are often neglected or already represented by the chosen water-activity term. Avoid counting an interaction twice when defining the components.

Net water flow is from higher total water potential to lower total water potential, provided a path for water exists. Higher means numerically greater: −0.2 MPa is higher than −0.8 MPa. For example a cell has solute potential −0.8 MPa and pressure potential +0.5 MPa, giving Ψw = −0.3 MPa. An external solution at atmospheric pressure with Ψs = −0.4 MPa has total potential −0.4 MPa. Water initially leaves the cell, despite its internal solute potential being more negative. Comparing solute potentials alone would give the wrong answer because turgor has raised the internal water potential.

As water leaves a living cell, both solute concentration and wall pressure can change. Concentration increases as accessible water volume falls if solute is retained, making solute potential more negative. Turgor generally decreases as the wall and protoplast relax. Both effects tend to lower cell water potential until the difference is reduced. A final equilibrium calculation that treats solute potential as fixed despite a large volume change may therefore be invalid unless the question explicitly provides the final solute concentration or assumes a controlled constant value.

Consider a cell with final Ψs = −0.7 MPa equilibrated with a large external solution of Ψw = −0.2 MPa. Equality of water potentials implies −0.7 + Ψp = −0.2, so Ψp = +0.5 MPa. This is a positive hydrostatic pressure relative to the reference, supported by the wall. It is not a pressure generated by pumping water molecules using ATP. Solute accumulation may have required metabolism, but water entry itself followed a gradient of chemical potential.

A useful bridge to older examination terminology is the diffusion pressure deficit, sometimes expressed as osmotic pressure minus turgor pressure under simplified conditions. In the same reference convention this corresponds to −Ψw for the usual cell-scale model with Ψs = −π. Modern water-potential terminology is more general because pressure, gravity and matrix effects can all be treated consistently. Do not interpret the older phrase as an independent physical force in addition to water potential.

10. Turgor, flaccidity, plasmolysis and recovery

Follow a mature plant cell through a sequence of external environments. In a dilute medium, water tends to enter, the vacuole expands and the protoplast presses outward against the wall. The wall stretches to a limited extent and exerts an opposing stress. Positive turgor is established. Equilibrium occurs when the pressure contribution balances the difference in solvent activity. The cell can be turgid even while the external solution has a lower solute concentration than its cytoplasm. Turgor provides mechanical support to many nonwoody tissues and contributes to the force available for growth when wall properties permit expansion.

If the surrounding medium becomes more concentrated, water may leave the cell, decreasing its internal volume and turgor. At a state of zero turgor, the protoplast is no longer exerting significant positive pressure against the wall. The cell is flaccid. In the simple model, incipient plasmolysis is the point at which the protoplast begins to separate from the wall and pressure potential is approximately zero. Further water loss leads to visible plasmolysis: the protoplast withdraws from the cell wall while the wall itself largely retains its external outline. Adhesions can cause the retraction to be spatially uneven, so the appearance is not always a perfectly uniform gap.

The wall is not the selectively permeable barrier responsible for this process. Water and many small solutes can move through its hydrated porous matrix. The plasma membrane retains much of the intracellular solute, so the osmotic difference acts across it. When the protoplast contracts, the space between plasma membrane and wall contains external solution accessible through the wall. It is not an empty vacuum. The tonoplast participates in vacuolar water exchange, and the relative sizes of the intracellular compartments can change during the response.

If the plasmolyzed cell remains viable and is returned to a medium of sufficiently high water potential, water re-enters, the protoplast expands and contact with the wall can be restored. This reversal is called deplasmolysis. Severe or prolonged exposure may damage membranes or other cellular structures, preventing complete recovery. Also, if the osmotic solute enters the cell gradually, apparent deplasmolysis may occur even without replacing the external solution, because penetration alters the internal water potential. This is an important experimental distinction between a reversible osmotic response and an inference that a membrane has suddenly become water impermeable.

The incipient-plasmolysis method provides an approximate estimate of cellular solute potential. Samples of epidermal cells can be exposed to a series of concentrations of a solute that is retained during the observation period. Around the concentration at which cells begin to plasmolyze, turgor is near zero, and after approximate equilibrium Ψs,cell ≈ Ψw,bath. If the bath is 0.30 M sucrose at 298 K, its ideal Ψs is −8.314 × 298 × 300 Pa = −0.743 MPa. The estimate refers to the cell at incipient plasmolysis, not necessarily its original turgid state, because water loss has concentrated its solutes.

Real tissue contains cells with different solute contents, sizes and wall mechanics. A practical population measurement may identify the concentration at which approximately half the observed cells show initial plasmolysis rather than assign every cell the same threshold. Observation time, solute penetration, tissue injury and adhesion affect the estimate. These limitations do not invalidate the underlying principle; they tell us what the measured number represents. A graph of fraction plasmolyzed against concentration can therefore be more informative than a single microscope photograph.

11. Aquaporins: how water can cross a hydrophobic membrane rapidly

Water is a small polar molecule. It can cross a lipid bilayer even without a specialized transport protein, but the hydrophobic interior presents an energetic barrier. Different membranes therefore have different basal water permeabilities. The observation that some cells move water far more rapidly than a simple bilayer suggested the presence of specialized pathways. A water channel would solve a kinetic problem: it could provide a favorable route across the membrane without altering the equilibrium condition established by water chemical potential.

Aquaporins are integral membrane proteins that provide narrow pathways for water. In typical AQP1-like proteins, four subunits associate in a tetramer, with each monomer containing its own water-conducting pore. A monomer has six major membrane-spanning helices and two shorter reentrant helical regions bearing conserved NPA motifs (asparagine–proline–alanine). The two reentrant regions approach one another within the membrane. A narrow aromatic/arginine selectivity region and interactions around the central region constrain the molecules passing through the pore. Water is able to traverse this hydrophilic pathway without exposing its polar surface to the entire hydrocarbon core of the membrane.

The channel must also preserve ion gradients. If a continuous hydrogen-bonded water chain freely passed protons across the membrane, it would undermine energy-transducing and electrical gradients. The geometry, electrostatic environment and orientation of water molecules within typical aquaporin pores help interrupt proton transfer, while the narrow selectivity filter excludes most larger hydrated solutes. Water channel selectivity therefore depends on both size and molecular interactions, not simply on the existence of a hole. Some relatives, called aquaglyceroporins, also conduct glycerol and other small neutral molecules. It is not correct to claim that every member of the aquaporin family transports water exclusively.

Passive water channels do not hydrolyze ATP to force water through the membrane. They permit bidirectional water exchange, with the net direction determined by the chemical-potential difference. Increasing channel abundance can greatly increase the rate of swelling in a hypotonic medium and the rate of shrinkage in a hypertonic medium. But if two identical sealed vesicles contain the same retained solute amount and reach equilibrium with the same bath under the same mechanical conditions, introducing a water-selective channel changes the time required, not their final equilibrium volume. This rate-versus-endpoint distinction is central to experimental reasoning.

Channel availability may itself be regulated. Plants can alter water permeability through changes in aquaporin abundance or gating in plasma membranes and tonoplasts. In animals, the controlled insertion of AQP2 into a membrane is a well-known example of regulation of water permeability. The hormonal and whole-organ aspects of that example belong to animal physiology; here the essential point is that a cell can regulate the conductance of the passive route without directly supplying energy to each transported water molecule.

12. Experimental evidence for water channels: from observation to causation

Rapid water exchange alone does not identify a specific protein. To establish a causal role, an experiment must change the presence of a candidate while holding other relevant conditions as similar as possible. In 1992 Preston, Carroll, Guggino and Agre reported experiments using Xenopus oocytes expressing a red-cell membrane protein then known as CHIP28, now associated with AQP1. Oocytes receiving RNA encoding the candidate protein were compared with control oocytes. On transfer to a dilute medium, the expressing cells exhibited a much greater osmotic water permeability. The study provided evidence that expression of this protein was sufficient to confer a rapid water pathway in the experimental cell membrane.

Why does this design work? The externally imposed osmotic gradient supplies the thermodynamic driving force to both sets of oocytes. If the initial cell geometry and solution conditions are comparable, a difference in the early swelling rate reflects a difference in the ability of water to cross the membrane. A protein that merely caused the cell to accumulate an unrelated internal osmolyte could also alter swelling, so experimental controls, solute-selectivity evidence and later structural information strengthen the interpretation. The original paper additionally reported reversible inhibition of the enhanced water permeability by mercuric chloride. This pharmacological observation supported the distinction from basal water permeability but alone would not identify a specific channel, because chemical inhibitors can have multiple effects.

In a simplified teaching experiment, compare two groups of spherical cells exposed to the same osmotic step. At 0 seconds both have relative volume 1.00. At 10 seconds the control group has 1.03 and the aquaporin-expressing group has 1.12. At 20 seconds the corresponding values are 1.06 and 1.22. After sufficient time both approach 1.25 without rupture. The data are illustrative, not a reproduction of the published measurements. The early increase in volume for the expressing group is about four times the control over the first ten seconds; the common endpoint suggests that the retained solute content and final equilibrium conditions are comparable. A larger early slope is evidence for faster net water transport, not for a greater final osmotic force.

To compare permeabilities quantitatively, volume change should be normalized to the membrane area and driving gradient. Two cells with different sizes can have different fractional swelling rates even if membrane permeability per unit area is identical. For a sphere the surface-area-to-volume ratio is 3/r, so a smaller sphere has a larger area relative to its starting volume. Likewise, comparing raw swelling rates at different temperatures or osmotic differences confounds transport properties with driving forces. A well-designed experiment uses matched initial geometry, identical baths, verified membrane integrity and appropriate control expression conditions.

Additional controls can distinguish transport from structural failure. A sudden release of intracellular dye or hemoglobin indicates membrane disruption, not simply enhanced water permeability. Recovery when the osmotic gradient is reversed supports a reversible water movement mechanism. A water-channel mutation that lowers swelling rate while leaving protein expression and localization intact supports a functional contribution of the altered region, whereas a mutation preventing trafficking to the membrane does not by itself reveal how the pore conducts water. The general logic is to connect the specific manipulated variable to the physical quantity being measured.

13. Water flux, hydraulic permeability and the reflection coefficient

Osmotic pressure describes a balancing force; it does not specify how rapidly water moves. Let Jv be the volume flux per unit membrane area, with units m³/(m²·s) = m/s. Define positive flux from inside to outside. Let ΔP = Pin − Pout and ΔCj = Cj,in − Cj,out. In a dilute, near-linear phenomenological description, water flux can be written as

Jv = Lp[ΔP − RT Σ σjΔCj].

Lp is hydraulic permeability (m Pa⁻¹ s⁻¹); multiplying by pressure in pascals gives a velocity. The reflection coefficient σ is dimensionless and, in the usual simple passive-membrane treatment, ranges from zero to one. A completely retained solute has σ close to one, so its concentration difference exerts the full ideal osmotic effect. A freely transmitted solute in the zero-reflection idealization has σ near zero, giving little direct contribution to water flux in this representation. Intermediate values describe partial coupling. The reflection coefficient is not simply the fraction of molecules that cannot cross in a fixed time; it is a transport parameter defined for the barrier and solute.

Take a cell whose internal effective solute concentration exceeds the external one by 50 mOsm/L at 300 K, with no pressure difference and σ = 1. Since 50 mOsm/L equals 50 mol/m³ of particles, the osmotic-pressure difference is 8.314 × 300 × 50 = 124,710 Pa. With Lp = 2 × 10⁻¹³ m Pa⁻¹ s⁻¹, Jv = −2.4942 × 10⁻⁸ m/s. The negative sign means flow is inward according to our chosen convention. If the area is 1000 μm², or 10⁻⁹ m², the initial rate of volume gain is 2.4942 × 10⁻¹⁷ m³/s, about 24.9 fL/s. This is an initial-rate calculation: concentration and area may change as the cell swells.

Now compare two solute gradients each 100 mol/m³ with reflection coefficients 1 and 0.25. At 300 K the effective osmotic contributions are 249,420 Pa and 62,355 Pa, respectively. The two baths may have equal osmolarity but produce different measured water flux across that membrane. If the solute with σ = 0.25 also permeates and gradually equilibrates, its concentration difference diminishes. The instantaneous flux formula and the long-term tonicity analysis are complementary, not contradictory: one describes coupled transport at a given state; the other asks how that state changes with time.

A related permeability used in swelling experiments is the osmotic water permeability Pf, in length per time. For a retained solute and negligible ΔP, an ideal relation is Jv = PfV̄w(Cout − Cin) for the positive outward convention. With V̄w ≈ 18 × 10⁻⁶ m³/mol and concentrations in mol/m³, the product of molar volume and concentration is dimensionless. The magnitude of the initial fractional volume-change rate is approximately (A/V)PfV̄w|ΔC|. If the membrane area-to-volume ratio doubles while Pf and ΔC remain fixed, the fractional rate doubles. The molecular mechanism of flow is not inferred from this equation alone; it requires independent evidence about the membrane.

These equations apply to well-defined dilute and approximately uniform compartments with stated permeability assumptions. Unstirred layers, solute transport, changes in membrane area and nonlinear mechanics can cause departure from the simple linear predictions. The sign convention is not universal across textbooks, so always define the positive direction before inserting a negative pressure or concentration difference. The physical direction must agree with the water chemical-potential argument.

14. Osmotic adjustment and regulation of cell volume

Passive osmotic equilibrium is not the same as a regulated steady state. A living cell uses transport and metabolism to maintain concentrations that would not necessarily persist in an isolated equilibrating bag. Intracellular proteins and other macromolecules are largely retained and many carry fixed negative charges. Small ions can redistribute through selective pathways, and the retained charges influence their distribution. A purely passive Donnan-like system can accumulate excess osmotically active ions on the side with retained charges, favoring water entry. In an animal cell without a rigid wall, such a tendency is countered by controlled solute transport and mechanical limits.

The sodium–potassium ATPase is a particularly important component of animal cell ionic homeostasis. Its action maintains sodium and potassium gradients and helps support the transport systems that regulate net intracellular solute content. Although the pump itself does not transport water, disrupting ion gradients can cause progressive osmotic swelling. The full stoichiometry, energetics and coupled transport mechanisms will be studied in the active-transport node. For current purposes, the distinction is that ATP-dependent solute movement can alter the passive driving force for water.

When many animal cells are suddenly exposed to a hypotonic bath, they first swell as water enters. Some then undergo regulatory volume decrease (RVD). Stretch-sensitive and other regulated pathways allow loss of osmolytes such as potassium, chloride or certain organic solutes, with water following the resulting change in chemical potential. Conversely hypertonic exposure initially shrinks the cell. Regulatory volume increase (RVI) can then involve net uptake of inorganic ions through coupled transport and, on longer timescales, adjustment of organic osmolyte content. These responses are not present to the same extent in all cell types and need not restore volume perfectly.

In interpreting a volume trace, separate the rapid osmotic phase from the slower regulatory phase. A cell that swells quickly and then returns toward its original volume while still in a dilute bath has not contradicted osmosis. Rather, the amount of retained osmolyte has changed. If initial and final accessible volume are both 100 units while extracellular retained solute concentration decreases from 300 to 240 units, restoration of equilibrium volume under negligible pressure requires intracellular retained osmoles to fall by 20 percent. The same physical equation applies before and after regulation, but the value N is no longer conserved across the entire experiment.

Plants, bacteria and fungi often survive external changes through a combination of mechanical support and osmotic adjustment. Plant cells exposed to reduced external water potential may accumulate solutes, lowering their internal solute potential and enabling water uptake or retention while preserving some turgor. Compatible solutes such as proline and soluble sugars can contribute without disrupting normal macromolecular function as strongly as excessive concentrations of some inorganic ions. Osmotic adjustment changes the internal chemical environment; it is distinct from a transient increase in aquaporin permeability, which mainly changes how quickly water responds.

Microorganisms with walls can tolerate turgor, but a large osmotic downshift may still be dangerous. Mechanosensitive channels in many bacteria release small solutes when membrane tension increases, reducing the drive for water influx. In a hyperosmotic environment, bacteria may accumulate ions and compatible organic solutes to restore cellular hydration. Freshwater protists that lack rigid walls can use contractile vacuoles to collect and expel excess water. These examples show that maintaining cell volume can require energy even though osmosis itself is passive: energy is spent on the organization of solute gradients or fluid removal rather than on each spontaneous water-crossing event.

15. Integrating observations: membrane permeability, cell mechanics and osmotic response

Imagine four preparations in identical hypotonic baths: an intact plant cell, a wall-free plant protoplast, an animal cell and a synthetic lipid vesicle. Each contains the same ideal concentration of retained solute. Initially the water chemical-potential gradient is similar, assuming equal pressures. Yet the intact plant cell may reach a high-pressure turgid state after only limited expansion, while the protoplast may expand substantially and become vulnerable to rupture. An animal cell's final behavior depends on its membrane reserve and regulation; a vesicle lacks active volume control. The wall-free plant protoplast is particularly informative because it distinguishes the contribution of the wall from that of the plasma membrane.

Suppose the intact plant cell exhibits almost no final volume change while the protoplast swells. It would be wrong to conclude that the intact cell is impermeable to water. Turgor can rise even if expansion is small, and the final net flux becomes zero at equal water potential rather than equal solute concentration. Conversely, a cell that changes volume slowly may have low water permeability, but a slow response can also reflect a small driving gradient or mechanical resistance. An informative experiment varies osmotic gradient independently of membrane composition and measures both initial rates and final states.

Mass changes in plant tissue offer another useful example. Equal initial masses of tissue exposed to different sucrose concentrations may gain mass in dilute baths and lose mass in concentrated baths. The external concentration at which the average mass change is zero provides an estimate of the tissue's initial effective water potential under appropriate assumptions. However, tissue mass includes extracellular fluid and cells with different water potentials. Solute entry, surface solution remaining during weighing, changes in metabolism and failure to reach equilibrium can shift the estimate. It is not automatically the concentration producing incipient plasmolysis, because cells in the tissue may already have positive turgor.

Consider a hypothetical series of tissue samples incubated for the same time at 25 °C. At 0.10, 0.20, 0.30 and 0.40 M sucrose, mean percentage mass changes are +8, +3, −2 and −7. A simple interpolation between 0.20 and 0.30 M predicts zero change at 0.20 + (3/5) × 0.10 = 0.26 M. The corresponding ideal external water potential at atmospheric pressure is −8.314 × 298.15 × 260 Pa ≈ −0.644 MPa. The interpolation assumes approximately linear behavior between the two measurements and equivalent samples. It yields an estimate, not a precise measurement of the solute potential of every cell. Repetition and error bars would establish how reproducible the estimate is.

Across all of these experiments, one should ask which quantities were directly measured. A rising liquid column measures a pressure difference under known geometry; the early rate of cell swelling can estimate water permeability when normalized correctly; the threshold of plasmolysis estimates a zero-turgor state; extracellular hemoglobin reports loss of red-cell integrity. Each readout is linked to a different physical quantity. A powerful Part C question often presents two readouts that appear inconsistent until the difference between driving force, flux and mechanical response is recognized.

16. Original CSIR-NET Part B and Part C practice

These original questions test the principles developed above. They are not reproduced previous-year papers. Attempt each item before opening its explanation.

B1. Which condition defines osmotic equilibrium across a membrane permeable to water?

A. Equal total solute concentration; B. Equal water chemical potential; C. Zero molecular crossings; D. Equal hydrostatic pressure

Answer and reasoning

B. Water exchanges without net transfer when its chemical potentials are equal. Solute concentrations and pressures can differ and compensate, so A and D are not required. C confuses dynamic equilibrium with cessation of molecular motion.

B2. Which concentration is expressed per kilogram of solvent?

A. Osmolarity; B. Molarity; C. Osmolality; D. Volume fraction

Answer and reasoning

C. Osmolality counts osmoles per kilogram of solvent. A and B use solution volume. Volume fraction is a ratio of volumes, not particles per mass.

B3. Which ideal solution is most concentrated in particles?

A. 0.10 M glucose; B. 0.08 M NaCl; C. 0.06 M CaCl₂; D. 0.12 M sucrose

Answer and reasoning

C. Particle concentrations are 0.10, 0.16, 0.18 and 0.12 Osm/L respectively. Complete dissociation gives three ions per CaCl₂ formula unit; mass concentration is irrelevant here.

B4. A cell is placed in an iso-osmotic solution of rapidly penetrating urea with no retained extracellular solute. Which eventual response is expected in the fixed-retained-solute model?

A. Stable volume; B. Shrinkage only; C. Swelling, possibly lysis; D. Water cannot cross

Answer and reasoning

C. Urea tends to equilibrate and cannot balance retained internal particles in a finite pressure-free volume. A confuses iso-osmotic with isotonic; B ignores the later phase; D contradicts the assumed water permeability.

B5. In a turgid plant cell at osmotic equilibrium with pure water at atmospheric pressure, which relation holds if gravity and matrix terms are ignored?

A. Ψs = Ψp; B. Ψs + Ψp = 0; C. Ψs = 0 necessarily; D. Ψp must be negative

Answer and reasoning

B. The external water potential is zero. Internal negative solute potential is balanced by positive turgor. A has the wrong sign, C ignores dissolved solute, and D contradicts turgidity.

B6. AQP1 is introduced into a membrane without altering solute permeability or retention. What is the direct expected effect?

A. Increased water permeability; B. Active water pumping; C. Permanent change in equilibrium volume in every system; D. Elimination of ion gradients

Answer and reasoning

A. AQP1 provides a passive water pathway. It does not supply energy or change the equilibrium condition; B and C are incorrect. The narrow pore normally excludes ions, unlike D.

B7. Which structure primarily supports positive turgor in a healthy plant cell?

A. Ribosome; B. Nucleolus; C. Cell wall; D. Chromosome

Answer and reasoning

C. The wall mechanically resists protoplast expansion. The other structures have unrelated roles and cannot support the external boundary stress.

B8. Assertion: Osmotic pressure of an ideal dilute solution is proportional to absolute temperature at fixed particle concentration. Reason: π = RT C_osm under ideal dilute conditions. Choose A both true and reason explains; B both true but unrelated; C assertion true reason false; D assertion false reason true.

Answer and reasoning

A. The proportionality follows directly from the stated equation with R and C fixed. Using Celsius or changing concentration during heating would invalidate that simple comparison.

B9. Assertion: A plasmolyzed cell always has a ruptured membrane. Reason: In plasmolysis water loss causes the protoplast to retract from the wall. Choose A both true and reason explains; B both true but unrelated; C assertion true reason false; D assertion false reason true.

Answer and reasoning

D. The reason describes plasmolysis correctly. The process can be reversible if membranes remain intact, so the assertion is false.

C10. A 100 fL cell has 20 fL inactive volume and 300 mOsm/L retained solute in accessible water. It enters a 240 mOsm/L bath. Predict final total volume.

A. 100 fL; B. 120 fL; C. 125 fL; D. 80 fL

Answer and reasoning

B. Accessible volume = 80 fL. It expands by 300/240 to 100 fL; adding inactive 20 gives 120. C ignores the inactive volume, A assumes isotonicity, D predicts shrinkage despite the dilute external bath.

C11. A bath contains 0.10 M NaCl and 0.05 M sucrose at 300 K. Both are retained and ideal. What is its osmotic pressure against pure water?

A. 0.374 MPa; B. 0.624 MPa; C. 0.249 MPa; D. 6.24 MPa

Answer and reasoning

B. Particle concentration = 0.25 Osm/L = 250 mol/m³. π = 8.314 × 300 × 250 = 623,550 Pa = 0.624 MPa. A counts NaCl as one particle; C uses 100 mol/m³ only; D has a decimal error.

C12. A plant cell has Ψs = −0.9 MPa and Ψp = +0.6 MPa. A bath has Ψw = −0.5 MPa. What happens initially?

A. Water enters; B. Water leaves; C. No net flow; D. Cannot decide even though both potentials are given

Answer and reasoning

B. Cell Ψw = −0.3 MPa, numerically higher than the bath at −0.5 MPa. Water leaves. A compares solute potential alone; C would require equality; D ignores the complete driving-force information.

C13. A cell equilibrates at Ψw = −0.35 MPa and has final Ψs = −0.85 MPa. Find its pressure potential.

A. −1.20 MPa; B. −0.50 MPa; C. +0.50 MPa; D. +1.20 MPa

Answer and reasoning

C. Ψp = Ψw − Ψs = −0.35 + 0.85 = +0.50 MPa. A and D add magnitudes incorrectly, B reverses the subtraction.

C14. At 300 K, the inside is 100 mOsm/L more concentrated with a retained solute. Inside pressure is 0.30 MPa above outside. What is the initial water direction?

A. Inward; B. Outward; C. No net flux; D. Inward because solute always dominates

Answer and reasoning

B. The osmotic difference is 0.2494 MPa. ΔP − Δπ = +0.0506 MPa for outward-positive flux, so water exits. A and D omit the pressure term; C would need equal contributions.

C15. Two equal-volume compartments contain retained solutes initially at 400 and 200 mOsm/L. They exchange water only; total volume is two units and pressure is equal. Find the higher-solute compartment's equilibrium volume.

A. 2; B. 1.33; C. 1; D. 0.67

Answer and reasoning

B. The retained amount ratio is 400:200. Equilibrium water volumes share that ratio, so 2 × 400/600 = 1.333. A assumes a fixed infinite dilute bath, C ignores gradient, D assigns the smaller compartment's final volume.

C16. A 300 mOsm/L cell enters a bath of 200 mOsm/L retained solute and 200 mOsm/L rapidly permeating solute. Assume water initially equilibrates faster. Which curve is expected if the cell survives and does not regulate?

A. No change; B. Shrinkage then expansion beyond baseline; C. Expansion then sustained shrinkage; D. Only shrinkage

Answer and reasoning

B. Initially total outside = 400 mOsm/L so water exits. Later the penetrating solute equilibrates, and retained concentration 200 outside requires final accessible volume 1.5 times initial. A, C and D fail to distinguish timescales.

C17. Two spherical vesicles have radii 5 and 10 μm, identical P_f and initial osmotic gradient. What is the ratio of their initial fractional swelling rates, small:large?

A. 1:2; B. 2:1; C. 4:1; D. 1:1

Answer and reasoning

B. Fractional rate is proportional to A/V = 3/r. Halving radius doubles it. Absolute surface area scales as r², but that is not the requested fractional rate, so C is wrong; A reverses the ratio and D ignores geometry.

C18. In an osmotic swelling experiment, group X changes relative volume from 1.00 to 1.10 in 5 s, group Y from 1.00 to 1.025. Both eventually reach 1.20. What is the most supported interpretation if geometry and gradients match?

A. X has about fourfold early average water flux per initial volume; B. X has twice the equilibrium osmotic pressure; C. Y has no water channels; D. X must actively pump water

Answer and reasoning

A. The early slopes are 0.020 and 0.005 s⁻¹, a fourfold ratio. Identical endpoints do not imply different equilibrium osmotic pressure (B); Y may still have channels (C); passive flux explains the data without D.

C19. A solute with σ = 0.4 has an internal-external concentration difference of 100 mol/m³ at 300 K. What pressure difference balances its effective osmotic contribution in the simple linear model?

A. 0.249 MPa; B. 0.100 MPa; C. 0.624 MPa; D. 0

Answer and reasoning

B. Effective difference = 0.4 × 8.314 × 300 × 100 = 99,768 Pa. A assumes complete retention, C divides instead of multiplying, D assumes zero reflection.

C20. Tissue mass changes are +4% at 0.20 M sucrose and −6% at 0.30 M at 298 K. Estimate the zero-change concentration and potential.

A. 0.24 M, −0.595 MPa; B. 0.26 M, +0.644 MPa; C. 0.25 M, −0.619 MPa; D. 0.24 M, +0.595 MPa

Answer and reasoning

A. Interpolation = 0.20 + 4/10 × 0.10 = 0.24 M; Ψs = −8.314 × 298 × 240 ≈ −0.595 MPa. B uses wrong fraction and sign; C takes midpoint despite asymmetric mass changes; D has wrong sign.

C21. A cell initially equilibrated at 300 mOsm/L is transferred to 240 mOsm/L. It later restores its original accessible volume while bath remains unchanged. What fraction of original retained particles must remain in the ideal negligible-pressure model?

A. 1.25; B. 1.00; C. 0.80; D. 0.60

Answer and reasoning

C. At unchanged V, N is proportional to equilibrium concentration. Nf/N0 = 240/300 = 0.80, implying 20% loss. A reverses ratio, B assumes conservation during regulation, D overestimates the loss.

C22. A plant protoplast and intact plant cell contain equivalent retained solute concentrations. In dilute medium, only the protoplast bursts. Which inference is justified?

A. Intact cell membrane excludes water; B. Wall support can balance osmotic entry with pressure; C. Protoplast cannot transport water; D. Wall produces ATP to pump water

Answer and reasoning

B. A wall can support turgor and limit expansion. A is not implied by survival, C is contradicted by swelling, and D invents a direct active water mechanism.

17. A unified reasoning method and common misconceptions

Begin with the actual system rather than with the word hypotonic. Identify the membrane, the solutes on each side, their permeability during the observation, the possibility of pressure differences and whether the external volume is effectively infinite. Count osmotic particles using the stated dissociation assumptions. For the initial direction, compare water chemical potential or effective driving pressure. For a final volume calculation, conserve the retained particles if the cell is not regulating them and apply the equilibrium conditions appropriate to the mechanical boundary.

If the question uses a plant cell, calculate total water potential, not merely solute potential. If the problem describes a penetrating solute, distinguish the first response from the response after solute equilibration. If a water channel is added, ask whether the measurement is an initial rate or a final endpoint. If the experiment reports a cell's volume returning toward baseline while external conditions remain unchanged, consider changes in intracellular solute content rather than inventing an active pump for water. The same framework allows one to solve questions that superficially seem to belong to unrelated chapters.

Several tempting shortcuts now have precise corrections. Water does not stop crossing at equilibrium; the net rate vanishes. A wall does not prevent water from entering; it can support the resulting pressure. Equal osmolarities do not guarantee equal tonicities. Osmotic pressure is positive for a solution, while its osmotic or solute potential is negative in the usual reference convention. Aquaporin expression changes permeability, not the equilibrium chemical potential of water. A concentrated solution can lose water if pressure is sufficiently high. The rate of volume change is not the same as the equilibrium change of volume.

These principles also explain why selective ion movement matters. By controlling the quantities and distribution of ions, a membrane affects not only cellular electrical behavior but also the osmotic contribution of those ions. The next chapter will investigate the molecular basis of ion selectivity and gating. Water channels and ion channels both offer pathways for passive transport, but ions additionally experience electrical driving forces. The water-potential reasoning established here will help distinguish the osmotic consequences of ion movement from the electrochemical forces that determine ion flow itself.
