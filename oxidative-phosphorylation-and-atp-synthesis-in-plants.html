1. Why plants need mitochondrial ATP

A growing root tip presents a useful puzzle. It contains living cells that divide, synthesize proteins, transport mineral ions and maintain their internal organization, yet most of these cells do not receive enough light to carry out photosynthesis. Their energy supply depends on organic molecules delivered from photosynthetic tissues or mobilized from reserves. Even a leaf that can produce ATP in chloroplasts continues to contain active mitochondria. To understand this arrangement, distinguish the source of carbon compounds from the immediate source of energy for a particular cellular reaction. Photosynthesis makes reduced carbon available; respiration transfers part of the energy of that carbon to a form that cells can use continuously.

Adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, is an immediate participant in reactions such as protein synthesis, membrane transport and biosynthesis. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate can be coupled to otherwise unfavorable processes. ATP is therefore continually consumed and must be regenerated. A small ATP pool may turn over rapidly; the size of the pool alone does not tell us how fast ATP is being made. A cell that maintains a constant ATP concentration while growing can actually be synthesizing and consuming ATP at high rates.

The respiratory pathway meets two different challenges. First, it transfers electrons from reduced organic substrates to a suitable acceptor. Second, it conserves some of the free energy of that transfer in ATP. The two tasks are connected, but neither is identical to the other. Oxygen can be reduced without ATP being synthesized, and some ATP can be generated without oxygen by substrate-level phosphorylation. Oxidative phosphorylation refers specifically to the process in which oxidation of respiratory electron donors supports phosphorylation of ADP by establishing a usable electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Consider a root cell taking up nitrate. The plasma membrane proton pump hydrolyzes ATP and builds a proton gradient across the cell surface. That gradient helps drive the uptake of many solutes through secondary transport proteins. The mitochondrion is not directly transporting nitrate across the plasma membrane; it is contributing to the ATP economy that makes this transport possible. Similarly, in a growing seedling, ATP is spent on cell-wall precursor synthesis, protein turnover and organelle maintenance. It would be misleading to count only the energy required for cell division and ignore the energy consumed by cells that are not dividing.

At night a leaf may draw on carbohydrate reserves accumulated during the preceding day. Starch degradation provides substrates for metabolism, but it does not itself guarantee that the released chemical energy becomes ATP. Glycolysis and mitochondrial carbon metabolism generate reduced electron carriers such as NADH. The respiratory chain regenerates oxidized carriers, allowing reactions that require NAD⁺ to continue. In an intact mitochondrion, the chain also establishes the conditions for ATP synthesis. These functions explain why respiratory metabolism can affect carbon balance, nitrogen metabolism and cellular redox balance simultaneously.

The energy benefit of respiration must therefore be described with at least three variables: substrate oxidation rate, electron transfer route and fraction of electrochemical energy conserved as ATP. A measurement of oxygen uptake primarily reports the first two in combination, not the third directly. Plants are especially useful for understanding this distinction because alternative respiratory enzymes offer ways to transfer electrons that do not conserve as much energy as the conventional proton-pumping route. A central task of this chapter is learning to predict how ATP synthesis changes when any one of these variables changes.

2. The inner membrane as an energy-conserving boundary

Mitochondria possess an outer membrane and an inner membrane. Between them is the intermembrane space. The central aqueous compartment is the matrix, which contains many enzymes of respiratory carbon metabolism. The inner membrane forms folds called cristae. These folds provide membrane area for respiratory complexes and ATP synthase without requiring an enormous organelle. The membrane is not merely a platform on which unrelated enzymes sit; it separates two aqueous environments and allows differences in proton electrochemical potential to persist.

The outer membrane contains porins that permit movement of many small metabolites, whereas the inner membrane is much more selectively permeable. Consequently, moving an ion from the matrix to the intermembrane space is not equivalent to releasing it into another part of a well-mixed solution. If protons were able to cross the inner membrane freely, respiratory pumping would establish little sustained electrochemical gradient. The energetic coupling depends on the membrane's resistance to uncontrolled ion passage and on the selective routes through which ions and metabolites do cross.

In diagrams, the matrix side is commonly designated the N side because it becomes electrically negative relative to the other side during conventional energy conservation. The intermembrane-space side is the P side. These labels are relative electrical descriptions, not statements that every molecule on one side is negatively charged. Bulk compartments remain close to electroneutral; relatively small charge separations across the membrane generate substantial voltage. Proton pumping also tends to make the matrix more alkaline than the outside. Buffering and ion transport influence how large the pH difference becomes.

The respiratory complexes use electron transfer to move protons toward the P side. ATP synthase then provides a controlled pathway for protons to return toward the N side. The catalytic head of plant mitochondrial ATP synthase projects into the matrix, so newly formed ATP initially appears there. To support reactions outside the matrix, adenine nucleotides must cross the inner membrane through transport proteins. The location of ATP formation is therefore distinct from the location of ATP use. Neither the outer membrane nor the plasma membrane can substitute for the energy-conserving inner boundary in this sequence.

An isolated mitochondrion with damaged inner membrane may still contain respiratory enzymes and consume oxygen for a period, yet its capacity for ATP production can be strongly compromised. This observation helps explain why the integrity of mitochondrial preparations matters when comparing respiratory rates. Measuring the amount of an enzyme protein or the activity of an isolated complex is also insufficient to prove that an intact system performs oxidative phosphorylation. The enzyme, the membrane, appropriate substrates, oxygen and nucleotide and phosphate transport must function together.

Cristae are often presented as a simple increase in surface area, but their physiological significance also includes the organization of respiratory and ATP-synthesizing machinery. At examination level the most important consequence is that the gradient is established across the inner membrane, not across the whole cell or across both mitochondrial membranes as a single barrier. If a question specifies that the outer membrane alone becomes permeable, that is not equivalent to introducing a proton leak across the inner membrane. If the inner barrier is disrupted, the direct prediction is a reduced capacity to maintain proton motive force, with the resulting consequences for ATP synthesis depending on the extent of the disruption.

3. How oxidation becomes a proton motive force

Electron transfer releases free energy when an electron donor transfers electrons to an acceptor with a more favorable reduction potential under the relevant conditions. The respiratory chain divides this transformation into linked steps. Matrix NADH supplies a pair of electrons through Complex I. Succinate oxidation feeds electrons to ubiquinone through Complex II. Reduced ubiquinone subsequently transfers electrons through Complex III and cytochrome c to Complex IV, where oxygen is reduced to water. These routes converge, but they need not move the same number of protons across the membrane.

For a standard textbook accounting of two electrons entering through Complex I and terminating at oxygen through the cytochrome pathway, Complex I translocates four protons, Complex III contributes four proton equivalents through its Q cycle and Complex IV pumps two protons. The total is ten proton equivalents per electron pair. Electrons entering at Complex II bypass Complex I and therefore provide six under the same conventions. Complex II is an electron transfer enzyme, not a proton pump. The chemical protons consumed in water formation at Complex IV must not simply be added to the quoted pumped-proton totals; the accounting distinguishes vectorial translocation from reaction stoichiometry.

The proton gradient is an intermediate, not a substance that carries phosphate. Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic proposal replaced the need for a hypothetical obligatory high-energy chemical intermediate between the respiratory chain and ATP synthesis with a membrane-based mechanism. A strong implication was that a suitable gradient could in principle drive ATP synthesis even if the gradient were established by an alternative energy source. Conversely, an intervention that dissipates the gradient should separate respiration from phosphorylation even when the electron transfer enzymes remain active. These predictions make the model experimentally distinguishable from a scheme in which electron transfer directly produces a phosphorylated chemical intermediate.

The electrochemical potential of a proton depends on its chemical concentration and electrical potential. For transfer from the intermembrane space (P) to the matrix (N), the molar free energy change is ΔG = RT ln(aH,N/aH,P) + F(ψN − ψP), where R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, F the Faraday constant and aH denotes proton activity. Because pH is minus the base-ten logarithm of proton activity, the chemical term becomes −2.303RT(pHN − pHP). Define the positive inward driving voltage D = ψP − ψN + (2.303RT/F)(pHN − pHP). Then ΔG for inward proton transfer is −FD. This definition avoids ambiguity about whether a textbook uses Δψ for inside minus outside or outside minus inside.

At 25 °C, 2.303RT/F is approximately 59.2 millivolts per pH unit. Suppose an illustrative mitochondrion has ψP − ψN = 150 mV and a matrix pH that is 0.5 units higher than the P-side pH. The pH contribution is 29.6 mV, giving D = 179.6 mV. One mole of protons moving inward releases approximately 96.485 kJ per volt per mole multiplied by 0.1796 V, or 17.3 kJ per mole. For three inward protons the corresponding free-energy decrease is about 52.0 kJ per mole of ATP, before accounting for any transport expenditure. The exercise uses an illustrative driving force, not a measurement from a particular plant tissue.

The phosphorylation potential varies with ATP, ADP and inorganic phosphate activities. It is not always equal to the biochemical standard free-energy change. An ATP-rich matrix with little ADP requires a larger free-energy investment for additional ATP synthesis than a matrix in which ATP is being rapidly hydrolyzed and ADP is plentiful. As the usable gradient declines, it may no longer suffice to support net ATP synthesis under the prevailing chemical conditions. ATP synthase is reversible in principle and can hydrolyze ATP while pumping protons if conditions favor that direction; regulation and the actual cellular circumstances influence the degree of reverse operation.

A pH difference alone can provide energy, even with no electrical gradient, and voltage alone can provide energy, even when the two compartments have the same pH. A common reasoning error is to describe a membrane potential measurement as if it measured the entire proton motive force. The voltage can be partly compensated by changes in pH difference. A selective ion carrier that lowers voltage may have effects different from a freely acting protonophore. To evaluate a proposed intervention, identify exactly which gradient component the intervention changes and whether other transport processes compensate.

The redox gradient and proton motive force are dynamically linked. When protons accumulate outside the matrix and the matrix becomes increasingly negative, further outward translocation requires more work. If ATP demand is low, this opposing force can slow electron transfer. If ATP consumption increases and ADP is returned to mitochondria, ATP synthase can use the gradient faster, allowing the respiratory chain to pump more readily. Thus a gradient is not simply a battery with a fixed charge; in a functioning organelle it reflects a balance between energy input and multiple routes of energy expenditure.

4. F₀F₁ ATP synthase and binding-change catalysis

ATP synthase is also known as Complex V, but unlike Complexes I through IV it does not belong to the sequence of electron carriers. Its membrane-embedded F₀ sector is coupled to a matrix-facing F₁ catalytic sector. The F₀ sector contains a ring of c subunits and a stationary a subunit. A central shaft transmits rotation to the catalytic head, while peripheral stalk elements hold the catalytic head relative to the stationary part of the membrane machinery. This arrangement makes it possible for an electrochemical gradient to drive mechanical motion and for motion to alter enzyme conformation.

The c ring does not form a simple open hole that allows protons to pass unregulated. In the usual conceptual model, the a subunit supplies two offset proton-access routes. A proton from the P side reaches an appropriate protonatable group on a c subunit, allowing that site to move in the membrane as the ring rotates. When the site reaches the region accessible from the matrix, the proton is released to the N side. The asymmetry of access paths and their electrochemical environment favors directed movement under ATP-synthesizing conditions. Describing these paths as half-channels emphasizes that each opens to a different side rather than forming a single continuously open pore.

The F₁ head contains three α and three β subunits arranged around the central rotor. The β subunits contain the major catalytic sites. In a simplified binding-change model, each catalytic site successively occupies open, loose and tight conformations. The open state permits nucleotide exchange, the loose state binds substrates, and the tight state favors formation and strong binding of ATP. Rotation of the central γ shaft changes the conformation of the surrounding catalytic sites in a coordinated sequence. During steady synthesis a full turn corresponds to the production of three ATP molecules, one at each catalytic site over the complete cycle.

The terms loose and tight describe binding properties rather than physically separate compartments containing ATP. The significance of the binding-change model is that the mechanical input is used to change the affinity of the sites and permit release of a strongly bound product. Merely forming ATP inside a catalytic pocket is not enough if that product cannot leave and another set of substrates cannot enter. Paul Boyer's biochemical interpretation of nucleotide binding and exchange and John Walker's structural work helped establish this picture (see the linked Boyer account and the 1997 rotational study in References). Direct observations of rotation in suitable experimental ATPase preparations supplied further evidence that the molecular machine behaves as a rotary motor.

There is an important stoichiometric consequence of ring rotation. If a hypothetical c ring has n proton-binding subunits, and each site transports one proton in a full revolution, n protons traverse F₀ per full revolution. If F₁ releases three ATP per revolution, the ideal motor ratio is n/3 protons per ATP. For an illustrative ten-subunit ring the ratio is 10/3, or approximately 3.33. That number is an example rather than a claim that every plant species has exactly this ring. The c-ring stoichiometry varies among biological ATP synthases, and an organism-specific ratio must be supported by evidence. A student should not assume that the F₁ catalytic head produces n ATP merely because the ring contains n c subunits.

Oligomycin interferes with proton conduction through the F₀ sector of susceptible mitochondrial ATP synthase. It therefore affects the return route for protons rather than directly stopping electrons at Complex I, III or IV. When the motor is blocked in a well-coupled organelle, proton motive force initially tends to rise, and the respiratory chain slows because further outward proton movement becomes more difficult. This indirect suppression of oxygen consumption is a valuable demonstration of coupling. A protonophore can relieve the opposing gradient even while the motor remains blocked, but it cannot make the inhibited motor synthesize ATP.

F₀F₁ is a reversible energy transducer. When ATP hydrolysis is favored and the electrochemical gradient is inadequate to drive synthesis, the catalytic head can drive rotation in the reverse direction and support outward proton translocation. Reversibility is conceptually important because it demonstrates that the same molecular machinery can couple different energy forms in either direction. It does not mean that all mitochondria hydrolyze ATP indiscriminately during stress: enzyme regulation, substrate availability and membrane conditions determine net behavior.

5. Transport of ADP, phosphate and ATP

A matrix-facing ATP synthase solves only part of the plant cell's energy problem. ADP generated by reactions elsewhere in the cell and phosphate from the cellular phosphate pool must reach the matrix. Newly synthesized ATP must become accessible to ATP-consuming reactions outside the mitochondrion. Adenine nucleotides and phosphate are charged at physiological pH and do not freely diffuse across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Specific carriers link the catalytic cycle inside the mitochondrion to the energy economy of the cell.

The adenine nucleotide translocase exchanges matrix ATP for external ADP in the usual energy-exporting direction. In the simplified charge representation, ATP⁴⁻ moves out as ADP³⁻ moves in. Each completed exchange transfers one net negative charge outward, which is favored by a matrix-negative membrane potential. The exchange uses the electrical component of the proton motive force; it should not be drawn as a separate protein pumping an additional proton across the membrane. Actual free nucleotide charge and magnesium binding depend on chemical conditions, but the electrogenic exchange model captures the central energy accounting for exams.

Inorganic phosphate transport is also required for sustained synthesis. A mitochondrial phosphate carrier can couple phosphate entry to proton movement, often described in terms of phosphate/proton symport or an equivalent phosphate/hydroxide exchange. The relevant cost is associated with the pH component of the gradient. Therefore nucleotide exchange and phosphate entry consume different components of the electrochemical gradient. In common simplified energetic accounting these transport demands together are represented by approximately one additional proton-motive-force equivalent per ATP made available outside the matrix, rather than blindly counting each carrier as a full extra physical proton crossing.

One can now distinguish the intrinsic H⁺/ATP ratio of the rotating enzyme from the effective cost of ATP production and delivery. If a question gives three protons per ATP for the enzyme and one equivalent for transport, use four as the effective cost. If another problem explicitly gives a ten-membered ring and a one-equivalent transport charge, use 10/3 + 1 = 13/3. The results differ because the stipulated assumptions differ. Both calculations can be internally correct. Neither justifies asserting that all mitochondria of all plants use exactly four protons for each ATP molecule under all physiological conditions.

A transport defect may mimic a deficiency of ATP synthase in a whole-organelle experiment. If ADP cannot reach the matrix, an externally supplied ADP pulse will fail to stimulate oxygen consumption normally even when the isolated ATP synthase protein retains catalytic competence. If phosphate is limiting, ADP addition alone cannot sustain net phosphorylation. Conversely, an assay measuring activity of purified F₁ in solution does not test ATP export or phosphate uptake. When diagnosing such a result, separate catalytic activity, substrate availability and transmembrane transport rather than assuming all of them are represented by a single ATP concentration.

The connection also clarifies why ATP made within a chloroplast is not a simple replacement for mitochondrial ATP throughout a plant. The chloroplast's own ATP synthase faces the stroma, where ATP supports carbon assimilation and other plastid reactions. Exchange of metabolites and adenylates between compartments is regulated and tissue dependent. The availability of ATP in the stroma does not establish a universally freely mixed ATP pool throughout the cell. Mitochondrial export remains especially important in nonphotosynthetic cells and during periods without photosynthesis.

6. Quantitative coupling and the variable ATP yield of plants

The P/O ratio describes ATP synthesized per oxygen atom reduced, equivalent to phosphorylation per pair of electrons delivered to half a molecule of oxygen. Because one oxygen molecule accepts four electrons to form water, ratios stated per whole O₂ molecule are twice those expressed per oxygen atom. A number is meaningless unless the denominator is clear. This distinction is particularly important when experimental oxygen consumption is reported in nanomoles of O₂ rather than nanomoles of oxygen atoms.

With the conventional textbook assumptions of ten proton equivalents from NADH oxidation via Complex I, six from succinate-linked entry, and four proton equivalents expended for each ATP delivered, the estimated yields are 10/4 = 2.5 and 6/4 = 1.5 respectively. Fractional values represent average flux ratios, not partial ATP molecules. If four NADH molecules supply forty proton equivalents, they could in this model support ten ATP molecules. The same arithmetic is valid for a larger population of mitochondria operating at steady state.

Now suppose the question specifies a ten-subunit c ring and one additional gradient equivalent for ATP export and phosphate supply. An effective requirement of 13/3 then gives 10 ÷ (13/3) = 30/13, approximately 2.31, for the NADH route and 6 ÷ (13/3) = 18/13, approximately 1.38, for succinate. These values are illustrative theoretical estimates under different specified assumptions. A measured biological P/O can fall below such an ideal because of proton leak, alternative respiratory electron pathways and energy requirements not included in the simple calculation.

Plants possess alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases that can feed electrons into the ubiquinone pool without the proton pumping associated with Complex I. A terminal alternative oxidase can transfer electrons from ubiquinol to oxygen without pumping protons at that enzyme and bypasses Complexes III and IV. For the simple model of matrix-facing electron entry, conventional Complex I followed by alternative oxidase retains only the four proton equivalents supplied upstream at Complex I. Alternative entry followed by the conventional cytochrome route retains the six equivalents associated with Complexes III and IV. A non-pumping matrix-facing entry followed by AOX has no conventional proton pumping in that route. This is an accounting of the stated route, not an assertion that a whole mitochondrion using alternative respiration must be incapable of maintaining a membrane potential. Sided electron and proton chemistry, concurrent routes and other membrane processes can matter in real systems.

Assume, for example, that 60% of matrix NADH electron pairs traverse the fully coupled cytochrome path and 40% traverse Complex I followed by AOX, with no additional pathways or leak in the exercise. Per NADH the weighted proton accounting is 0.6 × 10 + 0.4 × 4 = 7.6. Dividing by a stipulated effective cost of four yields 1.9 ATP per NADH. If the same flux of electron pairs now takes place but a larger fraction uses AOX, oxygen consumption could stay unchanged while ATP generation falls. This reasoning is the essential distinction between respiratory activity and respiratory energy efficiency.

In a second illustrative problem, eighty nanomoles of O₂ are consumed during a defined interval and 320 nanomoles of ATP are made available. The number of oxygen atoms reduced is 160 nanomoles, so the P/O ratio is 320/160 = 2.0. If a student divides by 80 and obtains four, the calculation expresses ATP per O₂, not conventional P/O. To interpret whether the ratio indicates a problem with Complex I, proton leak or bypass use, additional observations are required. The ratio alone does not reveal a unique mechanism.

The familiar totals of 36 or 38 ATP per glucose from older educational models should not be treated as an experimentally universal plant result. A whole-cell estimate includes glycolytic ATP investment and generation, substrate-level phosphorylation during the TCA cycle, oxidation of cytosolic and matrix reducing equivalents and the coupling efficiency of those oxidation routes. A model that assumes all ten NADH equivalent pairs enter via Complex I and two succinate-derived pairs enter at Complex II, plus four net substrate-level ATP equivalents, gives 25 + 3 + 4 = 32 ATP. Altering cytosolic reducing-equivalent transfer, carbon withdrawal, route fractions or transport costs changes the result. In plants carbon may enter biosynthesis rather than be oxidized completely. The most rigorous answer to a quantitative question is to state its assumptions, calculate the indicated yield and avoid assigning that answer to every tissue or physiological condition.

7. Respiratory control and interpreting an oxygen trace

Respiratory control describes the dependence of respiration on the demand for phosphorylation in a coupled mitochondrial preparation. Consider isolated plant mitochondria supplied with an oxidizable substrate, oxygen and phosphate. In the absence of sufficient added ADP, net ATP synthesis is limited. Proton pumping can establish a substantial gradient and oxygen consumption continues at a relatively low rate determined partly by proton leak and other demands. Adding ADP provides a substrate for ATP synthase, accelerates proton return and allows the respiratory chain to work against a smaller opposing force. Oxygen consumption commonly increases.

In classical terminology, the accelerated ADP-supported rate is state 3 respiration. When the added ADP has largely been converted to ATP, the slower post-ADP rate is state 4. The respiratory control ratio, RCR, is state 3 divided by state 4 under specified assay conditions. If the rates are 96 and 24 nmol O₂ per minute per mg mitochondrial protein, RCR is four. An oxygen electrode recording usually plots oxygen concentration against time, so consumption appears as a downward slope; the more negative slope corresponds to a greater positive consumption rate. Confusing the plotted concentration with its derivative reverses interpretation.

An elevated state 4 rate can arise when the inner membrane leaks more protons, allowing respiratory pumping to continue even with little ATP synthesis. A low state 3 rate can arise because of limited substrate oxidation, electron transport deficiency, inadequate ADP or phosphate entry, or reduced ATP synthase capacity. Both changes reduce RCR, but they imply different primary defects. RCR is an indicator of functional coupling, not a unique molecular diagnostic. A fair comparison uses the same substrate, temperature, oxygen availability and units of mitochondrial material.

Consider two fictional preparations. A has state 3 and state 4 rates of 100 and 20; B has rates of 100 and 50. The ratio falls from five to two even though the maximal measured ADP-stimulated rate is unchanged. The increased resting rate suggests an increased route for dissipation or a bypass, although a direct measurement of ATP and membrane potential is needed to discriminate. A third preparation with rates of 40 and 20 also has RCR two, but its reduction arises from a different pattern: the stimulated rate is impaired. The equal ratios do not prove equal mechanisms.

Plant respiratory pathways complicate classical state definitions. Alternative oxidase can consume oxygen without the same proton translocation as the cytochrome pathway. Alternative electron entry can change pumping per oxygen atom. A high oxygen consumption rate can therefore persist under conditions where the conventional cytochrome chain would otherwise be limited by proton motive force. The interpretation of state transitions is most secure when the active routes, substrates and inhibitors are specified, and the investigator measures ATP synthesis or nucleotide changes alongside oxygen consumption.

The ADP effect is also physiologically significant outside the isolated preparation. Increased ATP use by a growing or transporting cell returns ADP and phosphate to the energy-producing machinery. This may increase oxidative phosphorylation if substrates, oxygen and respiratory capacity are available. If oxygen is limiting, high ADP cannot overcome the lack of terminal electron acceptor. If the cell lacks respiratory substrate, a demand signal similarly cannot create electron donors. Regulation is a consequence of multiple constraints on flux rather than a universal rule that ADP always increases oxygen uptake by the same amount.

8. Inhibitors, uncouplers and causal diagnosis

Respiratory inhibitors and uncouplers are useful because they interrupt different connections in the pathway. A conventional electron transport inhibitor prevents a specified step of electron transfer. Rotenone inhibits susceptible Complex I activity; antimycin A inhibits the cytochrome bc₁ complex; cyanide inhibits cytochrome c oxidase. In a purely conventional route, blocking electron delivery to oxygen reduces the supply of energy for proton pumping and eventually limits ATP synthesis. Upstream carriers tend toward a more reduced state, while downstream carriers tend toward oxidation, although the details depend on the reaction and alternative pathways.

A protonophore such as FCCP or DNP facilitates proton movement across the membrane and diminishes the driving force. It does not need to directly bind the catalytic active site of ATP synthase to suppress phosphorylation. If the respiratory chain was slowed by a large gradient, dissipation of that gradient can increase electron transfer and oxygen uptake even while ATP production declines. Some released energy is dissipated as heat rather than being retained as ATP. If substrate oxidation is already maximal, or if an excessive concentration of the uncoupling compound impairs other functions, oxygen consumption need not increase. The prediction of stimulated respiration is conditional on the intact respiratory capacity and an initially coupled, constrained state.

Oligomycin and a protonophore therefore give opposite initial effects on the gradient in a well-coupled model. Oligomycin blocks the productive proton return route so that the gradient tends to increase and electron flow slows. A protonophore supplies an uncontrolled return route, tending to decrease the gradient and relieve the backpressure on electron transfer. Both interventions can lower ATP synthesis, but their oxygen responses differ. Sequential additions make the distinction clearer. If oligomycin first decreases respiration and an uncoupler then restores oxygen consumption, electron transfer capacity remains available despite the block in phosphorylation. ATP production should not be inferred from the recovery of respiration.

Suppose respiration with matrix NADH-generating substrates is inhibited by rotenone. In a simplified conventional system, addition of succinate can feed electrons through Complex II, bypassing Complex I and allowing electron transfer to continue if the downstream complexes and substrate transport remain functional. The restored route pumps fewer protons per pair of electrons and hence has lower theoretical ATP yield. In plants the initial rotenone response may itself be incomplete because of alternative dehydrogenases. Therefore recovery by succinate is compatible with a Complex I block, but residual respiration before succinate addition is not evidence that the inhibitor has no effect.

If cyanide blocks Complex IV in a plant mitochondrion, residual oxygen uptake may be supported by AOX, provided it is active and electrons can reach the ubiquinone pool. This response should not be described as restoration of conventional Complex IV activity. Measuring oxygen uptake alongside ATP production and redox state helps distinguish the terminal alternative route from an intact cytochrome pathway. Similarly, a chemical inhibitor's nominal target is not sufficient evidence of perfect specificity in a biological preparation. Controlled comparisons and independent observations make interpretation stronger.

Membrane potential-sensitive dyes provide a second class of observations, while oxygen electrodes provide flux data. A dye signal must be interpreted according to the dye's properties, distribution and calibration; fluorescence may increase or decrease with potential depending on the assay. ATP assays likewise need appropriate control for ATP consumption and substrate-level phosphorylation. An exam problem that provides a calibrated potential, a respiration rate and an ATP synthesis rate lets a student test whether these changes are consistent with an electron transport block, inhibition of the motor, uncoupling or altered alternative-pathway use.

9. Physiological integration in leaves, roots and seeds

A green plant has multiple spatially distinct energy transformations. Chloroplast photophosphorylation transfers energy from light-driven electron transport to ATP; mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation transfers energy from respiratory oxidation to ATP. Both depend on electrochemical gradients and related rotary enzymes, yet their membrane orientation is different. The mitochondrial gradient drives proton return into the matrix, whereas the thylakoid gradient drives proton return from the lumen into the stroma. The catalytic heads face their respective ATP-producing compartments. In both cases the side toward which protons flow through the synthase is also the side containing its catalytic head.

At night, photosynthetic ATP generation stops without light-driven electron transport, but mitochondrial respiration can continue when oxygen and respiratory substrates are available. Stored carbohydrates and imported sugars become important sources of carbon and reducing power. The demand for maintenance does not vanish at night: cellular membranes still require homeostasis, protein molecules still turn over and growth can proceed in many tissues. A high nighttime respiration rate can be associated with these demands, but it is not a universal claim that respiration is always higher at night than in daylight. Rates depend on tissue, temperature, carbon availability and regulation.

Growing roots make the link between respiration and mineral nutrition especially visible. Uptake and assimilation of nutrients impose energetic and carbon-skeleton demands. Respiration supports ATP supply, while respiratory intermediates can contribute to biosynthetic metabolism. When soils become waterlogged, oxygen diffusion to roots is restricted. An insufficient terminal electron acceptor limits oxidative phosphorylation and the cell increasingly relies on less energy-efficient pathways to regenerate oxidized cofactors. The resulting shortage of ATP can impair active transport and growth. A decrease in energy yield under hypoxia is therefore not merely a biochemical number; it has consequences for whole-plant nutrient acquisition and survival.

Germinating seeds face changing carbon and oxygen conditions. Mobilized storage compounds are transformed into respiratory substrates and biosynthetic intermediates. An ATP demand accompanies the synthesis of proteins and membranes as the seedling develops. Respiratory capacity depends on the state of mitochondrial development, substrate supply and oxygen access. It is not correct to estimate all the energy available for growth from complete oxidation of the seed's entire carbon reserve, because some carbon is incorporated into new plant material rather than oxidized to carbon dioxide.

During daylight, mitochondrial electron transport interacts with redox and metabolic processes in the photosynthetic cell. Photorespiratory glycine metabolism, for example, generates reducing equivalents in mitochondria. The full photorespiratory recycling pathway belongs to a later chapter, but the connection demonstrates that the mitochondrial electron donor supply need not come exclusively from the citric acid cycle. Similarly, cytosolic reducing equivalents can affect mitochondrial respiration through transport and alternative dehydrogenases. ATP production, redox balancing and biosynthetic carbon flow are linked, but they are different accounting problems.

Plants sometimes benefit from continuing respiratory electron transfer even when ATP demand does not require maximal proton pumping. Alternative routes may contribute to maintaining oxidized electron acceptors and avoiding excessive reduction of the ubiquinone pool. Their use generally reduces ATP conservation relative to the fully coupled conventional path for the same electron supply. It is not accurate to conclude that alternative oxidation is simply a defect because it lowers the theoretical ATP yield. A plant must balance growth, maintenance and redox homeostasis; under a given condition, a lower energy conservation ratio may be compatible with useful metabolic flexibility. The detailed regulation and biological role of AOX are considered in the next chapter.

10. From redox potential to useful work

It is possible to predict the direction of respiratory electron transfer without memorizing the entire list of carriers. The relevant physical quantity is the difference in reduction potential between an electron acceptor and an electron donor. A reduction potential expresses the tendency of a redox couple to accept electrons under defined chemical conditions. A positive difference, calculated as the acceptor potential minus the donor potential, favors electron transfer from donor to acceptor. The associated molar free energy is ΔG = −nFΔE, where n is the number of electrons transferred per reaction and F converts electrical work into chemical energy units. When potentials are expressed in volts and F in coulombs per mole, the result is joules per mole of reaction.

For orientation, use approximate biochemical standard potentials of −0.32 V for NAD⁺/NADH and +0.82 V for the oxygen/water couple under specified biochemical standard conditions. The potential difference for oxidation of one NADH equivalent is about 1.14 V. With two electrons transferred, ΔG is about −2 × 96.485 × 1.14 = −220 kJ per mole of NADH oxidized. This is an idealized standard-state energy estimate, not a measured value for a matrix. Actual NADH/NAD⁺ activity ratios, oxygen availability and pH influence the potentials of the couples. The large energy difference explains why the oxidation can in principle support substantial proton translocation and ATP formation.

Imagine a gradient whose inward driving voltage is 0.18 V. To move one mole of protons in the unfavorable direction from matrix to intermembrane space requires approximately 17.4 kJ. Moving ten proton equivalents would require about 174 kJ under these illustrative conditions. This is less than the approximate 220 kJ released by oxidation of NADH in the preceding standard calculation. The remaining difference is not necessarily all lost at one enzyme or deposited as a stored intermediate. Free energy is dissipated through the sequence of irreversible processes, and each step must satisfy its own thermodynamic constraints. A total energy budget is useful as a plausibility test but does not establish the mechanistic feasibility of a particular pumping stoichiometry at each site.

Suppose the gradient rises to 0.24 V. Moving ten protons now requires about 232 kJ per mole of NADH, exceeding the preceding idealized standard free-energy release. Under exactly those imposed assumptions, continuous ten-proton translocation would not be thermodynamically supported. In a real organelle, the reaction could slow, actual redox potentials could change or other routes could become significant. The numerical example demonstrates why increasing membrane voltage without limit cannot produce unlimited ATP. The respiratory chain is itself constrained by the free-energy demand of generating the gradient.

The relationship between redox potentials and chemical concentrations is expressed by the Nernst equation. For a specified reduction half-reaction, the potential can be written E = E° − RT/(nF) ln Q, using a reaction quotient consistent with that half-reaction. If a biochemical standard potential already fixes pH, one should not add an extra pH term a second time. In a simplified NAD⁺/NADH couple at fixed pH, increasing the proportion of reduced NADH lowers the reduction potential, strengthening its tendency to donate electrons to a given acceptor. Increasing the oxidized form has the opposite effect. This is why total NAD concentration and NADH/NAD⁺ ratio convey different information about metabolic state.

An important consequence of electron transfer blockage is accumulation of reduced cofactors. If NADH cannot be efficiently oxidized, the concentration ratio of NADH to NAD⁺ can increase. Dehydrogenase reactions that require NAD⁺ may then become constrained even though the enzymes themselves have not been directly inhibited. Carbon metabolism and ATP synthesis consequently influence one another through cofactor availability. A plant alternative respiratory route may permit oxidation of reduced cofactors even when the conventional route is inhibited or over-reduced. The energy yield of the alternative route can be lower, but maintaining the supply of oxidized cofactors can be metabolically valuable.

Free-energy changes must be distinguished from activation barriers. A favorable redox reaction can be slow without catalysis; a catalyst increases the rate at which a reaction approaches equilibrium but does not change the equilibrium free-energy difference. The respiratory complexes are enzymes that enable electron transfer and couple it to directed proton movement. They do not generate additional free energy. Similarly, ATP synthase catalyzes a reaction whose net direction depends on the combined energetics of phosphorylation and proton translocation. An increase in enzyme amount may increase capacity but does not alter the basic requirement that the coupled net reaction be energetically favorable.

In examination questions, inspect which quantity is being compared: the free energy per electron, per electron pair, per mole of oxygen, per proton or per mole of ATP. If the problem uses an electron-pair free energy but asks for the maximum work per whole oxygen molecule, the factor of two must be included. If it gives a potential in millivolts, convert it to volts before multiplying by F expressed in kilojoules per volt per mole. These small unit distinctions prevent errors that would otherwise produce ATP estimates hundreds or thousands of times larger than physically possible.

11. A gradient is a steady-state flux balance, not a fixed store

The proton motive force has both magnitude and dynamics. In a simple diagram, protons are shown accumulating in a compartment, but a living mitochondrion is continuously exchanging protons and other ions while its electron carriers cycle between oxidation states. A stable gradient does not imply that proton pumping has stopped. At steady state, outward pumping and inward gradient-dissipating processes balance when expressed in consistent electrochemical terms. A useful conceptual equation is Jpump = Jmotor + Jleak + Jother, where each J represents a flux in proton equivalents per unit time. Jmotor denotes productive flow through ATP synthase; Jleak represents nonproductive proton conductance; Jother includes demands such as metabolite and ion transport. This is a bookkeeping model, not a claim that each demand corresponds to the same physical transport reaction.

Consider two organelles at the same measured proton motive force. The first pumps 100 proton equivalents per second and returns 75 through the motor, 15 through leak and 10 through other processes. The second pumps 200 and returns 150 through the motor, 30 through leak and 20 through other processes. If motor stoichiometry is identical, the second synthesizes ATP twice as fast despite having the same gradient magnitude. A steady-state measurement of membrane potential cannot establish phosphorylation rate by itself. Conversely, a preparation with a large membrane potential may have little ATP output if ADP is absent, because the return path associated with synthesis is largely inactive.

The gradient may be thought of as a pressure difference that resists the pump and drives a controlled return. This analogy helps explain backpressure, but a physical liquid-pressure model does not capture the membrane electrical potential, acid-base equilibria, selective ion conductance or reversible enzyme catalysis. It is preferable to return to the electrochemical free-energy description when an experiment separately manipulates membrane voltage and pH. The energy available to a returning proton depends on both components; the pH of the external buffer alone cannot determine it.

If a proton leak opens while ADP concentration and respiratory substrate initially remain fixed, the gradient tends to fall because return flux increases. A lower gradient reduces the work required to pump additional protons, so respiratory flux may accelerate. The eventual steady state depends on how fast the electron transfer pathway can supply proton translocation relative to the new leak. If it can partially compensate, the gradient may remain appreciable despite increased oxygen consumption. If the leak is sufficiently large, the gradient can become too small to sustain phosphorylation. An uncoupled condition thus need not mean every proton gradient component is identically zero.

A common experiment compares an intact preparation before and after an uncoupler. If the oxygen consumption rate doubles while ATP output decreases, the system had unused respiratory capacity under its initial coupling constraint. This does not establish that every electron transfer complex doubled its intrinsic catalytic activity. The system-level change can arise from removal of an energetic constraint on turnover. A parallel comparison of NADH oxidation, oxygen uptake and substrate use can help confirm that electron flow has increased rather than the apparent oxygen signal reflecting a chemical artefact.

Membrane potential can also be affected by the movement of ions other than protons. Suppose potassium is allowed to enter the negatively charged matrix through a selective ion carrier. Positive charge influx tends to reduce the magnitude of the matrix-negative voltage. If respiratory pumping continues, the chemical pH component may increase because charge compensation makes additional proton separation possible. An intervention affecting only voltage therefore need not have the same result as a protonophore. The precise steady state depends on the ion concentrations, osmotic effects, other carriers and respiratory capacity. In a question where ΔpH is stipulated to remain constant, however, the effect of the voltage change can be calculated directly.

The membrane potential itself has functions beyond driving ATP synthesis. It helps support the import of many positively charged mitochondrial protein targeting sequences and contributes to metabolite and ion exchange. An organelle may therefore require an electrochemical gradient even under conditions of limited ATP synthesis. A perturbation that collapses the gradient can have consequences for protein import and mitochondrial maintenance that extend beyond immediate phosphorylation. These consequences should not be confused with a direct inhibitory action of the perturbation on every protein involved in those processes.

A quantitative energy balance also distinguishes ATP flux from efficiency. If 400 proton equivalents are pumped per second and 100 are lost to leak and competing demands, 300 remain available to a motor that requires three protons per ATP. The matrix ATP production rate in this simplified model is 100 molecules per second if units are molecules, or 100 moles per corresponding time if units are molar. If total pumping rises to 500 while losses increase to 250, productive flux is only 250. Respiratory activity has increased while phosphorylation has decreased. This example helps make sense of plant conditions in which maintaining redox flux is more important than obtaining the maximum ATP per substrate.

12. How experiments distinguished the mechanism from competing explanations

Before a membrane-gradient mechanism was accepted, researchers sought to understand how oxidation and phosphorylation were coupled chemically. A decisive scientific issue was whether an electron transfer chain must communicate with ATP synthesis through a particular chemical intermediate or whether the two processes can be linked through a physical state of a membrane. Mitchell's chemiosmotic hypothesis gave a clear basis for designing experiments that separated the identity of the proton-pumping system from the identity of the phosphorylating enzyme. If a membrane gradient alone was sufficient to communicate energy between the components, an unrelated proton pump could replace the ordinary respiratory complexes under appropriate conditions.

Racker and Stoeckenius reported such a reconstitution in 1974 (original paper). Their model membrane vesicles incorporated a light-driven proton-pumping system derived from purple membranes and mitochondrial ATPase components. Illumination supplied energy to the proton pump. The system was able to support phosphorylation of ADP and phosphate when the requisite components were combined. This experiment did not require a plant respiratory chain or a complete mitochondrion. Its relevance lies precisely in the separation of the normal source of the gradient from the enzyme that uses it. The original paper is linked in the references.

Consider the logical structure of a reconstitution experiment. A complete vesicle containing a proton pump and properly oriented ATP synthase is compared with vesicles lacking one or the other component. Illumination can activate a light-driven pump, whereas darkness supplies no such energy. A disruption of the proton gradient provides another useful comparison. If ATP synthesis depends on the complete illuminated arrangement and on the intact gradient, a membrane-coupling explanation is strongly supported. The exact control conditions and measured values must be taken from the original experiment if a question asks about the historical data. These are proposed reasoning controls for the conceptual design, not a claim that every control listed was performed in that report.

Orientation is essential to interpretation. A membrane protein can insert into an artificial vesicle with different orientations. If a proton pump establishes a gradient in the wrong direction relative to the catalytic machinery, the intended synthesis reaction may not occur even though both proteins are present. If vesicles leak protons, a pump may operate without generating a sufficient gradient. A failed reconstitution therefore does not automatically disprove the chemiosmotic model; one must verify the assembly and physical conditions required to test it. This illustrates the distinction between falsifying a scientific hypothesis and failing to create a system in which its predictions are applicable.

A second line of evidence addresses how gradient energy changes ATP synthase catalysis. Structural studies revealed a central shaft within an asymmetric arrangement of nucleotide-binding catalytic sites. Structures provide information about arrangements and conformations, but a static molecular structure cannot alone establish that a shaft actually rotates during catalysis. A biochemical mechanism predicting rotary motion gains stronger support when motion can be measured directly. In 1997, Noji and colleagues reported observation of rotation of individual F₁-ATPase molecules (original study). The experimental system allowed a visible marker attached to the rotor to report motion relative to the fixed catalytic portion of the enzyme.

An especially important distinction is that the isolated F₁ motor in such an experiment was driven by ATP hydrolysis. It is not direct filming of a plant mitochondrial ATP synthase producing ATP in an intact leaf. The result establishes rotary behavior in a related ATPase system under the experimental conditions and supports the reversible rotary mechanism. In a subsequent study, rotation in discrete angular steps was resolved. A student should connect the approximately threefold catalytic organization with the idea of a full cycle consisting of three major angular transitions, without assuming that all molecular substeps are exactly identical or that rotation rates are universal.

To examine an experimental diagram, identify the stationary and moving elements before interpreting arrows. If the α₃β₃ head is fixed to a surface and a marker is coupled to the γ shaft, rotation of the marker reports relative motion of the shaft. If a setup instead fixes the shaft, motion of the surrounding head might report the same relative mechanism in a different arrangement. A moving fluorescent marker is not itself a proton moving through the membrane. The experiment must be interpreted at the correct mechanistic level.

Plant mitochondria provide a complementary test of energy coupling using oxygen electrodes and nucleotide measurements. When ADP stimulates oxygen consumption in an intact, substrate-supplied preparation, it shows that phosphorylation demand can influence respiratory flux. A suppressive effect of oligomycin, followed by restoration of oxygen consumption by an uncoupler, demonstrates that the oxygen-consuming machinery can remain functional when phosphorylation is blocked. Such a sequence is particularly instructive when ATP measurements confirm that the respiratory recovery has not restored synthesis.

Each experimental technique resolves a different question. Reconstitution tests whether a gradient can act as an intermediate between unrelated energy-producing and energy-consuming machinery. Structural and rotational experiments test the physical motor mechanism. Oxygen electrode studies test system-level coupling and respiratory control in intact organelles. Combining these lines of evidence is stronger than treating any single image, rate measurement or inhibitor response as a complete proof of the entire mechanism. The same hierarchy of evidence is useful in analytical examination questions, which often ask what a particular observation establishes and what additional test would be required.

13. Designing and interpreting a plant mitochondrial coupling experiment

An investigator who wants to compare oxidative phosphorylation in two plant tissues must decide what is to be measured. Oxygen consumption can be measured in tissue or in isolated mitochondria, but the interpretation differs. A tissue contains multiple oxygen-consuming processes and changing substrate concentrations. An organelle preparation gives more control over added substrates and inhibitors, yet may lose native cellular interactions or suffer damage during isolation. Both systems can be scientifically useful, provided conclusions match what the method measures.

An oxygen electrode in a closed chamber reports dissolved oxygen concentration. When concentration is plotted against time, the slope of a segment reflects net oxygen consumption in that interval. To convert concentration change to an amount, multiply by chamber volume, with consistent units. Suppose a 1.5 mL chamber shows a decline of 40 µmol O₂ per liter over two minutes. Since 1 µmol per liter is 1 nmol per milliliter, the total consumed is 40 nmol/mL × 1.5 mL = 60 nmol. The rate is 30 nmol O₂ per minute. If the chamber contains 0.2 mg mitochondrial protein, the normalized rate is 150 nmol O₂ per minute per mg protein.

This simple calculation assumes the volume is effectively constant, the electrode is correctly calibrated and there is no significant oxygen entry from the surroundings. The linear region should be chosen before oxygen becomes so low that respiration is limited by its terminal acceptor. If a chamber leaks air or an intervention causes an unrelated oxygen-consuming chemical reaction, the measured slope may not equal mitochondrial respiration. An appropriate blank or background subtraction helps identify such contributions.

To estimate ATP synthesis, one can measure changes in adenine nucleotides or use an ATP-sensitive assay under controlled conditions. A measurement at the end of an interval yields net ATP accumulation, not necessarily gross synthetic rate. ATP hydrolysis by other enzymes may have occurred during the interval. A careful experiment distinguishes accumulated ATP from ATP consumed or exported, especially when comparing preparations with different metabolic activities. In the simplest ADP-pulse method, assumptions include that the added ADP is phosphorylated within the chosen transition and that competing reactions do not substantially alter the accounting. These assumptions should be explicit rather than hidden behind a reported P/O value.

Suppose two aliquots from a root mitochondrial preparation receive identical substrate, phosphate, ADP and oxygen. The control consumes 50 nmol oxygen atoms and accumulates 110 nmol ATP. The treated aliquot consumes 75 nmol oxygen atoms and accumulates 90 nmol ATP. The respective ratios are 2.2 and 1.2. The treated sample has more electron transfer to oxygen and less net ATP output over the measured interval. This is compatible with increased dissipation or a less energy-conserving electron route. It does not, by itself, identify which route has changed. A measured decrease in proton motive force could support the dissipation hypothesis, but alternative electron routing could also decrease gradient generation. Inhibitor responses, substrate specificity and independent protein or activity measurements can help discriminate.

An experimental dataset may report a low RCR and a nearly normal phosphorylation ratio during the state 3 portion. This combination is possible if the major defect is an increased resting respiratory rate rather than a profound decrease in coupling during high phosphorylation demand. Conversely, a normal-looking state transition does not ensure an ideal P/O ratio when alternative routes are active. RCR and P/O answer related but distinct questions. The former compares two rates under different demand states; the latter compares ATP and oxygen flux under a defined condition.

Experimental interpretation also benefits from knowing the time scale. An immediate effect of a compound on an isolated organelle is more likely to involve direct action on enzymes, membranes or transport than a transcriptional change requiring synthesis of new proteins. A chronic plant mutant or long-term stress treatment may exhibit compensation through altered protein expression, mitochondrial abundance or metabolism. The same decreased ATP concentration in an acute experiment and a chronic mutant may therefore arise from different causal pathways. An experiment comparing long-term and immediate responses can separate primary disruption from adaptation.

Replicates are essential because organelle yield, tissue developmental stage and preparation quality vary. Technical replication of an electrode measurement assesses measurement consistency for a particular preparation. Independent biological preparations from different plants are needed to estimate plant-to-plant variation. A difference between two representative traces may be educationally useful, but it is insufficient to establish a population-level physiological conclusion. In NET/SET questions, look for controlled tissue age, assay temperature, substrate supply and normalization before accepting claims of a treatment-specific effect.

A well-designed conceptual diagnosis proceeds in stages: first establish whether oxygen uptake and ATP production change, then determine whether the electrochemical gradient changes, and finally test whether a change in substrate entry, terminal pathway, productive proton return or nonproductive proton conductance can explain the pattern. No single indicator is infallible. The objective is not to memorize a fixed sign for every variable but to reason from the specific site of intervention and the conditions under which each observation was made.

14. Distinguishing direct phosphorylation from gradient-driven phosphorylation

Both substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation produce ATP or an equivalent nucleoside triphosphate, but the source of energy immediately coupled to nucleotide phosphorylation differs. In substrate-level phosphorylation an enzyme couples phosphate transfer or related chemical rearrangement of a metabolic intermediate directly to nucleotide phosphorylation. A large transmembrane proton motive force is not an obligatory intermediate. Glycolytic reactions catalyzed by phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase are standard examples. Within mitochondrial carbon metabolism, succinyl-CoA synthetase couples thioester breakdown to formation of a nucleoside triphosphate. The exact nucleotide specificity depends on the enzyme and biological context; for energy accounting a suitable nucleotide triphosphate may be considered an ATP equivalent when an appropriate interconversion is available.

Oxidative phosphorylation has a different immediate connection. The electrons released during carbon oxidation do not transfer a phosphate group from NADH to ADP. Instead, reduced carriers feed electron transfer pathways that support an electrochemical gradient. ATP synthase couples gradient dissipation to ATP formation. The phosphate incorporated into ATP is supplied as inorganic phosphate rather than transferred from an electron carrier. This distinction resolves a common misunderstanding of the phrase high-energy electron: an electron donor can provide free energy to a chain without being the direct source of any phosphate atom in ATP.

Consider a conceptual experiment in which the inner mitochondrial membrane becomes permeable to protons but all substrate-level enzymes remain catalytically competent. Gradient-driven ATP synthesis is compromised immediately because the physical coupling intermediate has been dissipated. A substrate-level phosphorylation reaction can still occur as long as its reactants and the necessary metabolic conditions are maintained. This does not mean that carbon metabolism continues indefinitely at its original rate after loss of oxidative phosphorylation. Redox imbalance, changes in metabolite concentrations and other indirect consequences can alter the activity of substrate-level reactions. The distinction concerns immediate reaction dependence, not a promise of complete physiological independence.

The reverse comparison is equally instructive. If an enzyme that performs substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis is absent, the respiratory chain might still synthesize ATP if supplied with an appropriate mitochondrial respiratory substrate and ADP. A block in carbon supply can, however, reduce the availability of electron donors and thereby affect oxidative phosphorylation indirectly. In a real plant cell these energy-conserving processes form an integrated system; in a causal examination problem, the task is to trace which connection is directly interrupted and which downstream effect follows.

Under low oxygen, many plant tissues increase reliance on glycolytic ATP formation and fermentation. Fermentation regenerates the NAD⁺ required for continued glycolysis, allowing glycolytic substrate-level ATP formation even when oxygen-dependent oxidation of NADH is constrained. In ethanolic fermentation, pyruvate decarboxylation releases carbon dioxide and acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol using NADH. The reduction step does not itself generate an additional ATP molecule. Its importance is redox regeneration. If a problem compares complete aerobic oxidation of glucose with fermentation, the limited ATP yield of fermentation follows because only substrate-level phosphorylation remains available from the standard pathway, while much of the carbon's chemical energy is retained in reduced fermentation products.

A root undergoing hypoxia may thus consume carbohydrate rapidly relative to the amount of ATP obtained. If the cell attempts to meet an unchanged ATP demand with a lower ATP yield per glucose, more substrate must be catabolized per unit ATP, provided that metabolic capacity permits. The carbon reserve may become limiting. This is one reason why short-term survival through fermentation cannot be equated with unlimited maintenance of aerobic growth. However, under oxygen shortage an alternative oxidase is not an oxygen-independent solution: AOX itself requires oxygen as terminal electron acceptor. Both respiratory terminal routes face the same basic need for an oxidant, even though their coupling efficiencies differ.

The expression ATP yield may refer to ATP generated at one biochemical step, net ATP after investment in a pathway, ATP per electron pair, ATP per oxygen molecule or ATP delivered to the cytosol. A glycolytic pathway, for example, invests ATP before generating more ATP at later steps, so gross and net yields are different. A mitochondrial matrix assay can give a different energetic accounting from an assay concerned with ATP exported beyond the inner membrane. The student must specify the system boundary before adding energy contributions. Otherwise a single ATP molecule can be counted twice when it is generated in one compartment and subsequently transported to another.

A useful test of conceptual mastery is to ask what must remain intact for each reaction. Substrate-level phosphorylation requires appropriate high-free-energy substrate chemistry and the relevant nucleotide and enzyme. Oxidative phosphorylation requires the membrane-dependent coupling machinery and a usable gradient, as well as ADP and phosphate. In the ordinary aerobic plant mitochondrion the gradient is maintained mainly through respiratory electron transfer, but an imposed gradient in an experimental system can demonstrate the motor's capability even without conventional carbon oxidation. This separation is fundamental to reasoning from evidence rather than from pathway labels.

15. The energetic threshold for ATP synthesis

A constant proton-to-ATP stoichiometry does not imply that ATP production continues at every gradient magnitude. The chemical reaction ADP + inorganic phosphate → ATP + water is energetically unfavorable under typical cellular conditions when considered alone. Its free-energy requirement depends on the chemical activities of reactants and products. A simplified biochemical expression is ΔG synthesis = ΔG°′ synthesis + RT ln(aATP/(aADP aPi)), with water activity included in the standard-state convention. The activities in the logarithm are dimensionless relative to standard states. A concentration approximation is often useful in exam problems but must use concentrations expressed relative to the standard concentration rather than inserting dimensional numbers without interpretation.

At 298 K, RT is about 2.48 kJ per mole. Taking a biochemical standard synthesis free energy of approximately +30.5 kJ per mole, suppose an illustrative matrix contains ATP at 3 mM, ADP at 0.3 mM and phosphate at 5 mM, and assume activities can be approximated by concentration relative to 1 M. The activity ratio is 0.003/(0.0003 × 0.005) = 2000. The natural logarithm of 2000 is approximately 7.60. The estimated synthesis free energy becomes 30.5 + 2.48 × 7.60 ≈ 49.4 kJ per mole. This is not a measurement of a particular plant matrix, and it neglects ionic strength and magnesium complexation effects.

For a motor requiring three protons per ATP, the reversible thermodynamic threshold is obtained by balancing the chemical cost against the gradient work. With inward driving voltage D, the combined free energy per mole ATP is approximately ΔG synthesis − 3FD. Setting this expression equal to zero gives D = 49.4/(3 × 96.485) V, or approximately 0.171 V. Net synthesis requires a favorable combined free energy under the assumed conditions. If D is only 0.12 V, the three protons supply about 34.7 kJ per mole, insufficient for the stated phosphorylation potential. If D rises to 0.19 V, they supply about 55.0 kJ per mole, making the combined reaction favorable in this ideal model.

The reversible threshold is not a prediction of maximal physiological rate. Near equilibrium the forward and reverse processes can nearly balance, yielding little net flux. A driving force beyond the threshold is generally necessary for a useful net rate. The motor's kinetic properties, the concentrations of substrates, product release and transport of nucleotides all affect that rate. A question may ask whether synthesis is energetically possible rather than how fast it occurs; the former can often be answered by a free-energy inequality while the latter needs kinetic information.

The same relationship explains why a change in ADP concentration can have more than one effect. More ADP provides substrate for ATP synthesis and changes the phosphorylation potential, reducing the activity ratio aATP/(aADP aPi) when other activities remain fixed. This can make phosphorylation more favorable. More ADP can also alter the rate of productive proton return, which changes the gradient and respiratory flux in turn. In an intact mitochondrion these variables are dynamically connected. A problem that changes ADP while artificially holding all other quantities fixed is an isolated mathematical exercise, not a description of the entire physiological response.

An ATP-rich state does not necessarily indicate high ongoing oxidative phosphorylation. A cell might have accumulated ATP while demand was low; the high ATP/ADP ratio can then constrain further phosphorylation, causing the respiratory chain to slow through the gradient. A cell with rapid ATP turnover might sustain a lower ATP/ADP ratio while synthesizing ATP at a much higher rate. Similarly, a decreased ATP/ADP ratio could arise because ATP demand has increased, ATP supply has decreased or both. Measuring only the ratio cannot discriminate among these possibilities without flux measurements and controlled interventions.

Sometimes an examination question uses a membrane potential expressed as −160 mV and a ΔpH of 0.6, where Δψ is defined as ψmatrix − ψoutside and ΔpH is pHmatrix − pHoutside. Using the positive inward driving-voltage convention from this chapter, the electrical contribution is +160 mV and the chemical contribution at 25 °C is about +35.5 mV, totaling 195.5 mV. The minus sign in the recorded voltage does not make the proton movement energetically unfavorable. Positive protons moving toward an electrically negative compartment release electrical free energy. Sign errors arise when different textbook definitions are combined without first identifying their orientation.

A more subtle question concerns transport work. The matrix synthesis threshold concerns the free energy of ATP formation inside the matrix. Export of ATP and import of ADP and phosphate impose additional energy requirements on the organelle as a whole. Do not put the carrier equivalent into the motor's catalytic stoichiometry if the problem asks specifically about torque or catalytic-site turnover. Include it when estimating ATP delivered to external reactions, provided the transport accounting has been defined. The membrane electrical and chemical components support these carrier operations in different ways, so the proton-equivalent approximation is best interpreted as an energy budget, not as a literal count of all proton particles moving through the F₀ ring.

This framework unifies qualitative and numerical reasoning. A large gradient favors inward proton movement, but the chemical phosphorylation potential sets a threshold. A high ATP/ADP ratio can raise that threshold, whereas increased ADP availability can lower it. A change in pH, voltage, motor stoichiometry or substrate activity can therefore shift the feasibility of net ATP production. The correct approach is to write the coupled reaction energy with a consistent sign convention, compare contributions in the same units, then distinguish feasibility from actual rate.

16. Accounting for mixed electron routes and redox priorities

A useful way to analyze plant respiration is to draw a branch point at the ubiquinone pool. Electron entry and electron exit are two separate decisions. On the entry side, conventional Complex I conserves some energy during oxidation of matrix NADH, whereas non-pumping dehydrogenases may supply electrons to ubiquinone without the same energy-conserving step. Succinate oxidation through Complex II also enters at the quinone level. On the exit side, the cytochrome route through Complexes III and IV conserves additional energy, whereas AOX accepts electrons from reduced quinone without pumping protons at AOX itself. The four combinations of entry and exit can therefore differ in their potential contribution to the gradient.

This branch-point approach is more reliable than memorizing a separate ATP value for every enzyme. In an elementary matrix-side model, assign four proton equivalents to an electron pair passing through Complex I and six to an electron pair proceeding through the cytochrome route after quinone reduction. Let fI be the fraction of electron pairs entering through Complex I and fC the fraction of quinone-derived pairs exiting through the cytochrome route, with all other pairs assumed to use the corresponding non-pumping alternatives. Under the model's assumptions, the mean conventional proton translocation per pair is 4fI + 6fC. The expression follows by summing the total contribution of each route component over all electron pairs, then dividing by the total number of pairs. It does not require the entry and exit choices to be statistically independent, because the contributions are additive and depend on their marginal totals.

Suppose 100 electron pairs arrive at the quinone pool, with 70 entering by Complex I and 30 by non-pumping matrix-facing routes. Eighty pairs proceed through the cytochrome pathway and twenty through AOX. Complex I contributes 70 × 4 = 280 proton equivalents; the cytochrome pathway contributes 80 × 6 = 480. The combined total is 760 proton equivalents for the 100 electron pairs. At four equivalents per ATP, the ideal exported ATP yield is 190 ATP, or 1.9 per pair. The oxygen requirement is 50 molecules of O₂ if every pair ultimately reduces oxygen to water. This gives the same conventional P/O value of 1.9 per oxygen atom. The electron accounting is conserved regardless of whether particular individual pairs choose the same branch combination.

If 15% of the conventionally translocated proton equivalents is unavailable for ATP production in this exercise, the usable amount becomes 0.85 × 760 = 646 equivalents. The corresponding effective yield is 161.5 ATP equivalents per 100 pairs or 1.615 per pair. This number describes an averaged flux; real ATP molecules are discrete. Notice that the leak fraction is taken from the total gradient contribution, not subtracted as 0.15 ATP per pair. Different definitions of a leakage percentage lead to different calculations, so the problem must specify whether the percentage refers to protons translocated, respiratory oxygen flux or ATP lost.

The model is deliberately limited to conventional proton-pumping accounting and matrix-side electron entry. Plant alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases can face different sides of the inner membrane, and vectorial electron and chemical proton reactions can influence the actual energetic accounting. A statement that a particular enzyme does not pump protons should not automatically be extended into a claim that its complete route contributes no electrical or chemical gradient under all conditions. The plant ATP-yield review discusses these sidedness effects. For examination calculations, use the defined stoichiometries and the route assumptions provided, while acknowledging that actual plant mitochondria are more complicated.

A branch fraction is a flux proportion, not an enzyme abundance. A plant could possess abundant AOX protein while transferring only a small fraction of its respiratory electrons through AOX under the measured condition. Conversely, a lower protein abundance might support appreciable flux if the enzyme is active and substrate is available. An inhibitor-induced change in oxygen consumption can suggest that a route was active, but the response may also involve redistribution of electrons to another route. A common pitfall is to interpret the respiratory capacity measured after blocking the competing pathway as identical to the flux through that pathway in the original untreated state.

Consider a fictional mitochondrion in which the cytochrome branch can carry up to 90 electron pairs per minute and AOX can carry up to 70. The electron supply in the untreated state is only 80 pairs per minute. It is physically impossible for both branches simultaneously to operate at their individual measured capacities because that would require 160 pairs per minute. An inhibitor experiment in which one branch is suppressed can reveal how much flux the other route supports under the altered condition. It does not necessarily reveal the initial distribution. To estimate actual partitioning, a method must account for the competition and changing metabolic conditions at the shared quinone pool.

The energy tradeoff can be understood without detailed enzyme regulation. If mitochondrial carbon metabolism supplies reduced electron carriers faster than ATP demand requires, the fully coupled route may face high gradient backpressure. A less strongly energy-conserving route can sustain electron transfer and regenerate oxidized cofactors with a smaller addition to the gradient. This may help maintain metabolism and redox homeostasis. When ATP demand is high, however, the same proportion of bypass flux can reduce ATP supply per substrate. Whether a lower coupling efficiency benefits the tissue depends on the balance between ATP requirement, substrate availability, oxygen supply and redox state.

Respiratory electron leakage leading to reactive oxygen species is a related but distinct process. Oxygen can be partially reduced at sites in the respiratory chain, producing reactive species rather than being fully reduced to water by the normal terminal reactions. Changes in the reduction state of carriers and proton motive force can influence such reactions. AOX and alternative dehydrogenases may affect redox conditions, but their presence does not guarantee that oxidative stress is prevented in every context. A decrease in ATP yield, an increase in oxygen consumption and a decrease in reactive oxygen species are three independent observations that may occur together under some conditions but should not be treated as obligatory equivalents.

This approach prepares the next chapter on alternative oxidase by establishing the quantitative consequence of bypassing an energy-conserving step. The present objective is not to memorize all AOX regulatory details, but to predict how a defined change in route fractions affects the proton gradient and ATP potential. A careful student can calculate the energetic outcome of many different pathway mixtures while preserving the distinction between theoretical coupling and experimentally measured ATP production.

17. Multiple constraints on respiratory ATP supply

A plant mitochondrion is not governed by a single dial. Its ATP synthesis depends on a supply of oxidizable carbon or reducing equivalents, adequate oxygen, functioning respiratory enzymes, an intact inner membrane, available nucleotides and phosphate, and pathways for nucleotide exchange. Limitation at any of these points can reduce ATP output. The pattern of other variables provides clues about the primary constraint. An electron donor shortage, for example, reduces the energy input to the respiratory chain. An ATP synthase block prevents one important route of energy use while the energy input machinery may remain capable of operating. A leak provides an additional route of energy dissipation. These mechanisms can produce similarly low ATP levels through different causes.

Suppose a plant mitochondrial mutant exhibits reduced ATP output and reduced oxygen consumption when supplied with a NADH-generating substrate. Two explanations are that the mutant has impaired electron entry through Complex I, or that its ATP synthase is inefficient and has indirectly slowed the respiratory chain by preventing productive proton return. If uncoupling restores oxygen consumption close to the control, the latter explanation becomes more plausible, provided the experiment shows that substrate supply and terminal electron transfer are adequate. If uncoupling fails to restore NADH-linked respiration but succinate-supported respiration is normal, an impairment of NADH-linked electron entry or substrate generation is more plausible. Additional assays are needed to distinguish these two upstream possibilities.

Consider a second mutant with low ATP output, near-normal oxygen consumption and a reduced membrane potential. This pattern might indicate an increased proton conductance or a change in electron routing toward pathways with lower conventional pumping. Measuring respiratory rates after suppressing alternative routes could help differentiate these possibilities, but an inhibitor can also change electron partitioning and energetic demand. Directly measuring proton conductance under appropriately controlled conditions would provide different evidence from simply observing a reduced potential. A robust inference draws on complementary measurements rather than relying on one sign pattern.

A third possibility is an adenine nucleotide transport defect. The ATP synthase may still perform well in an artificial preparation in which ADP reaches the catalytic sites freely. In intact mitochondria, externally supplied ADP may fail to stimulate respiration because it cannot enter efficiently. The same phenotype could be produced by reduced phosphate transport or an inhibited catalytic enzyme. An assay of nucleotide exchange, direct measurement of matrix nucleotide content or carefully designed comparison with permeabilized systems could identify the affected step. Permeabilization itself may disrupt the gradient, so its effects cannot be interpreted as a simple restoration of oxidative phosphorylation.

Carbon availability can impose a different limitation in whole plants. A dark-grown seedling with depleted reserves may have adequate mitochondrial enzymes and oxygen but little respiratory substrate. Supplying metabolizable carbon can increase respiration and ATP production if the machinery is otherwise capable. By contrast, a waterlogged root may contain abundant sugars but lack oxygen. Adding more sugar does not remove that terminal acceptor limitation. These situations distinguish substrate starvation from oxygen starvation. The ability to interpret them is important for understanding respiration during stress without assigning every energy deficit to damaged ATP synthase.

Temperature provides another source of variation. Within a physiological range, enzyme reaction rates and membrane transport processes can respond differently to temperature changes. Oxygen solubility also changes with temperature, and membrane proton conductance may vary. Comparing respiratory rates at two temperatures without controlling oxygen availability and tissue condition can therefore combine several effects. A faster respiratory rate at the higher temperature does not establish a higher coupling ratio; ATP output must be considered separately. Likewise, a lower temperature may reduce respiratory capacity while changing the relative importance of alternative routes. There is no universal fixed direction for every ratio in every plant species.

Phosphate status connects mitochondrial energetics with plant nutrition. Inorganic phosphate is a substrate for ATP synthesis and participates in numerous metabolic reactions. Limited phosphate availability can restrict phosphorylation and alter glycolytic and other metabolic fluxes. However, external phosphate deficiency is not equivalent to an instantaneous zero phosphate concentration in the mitochondrial matrix, because cells regulate internal pools and redistribute metabolites. An isolated organelle assay with a defined phosphate concentration tests a more specific condition than a whole-plant nutrient deprivation experiment. The distinction prevents an overgeneralized interpretation of a physiological phenotype.

In a growing tissue, mitochondrial abundance can also change. A measurement normalized per gram of tissue reflects both organelle abundance and activity per organelle, whereas normalization per milligram of mitochondrial protein emphasizes the isolated machinery. Suppose tissue A consumes twice as much oxygen per gram as tissue B, but their isolated mitochondria consume equal amounts per milligram of mitochondrial protein. If the isolation and normalization are comparable, a difference in mitochondrial abundance or substrate and demand conditions in intact tissues could explain the tissue-level result. The observation does not alone establish that the respiratory complexes of tissue A are intrinsically more active.

An examination problem may describe a mitochondrial defect and ask whether the plant compensates by increasing respiration. Compensation is possible only if the relevant metabolic capacity and substrates remain available. A plant can increase respiratory electron flow through alternative routes to preserve redox turnover, but that does not ensure recovery of ATP production. Increasing the abundance of ATP synthase may help if the motor limits flux and other components are adequate, but not if oxygen is absent or the membrane cannot hold a gradient. A sound explanation should identify the specific constraint being relieved and why it is causally relevant.

One can organize all these cases around four measurable classes: electron donor oxidation and oxygen flux, electrochemical gradient, phosphorylation output and metabolite exchange. The measurements are not substitutes for one another. Redox indicators help characterize electron availability, oxygen measurements characterize terminal flux, potential and pH measurements characterize stored electrochemical energy, and nucleotide measurements characterize output and demand. Their interpretation should be combined with substrate and inhibitor controls. This method makes unfamiliar experimental vignettes tractable even when they do not match a memorized textbook example.

18. Why mitochondrial and chloroplast energy systems cooperate

The energy-conserving mechanism in mitochondria and chloroplasts shares a common physical principle but solves different metabolic problems. In photosynthetic thylakoids, light energy drives electron transfer and proton accumulation in the lumen. ATP synthesis toward the stroma provides energy for carbon assimilation. In mitochondria, organic carbon oxidation and other reducing-equivalent sources supply electrons to an oxygen-reducing respiratory chain; the inner mitochondrial membrane establishes a gradient toward the matrix. The proton pumping direction can therefore be described in a consistent P-side to N-side framework even though the compartments have different biological names.

In an illuminated mesophyll cell, the mitochondrion can still perform respiration while chloroplasts simultaneously evolve oxygen through photosynthesis. A gas-exchange measurement records the net balance of these and other relevant processes, rather than automatically reporting each individual flux. A net oxygen evolution signal does not demonstrate the absence of mitochondrial oxygen consumption. Likewise, a net carbon dioxide uptake signal does not demonstrate the absence of respiratory carbon dioxide release. The actual contribution of mitochondria to total metabolism cannot always be extracted directly from one whole-leaf gas-exchange measurement.

The internal energy economy also differs among organelles because metabolites do not freely equilibrate across all their membranes. Chloroplast ATP is available to stromal reactions, and mitochondrial ATP becomes available to matrix reactions and external processes through suitable transport. A cell can transfer reducing equivalents and carbon compounds between compartments through regulated biochemical pathways without physically moving the original NADH molecule across every membrane. This distinction matters when counting electron input to mitochondria. A reduced carrier generated in the cytosol may reach the respiratory chain through mechanisms with energetic consequences different from direct oxidation of matrix NADH by Complex I.

Photorespiratory glycine metabolism is one illustration of a mitochondrial source of NADH during illuminated leaf metabolism. Glycine decarboxylation and associated reactions provide reducing equivalents, which must be metabolically managed. Some may be oxidized by the respiratory chain; some reducing equivalents may be transferred between compartments through other routes depending on the physiological context. One should not assign a universal quantity of ATP to every glycine molecule without specifying its electron fate and the degree of coupling. The full carbon, nitrogen and energy cost of photorespiration requires the separate pathway accounting developed in node 6.B.05.

Mitochondrial ATP also contributes to biosynthetic activity in heterotrophic plant tissues. A growing sink such as a root or developing storage organ imports organic carbon from source tissues. Some of that carbon is oxidized to meet energy requirements, while some is incorporated into structural or stored material. The ratio of respiration to growth is therefore not fixed by the chemical equation for complete glucose oxidation. A plant allocating carbon to cell walls and proteins must maintain both ATP supply and carbon-skeleton availability. Treating all imported carbon as fuel would overestimate ATP production and underestimate biomass formation.

Finally, the plant-specific flexibility of respiratory routes should be considered in terms of competing metabolic priorities rather than a ranking of enzymes from good to bad. A tightly coupled conventional route can provide more ATP per electron pair when ATP generation is the dominant objective. A bypass may be useful when regeneration of oxidized carriers or adjustment of redox state is a more pressing constraint. The advantage of either mode depends on physiological conditions and on the costs of substrate use. Recognizing this principle makes the next topic, alternative oxidase, a continuation of the energy-accounting argument rather than an exception that must be memorized separately.

19. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Plants do not respire in light

Correct concept: Mitochondria can respire in both light and dark. Why the confusion arises: Net gas exchange masks concurrent processes. Exam reasoning: Ask whether net O₂ evolution excludes mitochondrial consumption; it does not.

2. Oxygen uptake equals ATP synthesis

Correct concept: O₂ measures terminal electron flow, not coupling. Why the confusion arises: Respiration and phosphorylation usually occur together. Exam reasoning: Compare uncoupled and coupled preparations with equal O₂ flux.

3. Complex II pumps protons

Correct concept: Complex II reduces Q without pumping. Why the confusion arises: Students confuse membership in ETC with pumping. Exam reasoning: Use six, not ten, conventional equivalents for II entry.

4. Complex V transfers electrons

Correct concept: ATP synthase uses the gradient, not an electron transfer chain. Why the confusion arises: Numbering suggests an additional carrier. Exam reasoning: Predict oxygen recovery with FCCP despite blocked V.

5. ATP forms in the intermembrane space

Correct concept: The F₁ head faces matrix. Why the confusion arises: Students follow protons to the pump destination. Exam reasoning: Follow proton return through the motor instead.

6. Voltage is the whole proton motive force

Correct concept: Chemical pH difference also contributes. Why the confusion arises: Potential dyes measure voltage, not total gradient. Exam reasoning: Add signed electrical and chemical terms.

7. Negative matrix voltage repels protons

Correct concept: A positive proton is attracted toward negative matrix. Why the confusion arises: Sign of voltage is mistaken for sign of proton charge. Exam reasoning: Write ΔG = F(ψN−ψP) for inward electrical work.

8. P/O is ATP per O₂ molecule

Correct concept: Conventional P/O is per oxygen atom reduced. Why the confusion arises: Gas assays report molecular O₂. Exam reasoning: Double the oxygen amount in molecules before dividing ATP.

9. All plants yield 38 ATP per glucose

Correct concept: Yield depends on pathways, transport and leak. Why the confusion arises: Older idealized tables are memorized as universal facts. Exam reasoning: Calculate from specified assumptions.

10. Oligomycin directly blocks Complex IV

Correct concept: It blocks F₀-mediated proton return. Why the confusion arises: O₂ flux falls in both cases. Exam reasoning: Test if uncoupler restores respiration.

11. An uncoupler always raises respiration

Correct concept: Stimulation requires available respiratory capacity and substrates. Why the confusion arises: Simplified traces omit dose and state dependence. Exam reasoning: Check initial respiratory limitation.

12. AOX never allows mitochondrial ATP synthesis

Correct concept: AOX itself does not pump; upstream I or concurrent paths may support ATP. Why the confusion arises: A terminal bypass is confused with the whole organelle. Exam reasoning: Account for entry and exit separately.

13. RCR uniquely identifies a molecular defect

Correct concept: Low RCR can arise from high resting or low stimulated respiration. Why the confusion arises: Ratios hide numerator and denominator. Exam reasoning: Inspect absolute rates and complementary data.

14. c-ring size equals ATP per turn

Correct concept: Ideal F₁ produces three ATP per turn; c count sets proton requirement. Why the confusion arises: Rotor and catalytic site stoichiometries are confused. Exam reasoning: Use n/3 H⁺ per ATP.

15. ATP export is a second ATP synthesis event

Correct concept: Exchange moves ATP already made. Why the confusion arises: Compartment accounting double-counts molecules. Exam reasoning: Separate formation, delivery and consumption.

16. Fermentation makes extra ATP in the NADH oxidation step

Correct concept: Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ to sustain glycolysis. Why the confusion arises: The redox reaction is mistaken for phosphorylation. Exam reasoning: Identify which glycolytic reactions transfer phosphate.

17. AOX is an oxygen-independent rescue

Correct concept: AOX uses O₂ as its terminal acceptor. Why the confusion arises: Alternative is interpreted as anaerobic. Exam reasoning: Both terminal routes require oxygen.

18. A high membrane potential guarantees fast ATP output

Correct concept: A large potential can coexist with low ADP and slow synthesis. Why the confusion arises: A stored driving force is confused with flux. Exam reasoning: Measure ATP formation rate and substrate availability.

These original questions are designed for examination preparation; they are not claimed as official previous-year questions. The numerical and experimental data are illustrative unless explicitly identified otherwise.

CSIR-NET Part B — concepts and mechanisms

1. Which membrane must maintain the proton electrochemical gradient for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation?

  1. Outer membrane
  2. Plasma membrane
  3. Tonoplast
  4. Inner membrane
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Inner membrane. Respiratory complexes and ATP synthase are organized in the inner membrane. The outer membrane is relatively permeable to small solutes; the plasma membrane and tonoplast support other transport processes.

2. Which compartment receives ATP directly from the mitochondrial F₁ sector?

  1. Intermembrane space
  2. Cytosol
  3. Matrix
  4. Thylakoid lumen
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Matrix. F₁ faces the matrix. ATP may subsequently be exported through nucleotide exchange; its site of formation is not the site of proton accumulation.

3. Which conventional complex transfers electrons from succinate to Q without proton pumping?

  1. I
  2. II
  3. III
  4. IV
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. II. Succinate dehydrogenase is Complex II. It supplies quinone with electrons but lacks the pumping contribution of Complex I, III or IV.

4. Which carrier links Complex III to Complex IV?

  1. Cytochrome c
  2. NADH
  3. ATP
  4. Phosphate
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Cytochrome c. Cytochrome c transfers individual electrons on the P side of the membrane. Q connects upstream entry routes to III; ATP and phosphate are not the intervening electron carriers.

5. What is the immediate driving force of mitochondrial ATP synthase during synthesis?

  1. Proton electrochemical potential difference
  2. Oxygen binding directly to F₁
  3. NADH phosphorylation
  4. Carbon dioxide diffusion
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Proton electrochemical potential difference. The motor couples downhill proton movement to catalysis. Oxygen and NADH participate upstream, and carbon dioxide is not its driving substrate.

6. Which component of ATP synthase contains the major catalytic sites?

  1. a subunit
  2. c ring
  3. Outer membrane porin
  4. β subunits of F₁
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. β subunits of F₁. The β subunits provide the main catalytic sites; the a subunit and c ring form the membrane motor and porins do not catalyze ATP synthesis.

7. Which describes the peripheral stalk?

  1. Mobile electron shuttle
  2. Proton-binding ring
  3. Stationary linkage resisting rotation of the catalytic head
  4. ATP-export carrier
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Stationary linkage resisting rotation of the catalytic head. The peripheral stalk holds the catalytic head relative to the stationary membrane machinery. The c ring and central shaft constitute rotating components.

8. What does the tight conformation in the binding-change model favor?

  1. Oxygen reduction
  2. Binding and formation of ATP
  3. ATP transport across membrane
  4. NADH oxidation
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Binding and formation of ATP. The tight catalytic site favors ATP formation and strong product binding. Product release requires a conformational change; the other processes use different proteins.

9. What is the ideal number of ATP molecules produced per complete F₁ rotation?

  1. 1
  2. 3
  3. 2
  4. 10
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 3. Three catalytic β sites each complete one cycle per turn. The number of c subunits changes proton requirement, not the three-site catalytic organization.

10. Which process directly supplies phosphate in oxidative phosphorylation?

  1. Inorganic phosphate incorporation
  2. Direct transfer from NADH
  3. Electron binding to ADP
  4. CO₂ fixation
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Inorganic phosphate incorporation. Inorganic phosphate is combined with ADP by ATP synthase. Electron donors supply energy indirectly through gradient generation.

11. Which describes the usual adenine nucleotide translocase exchange?

  1. Matrix ADP for external ATP
  2. ATP for oxygen
  3. Phosphate for NADH
  4. Matrix ATP for external ADP
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Matrix ATP for external ADP. In the usual energy-exporting direction ATP leaves while ADP enters. Their differing net negative charges make the exchange electrogenic.

12. What is the classical state 3 condition?

  1. No substrate
  2. No oxygen
  3. ADP-supported respiration
  4. Completely inhibited Complex IV
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. ADP-supported respiration. State 3 is stimulated respiration with ADP available, in a suitable coupled preparation. Oxygen and respiratory substrates are also required.

13. What is the usual post-ADP state 4 condition?

  1. Oxygen deliberately absent
  2. ATP synthase absent in all cases
  3. ADP largely phosphorylated
  4. All electron carriers destroyed
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. ADP largely phosphorylated. After the supplied ADP is largely converted into ATP, respiration slows toward the resting condition. The other choices describe distinct defects.

14. What is the conventional denominator in a P/O ratio?

  1. Moles of glucose
  2. Oxygen atoms reduced
  3. Moles of water
  4. Moles of mitochondria
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Oxygen atoms reduced. P/O conventionally counts oxygen atoms, or half O₂ per electron pair. ATP per whole O₂ molecule is twice the corresponding P/O.

15. Which ATP synthase inhibitor impedes proton passage through F₀?

  1. Oligomycin
  2. Rotenone
  3. Cyanide
  4. Antimycin A
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Oligomycin. Oligomycin acts on the membrane motor in susceptible ATP synthases. Rotenone, cyanide and antimycin A target respiratory complexes.

16. Which substance is a classical protonophore?

  1. ADP
  2. Ubiquinone
  3. Cytochrome c
  4. FCCP
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. FCCP. FCCP facilitates proton movement across membranes and dissipates proton motive force. The other molecules serve metabolic or electron-transfer functions.

17. What is the direct terminal electron acceptor of plant AOX?

  1. NAD⁺
  2. ATP
  3. CO₂
  4. O₂
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. O₂. AOX reduces oxygen to water using electrons from ubiquinol. It is not an anaerobic alternative to oxygen-dependent respiration.

18. Which reaction is an example of substrate-level phosphorylation?

  1. Proton passage through F₀
  2. Cytochrome c oxidation
  3. Pyruvate kinase reaction
  4. ATP export
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Pyruvate kinase reaction. Pyruvate kinase couples substrate chemistry to ATP formation without an obligatory membrane gradient. The other choices concern respiratory coupling or transport.

19. Why does matrix NADH normally yield more ATP than succinate in the conventional model?

  1. NADH supplies four electrons
  2. Complex I adds proton translocation
  3. Succinate cannot donate electrons
  4. ATP synthase changes the number of β sites
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Complex I adds proton translocation. Both supply electron pairs, but succinate entry via II bypasses I. The motor need not change its catalytic stoichiometry.

20. Which statement about plant mitochondrial respiration is correct?

  1. It can operate in both light and darkness
  2. It occurs only at night
  3. It occurs only in roots
  4. It stops whenever photosynthesis produces oxygen
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. It can operate in both light and darkness. Respiration supplies energy and redox functions in many plant tissues in both conditions. Photosynthesis and respiration can occur simultaneously.

21. Which change directly lowers the electrical contribution to inward proton movement, other conditions fixed?

  1. Making matrix less negative
  2. Making matrix more negative
  3. Increasing external proton activity
  4. Increasing matrix pH
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Making matrix less negative. A smaller matrix-negative voltage reduces electrical work available to inward protons. External proton activity and matrix pH affect the chemical component.

22. What does the respiratory control ratio compare?

  1. ATP and oxygen amounts
  2. Matrix and cytosol ATP concentrations
  3. Complex I and II protein abundance
  4. State 3 and state 4 respiratory rates
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. State 3 and state 4 respiratory rates. RCR is the ratio of stimulated to resting respiration under specified conditions; P/O instead compares ATP formed with oxygen reduced.

23. Which feature is shared by mitochondrial and chloroplast ATP synthase?

  1. Both reduce molecular oxygen directly
  2. Both pump electrons through β subunits
  3. Both use an electrochemical gradient to drive rotary catalysis
  4. Both release ATP into the thylakoid lumen
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Both use an electrochemical gradient to drive rotary catalysis. The physical principle is shared. The energy source and compartments differ; chloroplast ATP is released toward the stroma.

24. Which condition is directly necessary for sustained ATP synthesis in an intact conventional mitochondrion?

  1. No ADP
  2. An adequate gradient plus ADP and Pi
  3. No phosphate
  4. Complete inhibition of oxygen reduction
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. An adequate gradient plus ADP and Pi. The gradient supplies work and ADP and phosphate are substrates. If respiratory oxygen reduction is blocked, a sustained conventional gradient cannot be maintained.

CSIR-NET Part C — quantitative and analytical reasoning

  1. 92.6 mV
  2. 187.4 mV
  3. 140 mV
  4. 940 mV
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 187.4 mV. The electrical term is +140 mV. The chemical term is 59.2 × 0.8 = 47.36 mV; together they give 187.36 mV. Subtraction reverses the specified pH orientation.

  1. 15.44 kJ
  2. 0.00166 kJ
  3. 60.3 kJ
  4. 15440 kJ
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. 15.44 kJ. Magnitude = FD = 96.485 × 0.16 = 15.4376 kJ/mol. The free energy for inward passage is negative; the question asks the magnitude released.

27. A motor has a c₁₂ ring. If delivery costs one additional proton equivalent per ATP, calculate ideal conventional NADH P/O with 10 pumped equivalents.

  1. 1.5
  2. 2.5
  3. 3.0
  4. 2.0
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 2.0. The ring requires 12/3 = 4 H⁺ per ATP. The delivery cost gives 5 equivalents, so 10/5 = 2.0. Using the usual 2.5 would ignore the given stoichiometry.

28. An assay reports consumption of 45 nmol O₂ and net formation of 180 nmol ATP. What is conventional P/O?

  1. 0.5
  2. 4
  3. 2
  4. 8
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 2. 45 nmol O₂ represents 90 nmol oxygen atoms, so 180/90 = 2. The value 4 uses the molecular O₂ denominator.

29. A preparation consumes oxygen at 84 and 21 units in states 3 and 4, respectively. Treatment gives 84 and 42. What interpretation is best?

  1. RCR increases from 4 to 8
  2. State 3 is halved
  3. RCR falls from 4 to 2 owing to higher resting flux
  4. The data prove a Complex I mutation
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. RCR falls from 4 to 2 owing to higher resting flux. 84/21 = 4 and 84/42 = 2. Stimulated rate is unchanged; resting respiration is higher. A leak or bypass is possible, but the data alone do not identify the molecule.

30. In a coupled conventional preparation oligomycin suppresses oxygen uptake. FCCP subsequently restores it but ATP synthesis remains low. What is the strongest inference?

  1. FCCP repaired ATP synthase
  2. Electron transfer capacity persists despite inhibited phosphorylation
  3. Oligomycin destroyed all respiratory complexes
  4. The initial organelle had no gradient
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Electron transfer capacity persists despite inhibited phosphorylation. FCCP removes gradient backpressure, allowing electron flow without productive motor activity. Respiration recovery does not imply restoration of ATP synthesis.

31. Following rotenone, succinate restores oxygen uptake in an otherwise conventional preparation. Which prediction follows under equal effective delivery costs?

  1. Succinate bypasses I and gives lower P/O
  2. Succinate route gives larger P/O
  3. Succinate bypasses IV
  4. No Q reduction occurs
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Succinate bypasses I and gives lower P/O. Complex II supplies Q downstream of I. It omits I's four conventional pumping equivalents, so the theoretical P/O is lower than the NADH route.

  1. 2.10
  2. 2.50
  3. 3.45
  4. 1.725
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 1.725. Mean conventional pumping = 4 × 0.60 + 6 × 0.75 = 6.9; dividing by four gives 1.725. Entry and exit contributions are additive in the specified model.

33. In the preceding model, 20% of pumped equivalents are lost before productive ATP delivery. What is the new mean yield?

  1. 0.345
  2. 1.525
  3. 2.07
  4. 1.38
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. 1.38. Available fraction is 0.8, so 1.725 × 0.8 = 1.38 ATP per pair. A 20% loss is not a fixed subtraction of 0.2 ATP.

34. A matrix phosphorylation potential is 48 kJ/mol. With 3 H⁺ per ATP, what inward voltage is the ideal reversible threshold?

  1. 0.055 V
  2. 0.498 V
  3. 0.166 V
  4. 1.49 V
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 0.166 V. At the reversible limit, 3FD = 48. Thus D = 48/(3 × 96.485) ≈ 0.166 V. This is a thermodynamic boundary, not an estimate of maximal flux.

  1. Equal
  2. 30 and 60 per second
  3. 90 and 180 per second
  4. 270 and 540 per second
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. 30 and 60 per second. ATP flux is productive proton flux divided by three. Identical driving forces do not imply identical throughput.

  3. 60 nmol/min/mg
  4. 600 nmol/min/mg

Answer: A. 200 nmol/min/mg. 30 µmol/L = 30 nmol/mL. Amount = 60 nmol; rate = 20 nmol/min; dividing by 0.1 mg gives 200.

  1. Defective NADH-linked entry or NADH supply
  2. Complete Complex IV loss
  3. Total inner membrane dissolution
  4. Only increased ATP demand
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Defective NADH-linked entry or NADH supply. Succinate can use downstream complexes. Failure of uncoupling to restore NADH-linked flux suggests an upstream substrate or entry problem rather than backpressure alone.

38. A plant mitochondrion consumes oxygen after cyanide addition. ATP yield falls. Which is a plausible interpretation?

  1. Cyanide always activates Complex IV
  2. Oxygen is no longer terminal acceptor
  3. All respiration must be fermentation
  4. AOX may carry residual flux
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. AOX may carry residual flux. A cyanide-resistant AOX route can reduce oxygen while bypassing III and IV. Residual oxygen consumption alone is not proof of a particular route, but this is compatible with plant physiology.

39. A hypothetical inhibitor blocks external ADP entry. Purified F₁ retains catalytic activity. Why may intact mitochondrial phosphorylation be low?

  1. F₁ cannot bind oxygen
  2. Complex II must start pumping
  3. Matrix lacks sufficient accessible ADP
  4. ATP is generated only outside
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Matrix lacks sufficient accessible ADP. A transport limitation deprives intact matrix-facing synthase of substrate despite preserved isolated catalytic capacity.

40. A membrane becomes more permeable to K⁺, reducing matrix-negative voltage. If pH difference rises by an exactly compensating amount, what follows?

  1. It must become zero
  2. Total proton motive force can remain unchanged
  3. It must double
  4. ATP synthesis must stop permanently
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Total proton motive force can remain unchanged. Voltage and chemical terms sum. A compensating pH change can preserve the net driving voltage, although ion effects and kinetics may still change.

41. A light-driven proton pump and ATP synthase together support ATP production in sealed artificial vesicles; disrupting the gradient prevents it. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. A respiratory chemical intermediate is obligatory
  2. A suitable membrane gradient can couple an unrelated pump to ATP synthesis
  3. All plant ATP is made by bacteriorhodopsin
  4. Oxygen directly binds catalytic β sites
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. A suitable membrane gradient can couple an unrelated pump to ATP synthesis. Reconstitution separates the energy source from the ATP-making motor. It supports gradient sufficiency under suitable orientation and membrane conditions.

42. A rotational assay observes isolated F₁ driven by ATP hydrolysis. What is directly demonstrated?

  1. Rotation of a catalytic motor under hydrolysis conditions
  2. Net photosynthesis in an intact leaf
  3. Exact P/O of all plants
  4. Proton pumping by Complex II
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Rotation of a catalytic motor under hydrolysis conditions. The assay demonstrates relative rotary motion in the isolated motor. Synthesis in intact plant mitochondria and plant-specific yields require additional evidence.

43. An oxygen trace has a downward slope of 12 units/min before ADP and 48 units/min after ADP. Which statement is correct?

  1. O₂ uptake decreases fourfold
  2. O₂ concentration increases
  3. The ratio is 0.25 for state 3/state 4
  4. O₂ uptake increases fourfold
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. O₂ uptake increases fourfold. The magnitude of the negative slope reflects consumption, so uptake rises by 48/12 = 4. The oxygen concentration itself declines faster.

44. A plant tissue produces 10 NADH equivalents, 2 succinate-derived pairs and 4 net substrate-level ATP equivalents per glucose under an explicitly idealized model. P/O values are 2.5 and 1.5. Total yield?

  1. 24
  2. 28
  3. 32
  4. 38
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. 32. 10 × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 + 4 = 32. This result applies only to the stipulated routing and accounting assumptions.

45. The NADH/O₂ standard potential difference is 1.14 V. Estimate standard free energy for one electron pair, using F = 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

  1. −110 kJ/mol
  2. +220 kJ/mol
  3. −220 kJ/mol
  4. −1.14 kJ/mol
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. −220 kJ/mol. ΔG = −nFΔE = −2 × 96.485 × 1.14 ≈ −220 kJ/mol. The negative sign indicates favorable transfer under stated conditions.

46. A preparation has increased oxygen uptake and reduced ATP output after treatment. Which additional observation would best strengthen a proton-leak interpretation over a pure ATP synthase block?

  1. A rise in motor protein abundance alone
  2. Increased proton conductance and lower driving force
  3. Unchanged tissue mass
  4. A larger ATP/ADP ratio alone
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Increased proton conductance and lower driving force. Increased leak directly provides a gradient-dissipating route. A pure motor block initially tends to increase backpressure in the conventional model. The other observations are nonspecific.

47. Two tissues show equal isolated mitochondrial oxygen uptake per mg mitochondrial protein, but one shows twice the rate per gram of intact tissue. Which explanation remains possible?

  1. Different mitochondrial abundance per gram
  2. Identical tissue-level respiration by definition
  3. A universal doubling of intrinsic complex activity
  4. Proof that oxygen is not consumed by respiration
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Different mitochondrial abundance per gram. Normalization changes the biological quantity. Mitochondrial abundance and intact tissue demand or substrate availability may differ even when isolated specific rates agree.

48. A root is oxygen-limited but rich in carbohydrate. What is the most justified prediction if more sugar is supplied without increasing oxygen?

  1. Terminal acceptor limitation is necessarily eliminated
  2. AOX becomes anaerobic
  3. ATP synthesis necessarily doubles
  4. Conventional oxidative phosphorylation remains oxygen constrained
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Conventional oxidative phosphorylation remains oxygen constrained. Extra sugar cannot replace oxygen as terminal electron acceptor. Fermentation may support glycolysis but does not restore normal oxygen-dependent oxidative phosphorylation.

Assertion–reason and SET-style integration

49. Assertion: a high matrix-negative voltage can accompany low ATP synthesis. Reason: a large gradient can persist when ADP is scarce and productive proton return is slow.

  1. Both true, reason does not explain
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both true, reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. Both true, reason explains assertion. Limited ADP reduces ATP synthesis and productive proton flux; the resulting backpressure can maintain a large voltage. Thus the reason explains the assertion.

50. Assertion: AOX activity guarantees zero ATP synthesis in a plant mitochondrion. Reason: AOX itself is not a proton pump.

  1. Both true and reason explains
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

Answer: C. Assertion false, reason true. AOX is non-pumping, but upstream Complex I and parallel conventional routes can support a gradient. The assertion incorrectly generalizes from one enzyme to the whole organelle.

51. Assertion: ATP export is a separate ATP synthesis reaction. Reason: ATP/ADP exchange changes nucleotide location and uses membrane potential.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion false, reason true
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

Answer: B. Assertion false, reason true. Export redistributes pre-existing ATP rather than forming it. Electrogenic exchange consumes electrical driving force.

52. In an illustrative experiment, 150 nmol ATP are made while 60 nmol oxygen atoms are consumed. Calculate P/O.

  1. 2.5
  2. 0.4
  3. 1.25
  4. 5
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. 2.5. 150/60 = 2.5. The denominator is already oxygen atoms, so do not double it again.

53. Which sequence correctly describes the energy transformation in ordinary mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation?

  1. Electron transfer → proton motive force → rotary catalysis → ATP
  2. ATP → electrons → oxygen → proton gradient
  3. Oxygen → ATP → NADH → phosphate
  4. Carbon dioxide → proton gradient → Complex II pumping
Answer and explanation

Answer: A. Electron transfer → proton motive force → rotary catalysis → ATP. Redox free energy supports gradient generation, which drives the motor and ATP production. The other sequences reverse or invent causal steps.

54. A leaf shows net O₂ evolution in bright light. Which claim is warranted?

  1. No mitochondrial oxygen consumption occurs
  2. All mitochondria are inactive
  3. Every ATP molecule is made in chloroplasts
  4. O₂ production exceeds total relevant O₂ consumption in the measured system
Answer and explanation

Answer: D. O₂ production exceeds total relevant O₂ consumption in the measured system. Net evolution reports production minus consumption. Concurrent mitochondrial respiration can occur despite the net positive gas exchange.

21. Revision and active recall

Respiratory electron transport and phosphorylation are coupled by the inner membrane proton motive force. The conventional I, III and IV route conserves ten proton equivalents per NADH pair, and II entry conserves six in the simple model. ATP synthase couples proton return to rotary catalysis; nucleotide and phosphate transport impose further energy demands. Plant bypasses and leak alter the relationship between oxygen and ATP flux.

Important terminology

Oxidative phosphorylation
Formation of ATP coupled to respiratory electron transfer via a transmembrane electrochemical gradient.
Proton motive force
Free-energy difference for proton transfer, with electrical and chemical components.
Chemiosmosis
Coupling of energy-conserving processes by an ion electrochemical gradient across a selectively permeable membrane.
Coupling
Retention of respiratory free energy in an intermediate gradient and use of that gradient to drive phosphorylation.
P side and N side
Relative positive and negative sides of the inner membrane; intermembrane space and matrix respectively in the normal respiratory state.
F₀ and F₁
Membrane motor and catalytic head of ATP synthase.
Phosphorylation potential
Free-energy requirement for ATP synthesis under the stated nucleotide and phosphate activities.
P/O
ATP formation per oxygen atom reduced, using the conventional definition.
Respiratory control ratio
ADP-supported state 3 rate divided by post-ADP state 4 rate.
Protonophore
An agent that increases proton conductance and thereby dissipates proton motive force.
Alternative oxidase
Terminal quinol oxidase that bypasses the conventional III–IV route without proton pumping at AOX itself.
Adenine nucleotide translocase
Electrogenic nucleotide exchanger supporting ATP delivery from the matrix and ADP supply to it.
