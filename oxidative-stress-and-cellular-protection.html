Oxidative Stress and Cellular Protection

1. Oxygen, oxidation and the biological problem

A leaf faces an unusual chemical problem. Its chloroplasts release oxygen whenever photosystem II extracts electrons from water, while its mitochondria require oxygen to support aerobic energy metabolism. The same gas that makes these processes possible can participate in reactions that damage the machinery responsible for them. Healthy plants nevertheless grow for many years in air. This observation tells us that oxygen-dependent chemistry is not intrinsically incompatible with life. The important problem is how a cell controls where oxygen is activated, how rapidly the resulting substances react, and how the cell repairs the consequences of unwanted oxidation.

Consider a leaf on a bright afternoon. Under moderate illumination, electrons extracted from water can be delivered to NADP⁺ and the reducing power can be consumed by carbon assimilation. If the soil dries, stomata tend to close and the internal supply of carbon dioxide may decline. Absorbed photons do not necessarily decline at the same rate. The chloroplast must therefore dissipate excess excitation, provide alternative destinations for electrons, or adjust its metabolism. If these compensations become insufficient, electrons and excitation energy can reach molecular oxygen through routes that generate reactive oxygen species. A similar mismatch between input and productive use can occur in mitochondria during a disturbance of respiration. The initiating stress is water deficit in the first example, but the additional chemical burden is oxidative stress.

Oxidation and reduction are complementary descriptions of electron transfer. A substance that loses electrons is oxidized; one that gains electrons is reduced. When a molecule changes oxidation state, its reaction partner must undergo the opposite electron transfer. Oxygen is a powerful electron acceptor, but it does not normally accept all available electrons indiscriminately. Biological catalysts help impose reaction specificity, making the oxidation of organic fuels productive rather than uncontrolled. In respiration, oxygen receives four electrons and four protons at the terminal oxidase to form water. Complete reduction is distinct from one-electron reduction of oxygen, which generates superoxide. The distinction explains why oxygen consumption alone is not evidence of damaging ROS production.

Reactive oxygen species, abbreviated ROS, are oxygen-containing substances with chemical reactivities that differ from those of ordinary molecular oxygen. The term includes both free radicals and nonradical species. A free radical contains at least one unpaired electron. The superoxide radical anion and the hydroxyl radical are important examples. Hydrogen peroxide is not a free radical; nevertheless it is an ROS because it can oxidize selected biological targets and can be transformed into more aggressive radicals. Singlet oxygen is an electronically excited state of molecular oxygen and must not be described as an obligatory intermediate in the reduction of oxygen to water. These distinctions are useful because the most suitable defense against one species is not necessarily useful against another.

A healthy cell maintains redox homeostasis: a regulated dynamic balance among the production, transformation and consumption of oxidants and reductants. Homeostasis is not the absence of chemical activity. For instance, a chloroplast that generates peroxide continuously can remain healthy if its scavenging and repair systems handle the flux. Conversely a cell with a modest production rate can experience injury if its enzymes or reductant supply are impaired. The rate at which ROS are formed is only one side of the balance. Oxidative stress refers to a disturbance of this control, involving disruptive oxidation, altered redox signaling or molecular injury. It should not be defined solely by whether an arbitrary concentration is exceeded.

The idea of redox homeostasis requires us to distinguish three outcomes of oxidation. The first is productive chemical transformation, such as oxygen reduction during respiration. The second is regulated signaling, in which a localized and limited oxidation event alters the activity of a protein or signaling system. The third is injury, in which oxidation disrupts structures or functions in ways that the cell cannot readily reverse. These categories may overlap over time. A rapid burst of hydrogen peroxide can initiate protective gene expression; a sustained burst in the same tissue can overwhelm the defense induced by that signal. Consequently, a diagram in which every ROS arrow leads to cell death is as misleading as one in which every ROS arrow leads to adaptation.

A useful way to understand the origin of antioxidant systems is to consider the evolutionary association of oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic metabolism with oxygen chemistry. Photosynthetic organisms exploit the energy of light to drive water oxidation, while aerobic organisms exploit oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor. Repeated exposure to partially reduced oxygen created strong selective pressure for systems able to limit radical formation, convert oxidants into less reactive products and restore oxidized molecules. Modern plants inherit and elaborate multiple such systems in chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes, cytoplasm and extracellular spaces. Their distribution reflects the fact that no single antioxidant system could control every possible source of oxidation.

The chemical environment of each compartment matters. A thylakoid membrane contains pigments, electron carriers and polyunsaturated lipids exposed to light. A peroxisome contains oxidases that transfer electrons directly to oxygen. The apoplast contains the cell wall and extracellular fluid, with a redox environment unlike that of the cytosol. Even when two compartments contain hydrogen peroxide, the biological implications can differ according to neighboring enzymes, membranes, buffering capacity and transport routes. It is therefore more accurate to speak of a cellular redox network than a single uniform pool of oxidative stress.

Throughout this chapter, ask four questions whenever an oxidative response is described. Which oxygen species is being formed? What reaction or cellular site produces it? Which molecular targets or signaling systems encounter it? What processes remove it or repair its consequences? Answering all four makes it possible to reason about seemingly contradictory results, such as a plant with increased antioxidant activity but greater oxidative injury. Increased activity can be a response to the injury rather than proof that the response is adequate.

The historical development of plant antioxidant biology illustrates a change in scientific interpretation. Early work emphasized protection against oxygen toxicity. As investigators identified electron transport reactions that generate superoxide and peroxide, the problem became one of assigning ROS sources to particular organelles. Work on chloroplast glutathione reductase and ascorbate-dependent peroxide removal subsequently established a pathway connecting ROS elimination to the supply of cellular reducing equivalents. More recent genetic and physiological studies showed that deliberate ROS production participates in communication between cells. These discoveries did not invalidate the earlier protective view; they expanded it. The same chemical systems that constrain damaging oxidation also help define the duration and position of a redox signal.

2. The four principal reactive oxygen species

Ordinary oxygen is often represented by the formula O₂, but its electronic structure is unusual: its ground state contains two unpaired electrons. Spin constraints limit its direct reaction with many molecules whose electrons are paired. This provides a measure of kinetic protection in biological environments. Electron transfer from a suitable donor can generate superoxide, O₂•⁻, by adding one electron. Its negative charge makes movement across the hydrophobic core of lipid membranes unfavorable, and its reactivity and local fate depend on pH and nearby targets. The protonated form, hydroperoxyl radical HO₂•, is more membrane soluble; its fraction increases as pH falls. At typical cytosolic pH the superoxide anion predominates. These features prevent us from treating superoxide as a freely equilibrating messenger throughout an entire plant.

Superoxide is produced when reduced electron carriers or enzymes deliver individual electrons to oxygen. It can damage susceptible metal-containing proteins, including some iron–sulfur centers, but it can also be converted efficiently into hydrogen peroxide by superoxide dismutase. Dismutation is a reaction in which two molecules of the same starting species undergo different fates: one superoxide is oxidized back to oxygen and the other is reduced to peroxide. Protons provide the hydrogen atoms of the peroxide molecule. The balanced overall reaction is 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Counting oxygen atoms gives four on each side; counting charge gives zero overall. The equation is useful for exam calculations because a flux of two superoxide molecules produces one peroxide molecule when this route predominates.

Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, contains an oxygen–oxygen bond and is neither a radical nor merely a neutral version of superoxide. Its lifetime is generally longer than those of extremely reactive radicals, although that lifetime varies substantially with local enzymes and substrates. Some aquaporins can facilitate H₂O₂ passage across biological membranes. This property allows peroxide to participate in signaling between compartments, but does not imply that every aquaporin conducts it or that all peroxide molecules reach the nucleus. Peroxidases, catalase, peroxiredoxins and other reactions can consume it before it travels far. The resulting gradient can convey local information while restricting injury.

Hydrogen peroxide can react selectively with susceptible sulfur-containing groups in proteins. A cysteine side chain may be converted from a thiol to a sulfenic acid or form a disulfide bond. Such transformations can alter enzyme activity, protein interactions or signaling. The reaction rate depends strongly on the chemical environment of the cysteine and on whether it is present as a more reactive thiolate. Some proteins are poor direct peroxide targets; redox relays can help transmit oxidation from a particularly reactive protein to another target. Reversible changes of this kind differ from widespread protein unfolding or irreversible oxidative damage.

The hydroxyl radical, •OH, is generated through reactions including the Fenton reaction, in which reduced iron reacts with hydrogen peroxide. Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH is a simplified balanced representation. The hydroxyl radical reacts with many nearby biomolecules at extremely high rates. Its short effective range makes the place of its formation especially important. A radical generated near a DNA molecule may attack that DNA before a scavenger located elsewhere can intercept it. A cell cannot rely on a dedicated freely diffusing enzyme to remove every hydroxyl radical after formation. Its major protective strategies are to restrict peroxide accumulation, control the availability of catalytic metal ions and prevent vulnerable targets from being exposed to the radical-generating conditions.

Transition metals illustrate the importance of chemical context. Iron is essential for electron transport, chlorophyll-related metabolism and the activity of several antioxidant enzymes. Iron incorporated into a properly organized protein or stored in ferritin is not equivalent to freely available redox-active iron. When injury releases or redistributes metal ions, the probability of unwanted Fenton chemistry can rise. A plant can thus suffer more oxidative injury without a proportionate increase in measured bulk peroxide concentration. Changes in the availability of catalytic iron can alter the fraction of peroxide converted into aggressive radicals.

The fourth major species, singlet oxygen, is written ¹O₂. Unlike superoxide and peroxide, it arises in chloroplasts largely through the transfer of excitation energy from a sensitizer such as triplet chlorophyll to ground-state oxygen. A photon first excites chlorophyll. Most absorbed excitation in a functioning photosystem is transferred to a reaction center or dissipated. If excitation reaches a triplet state, interaction with oxygen can transfer energy without transferring a net electron. The resulting singlet oxygen has different electronic properties and reacts readily with unsaturated lipids, pigments and other compounds. Its short effective range and distinct origin explain why membrane-associated defenses such as carotenoids are so important in illuminated chloroplasts.

It is possible for singlet oxygen and superoxide to rise simultaneously in a highly illuminated leaf, but the two need not arise from the same immediate process. Singlet oxygen often reflects excess excited chlorophyll or charge recombination associated with photosystem II, whereas superoxide can arise through reduction of oxygen by electron carriers, notably on the acceptor side of photosystem I. An intervention that decreases excitation transfer to oxygen might suppress singlet oxygen without eliminating electron leakage. Likewise increasing superoxide dismutase does not directly quench excited oxygen. The specificity of the chemistry provides a basis for selecting the appropriate antioxidant and interpreting mutants.

It is tempting to rank all ROS in order of danger. A more informative comparison concerns both chemical reactivity and effective lifetime. Hydroxyl radicals react rapidly and close to their place of formation; hydrogen peroxide is relatively selective and may travel farther before reacting. Singlet oxygen can cause substantial damage in a membrane despite its short diffusion range. Superoxide has its own chemical targets and can serve as a precursor of peroxide. Which species matters most depends on the biological question. If the question concerns oxidation of distant signaling proteins, peroxide is often a plausible candidate. If it concerns damage adjacent to a metal center, local radical generation is especially relevant.

The identity of a species cannot always be determined from the injury it produces. Lipid oxidation, for example, can result from several ROS or from enzymatic reactions. A membrane with increased lipid hydroperoxide concentration is evidence of altered lipid oxidation, but not automatically evidence of hydroxyl radical production in a specific organelle. Similarly, the oxidation of a fluorescent probe may be caused by an indirect product rather than the oxygen species that initiated the process. This is why independent measurements, species-selective controls and knowledge of the reaction chemistry are important.

A quantitative distinction between pool size and flux can be illustrated with an idealized peroxide-producing compartment. If it forms 8 nmol of superoxide per minute and all of this superoxide undergoes dismutation, the associated peroxide production is 4 nmol per minute. If a peroxidase removes 3 nmol of peroxide per minute and catalase removes 1 nmol per minute, the peroxide pool does not increase under those assumptions. The active chemistry could be missed by an assay that measures only peroxide concentration at the end of the experiment. If peroxide removal falls to 2 nmol per minute while production remains unchanged, the pool rises initially by 2 nmol per minute. Actual removal rates depend on concentrations and enzyme kinetics, so the constant-flux approximation is suitable only over a limited interval.

3. ROS production in chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are central to oxidative stress because they combine high-energy photochemistry, oxygen evolution, electron transfer and abundant vulnerable membrane lipids. The reactions of photosystem II remove electrons from water, releasing protons and oxygen. The electrons pass through plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f and plastocyanin to photosystem I, where a second light-driven reaction raises their reducing power. Ferredoxin and ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase help deliver electrons to NADP⁺. Productive linear electron transport requires coordination between the capacity to absorb light, the availability of electron acceptors and the downstream reactions consuming ATP and NADPH.

When carbon fixation is restricted, reduced intermediates may accumulate. However, the relationship between a reduced carrier and ROS production is not a universal proportionality. Electron transfer to oxygen depends on the energetics of the donor, the accessibility of oxygen and competition with alternative acceptors. On the acceptor side of photosystem I, oxygen can receive electrons and form superoxide in a reaction associated with the classical Mehler reaction. Superoxide dismutase converts it to hydrogen peroxide. Chloroplast ascorbate peroxidase can then remove the peroxide using ascorbate as electron donor. The water–water cycle describes a coupled route in which electrons originating from water are ultimately used in oxygen reduction and peroxide removal, returning oxygen-derived intermediates toward water.

The water–water cycle does not mean that peroxide is never generated. On the contrary, peroxide is a chemical intermediate that must be removed promptly. Nor does it mean that all excess photosynthetic electrons go through this route. In intact plants, cyclic electron flow, photorespiration, alternative metabolic sinks and nonphotochemical dissipation also contribute to photoprotection. Their relative importance depends on environmental conditions, species and developmental state. The essential point for a student is that preventing ROS formation and scavenging ROS after formation are complementary strategies.

Photosystem II presents a different hazard. When excitation energy cannot be used efficiently, chlorophyll may populate a triplet excited state. Energy transfer from triplet chlorophyll to oxygen forms singlet oxygen. In antenna complexes, carotenoids can quench chlorophyll triplet states and help prevent oxygen excitation. Carotenoids also participate in energy dissipation mechanisms that reduce the probability of generating harmful excited states. Damage is especially problematic near reaction centers, where charge separation and recombination can produce excited intermediates. The D1 protein is an important component of photosystem II that can be damaged and replaced during the PSII repair cycle.

Photoinhibition refers to a reduction in photosynthetic function associated with illumination, particularly where photodamage exceeds repair or sustained regulatory quenching depresses measured efficiency. It is not synonymous with ROS accumulation. A plant can lower its fluorescence-based efficiency temporarily through protective energy dissipation without suffering irreversible destruction of photosystem II. Conversely a leaf with impaired protein synthesis may accumulate damaged PSII centers because repair slows even at a light intensity that a healthy leaf tolerates. Distinguishing damage from failed repair is therefore part of interpreting measurements of photosynthetic performance.

A common experiment shifts plants from low light to high light and compares wild-type plants with a mutant unable to execute a specific photoprotective process. A lower maximum quantum efficiency of PSII in the mutant after illumination is compatible with enhanced photoinhibition. It does not by itself establish that singlet oxygen caused the phenotype. To strengthen that conclusion, researchers need suitable controls and measurements more directly related to singlet oxygen or its oxidation products. Recovery after returning to low light provides additional information: rapid recovery is compatible with reversible regulation or efficient repair, while persistent depression suggests sustained quenching or injury. The initial dark-adapted state, temperature and leaf age must also be matched.

An additional complication is the relationship between photosynthesis and respiration. Chloroplast carbon metabolism produces substrates and reductants that are exchanged indirectly with other cellular compartments. Mitochondria can help maintain metabolic flexibility by oxidizing substrates produced from photosynthesis. Photorespiration can consume energy and reducing power but produces peroxide in peroxisomes. Thus an intervention that decreases chloroplast ROS may increase the burden of peroxide removal in another organelle. A measurement made on whole leaf tissue integrates these processes and cannot reveal which organelle initiated the change without further evidence.

The balance of light capture and carbon assimilation explains why environmental stresses often converge on chloroplast redox disturbances. During drought, stomatal closure restricts CO₂ uptake; under chilling, enzyme reactions consuming photochemical products may slow more than light absorption; under heat, protein damage can impair several metabolic systems. None of these conditions implies that ROS must always increase in every tissue. A shade leaf, for example, experiences a different excitation burden from a sun-exposed leaf. A plant that efficiently lowers light absorption, dissipates excitation or redirects electrons can limit ROS generation despite severe external stress. These are testable physiological differences, not simply labels of tolerance.

Consider two plants grown under identical photon flux. Plant A increases nonphotochemical quenching when CO₂ supply becomes limiting. Plant B has poor quenching but high ascorbate peroxidase activity. The first plant reduces the probability that excess excitation produces singlet oxygen. The second improves removal of peroxide after production. It is not possible to infer which plant will perform better under every stress, because the interventions act on different branches of oxygen chemistry. A mixed stress may require both defenses. This distinction is especially valuable for questions asking whether a single engineered antioxidant can protect all plant compartments.

Chloroplast antioxidants also depend on the energy system they protect. Ascorbate regeneration can use reducing equivalents ultimately supplied by metabolism, including photosynthetic NADPH. During severe stress, the impairment of electron transport or carbon metabolism may restrict reductant supply even if antioxidant enzymes remain abundant. This creates a feedback problem: the ability to remove peroxide depends partly on the integrity of the machinery being protected. An enzyme assay performed with saturating substrate and NADPH in a test tube therefore measures capacity under assay conditions rather than guaranteed operation in the stressed chloroplast.

The chloroplast sends information about its internal state to the nucleus through retrograde signaling. Different ROS and redox disturbances can stimulate distinct yet overlapping responses, including changes in nuclear gene expression. A short-lived species such as singlet oxygen need not physically reach the nucleus to influence transcription. Local oxidation can generate secondary chemical signals or trigger signaling proteins that transmit information. This is a general principle of intracellular communication: the messenger detected at a distant site need not be identical to the original initiating molecule.

4. Mitochondria, peroxisomes and the cell surface

Mitochondria provide ATP by coupling electron transfer to proton movement and oxidative phosphorylation. Complexes of the respiratory chain accept reducing equivalents from NADH and other metabolic substrates and transfer electrons through ubiquinone and cytochromes toward oxygen. Complex IV catalyzes the controlled reduction of oxygen to water. In some circumstances, reduced components of the chain can instead transfer individual electrons to oxygen. Complex I and complex III are important potential sites of mitochondrial superoxide generation. Other mitochondrial enzymes can also contribute, and the relative contribution of each site is not fixed across all plant tissues and conditions.

When the supply of respiratory substrates exceeds the capacity of downstream reactions, electron carriers can become highly reduced. A high proton motive force can also constrain further proton-pumping electron transport. These changes may favor electron leakage under some conditions. However, severely damaged respiration may decrease total electron delivery, so a decline in oxygen consumption need not always be accompanied by an increase in ROS. A student should examine the specific site of inhibition, direction of electron flow, availability of oxygen and physiological state instead of assuming a simple inverse relationship between respiration and ROS formation.

Plant mitochondria possess alternative oxidase, abbreviated AOX, which accepts electrons from ubiquinol and reduces oxygen to water without passing them through complexes III and IV. This route bypasses two proton-pumping steps, so less proton motive force and therefore less ATP are conserved per electron pair than through the usual cytochrome pathway, all else being equal. AOX can help oxidize an excessively reduced ubiquinone pool and thereby reduce the tendency toward some forms of ROS generation. It is not itself a superoxide dismutase or a peroxide-scavenging enzyme. Its protective effect arises upstream from its influence on the organization of electron flow. The distinction between ROS prevention and ROS removal reappears here.

An AOX inhibitor experiment illustrates the limitations of causal interpretation. If inhibition increases a mitochondrial ROS indicator and decreases respiration through the alternative pathway, the result supports a role of AOX in limiting electron-chain reduction under those conditions. Yet inhibitors can have other effects, and whole cells may alter substrate oxidation or antioxidant responses. A stronger design compares genetic loss of AOX, restoration by a functional transgene and suitable controls for chemical treatment. An unchanged ROS indicator in a mutant does not necessarily exclude AOX-mediated protection: another pathway may compensate or the experimental conditions may not generate the relevant electron pressure.

Peroxisomes differ fundamentally from mitochondria. They contain several oxidases that transfer electrons directly to oxygen, frequently yielding hydrogen peroxide as a normal metabolic product. In a photosynthesizing C₃ leaf, an especially important source is glycolate oxidase in photorespiration. Oxygenation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate by Rubisco produces 2-phosphoglycolate, which is processed to glycolate in the chloroplast. Glycolate enters the peroxisome, where glycolate oxidase converts it to glyoxylate while reducing oxygen to hydrogen peroxide. The peroxide production is therefore linked to a necessary metabolic salvage pathway rather than simply to random electron leakage.

Catalase is abundant in many plant peroxisomes and provides a high-capacity mechanism for peroxide removal. The reaction is 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. For every two peroxide molecules consumed, one oxygen molecule is released. Unlike a peroxidase reaction with ascorbate, catalase does not require a stoichiometric external electron donor in its catalatic reaction. This makes it well suited to situations in which large peroxide fluxes accompany oxidase activity. Nevertheless catalase does not prevent the initial production of peroxide, and other scavenging systems contribute to peroxisomal redox control.

The importance of photorespiratory peroxide can be explored through a plant with reduced catalase capacity. Under conditions that favor Rubisco oxygenation, such as lower CO₂ availability relative to oxygen, glycolate oxidation and associated peroxide formation may be substantial. The catalase-deficient plant may therefore experience greater redox disturbance or injury than a wild type. Growing both genotypes under elevated CO₂ can suppress photorespiration and may alleviate the phenotype. The logic of this conditional rescue is stronger than observing peroxide accumulation alone: it links the defect in removal to a predicted metabolic source. Elevated CO₂ also alters other physiological processes, so the result must be interpreted with appropriate controls.

Peroxisomes in germinating oil-rich seeds carry out fatty acid β-oxidation, in which acyl-CoA oxidase can transfer electrons to oxygen and generate peroxide. Such tissue is not primarily exposed to photosynthetic high light, yet it may have a significant oxidative burden. This example shows why antioxidant defenses cannot be understood only as adaptations to chloroplast activity. The predominant source of ROS changes with organ type, metabolic state and developmental stage. A germinating seed, root meristem and mature leaf all require redox protection for different reasons.

ROS can also be produced deliberately at the plasma membrane. Plant respiratory burst oxidase homologs, or RBOHs, are NADPH oxidases. They accept electrons from cytosolic NADPH and transfer them through membrane-associated cofactors to oxygen on the extracellular side, forming superoxide in the apoplast. Dismutation then produces peroxide. Regulatory domains of RBOH proteins respond to calcium and phosphorylation, connecting their activity with stress perception. The enzyme is not a mitochondrial respiratory complex despite its historical name. Its function is to generate a regulated ROS flux at the cell surface.

The location of RBOH activity is crucial. A burst of extracellular superoxide is not the same as a burst of superoxide in the chloroplast stroma. The cell wall and extracellular space contain different scavengers and molecular targets, and apoplastic peroxide can participate in processes such as wall crosslinking, defense and communication across the plasma membrane. Some cell-wall peroxidases can consume peroxide in one reaction context and contribute to ROS production in another. Their effect must be interpreted in terms of substrates and reaction conditions rather than assigned permanently to a single category.

In an immune response, recognition of a microbial pattern can activate kinases and calcium-dependent events that stimulate RBOH-mediated ROS production. The early apoplastic burst is often measurable within minutes of treatment. In drought-related guard-cell signaling, particular RBOH isoforms can also contribute to ROS-dependent signaling downstream of hormonal regulation. These examples illustrate a shared molecular mechanism used in different biological situations; the detailed immune receptor pathways and ABA responses belong to subsequent nodes. Here the central question is how a plant creates an oxidizing signal at a defined location without allowing it to cause indiscriminate damage.

Other reactions, including those associated with protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum, oxidases in the cell wall and metabolic reactions in the cytosol, can contribute to the overall ROS network. Their mention is important because the four major sources in this node are representative rather than exhaustive. In an experiment, a residual ROS signal in an RBOH mutant does not prove that the mutation was ineffective. The remaining signal may come from other enzymes or organelles, or may reflect incomplete specificity of the measurement. The useful inference is the fraction of a defined response that depends on the perturbed component, not the claim that one enzyme accounts for all ROS.

When a plant experiences combined stresses, sources interact. For example, salinity alters ion balance, osmotic conditions and metabolic demands; chloroplast electron acceptor limitation may increase at the same time as signaling through plasma membrane oxidases. Mitochondrial respiration can adjust in response to changes in carbon availability, while peroxisomal reactions continue their own metabolic production of peroxide. A whole-cell antioxidant strategy must therefore be distributed and coordinated. The next sections examine the damage these sources can cause and the protective mechanisms that distinguish productive oxidation from uncontrolled injury.
