1. Oxygen, oxidation and the biological problem

A leaf faces an unusual chemical problem. Its chloroplasts release oxygen whenever photosystem II extracts electrons from water, while its mitochondria require oxygen to support aerobic energy metabolism. The same gas that makes these processes possible can participate in reactions that damage the machinery responsible for them. Healthy plants nevertheless grow for many years in air. This observation tells us that oxygen-dependent chemistry is not intrinsically incompatible with life. The important problem is how a cell controls where oxygen is activated, how rapidly the resulting substances react, and how the cell repairs the consequences of unwanted oxidation.

Consider a leaf on a bright afternoon. Under moderate illumination, electrons extracted from water can be delivered to NADP⁺ and the reducing power can be consumed by carbon assimilation. If the soil dries, stomata tend to close and the internal supply of carbon dioxide may decline. Absorbed photons do not necessarily decline at the same rate. The chloroplast must therefore dissipate excess excitation, provide alternative destinations for electrons, or adjust its metabolism. If these compensations become insufficient, electrons and excitation energy can reach molecular oxygen through routes that generate reactive oxygen species. A similar mismatch between input and productive use can occur in mitochondria during a disturbance of respiration. The initiating stress is water deficit in the first example, but the additional chemical burden is oxidative stress.

Oxidation and reduction are complementary descriptions of electron transfer. A substance that loses electrons is oxidized; one that gains electrons is reduced. When a molecule changes oxidation state, its reaction partner must undergo the opposite electron transfer. Oxygen is a powerful electron acceptor, but it does not normally accept all available electrons indiscriminately. Biological catalysts help impose reaction specificity, making the oxidation of organic fuels productive rather than uncontrolled. In respiration, oxygen receives four electrons and four protons at the terminal oxidase to form water. Complete reduction is distinct from one-electron reduction of oxygen, which generates superoxide. The distinction explains why oxygen consumption alone is not evidence of damaging ROS production.

Reactive oxygen species, abbreviated ROS, are oxygen-containing substances with chemical reactivities that differ from those of ordinary molecular oxygen. The term includes both free radicals and nonradical species. A free radical contains at least one unpaired electron. The superoxide radical anion and the hydroxyl radical are important examples. Hydrogen peroxide is not a free radical; nevertheless it is an ROS because it can oxidize selected biological targets and can be transformed into more aggressive radicals. Singlet oxygen is an electronically excited state of molecular oxygen and must not be described as an obligatory intermediate in the reduction of oxygen to water. These distinctions are useful because the most suitable defense against one species is not necessarily useful against another.

A healthy cell maintains redox homeostasis: a regulated dynamic balance among the production, transformation and consumption of oxidants and reductants. Homeostasis is not the absence of chemical activity. For instance, a chloroplast that generates peroxide continuously can remain healthy if its scavenging and repair systems handle the flux. Conversely a cell with a modest production rate can experience injury if its enzymes or reductant supply are impaired. The rate at which ROS are formed is only one side of the balance. Oxidative stress refers to a disturbance of this control, involving disruptive oxidation, altered redox signaling or molecular injury. It should not be defined solely by whether an arbitrary concentration is exceeded.

The idea of redox homeostasis requires us to distinguish three outcomes of oxidation. The first is productive chemical transformation, such as oxygen reduction during respiration. The second is regulated signaling, in which a localized and limited oxidation event alters the activity of a protein or signaling system. The third is injury, in which oxidation disrupts structures or functions in ways that the cell cannot readily reverse. These categories may overlap over time. A rapid burst of hydrogen peroxide can initiate protective gene expression; a sustained burst in the same tissue can overwhelm the defense induced by that signal. Consequently, a diagram in which every ROS arrow leads to cell death is as misleading as one in which every ROS arrow leads to adaptation.

A useful way to understand the origin of antioxidant systems is to consider the evolutionary association of oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic metabolism with oxygen chemistry. Photosynthetic organisms exploit the energy of light to drive water oxidation, while aerobic organisms exploit oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor. Repeated exposure to partially reduced oxygen created strong selective pressure for systems able to limit radical formation, convert oxidants into less reactive products and restore oxidized molecules. Modern plants inherit and elaborate multiple such systems in chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes, cytoplasm and extracellular spaces. Their distribution reflects the fact that no single antioxidant system could control every possible source of oxidation.

The chemical environment of each compartment matters. A thylakoid membrane contains pigments, electron carriers and polyunsaturated lipids exposed to light. A peroxisome contains oxidases that transfer electrons directly to oxygen. The apoplast contains the cell wall and extracellular fluid, with a redox environment unlike that of the cytosol. Even when two compartments contain hydrogen peroxide, the biological implications can differ according to neighboring enzymes, membranes, buffering capacity and transport routes. It is therefore more accurate to speak of a cellular redox network than a single uniform pool of oxidative stress.

Throughout this chapter, ask four questions whenever an oxidative response is described. Which oxygen species is being formed? What reaction or cellular site produces it? Which molecular targets or signaling systems encounter it? What processes remove it or repair its consequences? Answering all four makes it possible to reason about seemingly contradictory results, such as a plant with increased antioxidant activity but greater oxidative injury. Increased activity can be a response to the injury rather than proof that the response is adequate.

The historical development of plant antioxidant biology illustrates a change in scientific interpretation. Early work emphasized protection against oxygen toxicity. As investigators identified electron transport reactions that generate superoxide and peroxide, the problem became one of assigning ROS sources to particular organelles. Work on chloroplast glutathione reductase and ascorbate-dependent peroxide removal subsequently established a pathway connecting ROS elimination to the supply of cellular reducing equivalents. More recent genetic and physiological studies showed that deliberate ROS production participates in communication between cells. These discoveries did not invalidate the earlier protective view; they expanded it. The same chemical systems that constrain damaging oxidation also help define the duration and position of a redox signal.

2. The four principal reactive oxygen species

Ordinary oxygen is often represented by the formula O₂, but its electronic structure is unusual: its ground state contains two unpaired electrons. Spin constraints limit its direct reaction with many molecules whose electrons are paired. This provides a measure of kinetic protection in biological environments. Electron transfer from a suitable donor can generate superoxide, O₂•⁻, by adding one electron. Its negative charge makes movement across the hydrophobic core of lipid membranes unfavorable, and its reactivity and local fate depend on pH and nearby targets. The protonated form, hydroperoxyl radical HO₂•, is more membrane soluble; its fraction increases as pH falls. At typical cytosolic pH the superoxide anion predominates. These features prevent us from treating superoxide as a freely equilibrating messenger throughout an entire plant.

Superoxide is produced when reduced electron carriers or enzymes deliver individual electrons to oxygen. It can damage susceptible metal-containing proteins, including some iron–sulfur centers, but it can also be converted efficiently into hydrogen peroxide by superoxide dismutase. Dismutation is a reaction in which two molecules of the same starting species undergo different fates: one superoxide is oxidized back to oxygen and the other is reduced to peroxide. Protons provide the hydrogen atoms of the peroxide molecule. The balanced overall reaction is 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Counting oxygen atoms gives four on each side; counting charge gives zero overall. The equation is useful for exam calculations because a flux of two superoxide molecules produces one peroxide molecule when this route predominates.

Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, contains an oxygen–oxygen bond and is neither a radical nor merely a neutral version of superoxide. Its lifetime is generally longer than those of extremely reactive radicals, although that lifetime varies substantially with local enzymes and substrates. Some aquaporins can facilitate H₂O₂ passage across biological membranes. This property allows peroxide to participate in signaling between compartments, but does not imply that every aquaporin conducts it or that all peroxide molecules reach the nucleus. Peroxidases, catalase, peroxiredoxins and other reactions can consume it before it travels far. The resulting gradient can convey local information while restricting injury.

Hydrogen peroxide can react selectively with susceptible sulfur-containing groups in proteins. A cysteine side chain may be converted from a thiol to a sulfenic acid or form a disulfide bond. Such transformations can alter enzyme activity, protein interactions or signaling. The reaction rate depends strongly on the chemical environment of the cysteine and on whether it is present as a more reactive thiolate. Some proteins are poor direct peroxide targets; redox relays can help transmit oxidation from a particularly reactive protein to another target. Reversible changes of this kind differ from widespread protein unfolding or irreversible oxidative damage.

The hydroxyl radical, •OH, is generated through reactions including the Fenton reaction, in which reduced iron reacts with hydrogen peroxide. Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH is a simplified balanced representation. The hydroxyl radical reacts with many nearby biomolecules at extremely high rates. Its short effective range makes the place of its formation especially important. A radical generated near a DNA molecule may attack that DNA before a scavenger located elsewhere can intercept it. A cell cannot rely on a dedicated freely diffusing enzyme to remove every hydroxyl radical after formation. Its major protective strategies are to restrict peroxide accumulation, control the availability of catalytic metal ions and prevent vulnerable targets from being exposed to the radical-generating conditions.

Transition metals illustrate the importance of chemical context. Iron is essential for electron transport, chlorophyll-related metabolism and the activity of several antioxidant enzymes. Iron incorporated into a properly organized protein or stored in ferritin is not equivalent to freely available redox-active iron. When injury releases or redistributes metal ions, the probability of unwanted Fenton chemistry can rise. A plant can thus suffer more oxidative injury without a proportionate increase in measured bulk peroxide concentration. Changes in the availability of catalytic iron can alter the fraction of peroxide converted into aggressive radicals.

The fourth major species, singlet oxygen, is written ¹O₂. Unlike superoxide and peroxide, it arises in chloroplasts largely through the transfer of excitation energy from a sensitizer such as triplet chlorophyll to ground-state oxygen. A photon first excites chlorophyll. Most absorbed excitation in a functioning photosystem is transferred to a reaction center or dissipated. If excitation reaches a triplet state, interaction with oxygen can transfer energy without transferring a net electron. The resulting singlet oxygen has different electronic properties and reacts readily with unsaturated lipids, pigments and other compounds. Its short effective range and distinct origin explain why membrane-associated defenses such as carotenoids are so important in illuminated chloroplasts.

It is possible for singlet oxygen and superoxide to rise simultaneously in a highly illuminated leaf, but the two need not arise from the same immediate process. Singlet oxygen often reflects excess excited chlorophyll or charge recombination associated with photosystem II, whereas superoxide can arise through reduction of oxygen by electron carriers, notably on the acceptor side of photosystem I. An intervention that decreases excitation transfer to oxygen might suppress singlet oxygen without eliminating electron leakage. Likewise increasing superoxide dismutase does not directly quench excited oxygen. The specificity of the chemistry provides a basis for selecting the appropriate antioxidant and interpreting mutants.

It is tempting to rank all ROS in order of danger. A more informative comparison concerns both chemical reactivity and effective lifetime. Hydroxyl radicals react rapidly and close to their place of formation; hydrogen peroxide is relatively selective and may travel farther before reacting. Singlet oxygen can cause substantial damage in a membrane despite its short diffusion range. Superoxide has its own chemical targets and can serve as a precursor of peroxide. Which species matters most depends on the biological question. If the question concerns oxidation of distant signaling proteins, peroxide is often a plausible candidate. If it concerns damage adjacent to a metal center, local radical generation is especially relevant.

The identity of a species cannot always be determined from the injury it produces. Lipid oxidation, for example, can result from several ROS or from enzymatic reactions. A membrane with increased lipid hydroperoxide concentration is evidence of altered lipid oxidation, but not automatically evidence of hydroxyl radical production in a specific organelle. Similarly, the oxidation of a fluorescent probe may be caused by an indirect product rather than the oxygen species that initiated the process. This is why independent measurements, species-selective controls and knowledge of the reaction chemistry are important.

A quantitative distinction between pool size and flux can be illustrated with an idealized peroxide-producing compartment. If it forms 8 nmol of superoxide per minute and all of this superoxide undergoes dismutation, the associated peroxide production is 4 nmol per minute. If a peroxidase removes 3 nmol of peroxide per minute and catalase removes 1 nmol per minute, the peroxide pool does not increase under those assumptions. The active chemistry could be missed by an assay that measures only peroxide concentration at the end of the experiment. If peroxide removal falls to 2 nmol per minute while production remains unchanged, the pool rises initially by 2 nmol per minute. Actual removal rates depend on concentrations and enzyme kinetics, so the constant-flux approximation is suitable only over a limited interval.

3. ROS production in chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are central to oxidative stress because they combine high-energy photochemistry, oxygen evolution, electron transfer and abundant vulnerable membrane lipids. The reactions of photosystem II remove electrons from water, releasing protons and oxygen. The electrons pass through plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f and plastocyanin to photosystem I, where a second light-driven reaction raises their reducing power. Ferredoxin and ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase help deliver electrons to NADP⁺. Productive linear electron transport requires coordination between the capacity to absorb light, the availability of electron acceptors and the downstream reactions consuming ATP and NADPH.

When carbon fixation is restricted, reduced intermediates may accumulate. However, the relationship between a reduced carrier and ROS production is not a universal proportionality. Electron transfer to oxygen depends on the energetics of the donor, the accessibility of oxygen and competition with alternative acceptors. On the acceptor side of photosystem I, oxygen can receive electrons and form superoxide in a reaction associated with the classical Mehler reaction. Superoxide dismutase converts it to hydrogen peroxide. Chloroplast ascorbate peroxidase can then remove the peroxide using ascorbate as electron donor. The water–water cycle describes a coupled route in which electrons originating from water are ultimately used in oxygen reduction and peroxide removal, returning oxygen-derived intermediates toward water.

The water–water cycle does not mean that peroxide is never generated. On the contrary, peroxide is a chemical intermediate that must be removed promptly. Nor does it mean that all excess photosynthetic electrons go through this route. In intact plants, cyclic electron flow, photorespiration, alternative metabolic sinks and nonphotochemical dissipation also contribute to photoprotection. Their relative importance depends on environmental conditions, species and developmental state. The essential point for a student is that preventing ROS formation and scavenging ROS after formation are complementary strategies.

Photosystem II presents a different hazard. When excitation energy cannot be used efficiently, chlorophyll may populate a triplet excited state. Energy transfer from triplet chlorophyll to oxygen forms singlet oxygen. In antenna complexes, carotenoids can quench chlorophyll triplet states and help prevent oxygen excitation. Carotenoids also participate in energy dissipation mechanisms that reduce the probability of generating harmful excited states. Damage is especially problematic near reaction centers, where charge separation and recombination can produce excited intermediates. The D1 protein is an important component of photosystem II that can be damaged and replaced during the PSII repair cycle.

Photoinhibition refers to a reduction in photosynthetic function associated with illumination, particularly where photodamage exceeds repair or sustained regulatory quenching depresses measured efficiency. It is not synonymous with ROS accumulation. A plant can lower its fluorescence-based efficiency temporarily through protective energy dissipation without suffering irreversible destruction of photosystem II. Conversely a leaf with impaired protein synthesis may accumulate damaged PSII centers because repair slows even at a light intensity that a healthy leaf tolerates. Distinguishing damage from failed repair is therefore part of interpreting measurements of photosynthetic performance.

A common experiment shifts plants from low light to high light and compares wild-type plants with a mutant unable to execute a specific photoprotective process. A lower maximum quantum efficiency of PSII in the mutant after illumination is compatible with enhanced photoinhibition. It does not by itself establish that singlet oxygen caused the phenotype. To strengthen that conclusion, researchers need suitable controls and measurements more directly related to singlet oxygen or its oxidation products. Recovery after returning to low light provides additional information: rapid recovery is compatible with reversible regulation or efficient repair, while persistent depression suggests sustained quenching or injury. The initial dark-adapted state, temperature and leaf age must also be matched.

An additional complication is the relationship between photosynthesis and respiration. Chloroplast carbon metabolism produces substrates and reductants that are exchanged indirectly with other cellular compartments. Mitochondria can help maintain metabolic flexibility by oxidizing substrates produced from photosynthesis. Photorespiration can consume energy and reducing power but produces peroxide in peroxisomes. Thus an intervention that decreases chloroplast ROS may increase the burden of peroxide removal in another organelle. A measurement made on whole leaf tissue integrates these processes and cannot reveal which organelle initiated the change without further evidence.

The balance of light capture and carbon assimilation explains why environmental stresses often converge on chloroplast redox disturbances. During drought, stomatal closure restricts CO₂ uptake; under chilling, enzyme reactions consuming photochemical products may slow more than light absorption; under heat, protein damage can impair several metabolic systems. None of these conditions implies that ROS must always increase in every tissue. A shade leaf, for example, experiences a different excitation burden from a sun-exposed leaf. A plant that efficiently lowers light absorption, dissipates excitation or redirects electrons can limit ROS generation despite severe external stress. These are testable physiological differences, not simply labels of tolerance.

Consider two plants grown under identical photon flux. Plant A increases nonphotochemical quenching when CO₂ supply becomes limiting. Plant B has poor quenching but high ascorbate peroxidase activity. The first plant reduces the probability that excess excitation produces singlet oxygen. The second improves removal of peroxide after production. It is not possible to infer which plant will perform better under every stress, because the interventions act on different branches of oxygen chemistry. A mixed stress may require both defenses. This distinction is especially valuable for questions asking whether a single engineered antioxidant can protect all plant compartments.

Chloroplast antioxidants also depend on the energy system they protect. Ascorbate regeneration can use reducing equivalents ultimately supplied by metabolism, including photosynthetic NADPH. During severe stress, the impairment of electron transport or carbon metabolism may restrict reductant supply even if antioxidant enzymes remain abundant. This creates a feedback problem: the ability to remove peroxide depends partly on the integrity of the machinery being protected. An enzyme assay performed with saturating substrate and NADPH in a test tube therefore measures capacity under assay conditions rather than guaranteed operation in the stressed chloroplast.

The chloroplast sends information about its internal state to the nucleus through retrograde signaling. Different ROS and redox disturbances can stimulate distinct yet overlapping responses, including changes in nuclear gene expression. A short-lived species such as singlet oxygen need not physically reach the nucleus to influence transcription. Local oxidation can generate secondary chemical signals or trigger signaling proteins that transmit information. This is a general principle of intracellular communication: the messenger detected at a distant site need not be identical to the original initiating molecule.

4. Mitochondria, peroxisomes and the cell surface

Mitochondria provide ATP by coupling electron transfer to proton movement and oxidative phosphorylation. Complexes of the respiratory chain accept reducing equivalents from NADH and other metabolic substrates and transfer electrons through ubiquinone and cytochromes toward oxygen. Complex IV catalyzes the controlled reduction of oxygen to water. In some circumstances, reduced components of the chain can instead transfer individual electrons to oxygen. Complex I and complex III are important potential sites of mitochondrial superoxide generation. Other mitochondrial enzymes can also contribute, and the relative contribution of each site is not fixed across all plant tissues and conditions.

When the supply of respiratory substrates exceeds the capacity of downstream reactions, electron carriers can become highly reduced. A high proton motive force can also constrain further proton-pumping electron transport. These changes may favor electron leakage under some conditions. However, severely damaged respiration may decrease total electron delivery, so a decline in oxygen consumption need not always be accompanied by an increase in ROS. A student should examine the specific site of inhibition, direction of electron flow, availability of oxygen and physiological state instead of assuming a simple inverse relationship between respiration and ROS formation.

Plant mitochondria possess alternative oxidase, abbreviated AOX, which accepts electrons from ubiquinol and reduces oxygen to water without passing them through complexes III and IV. This route bypasses two proton-pumping steps, so less proton motive force and therefore less ATP are conserved per electron pair than through the usual cytochrome pathway, all else being equal. AOX can help oxidize an excessively reduced ubiquinone pool and thereby reduce the tendency toward some forms of ROS generation. It is not itself a superoxide dismutase or a peroxide-scavenging enzyme. Its protective effect arises upstream from its influence on the organization of electron flow. The distinction between ROS prevention and ROS removal reappears here.

An AOX inhibitor experiment illustrates the limitations of causal interpretation. If inhibition increases a mitochondrial ROS indicator and decreases respiration through the alternative pathway, the result supports a role of AOX in limiting electron-chain reduction under those conditions. Yet inhibitors can have other effects, and whole cells may alter substrate oxidation or antioxidant responses. A stronger design compares genetic loss of AOX, restoration by a functional transgene and suitable controls for chemical treatment. An unchanged ROS indicator in a mutant does not necessarily exclude AOX-mediated protection: another pathway may compensate or the experimental conditions may not generate the relevant electron pressure.

Peroxisomes differ fundamentally from mitochondria. They contain several oxidases that transfer electrons directly to oxygen, frequently yielding hydrogen peroxide as a normal metabolic product. In a photosynthesizing C₃ leaf, an especially important source is glycolate oxidase in photorespiration. Oxygenation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate by Rubisco produces 2-phosphoglycolate, which is processed to glycolate in the chloroplast. Glycolate enters the peroxisome, where glycolate oxidase converts it to glyoxylate while reducing oxygen to hydrogen peroxide. The peroxide production is therefore linked to a necessary metabolic salvage pathway rather than simply to random electron leakage.

Catalase is abundant in many plant peroxisomes and provides a high-capacity mechanism for peroxide removal. The reaction is 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. For every two peroxide molecules consumed, one oxygen molecule is released. Unlike a peroxidase reaction with ascorbate, catalase does not require a stoichiometric external electron donor in its catalatic reaction. This makes it well suited to situations in which large peroxide fluxes accompany oxidase activity. Nevertheless catalase does not prevent the initial production of peroxide, and other scavenging systems contribute to peroxisomal redox control.

The importance of photorespiratory peroxide can be explored through a plant with reduced catalase capacity. Under conditions that favor Rubisco oxygenation, such as lower CO₂ availability relative to oxygen, glycolate oxidation and associated peroxide formation may be substantial. The catalase-deficient plant may therefore experience greater redox disturbance or injury than a wild type. Growing both genotypes under elevated CO₂ can suppress photorespiration and may alleviate the phenotype. The logic of this conditional rescue is stronger than observing peroxide accumulation alone: it links the defect in removal to a predicted metabolic source. Elevated CO₂ also alters other physiological processes, so the result must be interpreted with appropriate controls.

Peroxisomes in germinating oil-rich seeds carry out fatty acid β-oxidation, in which acyl-CoA oxidase can transfer electrons to oxygen and generate peroxide. Such tissue is not primarily exposed to photosynthetic high light, yet it may have a significant oxidative burden. This example shows why antioxidant defenses cannot be understood only as adaptations to chloroplast activity. The predominant source of ROS changes with organ type, metabolic state and developmental stage. A germinating seed, root meristem and mature leaf all require redox protection for different reasons.

ROS can also be produced deliberately at the plasma membrane. Plant respiratory burst oxidase homologs, or RBOHs, are NADPH oxidases. They accept electrons from cytosolic NADPH and transfer them through membrane-associated cofactors to oxygen on the extracellular side, forming superoxide in the apoplast. Dismutation then produces peroxide. Regulatory domains of RBOH proteins respond to calcium and phosphorylation, connecting their activity with stress perception. The enzyme is not a mitochondrial respiratory complex despite its historical name. Its function is to generate a regulated ROS flux at the cell surface.

The location of RBOH activity is crucial. A burst of extracellular superoxide is not the same as a burst of superoxide in the chloroplast stroma. The cell wall and extracellular space contain different scavengers and molecular targets, and apoplastic peroxide can participate in processes such as wall crosslinking, defense and communication across the plasma membrane. Some cell-wall peroxidases can consume peroxide in one reaction context and contribute to ROS production in another. Their effect must be interpreted in terms of substrates and reaction conditions rather than assigned permanently to a single category.

In an immune response, recognition of a microbial pattern can activate kinases and calcium-dependent events that stimulate RBOH-mediated ROS production. The early apoplastic burst is often measurable within minutes of treatment. In drought-related guard-cell signaling, particular RBOH isoforms can also contribute to ROS-dependent signaling downstream of hormonal regulation. These examples illustrate a shared molecular mechanism used in different biological situations; the detailed immune receptor pathways and ABA responses belong to subsequent nodes. Here the central question is how a plant creates an oxidizing signal at a defined location without allowing it to cause indiscriminate damage.

Other reactions, including those associated with protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum, oxidases in the cell wall and metabolic reactions in the cytosol, can contribute to the overall ROS network. Their mention is important because the four major sources in this node are representative rather than exhaustive. In an experiment, a residual ROS signal in an RBOH mutant does not prove that the mutation was ineffective. The remaining signal may come from other enzymes or organelles, or may reflect incomplete specificity of the measurement. The useful inference is the fraction of a defined response that depends on the perturbed component, not the claim that one enzyme accounts for all ROS.

When a plant experiences combined stresses, sources interact. For example, salinity alters ion balance, osmotic conditions and metabolic demands; chloroplast electron acceptor limitation may increase at the same time as signaling through plasma membrane oxidases. Mitochondrial respiration can adjust in response to changes in carbon availability, while peroxisomal reactions continue their own metabolic production of peroxide. A whole-cell antioxidant strategy must therefore be distributed and coordinated. The next sections examine the damage these sources can cause and the protective mechanisms that distinguish productive oxidation from uncontrolled injury.

Evidence from conditional catalase deficiency

Queval and colleagues (2007) investigated Arabidopsis plants deficient in the CAT2 catalase isoform. They asked how peroxide generated during photorespiration influences plant growth, redox state and the conditions under which injury develops. A key comparison exploited carbon dioxide concentration: elevated CO₂ suppresses Rubisco oxygenation, reducing the photorespiratory burden without restoring the missing catalase gene. Plants grown under high CO₂ were compared with plants exposed to air, including transfer between these environments. The catalase-deficient plants displayed conditional redox and growth phenotypes under photorespiratory conditions, while high CO₂ alleviated these effects. The experiments also showed that day length mattered for the manifestation of cell death. Thus peroxide-associated redox changes and tissue death were not equivalent outcomes. The conditional phenotype supports a connection between catalase capacity and photorespiratory peroxide, while the effect of photoperiod warns against assuming that catalase deficiency inevitably produces visible lesions. An experiment based only on ambient-air growth would have missed the importance of physiological context.

5. Oxidative injury and molecular repair

A plant membrane is an organized barrier containing lipids and proteins that establish electrochemical gradients, permit selective transport and provide a suitable environment for enzymes. Many plant membrane lipids contain polyunsaturated fatty acids. Their double bonds help determine membrane fluidity, but also create sites at which oxidation can occur. Oxidative injury to membranes is consequential because disruption of even a small region can interfere with energy conservation and ion homeostasis across a much larger cellular system.

The radical chain mechanism of lipid peroxidation has three stages: initiation, propagation and termination. Represent an oxidizable lipid as LH, where H denotes an abstractable hydrogen atom. During initiation, a reactive radical abstracts hydrogen, leaving a lipid radical L•. This radical reacts with oxygen to form a lipid peroxyl radical LOO•. The peroxyl radical can abstract a hydrogen atom from a neighboring lipid, generating a lipid hydroperoxide LOOH and another lipid radical. The new radical continues the chain. One initiation event can therefore lead to oxidation of multiple lipids. The chain is terminated when radicals react to yield nonradical products or when a chain-breaking antioxidant donates a hydrogen atom without efficiently continuing propagation.

This mechanism explains why the magnitude of membrane injury need not correspond exactly to the number of initiating radicals. A radical produced at a membrane surface may initiate a chain reaction extending through adjacent lipids. Susceptibility depends on fatty-acid composition, oxygen availability, metal chemistry and chain-breaking antioxidants. In a simplified comparison a membrane with more vulnerable polyunsaturated chains may undergo greater propagation, all else being equal. But living plants can compensate by changing tocopherol levels, lipid turnover and other defenses. Unsaturation is also essential for maintaining functional membranes at low temperatures.

Singlet oxygen can oxidize lipids without the same initial hydrogen abstraction as a classical radical chain. Lipoxygenases also catalyze oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids during normal metabolism and signaling. Some lipid oxidation products serve as precursors or signals for defense. An increase in lipid hydroperoxide abundance therefore does not establish that nonenzymatic radical propagation was the sole cause. The origin, identity and fate of lipid oxidation products must be distinguished.

Lipid hydroperoxides can decompose, producing reactive aldehydes and other products. Malondialdehyde, often abbreviated MDA, is used as a marker of lipid oxidation. Secondary products can react with proteins at sites beyond the original membrane injury. The initial event can therefore lead to secondary damage with a different chemical mechanism. Measurements based on thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances are widely used as approximate indicators of lipid peroxidation, but are not entirely specific for MDA and can be affected by interfering substances in plant extracts.

One consequence of membrane injury is increased electrolyte leakage. Cells normally maintain ion distributions through membrane pumps, channels and selective permeability. When membrane integrity deteriorates, ions can escape into a bathing solution, increasing its electrical conductivity. This measurement reflects a functional property of tissue. It does not identify the initiating ROS or prove that a particular lipid was oxidized. Heat, mechanical injury and other disturbances can also increase leakage. An experiment linking oxidative stress to membrane injury should combine electrolyte leakage with independent evidence of oxidation and handling controls.

Proteins perform catalytic, transport, structural and regulatory functions through organized amino acid side chains. The effect of oxidation depends on which residue is modified, its location and whether the modification can be reversed. Cysteine residues at an active site can be particularly important because changing the chemical state of sulfur can alter catalytic activity. Reversible thiol oxidation can be a regulatory switch, whereas extensive oxidation may disrupt the active site and suppress metabolism. The presence of an oxidized cysteine alone is not proof of damage.

Methionine can be oxidized to methionine sulfoxide. Methionine sulfoxide reductases can reverse certain forms of this modification using cellular reducing systems. Cysteine disulfides can often be reduced by thioredoxins. Many forms of carbonylation and extensive cysteine oxidation, however, are difficult or impossible to reverse through ordinary reducing pathways. Irreversibly damaged proteins may need to be degraded and replaced. A stress-tolerant cell requires both antioxidant chemistry and functioning protein quality control.

Imagine that a Calvin cycle enzyme is inactivated by oxidation. Carbon assimilation declines and consumption of reducing power may fall, potentially increasing electron pressure in the photosynthetic chain. If ROS production increases, the original injury is reinforced. Similarly, oxidation of mitochondrial proteins may impair respiration and alter the reduction state of electron carriers. Such feedback loops connect molecular injury with energy metabolism.

Hydroxyl radicals generated near DNA can attack bases or the deoxyribose backbone, potentially producing modified bases, abasic sites and strand breaks. Oxidized guanine, including 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine, is a well-known example. If a damaged base is not repaired before replication, incorrect pairing can contribute to mutation. But DNA damage is not equivalent to a heritable mutation: the lesion may be repaired, the cell may cease dividing or it may die.

Plant DNA occurs in the nucleus, chloroplasts and mitochondria. The proximity of organellar genomes to ROS-generating metabolism makes local protection and DNA repair important. Base excision repair removes certain oxidized bases. A DNA glycosylase recognizes and removes a damaged base, initiating processing of the site and restoration of the DNA strand. The detailed repair pathways belong to Unit 03. Here the essential point is that the impact of ROS depends on lesion formation and repair capacity together.

In severe stress, membrane leakage alters ion gradients, protein oxidation impairs enzyme activities, and nucleic acid lesions threaten gene expression and replication. Energy production can decline at the same time as demands for repair increase. The transition from reversible disturbance to irreversible injury therefore depends on several interconnected processes rather than a universal peroxide concentration.

Consider two leaves with similar peroxide levels after treatment. One recovers normal photosynthesis; the other shows persistent electrolyte leakage and poor photosynthetic activity. Different membrane composition, catalytic metal availability, prior oxidative history, repair capacity or spatial ROS distribution could explain the contrast. A single endpoint cannot distinguish among these possibilities. A time course of molecular markers, physiological measurements and recovery is more informative than a single value.

6. Enzymatic antioxidant systems

The immediate problem in controlling partially reduced oxygen is often to convert a reactive species into a form that can be handled by other reactions. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) accelerates the conversion of superoxide into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen. It does not complete reduction of oxygen to water. It changes the chemical identity and distribution of the oxidative burden.

SOD operates through a metal center cycling between oxidation states. In one part of the cycle, superoxide transfers an electron to the metal and becomes oxygen. In another, superoxide accepts an electron from the reduced metal and, with protons, becomes peroxide. The enzyme is regenerated. The overall stoichiometry is two superoxide radicals per peroxide molecule, regardless of the particular metal involved.

Plants possess classes of SOD named according to their metal cofactors. Copper/zinc SODs are found in the cytosol and chloroplasts of many plants, iron SODs occur notably in chloroplasts, and manganese SOD is commonly associated with the mitochondrial matrix. Additional localization patterns exist. These are common distributions, not a universal map for all species and tissues. Isoenzymes permit local control and adaptation to differences in cofactor availability.

Overexpressing a cytosolic SOD may not protect an electron carrier exposed to superoxide inside a chloroplast. The superoxide anion has restricted membrane permeability and can be converted or consumed before reaching the ectopic enzyme. Chloroplast-targeted and cytosolic SODs may have equal catalytic activity in an extract but different physiological effects in vivo. Comparisons must therefore include protein targeting, expression and substrate access.

An increase in SOD activity is not universally beneficial. If the fraction of superoxide converted to peroxide rises but peroxide clearance remains limiting, the result can be a shift in oxidative chemistry rather than complete resolution. Peroxide can contribute to hydroxyl radical generation in the presence of catalytic iron. The outcome depends on competing reactions and the preexisting balance between peroxide production and removal. Antioxidant engineering should be assessed as a network intervention, not an isolated enzyme increase.

Catalase converts two peroxide molecules into two water molecules and one oxygen molecule. Its heme-containing catalytic center transiently becomes highly oxidized. A second peroxide molecule restores the original state of the enzyme. One peroxide molecule is effectively reduced while the other is oxidized. The overall catalatic reaction is a disproportionation and does not consume NADPH, glutathione or ascorbate in stoichiometric amounts.

Catalase is important in peroxisomes, where oxidases can continuously generate peroxide. If a hypothetical oxidase forms 60 µmol of peroxide per hour and catalase accounts for all removal, catalase consumes 60 µmol peroxide and produces 30 µmol oxygen per hour. This oxygen release is not evidence of photosynthesis. If catalase capacity drops below production and other scavengers do not compensate, peroxide can accumulate or move to other compartments.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) differs from catalase in its requirement for an external reductant. APX oxidizes ascorbate by one electron at a time, producing monodehydroascorbate radicals. Two one-electron donations are required to reduce one hydrogen peroxide molecule to water. APX therefore relies on the continuous availability of reduced ascorbate. Chloroplast APX is particularly susceptible to inactivation under ascorbate depletion, which reinforces the importance of recycling.

APX isoforms occur in chloroplasts, cytosol, mitochondria and other plant compartments. Stromal and thylakoid-associated chloroplast APX help handle peroxide near photosynthetic machinery. Catalase is generally suited to processing large peroxide fluxes, whereas APX can be effective at lower peroxide concentrations when its reductant is available. Exact kinetic constants depend on enzyme, pH, temperature and assay conditions. There is no universal Michaelis constant for all isoenzymes.

A simplified Michaelis–Menten model illustrates capacity: v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]). Here v is reaction rate, Vmax is the limiting rate, [S] is substrate concentration and Km is the concentration giving half the limiting rate, assuming other substrates are constant. Consider a hypothetical peroxidase with Vmax of 12 nmol min⁻¹ and apparent Km of 4 µM peroxide. At 4 µM its predicted rate is 6 nmol min⁻¹; at 12 µM the rate is 9 nmol min⁻¹. A threefold increase in concentration results in only a 1.5-fold increase in rate. If production reaches 13 nmol min⁻¹, this scavenger alone cannot maintain steady state in the model. Real catalase and APX kinetics are more complex, and reductant supply can itself be limiting.

Peroxiredoxins reduce peroxides using reactive cysteine residues and can be restored by thioredoxin-related systems. Plant glutathione peroxidase-like proteins often preferentially use thioredoxin as electron donor despite their traditional name. These systems can handle peroxide or organic hydroperoxides, with isoform-specific preferences. They also affect redox signaling. The physiological electron donor should not be inferred solely from the name of an animal homolog.

Cell-wall class III peroxidases can oxidize phenolic substrates using peroxide, contributing to wall modification. Under other reaction conditions some participate in ROS-generating chemistry. Their net effect is context dependent. An assay of peroxidase activity cannot establish whether an enzyme consumes or generates ROS in every physiological setting.

SOD assays can measure inhibition of a superoxide-dependent reporter reaction. Catalase assays can measure peroxide disappearance or oxygen formation. APX assays can follow ascorbate oxidation. Measurements are commonly normalized to protein mass, but the definition of a unit varies with the assay. SOD units defined through inhibition cannot be added to catalase units defined as substrate consumption. An extract may have active enzymes even if intact tissue has inadequate reductant supply, or extraction may inactivate a labile enzyme. Appropriate blanks, comparable handling, linear time intervals and consistent normalization are required.

7. Ascorbate–glutathione recycling

Ascorbate peroxidase can reduce peroxide only if reduced ascorbate is available. In a healthy cell, ascorbate is not consumed once and discarded after every oxidation event. It participates in a recycling network transferring reducing equivalents from metabolism to peroxide. The ascorbate–glutathione cycle, often called the Foyer–Halliwell–Asada pathway, illustrates how small antioxidant molecules and enzymes cooperate.

Research by Foyer and Halliwell in 1976 connected chloroplast glutathione reductase with antioxidant metabolism. Subsequent identification of APX and the enzymes regenerating oxidized ascorbate helped establish a coordinated peroxide-scavenging pathway. The model explained how reducing power could protect photosynthetic machinery. Related reactions were later identified beyond chloroplasts, revealing the broad significance of antioxidant recycling throughout the plant.

The first step is APX. For every peroxide molecule reduced to water, two one-electron oxidation events are required. Reduced ascorbate donates an electron and forms monodehydroascorbate (MDHA), a comparatively manageable radical. The creation of a radical intermediate does not contradict antioxidant function. Controlled electron transfer can convert a problematic oxidant into an intermediate whose fate is more readily controlled.

MDHA can be reduced directly to ascorbate by monodehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR). This enzyme uses NADH or NADPH depending on the isoform and conditions. Two MDHA radicals require two one-electron reductions, so one two-electron equivalent from a nicotinamide nucleotide can in principle support their regeneration. Reduced ferredoxin can also contribute in chloroplasts. Thus glutathione oxidation is not obligatory for every peroxide molecule removed by APX.

Two MDHA radicals can instead disproportionate to form one ascorbate and one dehydroascorbate (DHA). DHA is the two-electron oxidized form of ascorbate. If DHA is not reduced, it can undergo further degradation, diminishing the recyclable pool. The total amount of ascorbate and the proportion maintained in its reduced form must be distinguished.

Dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR) uses reduced glutathione to regenerate ascorbate from DHA. Glutathione is a tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine and glycine with a distinctive γ-glutamyl linkage. Its cysteine thiol is central to its redox chemistry. Two GSH molecules provide the two electrons required to reduce DHA and yield one oxidized glutathione disulfide, GSSG. The simplified net reaction is DHA + 2 GSH → ascorbate + GSSG, with ionic forms dependent on pH.

Glutathione reductase (GR) restores GSSG to two GSH molecules using NADPH: GSSG + NADPH + H⁺ → 2 GSH + NADP⁺. The sulfur–sulfur bond is reduced to two thiols. GR connects maintenance of the glutathione pool to the availability of cellular reducing power. It does not synthesize glutathione from amino acids; de novo synthesis requires separate ATP-dependent reactions.

Combine the DHA route to derive the net reaction. APX consumes one peroxide and two ascorbate molecules, yielding two MDHA. Disproportionation returns one ascorbate and forms one DHA. DHAR consumes two GSH to regenerate the other ascorbate. GR consumes one NADPH to restore GSH. After canceling recycled intermediates, the net process is H₂O₂ + NADPH + H⁺ → 2 H₂O + NADP⁺. One two-electron reducing equivalent is needed per peroxide molecule fully reduced to water.

In a hypothetical compartment that removes 24 nmol peroxide per minute exclusively by APX and the DHA–DHAR–GR route, APX oxidizes 48 nmol ascorbate per minute. Disproportionation returns 24 nmol ascorbate and generates 24 nmol DHA. DHAR regenerates the remaining ascorbate, consuming 48 nmol GSH and producing 24 nmol GSSG. GR consumes 24 nmol NADPH per minute. If the pools remain constant, ascorbate and GSH are not net consumed. If NADPH supply fails to meet demand, oxidized pools can accumulate.

Real cells partition MDHA among direct reduction and disproportionation. GSH participates in additional reactions, while catalase and peroxiredoxins remove some peroxide. Measured NADPH consumption cannot be assigned entirely to the cycle. The accounting is useful only when the reaction boundaries and assumptions are specified.

GSH/GSSG is frequently used as an indicator of the glutathione redox state. However, the redox potential depends on absolute concentrations as well as the ratio because two GSH molecules form one GSSG. At fixed pH a simplified Nernst equation is E = E°′ + (RT/2F) ln(aGSSG/aGSH²). E is the potential, E°′ the transformed standard potential, R the gas constant, T absolute temperature, F the Faraday constant and a dimensionless activity. For dilute idealized solutions, activity can be approximated by concentration relative to the standard concentration.

At equal temperature and pH, compare a solution containing 2 mM GSH and 0.02 mM GSSG with a second containing 1 mM GSH and 0.01 mM GSSG. Both ratios equal 100. The reaction quotient in the second solution is twice that in the first. At 298 K the potential difference is (RT/2F) ln 2, approximately +8.9 mV, so the second solution has the more positive potential. This does not imply that every protein is at equilibrium with glutathione; cellular reaction networks can be kinetically separated.

Antioxidant pools must be replenished as well as recycled. Ascorbate biosynthesis in plants commonly uses the L-galactose pathway. Glutathione synthesis depends on cysteine, glutamate, glycine and ATP. Sulfur limitation may restrict cysteine supply and influence glutathione synthesis. The actual outcome depends on sulfur allocation and other responses. This provides a direct connection with the nitrogen and carbon metabolism studied earlier in Unit 06.

Whole-leaf assays sum pools across compartments and cell types. An important local redox change may not substantially alter the bulk value. Extraction can mix pools whose redox states were distinct in vivo. Genetically encoded redox-sensitive fluorescent proteins can provide compartment-specific information if properly calibrated, but each sensor must be interpreted according to its chemistry. A glutathione redox sensor does not directly measure every ROS.

Consider a GR-deficient plant under increasing peroxide burden. Restricted reduction of GSSG may oxidize the glutathione pool, limiting DHAR-dependent ascorbate recycling. Alternative isoforms and MDHAR can compensate. If the latter remains active, the ascorbate phenotype can be less severe than a simple linear diagram predicts. The branched structure of the pathway must be considered in genetic reasoning.

A plant may induce APX, DHAR and GR transcripts but still fail to maintain reduced ascorbate and GSH if metabolic energy and reductant generation collapse. The response can be protective yet insufficient. A strong physiological analysis combines antioxidant pool size, oxidation state, enzyme capacity and recovery. The cycle solves the fundamental problem of transferring reducing equivalents from metabolism to peroxide while retaining recyclable antioxidants.

How a pathway was established

In their 1976 paper, Foyer and Halliwell investigated the presence of glutathione and glutathione reductase in chloroplasts and proposed a role for this system in ascorbate metabolism. Detecting both a redox-active metabolite and the enzyme capable of regenerating its reduced form supplied an important basis for a functional model. The observations alone did not establish the flux through every reaction in an intact leaf. Identification of the enzymes of peroxide removal and ascorbate recycling supplied additional mechanistic links. This progression illustrates how a biochemical pathway is assembled from multiple lines of evidence: localization of the reactants, demonstration of feasible enzyme activities, stoichiometry of coupled reactions and physiological tests of the pathway under conditions that alter its demand. The pathway is useful precisely because it predicts the consequences of changes in reductant supply and enzyme activity, not merely because the enzymes are present in the same organelle.

8. Nonenzymatic antioxidants and membrane protection

The defense system described so far depends on enzymes that accelerate reactions with particular ROS. An enzyme is effective only where its substrates can reach it and where the enzyme itself remains functional. This creates a special problem in the hydrophobic interior of a membrane. Many soluble antioxidant enzymes cannot directly intercept a radical propagating among fatty-acid chains. Plants overcome this limitation by using small molecules with chemical properties suited to different cellular environments.

Carotenoids are conjugated pigments present in photosynthetic membranes. Their extended systems of alternating bonds allow them to absorb light and interact with excited electronic states. In photosynthetic antennae, a carotenoid can accept energy from a triplet chlorophyll molecule, returning the chlorophyll toward its ground state without allowing energy transfer to oxygen. The carotenoid then dissipates the excitation, largely through thermal processes. This photophysical protection acts before singlet oxygen has formed. The process is often called triplet quenching. Carotenoids can also quench singlet oxygen itself, accepting its excitation energy and returning oxygen toward the ground state.

Distinguishing physical quenching from chemical scavenging is important. In physical quenching, excitation energy is transferred to a carotenoid and the pigment can return to its original chemical state. It may participate repeatedly. Chemical reaction with singlet oxygen, by contrast, can oxidize the pigment and form products. These routes need not be mutually exclusive in a living membrane. The relative contribution depends on pigment position, accessibility, excitation dynamics and neighboring molecules. The term antioxidant includes both protection through energy transfer and protection through reaction chemistry.

The xanthophyll cycle is another aspect of carotenoid-associated protection. Under illumination that exceeds the capacity for productive photochemistry, acidification of the thylakoid lumen promotes conversion of violaxanthin through antheraxanthin toward zeaxanthin. Together with changes in antenna organization and the participation of PsbS in many higher plants, this favors rapid nonphotochemical dissipation. The process limits the population of excited chlorophyll states that could lead to harmful reactions. It is not simply a chemical scavenger acting on peroxide. The distinction connects this chapter with photoprotection in Subunit 6.A.

A plant lacking adequate carotenoid protection may be particularly susceptible to illumination even if its catalase activity is normal. Conversely a carotenoid-rich membrane cannot eliminate a large peroxide flux generated by peroxisomal glycolate oxidase. The two systems have different substrates and locations. In an examination problem, identify the initiating species and compartment before predicting which mutation or treatment should alter the phenotype. A high-light experiment might implicate several mechanisms at once, so measurements should distinguish excitation pressure, specific oxidation products and functional photosynthetic performance.

Tocopherols are lipid-soluble antioxidants associated with plant membranes, particularly plastid membranes. Their structure combines a chemically reactive chromanol group with a hydrophobic tail. The tail favors positioning in lipid-rich environments, while the reactive group can donate a hydrogen atom to a lipid peroxyl radical. The product is a lipid hydroperoxide and a tocopheroxyl radical. The latter is less likely to propagate lipid oxidation than the original lipid peroxyl radical. This is the basis of chain-breaking protection. Termination by tocopherol therefore differs from SOD-mediated superoxide dismutation and from catalase-mediated peroxide decomposition.

Tocopherol oxidation does not necessarily lead to permanent loss of antioxidant function. Reducing agents, including ascorbate in appropriate membrane-associated contexts, can participate in regeneration of the reduced tocopherol form. This interaction provides a chemical bridge between membrane protection and water-soluble antioxidant metabolism. If ascorbate recycling becomes inadequate, membrane defense may also be affected. However, the efficiency and routes of tocopherol regeneration depend on the local membrane and available reductants; the entire cellular antioxidant network should not be reduced to a single obligatory recycling reaction.

The location of a membrane antioxidant is as important as its total abundance. A tocopherol molecule located close to a propagating lipid radical may terminate the chain efficiently. A soluble scavenger confined to an aqueous compartment cannot necessarily intercept the same radical. Conversely, increasing lipid-soluble antioxidants may have little direct influence on a soluble peroxide signal originating at the plasma membrane. When a student interprets measurements of total antioxidant capacity in plant tissue, these differences in access must be considered. A chemical assay can combine molecules after extraction that would not interact in the same way in the intact cell.

Glutathione is a soluble antioxidant with roles beyond the ascorbate cycle. Its thiol group can participate in redox buffering and in reactions that protect protein thiols. Glutathione-dependent transferases can conjugate GSH to certain electrophilic compounds, including some reactive products of oxidative metabolism. The resulting conjugates may be further metabolized or transported. Such detoxification can consume glutathione rather than merely cycling between GSH and GSSG. Therefore the restoration of reduced glutathione by GR and the biosynthesis of new glutathione solve different problems. One restores reducing capacity after disulfide formation; the other replenishes material lost through turnover or conjugation.

Under oxidative conditions a protein cysteine can form a mixed disulfide with glutathione, a process called glutathionylation. In suitable contexts this reversible modification can protect a sensitive cysteine from more extensive oxidation or alter protein activity. Glutaredoxins contribute to the regulation of certain thiol-disulfide exchange reactions. It would be misleading to portray all glutathionylation as beneficial or all as damage. The biological effect depends on the protein target and the duration of modification. A regulatory enzyme may be transiently inhibited and subsequently reactivated when the redox environment is restored.

Ascorbate is often abundant in photosynthetic tissues. Its role as an APX substrate is central, but it also participates in other chemical reactions and serves as a cofactor for selected enzymes. Ascorbate is required for violaxanthin de-epoxidase activity in the xanthophyll cycle, providing an additional connection between soluble redox metabolism and photoprotection. A plant deficient in ascorbate may therefore show more than one type of light-related phenotype. Increased peroxide sensitivity, altered energy dissipation and changes in other ascorbate-dependent processes are all possible. The precise causal pathway must be established rather than inferred from the presence of a single metabolite deficiency.

Phenolic compounds are another diverse class of metabolites capable of interacting with oxidants or radicals in some contexts. Their chemistry depends on the arrangement of hydroxyl groups and other structural features. Flavonoids and related compounds can influence light screening, chemical defense and redox processes. Yet a high total phenolic content in an extract does not demonstrate that those molecules reach a particular intracellular ROS source. Vacuolar phenolics, for example, may be physically separated from chloroplast reactions. The detailed biosynthetic pathways belong to Subunit 6.G. Their significance here lies in the relationship between chemical properties, tissue location and protective function.

It is useful to classify protective molecules according to the immediate event they affect. A carotenoid can prevent formation of singlet oxygen by quenching a triplet chlorophyll. Tocopherol can interrupt lipid-radical propagation. Ascorbate supplies electrons for enzymatic peroxide reduction. GSH supports ascorbate regeneration and other thiol reactions. These are not interchangeable actions, even though all can contribute to a plant's overall ability to withstand oxidative disturbances. An exam may ask which combination provides complementary protection rather than which single antioxidant is universally strongest.

A growing plant also invests resources in synthesizing and maintaining antioxidants. Carbon skeletons, sulfur, nitrogen, ATP and reducing equivalents must be allocated among growth, defense and repair. Under favorable conditions, constitutive antioxidant pools help manage ordinary oxygen metabolism. Under stress, the demand for those pools can rise, while the resources required to replenish them may become limited. Increasing every antioxidant pathway without regard to the cost could alter growth or redox signaling. This is one reason why high antioxidant abundance in isolation is not a sufficient criterion for crop improvement.

The best way to evaluate a protective metabolite is to combine chemistry with physiology. A deficient mutant may accumulate more specific lipid oxidation products under high light, exhibit impaired recovery and show rescue when the missing biosynthetic function is restored. A complementary intervention can test whether a different protective pathway compensates for the deficiency. Such evidence goes beyond measuring how efficiently an extracted compound reduces an artificial chemical reagent. The biological question is whether the molecule protects the intended target under the conditions that make the target vulnerable.

Evidence from tocopherol-deficient seeds

Sattler and colleagues (2004) examined Arabidopsis mutants deficient in tocopherols to determine whether vitamin E contributes to seed longevity and protection during germination. The study compared mutations affecting distinct steps of tocopherol biosynthesis: vte1 impairs tocopherol cyclase and can accumulate a redox-active intermediate, whereas vte2 impairs a step needed for formation of the tocopherol pathway intermediates. This difference provides a useful conceptual control. Loss of a final product is not necessarily equivalent in two mutants if one retains a potentially protective intermediate. The reported seed and lipid oxidation phenotypes demonstrated a physiological role for tocopherols, particularly in the context of seed longevity and germination. They also remind us that antioxidant protection is not restricted to photosynthetically active tissues. Stored lipids are vulnerable substrates when metabolic activity resumes. The experiment does not imply that tocopherol is the only determinant of seed vigor or that the magnitude of the phenotype will be identical in every species.

9. ROS as signals: timing, location and systemic communication

If ROS were merely unwanted by-products, eliminating every molecule of peroxide as quickly as possible might appear to be the optimal response. Plants do something more complex. They deliberately produce ROS when environmental disturbances must be translated into cellular information. A signaling event differs from uncontrolled injury because production, target response and termination are regulated. A small or transient oxidizing signal may initiate protective changes that reduce injury later. This principle is central to understanding why some antioxidant manipulations have unexpected consequences.

A signal must possess specificity. Hydrogen peroxide does not have a unique universal receptor that explains every response. Some proteins contain cysteine residues that can undergo reversible oxidation, altering their activity. Other pathways depend on highly reactive redox proteins that relay oxidation to less reactive targets. Membrane transport and localized scavenging determine which proteins encounter peroxide and for how long. These features restrict signaling in space and time. The same concentration applied externally to two different tissues need not reproduce the same physiological response, because the intracellular delivery and buffering differ.

The concept of a threshold is often useful, but a single numerical threshold cannot separate all signaling from damage. A short pulse near a redox-sensitive protein can activate a response with little structural injury. A prolonged lower flux may progressively deplete reductants, accumulate irreversible modifications and compromise recovery. The amplitude, duration, frequency and source of the signal matter. An experiment using one endpoint measurement cannot determine whether a peroxide increase was brief, sustained or repeated.

The plasma membrane NADPH oxidases provide a regulated source of extracellular superoxide. Their cytosolic regulatory regions respond to calcium binding and phosphorylation. The superoxide is converted to peroxide in the apoplast. Peroxide can influence cell-surface signaling systems and enter cells through some aquaporins. This creates a pathway in which a stimulus outside a cell can lead to an oxidative signal inside it, even though superoxide itself does not freely diffuse across the lipid bilayer.

Calcium and ROS can form interacting feedback loops. A rise in cytosolic calcium can stimulate particular RBOH proteins directly or through calcium-responsive kinases. In certain signaling contexts, ROS can promote calcium entry, strengthening or propagating the response. Such positive feedback is useful for amplification, but it also requires mechanisms that limit the response. Calcium pumps and sequestration, phosphatases, changes in kinase activity and antioxidant reactions can contribute to termination. A diagram showing calcium and ROS as a one-way pathway omits this potential reciprocal regulation.

The distinction between a signal and a source becomes especially important in a mutant analysis. Suppose a calcium channel mutant exhibits a reduced ROS burst after stimulation. This result is consistent with calcium contributing to oxidase activation. It does not establish that calcium acts exclusively through that oxidase, since calcium regulates numerous proteins. Suppose an oxidase mutant exhibits a reduced second calcium increase while an initial increase remains. That observation would be consistent with an ROS-dependent amplification phase. Time-resolved measurements of both variables, appropriate genetic complementation and tests of downstream responses would clarify the proposed sequence.

Plants use these interactions beyond the initially stimulated cell. A local disturbance can induce rapid responses at distant tissues. One mechanism involves an RBOHD-dependent ROS wave, in which cells along the route of propagation activate their own ROS production. It is not necessary for one peroxide molecule generated in a wounded leaf to travel intact through the entire plant. Local regeneration of the signal allows propagation across multiple cells despite limited chemical lifetime. This principle resembles a relay, not passive diffusion over the entire distance.

Miller and colleagues reported in 2009 that a rapid systemic signal in Arabidopsis depended on RBOHD. Their work used a stress-responsive reporter and genetic analysis to investigate long-distance communication. The comparison between plants with functional RBOHD and plants defective in RBOHD was informative because it tested whether the oxidase was required for normal propagation of the measured response. The reported rapid movement was evidence of a systemic signaling process, not proof that all systemic signals in plants use the same mechanism. Different stimuli can recruit electrical changes, calcium signaling, hydraulic responses, hormones and metabolites as well.

An exam question might describe a local high-light treatment and measure ROS-related signaling in a distant, unilluminated leaf. If loss of RBOHD reduces the distant response while a local response remains, the result suggests that RBOHD contributes to long-distance propagation rather than being necessary for every local stress response. Other sources, such as chloroplasts, can still generate ROS at the treated site. If complementation restores propagation, the genetic evidence becomes stronger. To establish an adaptive function, the experiment must also measure the ability of distant tissue to withstand a subsequent challenge, not simply reporter activation.

Chloroplasts communicate with the nucleus through retrograde signaling. A change in photosynthetic electron flow or excitation balance can trigger nuclear responses that adjust chloroplast composition, metabolism or protection. Singlet oxygen is especially instructive because its high local reactivity restricts how far it can travel. Its formation may alter membrane constituents or signaling proteins, generating secondary signals that influence transcription. It is therefore possible to observe nuclear gene expression dependent on a chloroplast ROS without requiring that ROS to diffuse from the chloroplast to the nucleus.

Peroxisomal and mitochondrial redox disturbances can also change nuclear gene expression. A cell receives multiple signals from its organelles, and the patterns can overlap. A transcript that responds to both high light and respiratory inhibition is not necessarily a unique marker of chloroplast peroxide. Conversely, a marker responding preferentially to one condition may reflect compartment-specific signaling, stimulus intensity or time of sampling. A robust study uses multiple indicators and targeted perturbations rather than declaring a single gene a universal ROS detector.

In guard cells, ABA-dependent signaling during water limitation can involve RBOH-mediated ROS production and calcium-dependent changes in ion transport. The physiological outcome may be stomatal closure, reducing water loss. This response illustrates a tradeoff: restricting CO₂ entry can increase excitation pressure in the surrounding photosynthetic tissue. The benefit of a redox signal in one cell type can therefore change the oxidative burden in another. The detailed ABA pathway will be developed in the drought node. Here the key principle is that local redox signaling connects molecular events with whole-plant resource management.

During pathogen recognition, an early ROS burst can contribute to defense signaling and wall modification. Later, excessive or persistent oxidation may accompany cell death. Local cell death can restrict some pathogens dependent on living host tissue, whereas pathogens that exploit dead tissue may benefit from injury. A strong oxidative burst is consequently not a universal measure of disease resistance. Pathogen lifestyle, the timing of defense, the distribution of cell death and eventual pathogen growth all matter. These distinctions prepare the later chapters on pattern-triggered and effector-triggered immunity.

Antioxidants do not simply oppose signaling. They define the boundaries within which signaling occurs. Consider a localized peroxide source surrounded by cells with high removal capacity. Peroxide can influence nearby targets, but its lifetime and travel distance are limited. If clearance falls, a signal may spread or persist beyond the usual range. If clearance rises substantially, an adaptive signal may become too weak to activate downstream responses. Hence changing antioxidant capacity can alter the amplitude and duration of signaling even if the production machinery is unchanged.

A useful mathematical abstraction treats peroxide concentration H as a function of production rate P and first-order removal kH, so dH/dt = P − kH. Here H can be measured in µM, P in µM per minute, and k in inverse minutes. If P is constant and k remains constant, steady concentration is P/k. When production stops, concentration decays exponentially with a time constant of 1/k and a half-life of ln 2 divided by k. The model does not include transport, enzyme saturation or feedback, but it shows why a similar production pulse can have a longer duration in cells with weaker scavenging. It also shows how a high flux can be accompanied by a small steady pool when removal is rapid.

For an illustrative calculation, let P = 6 µM min⁻¹ and k = 2 min⁻¹. The predicted steady peroxide concentration is 3 µM. If antioxidant capacity falls so k = 0.5 min⁻¹ while production stays at 6 µM min⁻¹, steady concentration rises to 12 µM and the decay half-life increases from about 0.35 to 1.39 minutes. These are invented values for understanding the model, not published measurements. In an actual cell, the elevated concentration may change the production rate, saturate scavengers or trigger adaptive gene expression, making k and P time dependent.

Oxidative signaling is also regulated by transcriptional and metabolic responses. Stress can induce genes coding for antioxidant enzymes, transporters, chaperones and other protective proteins. This delayed response may increase the capacity to handle a later challenge. It is distinct from the rapid oxidative event that initiated it. A plant can therefore be in a state of acclimation after the initial signal has subsided. A priming experiment should distinguish constitutive differences from changes induced by a previous mild treatment and should compare performance under a standardized subsequent stress.

When studying a redox response, four temporal stages are useful: initial sensing, early ROS generation, downstream regulatory adjustment and recovery or injury. Measurements at only one stage can give misleading conclusions. Higher peroxide early in a tolerant genotype may indicate effective signaling, while lower peroxide during the subsequent challenge may reflect successful acclimation. The opposite pattern may indicate inadequate protection. A complete interpretation requires identifying what was measured, when, where and how it relates to later function.

10. Experimental reasoning: measuring oxidation and protection

A stress experiment must distinguish the initial chemical disturbance from its physiological consequences. A plant with high peroxide concentration might have increased production, decreased removal, redistribution between compartments or a combination of these changes. A plant with high antioxidant enzyme activity might be well protected or might be attempting to compensate for severe injury. The purpose of experimental design is to make these competing interpretations distinguishable. Begin with a clear hypothesis specifying the component, tissue, stress condition and predicted relationship between molecular and physiological outcomes.

A useful comparison uses plants of the same developmental stage grown under matched light, temperature, nutrient and water conditions. The experimental group receives a defined stress and a control group receives equivalent handling without the stress. Genetically distinct plants require their own unstressed controls because the baseline phenotype may differ. Replication must involve independent biological units, such as separate plants, rather than repeated readings of one extract alone. Technical replicates improve precision of a measurement but do not substitute for biological replication. Sampling leaves at comparable positions helps prevent differences in age or developmental state from being mistaken for effects of the treatment.

Sampling at several times is particularly important in ROS studies. Early samples can reveal an oxidative burst before substantial membrane damage appears. Later samples can show enzyme induction, changes in antioxidant pools and the accumulation of oxidized products. Recovery measurements after the treatment has ended help determine whether changes are reversible. If a plant displays a large early peroxide rise but little later injury, it may have mounted an effective adaptive response. Another plant with the same early peak may fail to restore redox balance and suffer sustained injury. The early signal alone does not distinguish the outcomes.

Histochemical staining can provide information about spatial patterns of oxidative reactions. Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) can be reduced to an insoluble colored formazan and is frequently used as an indicator of superoxide-related activity. However, reduction can also be affected by other cellular reductants and reaction conditions. Differences in penetration, tissue thickness and metabolism can alter the signal. Suppression of the reaction by SOD under appropriate assay conditions supports involvement of superoxide, but a stain is not an absolute concentration measurement. Quantitative claims require a suitably validated method and relevant controls.

Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining is frequently used to visualize peroxide-associated reactions in tissues. In the presence of peroxide and suitable peroxidase activity, DAB can form a colored insoluble product. The deposition depends not only on peroxide availability but also on the local capacity of the tissue to catalyze the reaction and on access of the staining reagent. A genotype with altered peroxidase activity might therefore stain differently even if peroxide concentration were unchanged. The stain must be interpreted alongside other measurements rather than as a direct universal map of intracellular peroxide.

Fluorescent probes can provide more dynamic information, but the chemistry of each probe matters. A probe that becomes fluorescent after oxidation does not necessarily react exclusively with one ROS. Some require intermediate reactions, and illumination can change probe behavior. Loading, pH, compartmental distribution, photobleaching and autofluorescence can influence the observed signal. In leaf tissue, chlorophyll and other endogenous pigments create particular challenges for optical measurements. A stress-induced increase in fluorescence may reflect altered probe oxidation, but specificity requires independent validation.

Genetically encoded sensors are useful for following changes in particular compartments. A sensor targeted to the chloroplast stroma can provide information that would be lost when a whole leaf is homogenized. Redox-sensitive GFP systems coupled to glutaredoxin can report the redox state of the glutathione couple, while peroxide-responsive systems use appropriate reactive domains to couple peroxide detection to a fluorescent response. The reporter should be calibrated in its oxidized and reduced states, and its sensitivity to pH and other conditions should be understood. Changes in sensor oxidation represent the balance of oxidation and reduction reactions affecting that sensor. They do not automatically provide an absolute local peroxide concentration.

An activity assay answers a different question from a direct measurement of oxidation. Catalase activity can be followed by measuring the decrease in peroxide absorbance or the formation of oxygen. APX activity can be inferred from ascorbate oxidation. GR activity can be monitored through the consumption of NADPH in an assay containing GSSG. In each case, a blank helps account for nonenzymatic changes and background absorbance. The initial linear portion of the reaction is used to estimate the rate before substantial substrate depletion or product accumulation changes the conditions.

The Beer–Lambert relationship connects an absorbance change to concentration under suitable conditions: A = εlc, where A is dimensionless absorbance, ε is the molar absorptivity, l is the optical path length and c is concentration. If an assay monitors a decrease in NADPH absorbance at 340 nm, a conventional approximate molar absorptivity for NADPH at this wavelength is 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹. For a 1 cm path and an absorbance decrease of 0.0622 per minute after blank correction, the corresponding NADPH consumption rate is 0.010 mM per minute. In a 1 mL reaction, this is 0.010 µmol per minute. If the assay contains 0.10 mg of extract protein, the activity is 0.10 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹. These hypothetical numbers assume a linear range, a known path length, negligible interference and an appropriate reaction-specific blank.

The calculation measures capacity under the assay conditions. It does not establish the same flux inside a living organelle. Extracting the tissue removes some constraints on substrate concentration, localization and availability of reductant. An activity normalized to total protein may also change when the relative abundance of other proteins changes. For this reason it is helpful to combine activity measurements with protein abundance, transcript data and redox pool measurements. Increased transcript abundance followed by increased protein and catalytic activity provides a stronger account of induction than transcription alone.

Electrolyte leakage offers a way to relate molecular injury to a membrane-level physiological consequence. Equal quantities of comparable leaf tissue are placed in an appropriate bathing medium, and conductivity is measured after a defined interval. A subsequent treatment that releases the remaining electrolytes provides an estimate of total releasable conductivity. Under comparable conditions, relative leakage is calculated as 100 × C₁/C₂, where C₁ is the initial conductivity and C₂ is conductivity after complete release, with background correction where appropriate. Both readings must be expressed in the same units. The percentage indicates the fraction of the measured releasable electrolyte signal that escaped before total release.

Suppose the initial conductivity of an unstressed tissue sample is 12 µS cm⁻¹ and its final conductivity is 120 µS cm⁻¹. Its relative leakage is 10%. A stressed sample yields initial conductivity of 42 µS cm⁻¹ and final conductivity of 140 µS cm⁻¹, giving 30%. Comparing initial conductivities alone would suggest a 3.5-fold increase, but the relative leakage values show a threefold increase in the fraction of releasable electrolytes. Total ion content differs between the samples, which is why normalization is important. The values are illustrative. Real assays require equivalent tissue preparation, incubation time and temperature.

Leaf disks can be injured by cutting, and variations in surface area can affect ion escape. Washing and consistent handling reduce these artifacts, while unstressed disks reveal the background caused by the preparation. Ion composition and external solution conductivity also influence the reading. A high relative leakage is compatible with loss of membrane integrity or altered ion transport, but does not identify the ROS responsible. It should not be used as an exclusive measurement of lipid peroxidation. Pairing leakage with an independent lipid oxidation marker and a recovery experiment improves interpretation.

Chlorophyll fluorescence provides a complementary measurement focused on photosynthetic function. Absorbed light energy can drive photochemistry, be dissipated as heat or be emitted as fluorescence. The distribution among these fates changes with the state of the photosynthetic apparatus. In a dark-adapted leaf, a weak measuring light is used to obtain minimum fluorescence, F₀, when PSII reaction centers are predominantly open. A saturating pulse transiently closes the PSII centers and yields maximum fluorescence, Fm, under the measurement conditions. Variable fluorescence is Fv = Fm − F₀. The ratio Fv/Fm estimates the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII photochemistry in the dark-adapted state.

For an illustrative control leaf with F₀ = 180 and Fm = 1000 arbitrary fluorescence units, Fv = 820 and Fv/Fm = 0.82. A stressed leaf with F₀ = 250 and Fm = 800 has Fv = 550 and Fv/Fm = 0.6875. The ratio has decreased, indicating altered PSII efficiency under the measurement conditions. In many healthy dark-adapted leaves of higher plants, the value is near 0.8 to 0.83, but species, leaf age and physiological state influence the value. A lower ratio can result from PSII inactivation or sustained nonphotochemical quenching. It is not a direct quantitative measure of peroxide, singlet oxygen or lipid oxidation.

Measurements made during illumination address a different question. The operational quantum yield of PSII is often estimated by (Fm′ − Fs)/Fm′, where Fs is steady fluorescence under actinic light and Fm′ is maximum fluorescence obtained with a saturating pulse in that illuminated state. This yield is not identical to dark-adapted Fv/Fm. Nonphotochemical quenching is often expressed as NPQ = (Fm − Fm′)/Fm′. This index describes a fluorescence-based change associated with energy dissipation, not the absolute amount of heat in joules. The interpretation requires appropriate adaptation, measurement settings and control of the light environment.

Assume a hypothetical leaf has dark-adapted Fm = 1000, light-adapted Fm′ = 600 and steady fluorescence Fs = 300. The operational yield is (600 − 300)/600 = 0.50, while NPQ = (1000 − 600)/600 ≈ 0.67. Both numbers are dimensionless. A second leaf could have a higher NPQ because it dissipates excess excitation more effectively, yet have a lower operational yield because a greater fraction of absorbed energy is not used photochemically at that moment. These changes are not necessarily evidence of irreversible injury. Dark recovery and other independent physiological measurements help separate photoprotection from sustained dysfunction.

Fluorescence and electrolyte leakage can reveal different targets of a stress. A plant may show reduced PSII efficiency without substantial electrolyte leakage if its primary disturbance is photosynthetic or regulatory. Another may show marked leakage with relatively preserved PSII efficiency if the injury is concentrated in other membranes or tissues. Both measurements can deteriorate in severe oxidative stress, but their correlation does not prove that one caused the other. This distinction is valuable for Part C questions that present several indicators and ask which interpretation is best supported.

To connect enzyme activity, peroxide and damage, imagine a factorial experiment comparing two genotypes under control and high-light conditions. One genotype has a defect in a chloroplast-targeted antioxidant enzyme. Before treatment both genotypes have similar Fv/Fm values and low leakage. After high light, the mutant displays a greater peroxide-associated signal, a stronger decline in Fv/Fm and increased leakage. If complementation restores the enzyme and substantially rescues these outcomes, the hypothesis that the enzyme contributes to protection is supported. However, this does not establish that peroxide was the only damaging species or that the mutant has no other physiological differences. A second independent allele and a compartment-specific reporter would increase confidence.

The timing of rescue is equally useful. If expression of the functional antioxidant only during stress is sufficient to restore protection, the enzyme may act directly in managing the oxidative burden. If rescue requires expression throughout development, the phenotype may also depend on changes in organelle structure or development. A constitutive knockout may have compensatory changes in other pathways. These issues do not invalidate genetic evidence, but they show why a mechanistic conclusion should be framed at the level directly supported by the experiment.

A chemical antioxidant treatment can provide additional evidence, but interpretation is more difficult when the reagent is not selective. A compound might alter ROS production, inhibit an enzyme, affect membrane properties or act as a reducing substrate. A reduction in injury after treatment does not necessarily establish which ROS species initiated the injury. Conversely, a treatment that fails to protect may not have reached the relevant compartment. Dose, timing and delivery matter. Genetic perturbation, chemical controls and targeted measurements are complementary rather than mutually exclusive strategies.

A particularly instructive dataset compares the control, the immediate response and the recovery stage. If wild-type and mutant leaves have equal initial peroxide levels, but the mutant retains a high peroxide signal after return to normal light, defective clearance or continued production is plausible. If both genotypes clear peroxide similarly but the mutant remains functionally impaired, the difference may lie in susceptibility to damage or repair. If the mutant has higher peroxide early but ultimately shows less injury, enhanced signaling or a different baseline tolerance may be involved. There is no single interpretation that fits all possible time courses.

The final outcome of interest depends on the biological objective. Survival is useful in acute lethal stress, but an agricultural plant can survive while losing most of its productivity. Relative growth rate, biomass accumulation, reproductive success and yield under realistic stress conditions complement biochemical indicators. Laboratory high-light assays offer mechanistic insight but do not alone establish field performance. Light intensity varies in nature, and oxidative disturbances often coincide with drought, temperature extremes or nutrient limitation. A convincing application of antioxidant biology must therefore link specific protective mechanisms with performance under relevant conditions.

11. Integrating protection, repair and acclimation

Antioxidant systems are best understood as successive layers of protection rather than a single reaction that neutralizes oxidative stress. The first layer prevents production of unnecessary ROS. Nonphotochemical quenching, adjustment of metabolic sinks and alternative respiratory electron flow can reduce electron or excitation pressure. The second layer converts and removes reactive molecules, using SOD, catalase, APX and other scavengers. The third layer interrupts reactions that have already begun, such as lipid radical propagation. The fourth layer repairs or replaces damaged components. Each layer has a different immediate target, and an effective plant may use several at once.

Preventive responses can be particularly efficient because avoiding an unwanted excitation transfer reduces the need for downstream scavenging. Nevertheless prevention cannot remove all ROS without interfering with ordinary metabolism and signaling. Some peroxide is generated as a normal product of oxidase activity; some ROS are produced deliberately during stress perception. An adaptive system must permit these functions while limiting injury. High antioxidant capacity therefore means the capacity to regulate redox conditions, not an absence of detectable oxidation.

When a membrane lipid is oxidized, chain-breaking protection prevents amplification but does not necessarily restore the original lipid. The damaged lipid may need to be removed and replaced. In chloroplasts, repair of photosystem II involves removal of damaged components including D1 protein and synthesis of replacement protein. The existence of repair means that photosynthetic performance reflects the difference between the rate of damage and the rate of recovery. Two plants with similar ROS production can differ substantially in their functional tolerance if one replaces damaged proteins more efficiently.

Repair requires energy and biosynthetic resources. A prolonged respiratory disturbance may reduce ATP supply while increasing demand for the removal and replacement of damaged proteins. Under severe photosynthetic impairment, carbon assimilation can fall at the same time as the need for new membrane lipids and antioxidant metabolites rises. These competing requirements help explain why stress duration matters. A short stress may be manageable even with temporary oxidative injury, while prolonged exposure can exhaust reserves and limit recovery. The distinction between acute protection and sustained tolerance is important in both experiments and crop physiology.

Acclimation occurs when prior exposure modifies the physiological response to later conditions. In an illustrative design, one group of plants receives a mild nonlethal light stress, is allowed to recover, and then receives a stronger challenge. A second group receives only the stronger challenge. If the pretreated plants show less later membrane leakage and recover photosynthetic function more effectively, the data support acquired tolerance under the chosen conditions. If antioxidant gene expression or enzyme activity rises during the recovery interval, that change is a plausible contributor. Establishing causal necessity requires a perturbation of the proposed protective pathway.

An alternative explanation for the pretreated phenotype could be altered leaf optical properties, stomatal conductance or growth. The pretreatment might reduce the absorbed light burden rather than directly enhance scavenging. An experimental comparison should therefore measure the effective stress experienced by the tissue, not only the external treatment. Acclimation can operate by avoidance, changes in resource use, strengthened scavenging or more effective repair. The correct interpretation depends on the evidence.

The same reasoning applies to drought, the next node in the Academy sequence. Water shortage may close stomata and constrain CO₂ uptake, increasing the imbalance between light absorption and productive electron use. ABA and ROS may simultaneously participate in adaptive guard-cell signaling. Osmotic adjustment and protection of proteins and membranes can influence the ability to tolerate dehydration. When watering resumes, photosynthetic and respiratory metabolism may change rapidly, creating a new redox challenge. Thus oxidative stress is both a consequence of the original disturbance and a factor influencing recovery. It is not a separate universal stress pathway detached from plant water relations.

In salinity, osmotic effects and ion imbalance can alter metabolic activity and membrane function. In cold, slowed biochemical reactions may make existing light absorption excessive; in heat, protein and membrane stability can decline. In flooding, oxygen shortage limits aerobic respiration, while reoxygenation can provoke oxidative disturbances. The detailed responses to these stresses differ, but the same framework of production, scavenging, signaling and repair helps predict common cellular outcomes. This is why oxidative stress and cellular protection occupy a central position in the learning sequence before individual environmental stress chapters.

A plant's antioxidant network is dynamic across development. A germinating seed that mobilizes stored lipids can rely heavily on peroxisomal protection. A young illuminated leaf has strong demands on chloroplast photoprotection. Senescing tissue may alter redox metabolism as nutrients are remobilized. A comparison of tissues must account for these baseline differences. High catalase activity in a germinating seed need not indicate greater drought resistance than a mature leaf. The relevant question concerns the oxidative challenge and protective machinery in that tissue.

The most transferable examination strategy is to reconstruct the immediate chemical sequence and then ask where the regulation occurs. If a mutation affects a source, predict which ROS formation step changes. If it affects SOD, trace the altered conversion between superoxide and peroxide. If it affects APX, ask whether ascorbate and the recycling pathways can compensate. If it affects GR, distinguish restoration of GSH from de novo synthesis. If a lipid-soluble antioxidant is deficient, examine membrane oxidation and recovery. Finally, determine whether the data measure a source, a pool, a scavenging capacity, an injury or a whole-plant outcome. Such reasoning prevents the common error of inferring a complete causal mechanism from one oxidative marker.

Redox homeostasis in plants is therefore an active property of an interconnected biological system. Oxygen chemistry sustains the energy metabolism that finances cellular protection, while protective pathways preserve the machinery that generates energy. ROS signal disturbances and help initiate changes in defense, but those changes must be regulated to avoid damaging the cell. Understanding this balance provides the foundation for the subsequent chapters on drought, salinity, temperature, flooding and plant immunity.

Examination traps and misconceptions

  1. Incorrect: Every ROS is a free radical.

    Correct: Hydrogen peroxide and singlet oxygen are nonradical ROS. The term describes oxygen reactivity, not a universal unpaired electron. Reasoning: Students equate ROS with radical. Identify the electronic state before choosing a reaction.

  2. Incorrect: Singlet oxygen is made by one-electron reduction.

    Correct: Excitation-energy transfer can generate ¹O₂; one-electron reduction makes O₂•⁻. Reasoning: Both appear in chloroplasts, but their production mechanisms and defenses differ.

  3. Incorrect: SOD removes all oxidative risk.

    Correct: SOD produces H₂O₂; adequate downstream clearance is still needed. Reasoning: Do the balanced reaction before predicting a phenotype.

  4. Incorrect: Catalase requires NADPH for every peroxide turnover.

    Correct: Catalatic disproportionation uses peroxide as both oxidant and reductant. Reasoning: Distinguish CAT from APX plus recycling.

  5. Incorrect: APX directly oxidizes glutathione.

    Correct: Ascorbate is its immediate substrate; GSH supports the DHA branch. Reasoning: Trace the electron donor at each enzyme.

  6. Incorrect: DHAR and MDHAR are interchangeable.

    Correct: DHAR reduces DHA using GSH; MDHAR reduces MDHA using reducing equivalents. Reasoning: The names sound similar; identify the oxidation state of ascorbate.

  7. Incorrect: GR synthesizes glutathione from amino acids.

    Correct: It reduces GSSG to GSH; biosynthesis is separate. Reasoning: Examine whether the process changes redox state or adds new molecules.

  8. Incorrect: All peroxide originates from SOD.

    Correct: Glycolate oxidase and other oxidases can directly generate peroxide. Reasoning: Distinguish production from conversion.

  9. Incorrect: RBOH is a mitochondrial respiratory complex.

    Correct: Plant RBOHs transfer electrons across the plasma membrane to extracellular oxygen. Reasoning: The name respiratory burst oxidase is misleading if location is ignored.

  10. Incorrect: ROS must diffuse intact to distal tissues.

    Correct: Regenerated ROS production can propagate a wave between cells. Reasoning: Do not infer long-distance transport of a short-lived radical from a systemic response.

  11. Incorrect: High antioxidant activity always means high tolerance.

    Correct: It may indicate a compensatory response to severe stress; activity in extracts need not equal cellular flux. Reasoning: Compare injury and recovery, not just enzyme capacity.

  12. Incorrect: A high GSH/GSSG ratio fully defines redox potential.

    Correct: The reaction quotient includes squared GSH activity, so absolute concentration and pH matter. Reasoning: Use the Nernst relationship rather than the ratio alone.

  13. Incorrect: All lipid oxidation is nonenzymatic radical damage.

    Correct: Lipoxygenases and singlet oxygen can generate lipid oxidation products through distinct chemistry. Reasoning: Identify the oxidation route before assigning cause.

  14. Incorrect: DAB staining gives exact peroxide concentration.

    Correct: It also depends on peroxidase activity, penetration and conditions. Reasoning: Use independent measurements and appropriate controls.

  15. Incorrect: Fv/Fm is a ROS concentration measurement.

    Correct: It estimates maximum dark-adapted PSII photochemical efficiency. Reasoning: Identify the physiological quantity measured, not a possible upstream cause.

  16. Incorrect: Electrolyte leakage uniquely identifies hydroxyl radical damage.

    Correct: Leakage reports membrane-level permeability or ion loss and has multiple causes. Reasoning: Normalize to total release and combine with other evidence.

  17. Incorrect: More scavenging is always desirable.

    Correct: Excess clearance can attenuate an adaptive oxidative signal. Reasoning: Consider dose, timing, cell type and physiological outcome.

  18. Incorrect: AOX is an antioxidant scavenger.

    Correct: AOX modifies the electron transport route and can limit some ROS formation upstream. Reasoning: Distinguish prevention from direct chemical removal.

Part B — 25 concept questions

  1. Which of the following ROS lacks an unpaired electron?

    1. Hydrogen peroxide
    2. Hydroperoxyl radical
    3. Superoxide
    4. Hydroxyl radical
    Answer and explanation

    A. H₂O₂ is a nonradical peroxide. The other listed species contain an unpaired electron; nonradical does not mean chemically harmless.

  2. Singlet oxygen formation in illuminated chloroplasts is most directly associated with which process?

    1. Reduction of oxygen by one electron
    2. Energy transfer from triplet chlorophyll
    3. Catalase turnover
    4. Oxidation of GSH by DHAR
    Answer and explanation

    B. Triplet chlorophyll can transfer excitation energy to ground-state oxygen. The one-electron route makes superoxide; catalase and DHAR belong to peroxide-related metabolism.

  3. Which balanced reaction is catalyzed by SOD?

    1. GSSG + NADPH + H⁺ → 2 GSH + NADP⁺
    2. 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂
    3. 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂
    4. O₂ + 4 e⁻ + 4 H⁺ → 2 H₂O
    Answer and explanation

    C. Two superoxide molecules disproportionate. The other reactions correspond to catalase, complete oxygen reduction, and GR respectively.

  4. Which pair supplies the reactants for a classical Fenton reaction?

    1. Fe³⁺ and water
    2. NADP⁺ and ascorbate
    3. O₂ and GSSG
    4. Fe²⁺ and H₂O₂
    Answer and explanation

    D. Reduced iron reacts with peroxide to form Fe³⁺, hydroxide and hydroxyl radical. The other pairs do not describe this chemistry.

  5. Where does glycolate oxidase make peroxide during leaf photorespiration?

    1. Peroxisome
    2. Vacuole
    3. Nucleus
    4. Thylakoid lumen
    Answer and explanation

    A. Glycolate enters peroxisomes and is oxidized to glyoxylate. Chloroplast Rubisco initiates photorespiration but is not the site of this oxidase reaction.

  6. Plant RBOH proteins transfer electrons primarily from which donor to which acceptor?

    1. Apoplastic NADPH to cytosolic oxygen
    2. Cytosolic NADPH to apoplastic oxygen
    3. GSSG to NADP⁺
    4. Water to chlorophyll
    Answer and explanation

    B. RBOHs are plasma-membrane NADPH oxidases that generate extracellular superoxide. The direction is outward, not inward.

  7. What is the principal immediate biochemical product of RBOH oxygen reduction?

    1. Singlet oxygen
    2. Ozone
    3. Superoxide
    4. Hydroxyl radical
    Answer and explanation

    C. The initial one-electron reduction produces O₂•⁻. H₂O₂ can subsequently form by dismutation; hydroxyl radical requires other chemistry.

  8. Which SOD isoform is typically associated with the plant mitochondrial matrix?

    1. Catalase
    2. APX
    3. Glutathione reductase
    4. MnSOD
    Answer and explanation

    D. MnSOD is commonly mitochondrial. Catalase, APX and GR have different substrates and functions, not SOD metal classes.

  9. Which enzyme removes two H₂O₂ molecules without consuming an external stoichiometric reductant in its catalatic reaction?

    1. Catalase
    2. DHAR
    3. MDHAR
    4. APX
    Answer and explanation

    A. Catalase disproportionates peroxide to water and oxygen. APX uses ascorbate; DHAR and MDHAR regenerate antioxidants.

  10. APX uses which immediate electron donor?

    1. NADP⁺
    2. Ascorbate
    3. Ferrous iron
    4. Glycolate
    Answer and explanation

    B. Ascorbate supplies one electron at a time. NADPH can provide reducing equivalents indirectly through antioxidant recycling.

  11. Which reaction restores GSH from GSSG?

    1. Lipoxygenase
    2. SOD
    3. GR
    4. Glycolate oxidase
    Answer and explanation

    C. Glutathione reductase consumes NADPH to reduce the disulfide bond. The other enzymes have unrelated substrates.

  12. Which enzyme uses GSH to reduce DHA?

    1. MDHAR
    2. Catalase
    3. SOD
    4. DHAR
    Answer and explanation

    D. DHAR couples DHA reduction to oxidation of two GSH. MDHAR reduces monodehydroascorbate rather than DHA.

  13. Which molecule can directly serve as a chain-breaking antioxidant in membranes?

    1. Tocopherol
    2. Glyoxylate
    3. Rubisco
    4. NADP⁺
    Answer and explanation

    A. Tocopherol intercepts lipid peroxyl radicals in lipid-rich regions. The others do not perform this membrane chain-breaking role.

  14. Which protective action is characteristic of carotenoids in photosynthetic complexes?

    1. Hydrolysis of peroxide
    2. Quenching triplet chlorophyll
    3. Repairing oxidized DNA bases
    4. Reduction of GSSG
    Answer and explanation

    B. Carotenoids help suppress singlet oxygen formation by accepting excitation energy. They do not replace GR, catalase or DNA repair.

  15. What are the amino acid constituents of glutathione?

    1. Aspartate, lysine and alanine
    2. Glutamate, proline and tyrosine
    3. Glutamate, cysteine and glycine
    4. Glutamine, methionine and serine
    Answer and explanation

    C. Glutathione is γ-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine. Its cysteine thiol participates in reversible redox chemistry.

  16. Which oxidized amino acid modification can be repaired by methionine sulfoxide reductase?

    1. DNA strand break
    2. Lipid hydroperoxide
    3. Protein carbonylation at all residues
    4. Methionine sulfoxide
    Answer and explanation

    D. Methionine sulfoxide reductases reverse selected methionine oxidation states. Carbonyl damage generally requires turnover; DNA and lipid lesions involve other systems.

  17. Which variable describes dark-adapted maximum PSII quantum efficiency?

    1. (Fm − F₀)/Fm
    2. (Fm − Fm′)/Fm′
    3. Fm/F₀
    4. F₀/Fm
    Answer and explanation

    A. Fv/Fm = (Fm − F₀)/Fm. The last expression is NPQ; the first two are not the maximum quantum efficiency.

  18. Which observation directly measures relative electrolyte leakage?

    1. Fm divided by F₀
    2. Conductivity before release divided by conductivity after total release
    3. Peroxide absorbance divided by chlorophyll content
    4. GSH divided by GSSG
    Answer and explanation

    B. The conductivity ratio estimates the fraction of releasable electrolytes lost before total release. Other ratios describe different properties.

  19. Which is a likely consequence of hydroxyl radical production near DNA?

    1. Guaranteed adaptive gene expression
    2. Selective NADPH synthesis
    3. Oxidized bases and strand lesions
    4. Direct catalase formation
    Answer and explanation

    C. Hydroxyl radicals react locally with bases and the backbone. Lesions can be repaired or lead to mutation or cell injury; adaptive expression is not guaranteed.

  20. Which statement about AOX is correct?

    1. It generates ATP directly
    2. It dismutates superoxide
    3. It removes peroxide using ascorbate
    4. It bypasses complexes III and IV using ubiquinol
    Answer and explanation

    D. AOX changes the respiratory electron route, potentially limiting electron pressure, and conserves less proton motive force than the cytochrome pathway.

  21. Which antioxidant reaction is an example of reductant recycling rather than de novo synthesis?

    1. GR-mediated reduction of GSSG
    2. Synthesis of cysteine
    3. Lipid biosynthesis
    4. Formation of GSH from constituent amino acids
    Answer and explanation

    A. GR restores the reduced form of existing glutathione. Biosynthetic processes produce new molecular material.

  22. Which statement about lipid peroxidation is most accurate?

    1. It always requires lipoxygenase
    2. Propagation can amplify initial radical injury
    3. Every lipid hydroperoxide proves hydroxyl radical formation
    4. Each initiating radical can oxidize only one lipid
    Answer and explanation

    B. Peroxyl radicals can create new lipid radicals, propagating oxidation. Both enzymatic and nonenzymatic lipid oxidation occur.

  23. A plant ROS wave is best described as:

    1. A form of antibody-dependent immunity
    2. Passive travel of one hydroxyl radical through the plant
    3. Regeneration of local ROS production along a signaling route
    4. Continuous bulk diffusion of superoxide through membranes
    Answer and explanation

    C. RBOHD-dependent signaling can be regenerated in successive cells, with other signals cooperating. It is not animal adaptive immunity or unrestricted radical diffusion.

  24. Why does an antioxidant enzyme activity measurement in a homogenate not equal its in vivo flux?

    1. Activity is identical to gene expression
    2. All enzymes consume the same substrate
    3. Enzymes never work after extraction
    4. Assay conditions may remove substrate and compartment constraints
    Answer and explanation

    D. Defined assay conditions measure potential capacity; intact cellular substrate supply, localization and regulation can differ.

  25. Which claim best describes ROS homeostasis?

    1. Production, removal, signaling and repair must be coordinated
    2. All oxidants must be absent
    3. ROS production alone predicts all injury
    4. Only catalase matters
    Answer and explanation

    A. ROS have normal metabolic and signaling roles. Homeostasis requires control of the network and preservation of function.

Part C — 27 analytical questions

  1. A compartment produces 18 nmol superoxide min⁻¹, all converted by SOD. Direct oxidases add 5 nmol H₂O₂ min⁻¹. What total peroxide production is predicted?

    1. 9
    2. 14
    3. 23
    4. 36
    Answer and explanation

    B. SOD generates one H₂O₂ per two superoxide: 18/2 = 9. Adding direct peroxide production gives 14 nmol min⁻¹. Adding 18 directly ignores stoichiometry.

  2. A peroxisome generates 40 µmol H₂O₂ h⁻¹. Catalase accounts for 75% of removal at steady state. How much O₂ does catalase release per hour?

    1. 40 µmol
    2. 10 µmol
    3. 15 µmol
    4. 30 µmol
    Answer and explanation

    C. Catalase consumes 0.75 × 40 = 30 µmol peroxide per hour. Two peroxide yield one oxygen, so 15 µmol O₂ h⁻¹. Thirty is peroxide consumption, not oxygen production.

  3. A chloroplast removes 12 nmol peroxide min⁻¹ through APX and exclusively the DHA–DHAR–GR route. What GR-dependent NADPH consumption is expected?

    1. 24 nmol min⁻¹
    2. 48 nmol min⁻¹
    3. 6 nmol min⁻¹
    4. 12 nmol min⁻¹
    Answer and explanation

    D. Each peroxide requires two one-electron donations. After disproportionation, DHAR and GR, one NADPH supplies two electrons per peroxide. Two GSH molecules cycle per peroxide, not two NADPH.

  4. A mutant has twice the SOD capacity of wild type, unchanged APX and CAT capacity, and greater peroxide-associated injury under stress. What is most defensible?

    1. Peroxide clearance may be limiting after enhanced conversion
    2. Catalase is an SOD cofactor
    3. SOD directly makes hydroxyl radicals
    4. SOD necessarily increased superoxide production
    Answer and explanation

    A. SOD changes the partitioning of superoxide toward peroxide. A mismatch with downstream removal is plausible, but the data do not prove altered formation flux or Fenton chemistry.

  5. Two genotypes have identical dark-adapted Fm of 1000. F₀ is 180 in A and 350 in B. What follows?

    1. B necessarily has higher peroxide
    2. A has Fv/Fm 0.82, B 0.65
    3. Both are 1.0
    4. A is 0.18, B 0.35
    Answer and explanation

    B. Fv/Fm is (Fm−F₀)/Fm. B has lower apparent maximum PSII efficiency, but fluorescence alone cannot identify peroxide or injury mechanism.

  6. Unstressed tissue gives conductivity 15 then 150 µS cm⁻¹. Stressed tissue gives 60 then 200. Which interpretation is justified?

    1. A specific hydroxyl radical mechanism
    2. Fourfold relative leakage
    3. Threefold relative leakage
    4. Equal leakage
    Answer and explanation

    C. Relative leakage is 10% versus 30%, threefold. Initial conductivity is fourfold but total releasable conductivity differs. No species-specific inference follows.

  7. The first solution has GSH 4 mM, GSSG 0.04 mM. The second has GSH 2 mM, GSSG 0.02 mM. At fixed pH and temperature, which is true?

    1. Second is more negative by (RT/2F) ln 2
    2. First has no GSSG
    3. Redox potentials identical
    4. Second is more positive by (RT/2F) ln 2
    Answer and explanation

    D. Both ratios equal 100, but the quotient aGSSG/aGSH² doubles when both concentrations halve. The second potential is more positive. The ratio alone is insufficient.

  8. A peroxide pool follows dH/dt = P − kH. P = 8 µM min⁻¹ and k = 2 min⁻¹. After treatment k falls to 1 min⁻¹ with P unchanged. What are the two predicted steady concentrations?

    1. 4 and 8 µM
    2. 16 and 8 µM
    3. 8 and 4 µM
    4. 2 and 1 µM
    Answer and explanation

    A. At steady state H=P/k, so 4 µM initially and 8 µM after the change. This idealized calculation assumes constant production and first-order clearance.

  9. An enzyme has Vmax 20 nmol min⁻¹ and apparent Km 5 µM. At 15 µM peroxide and saturating donor, what rate does the simplified Michaelis–Menten model predict?

    1. 10
    2. 15
    3. 20 nmol min⁻¹
    4. 5
    Answer and explanation

    B. v=20×15/(5+15)=15 nmol min⁻¹. The maximum rate is approached only at high substrate and need not represent intact-cell kinetics.

  10. A catalase-deficient C₃ plant shows leaf injury at ambient CO₂ but a substantially milder phenotype at high CO₂. Which hypothesis is strengthened?

    1. High CO₂ necessarily eliminates all ROS
    2. Peroxisomes are photosystems
    3. Photorespiratory peroxide contributes to injury
    4. Catalase fixes carbon dioxide
    Answer and explanation

    C. Elevated CO₂ suppresses Rubisco oxygenation and associated glycolate oxidation. Conditional rescue is consistent with photorespiratory peroxide involvement, but other CO₂ effects require controls.

  11. A DAB-stained mutant has a weaker color than wild type but a peroxide-specific sensor shows comparable oxidation. What should be investigated first?

    1. Whether hydrogen peroxide is a radical
    2. Whether wild type lacks oxygen
    3. Whether DAB color is always exact concentration
    4. Altered peroxidase activity or stain penetration
    Answer and explanation

    D. DAB deposition depends on peroxide, peroxidase chemistry and access. A staining difference alone cannot establish peroxide concentration.

  12. A tocopherol-deficient mutant displays increased lipid oxidation after illumination despite near-normal catalase activity. Which rescue most directly tests the missing membrane defense?

    1. Restore tocopherol biosynthesis by genetic complementation
    2. Inhibit all respiration
    3. Increase external salt
    4. Increase nuclear DNA content
    Answer and explanation

    A. Restoring the missing biosynthetic function tests causal relevance. Normal catalase does not replace a lipid-phase chain-breaking antioxidant.

  13. A pigment mutant cannot efficiently quench triplet chlorophyll. Which early molecular change is most directly expected during excess excitation?

    1. Complete inhibition of peroxisomal glycolate oxidase
    2. Increased susceptibility to singlet oxygen formation
    3. Immediate loss of GR activity in all compartments
    4. Direct creation of GSSG by triplet energy transfer
    Answer and explanation

    B. Failure of triplet quenching favors energy transfer to oxygen. Other changes could occur secondarily but are not the immediate chemical prediction.

  14. After local illumination, the treated leaf of an rbohD mutant still responds but a distal reporter response is attenuated. Complementation restores distal signaling. What is supported?

    1. The reporter proves increased yield
    2. RBOHD is necessary for all local ROS generation
    3. RBOHD contributes to propagation of this systemic response
    4. The same superoxide molecule must travel to the distal leaf
    Answer and explanation

    C. The selective distal phenotype and rescue support a role in propagation. Local chloroplast or other sources can still operate; no yield conclusion follows.

  15. A chloroplast-targeted peroxide sensor oxidizes after high light while a cytosolic glutathione-redox sensor does not. Which interpretation is strongest?

    1. The cytosolic sensor measures singlet oxygen directly
    2. No ROS was produced
    3. Both sensors must be defective
    4. A local peroxide-related change need not alter the measured cytosolic glutathione state
    Answer and explanation

    D. The reporters sample different chemistry and locations. Compartment-specific buffering and limited transfer can uncouple responses.

  16. GR activity falls by 80%, but ascorbate remains mostly reduced during mild stress. Which observation would offer a mechanistic explanation?

    1. MDHAR activity is sufficient to recycle much of the MDHA
    2. All APX reactions require DHAR exclusively
    3. SOD directly reduces DHA
    4. Catalase synthesizes ascorbate
    Answer and explanation

    A. MDHAR bypasses the DHA/GSH branch by reducing MDHA directly. Multiple recycling routes can buffer a GR defect.

  17. An AOX-deficient mutant under excess respiratory substrate shows a more reduced ubiquinone pool and elevated mitochondrial oxidation indicator. What is most consistent?

    1. AOX is chloroplast APX
    2. AOX can limit electron pressure by oxidizing ubiquinol
    3. AOX directly consumes hydroxyl radical
    4. AOX pumps more protons than complexes III and IV
    Answer and explanation

    B. AOX provides an alternative outlet for ubiquinol electrons. Its protective role can be upstream of ROS generation rather than direct scavenging.

  18. In a GR assay at 340 nm, blank-corrected ΔA/min is 0.1244, ε = 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹, path = 1 cm, volume 2 mL and protein 0.20 mg. What is the activity?

    1. 0.02 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
    2. 0.10 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
    3. 0.20 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
    4. 2.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
    Answer and explanation

    C. Concentration change is 0.1244/6.22 = 0.020 mM min⁻¹. Multiplying by 2 mL yields 0.040 µmol min⁻¹, then dividing by 0.20 mg gives 0.20.

  19. A stressed leaf has Fm = 900, Fm′ = 450 and Fs = 270. What are its operational PSII yield and NPQ?

    1. 0.50 and 0.40
    2. 0.60 and 2.00
    3. 0.40 and 0.50
    4. 0.40 and 1.00
    Answer and explanation

    D. Yield=(450−270)/450=0.40. NPQ=(900−450)/450=1.00. The quantities are distinct and dimensionless.

  20. Two lines have identical peroxide at the end of stress. During recovery line A rapidly restores Fv/Fm; line B remains impaired despite peroxide falling equally quickly. Which explanation should be tested?

    1. Different susceptibility to injury or repair capacity
    2. Identical functional tolerance
    3. Peroxide clearance alone proves all recovery
    4. Line B has no antioxidant enzymes
    Answer and explanation

    A. Similar measured peroxide clearance with different recovery suggests damage history, vulnerability or repair differences. It does not prove a particular defect.

  21. A mutant overexpressing extracellular peroxide scavenging shows reduced early defense gene induction but no increase in membrane leakage. Which inference is most reasonable?

    1. ROS always harm plants
    2. Scavenging may have attenuated a necessary signaling pulse
    3. The scavenger necessarily generates singlet oxygen
    4. Membrane leakage is a direct measure of transcription
    Answer and explanation

    B. Controlled ROS are signals as well as oxidants. Lowering the pulse may attenuate defense even without evidence of greater injury; pathogen performance would need testing.

  22. Wild type and mutant have similar early ROS reporter peaks. Mutant retains high reporter oxidation after the challenge. Which experiment best separates persistent production from weak clearance?

    1. Assume production rates identical
    2. Measure only the original peak again
    3. Compare production inhibition and independent scavenging or recovery measurements with controls
    4. Measure leaf color only
    Answer and explanation

    C. The same peak does not constrain later fluxes. Source perturbations plus clearance measurements and sensor calibration can distinguish competing explanations.

  23. Mild pretreatment reduces subsequent leakage and increases APX activity. Which evidence best tests whether APX is required for acquired protection?

    1. Measure only APX transcript in one leaf
    2. Increase light for all plants without controls
    3. Infer causality from correlation
    4. Compare APX-deficient and complemented lines with and without pretreatment and challenge
    Answer and explanation

    D. A factorial genotype-by-pretreatment comparison with genetic rescue and matched challenge tests necessity more directly. Correlation alone cannot exclude altered light absorption or other adaptations.

  24. A plant has greater whole-leaf GSH abundance after stress but a more oxidized chloroplast glutathione sensor. Which conclusion is justified?

    1. Total abundance and compartment-specific redox state can change independently
    2. GSH abundance proves low chloroplast ROS
    3. The sensor measures total leaf GSH
    4. One result must be false
    Answer and explanation

    A. Whole-leaf pool size combines compartments and does not specify the local GSH/GSSG potential. Sensor readout and bulk abundance address different questions.

  25. A leaf has lower lipid hydroperoxide after treatment, but unchanged PSII efficiency and growth. Which is the most careful conclusion?

    1. The treatment improved all aspects of stress tolerance
    2. It altered a lipid oxidation endpoint but broader protection has not been shown
    3. It eliminated all ROS
    4. It restored nuclear DNA
    Answer and explanation

    B. A single marker may demonstrate a limited biochemical change. Functional benefit requires physiological or growth evidence; absence of change in other endpoints limits the conclusion.

  26. Consider a high-light leaf with inhibited catalase. Evaluate: I. SOD can continue generating H₂O₂ from superoxide. II. APX can remove peroxide if reduced ascorbate is available. III. Catalase inhibition necessarily abolishes every peroxide-removal route. Which statements are correct?

    1. I only
    2. II and III only
    3. I and II only
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and explanation

    C. SOD is upstream of peroxide removal and APX provides an alternative route. Catalase inhibition does not automatically abolish APX or other peroxidase reactions; their capacity and localization nevertheless limit compensation.

  27. In an illustrative time-course graph, peroxide rises after stress, reaches a peak and then falls while the stress treatment continues. Which experiment best distinguishes an increase in scavenging from a decrease in production?

    1. Measure total chlorophyll only
    2. Assume the decline proves catalase induction
    3. Measure peroxide only at its peak
    4. Compare independent estimates of source activity and peroxide clearance at early and late time points
    Answer and explanation

    D. Pool size reflects the difference between source and sink fluxes. Independent source and clearance measurements, with matching controls and validated probes, help separate these alternatives. Neither the peak nor a decline alone identifies catalase as the cause.

Assertion–reason — 4 additional questions

For each, select A if both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion; B if both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion; C if the assertion is true and the reason false; D if the assertion is false and the reason true.

  1. Assertion: Hydrogen peroxide may function as a local signal. Reason: Its reactivity and lifetime are regulated in part by compartment-specific removal, permitting selected oxidation events.

    Answer and explanation

    A. Both statements are true. The reason explains how a relatively persistent but regulated oxidant can participate in signaling.

  2. Assertion: A catalase-deficient leaf must show cell death in every light and CO₂ condition. Reason: Photorespiration generates peroxide in peroxisomes.

    Answer and explanation

    D. The assertion is false because phenotype depends on metabolic flux, alternative defenses and conditions. The reason is true; the cat2 study supports conditional rather than inevitable injury.

  3. Assertion: Two samples with identical GSH/GSSG ratios necessarily have identical glutathione redox potentials. Reason: The Nernst expression contains squared GSH activity and depends on absolute activities.

    Answer and explanation

    D. The assertion is false and the reason is true. Equal ratios can occur at different absolute concentrations, changing the reaction quotient.

  4. Assertion: Tocopherol can interrupt lipid-radical propagation. Reason: SOD catalyzes dismutation of superoxide.

    Answer and explanation

    B. Both statements are true, but SOD chemistry does not explain tocopherol-mediated chain termination. Tocopherol intercepts lipid peroxyl radicals by hydrogen donation.

Revision and active recall

Plant cells deliberately and incidentally generate ROS. The major production routes include photosynthetic excitation and electron transfer, mitochondrial electron leakage, peroxisomal oxidases and plasma membrane RBOHs. SOD converts superoxide to peroxide, catalase disproportionates peroxide, and APX uses ascorbate. MDHAR and DHAR–GR support antioxidant recycling. Carotenoids and tocopherol defend membrane-associated targets; repair restores functions not recovered by scavenging alone. ROS signals depend on their site, duration and clearance.
