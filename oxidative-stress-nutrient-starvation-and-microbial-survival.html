1. The problem of survival when growth becomes impossible

A bacterium growing in a nutrient-rich environment can increase its biomass, duplicate its genetic information and divide. These activities are possible only because a continuous supply of materials and energy supports them. In a natural habitat, however, growth-promoting conditions rarely remain constant. A soil particle may become dry, a freshwater habitat may lose an available carbon source, or a population living near an oxygen gradient may suddenly experience oxygen. The organism that produces the largest number of descendants during a brief favourable interval is not necessarily the one that leaves descendants after the environment changes. Survival between intervals of growth is a fundamental component of microbial physiology.

Consider the difference between a cell that cannot divide because its medium lacks nitrogen and a cell that has lost the ability to divide even when returned to a suitable medium. The first might still maintain its membrane, genome and enzymes and recover when nitrogen becomes available. The second has suffered irreversible injury under the conditions used to assess it. Thus growth arrest and death cannot be identified with one another. The distinction is particularly important in stationary-phase cultures, because a stable population count can conceal changes in the condition and behaviour of individual cells. The earlier chapters on growth and division establish how numbers increase. Here the question becomes how the capacity for future growth is preserved when current reproduction is no longer possible.

The cell faces two connected problems. The first is chemical damage. Oxygen can be a useful electron acceptor, yet incompletely reduced oxygen molecules can interfere with metabolic enzymes and other cellular structures. The second is resource shortage. A cell needs both molecular building blocks and energy to maintain its existing components, not merely to make new ones. Starvation may deprive it of the resources used to repair the damage. Conversely, damage to nutrient-metabolizing enzymes may create an internal shortage even when nutrients remain abundant outside.

A stress response is a physiological adjustment that reduces injury or helps maintain function under a perturbation. It may involve enzymes already present before the perturbation, or an induced response requiring changes in gene expression. Not every adjustment prevents all damage, and a response may come at a cost. A cell that invests in protective proteins has less immediate capacity for synthesizing ribosomes or rapidly expanding its biomass. This allocation problem explains why stress resistance and rapid growth are sometimes inversely related, although there is no universal numerical trade-off.

The study of stress therefore requires three separate observations: what the perturbation does to molecules, what the cell does in response, and what happens to the population after exposure. A molecular signal such as elevated expression of an antioxidant gene does not by itself establish that cells survive better. A slowly growing population is not necessarily dying. A survivor is not necessarily a genetically resistant mutant. Each inference requires an appropriate measurement and a comparison with a control.

The concepts developed here rely on redox chemistry, enzyme catalysis, membrane permeability, ATP generation and microbial population measurements. Oxidation means loss of electrons; reduction means gain of electrons. An enzyme accelerates a reaction but does not make atoms or electrons disappear. A colony-forming unit is an operational measure of a unit that gives rise to a visible colony under specified recovery conditions. These elementary ideas become especially useful when assessing oxidative damage and starvation survival. They allow us to move from the chemistry of an individual oxygen molecule to the interpretation of a survival curve.

2. The chemistry of partially reduced oxygen

The oxygen molecule used in aerobic respiration is a remarkably useful oxidant. Electrons ultimately pass from metabolic substrates through respiratory pathways to oxygen, and their transfer can be coupled to conservation of energy. Under normal operation the terminal reduction of molecular oxygen can be represented as O₂ + 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2H₂O. Four electrons are required because the two oxygen atoms in O₂ must each acquire two electrons to reach their oxidation state in water. The equation describes the net chemistry and does not imply that all respiratory enzymes transfer four free electrons simultaneously.

Reduction can also occur incompletely. Addition of one electron gives the superoxide radical anion, O₂•⁻. Addition of two electrons and two protons overall gives hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂. Under appropriate reaction conditions a peroxide may give rise to the hydroxyl radical, •OH. These compounds belong to the broad class of reactive oxygen species, or ROS. The class includes radical and nonradical species. A radical has an unpaired electron, whereas hydrogen peroxide is not a radical. It is incorrect to assume that every ROS is a radical or that every oxygen-containing molecule is an ROS.

The species differ in how far they travel and what they attack. At approximately neutral pH most superoxide is negatively charged rather than present as its protonated form, the hydroperoxyl radical HO₂•. Charged molecules generally cross lipid membranes less easily than comparable uncharged ones. Hydrogen peroxide is electrically neutral and can cross biological membranes to varying degrees, depending on membrane properties and available channels. The hydroxyl radical reacts extremely rapidly with molecules close to its site of formation. Consequently, a molecule that removes peroxide may protect a vulnerable target at a distance, but a hydroxyl radical produced immediately beside DNA is difficult to intercept before it reacts.

The chemical identity of the oxidant therefore predicts the kind of defence that will be useful. A cytosolic enzyme can remove cytosolic superoxide, but its presence does not guarantee protection from every extracellular oxidant. A periplasmic superoxide dismutase in a Gram-negative bacterium operates in a different compartment from a cytosolic superoxide dismutase. A peroxide-scavenging enzyme may lower the probability that a hydroxyl radical will be generated, even though the enzyme does not directly catalyse removal of hydroxyl radicals.

A second important distinction is between reactivity and abundance. A highly reactive species can exist at a very low steady concentration because it reacts almost immediately. A less reactive species may accumulate to a higher concentration and reach several potential targets. The amount of oxidant detected at one instant reflects both its production and removal. It cannot directly reveal the total number of damaging reactions over an earlier period.

Suppose two cells produce hydrogen peroxide at the same rate. One contains highly effective peroxide-removing enzymes, while the other lacks them. The second cell can accumulate peroxide even though both have identical production rates. Alternatively, two cells can exhibit similar peroxide concentrations while one produces and removes peroxide much more rapidly. In the latter case their fluxes, meaning amounts produced or removed per unit time, are different. This stock-versus-flow distinction will return when we interpret experiments.

Oxygen itself is not equivalent to oxidative stress. A well-adapted aerobe generally keeps internally formed oxidants low. Stress occurs when oxidant production and exposure exceed the effective capacity of prevention, detoxification and repair. This definition also allows oxygen-independent injury to occur in oxygen-sensitive organisms: oxygen may directly damage certain oxygen-sensitive enzymes, and not all oxygen toxicity is explained by the absence of a single antioxidant enzyme.

3. Where microbial oxidants come from

The cell does not need to encounter an external oxidizing chemical in order to experience oxidative damage. Many metabolic enzymes transfer electrons through cofactors such as flavins. A reduced cofactor holds electrons that can be transferred to another molecule. Most such transfers occur along controlled biochemical routes. Occasionally, molecular oxygen intercepts electrons from a reduced cofactor. This unintended reaction can produce superoxide or peroxide. An enzyme that carries out this incidental reaction is not necessarily defective; the side reaction may be a consequence of the chemistry that makes its normal catalytic function possible.

A familiar oversimplification attributes all intracellular ROS to leakage from a respiratory electron transport chain. Respiratory redox reactions are relevant, but soluble flavoproteins and other enzymes can also contribute substantially. In Escherichia coli, studies of endogenous oxidative stress have shown that accidental oxidation of reduced enzyme cofactors is a major source. The relative importance of different enzymes varies with organism, substrate, oxygen concentration and physiological state. A fermenting cell may therefore generate ROS when it encounters oxygen even if it is not conserving energy by aerobic respiration.

The external environment provides further sources. Microorganisms in close proximity compete for nutrients and space. Some release peroxide during their metabolism, and neighbouring cells may be exposed to it. Lactic acid bacteria provide examples of organisms whose oxygen-dependent reactions can produce extracellular hydrogen peroxide. The resulting chemical environment can inhibit susceptible neighbours. Such an ecological interaction does not require that every peroxide-generating organism evolved the reaction exclusively as an antimicrobial strategy.

Oxidants may also arise at boundaries between environments containing oxygen and environments containing reduced chemical compounds. For a microorganism located near such a boundary, a change in water flow or diffusion may alter exposure without changing its own metabolism. The external oxidant may enter the cell, react at its envelope or alter extracellular nutrients. A purely intracellular explanation would overlook these effects.

Another authentic biological example is the oxidative burst of phagocytic immune cells. Activation of an NADPH oxidase system transfers electrons to oxygen and produces superoxide in association with a phagocytic compartment. Downstream chemistry yields other oxidants. Microorganisms engulfed by a phagocyte may therefore encounter oxidative stress together with other challenges, including nutrient limitation and changes in pH. It would be misleading to assign every loss of microbial viability in that setting to peroxide alone, because the compartment contains several potentially damaging factors. The example demonstrates why microbial stress responses are studied as interacting systems rather than isolated textbook reactions.

Redox cycling provides a useful chemical principle. A redox-active compound may receive electrons from cellular donors and then transfer them to oxygen, regenerating its original oxidation state. If the cycle repeats, many electron transfers to oxygen can occur without consuming one molecule of the redox-active compound for every oxygen molecule reduced. The cell experiences two burdens: formation of ROS and consumption of its reducing resources. A response to such a compound can consequently include reducing its entry or increasing its removal, in addition to antioxidant enzymes. This distinction explains why the bacterial SoxR regulatory system should not simply be described as a universal direct superoxide detector.

Oxygen availability also varies among cells. A small population within a dense aggregate may consume oxygen near the exterior faster than oxygen reaches the interior. Cells occupying different positions can consequently have different electron-transfer chemistry and stress histories. Bulk measurements average those differences. The same bulk oxygen concentration is not proof that all cells have received the same oxidative challenge.

Microbial ecology and intracellular metabolism thus meet at the point of oxidant exposure. To identify the source in an experiment, it is useful to compare cells with an appropriate unstressed baseline, examine whether the oxidant is supplied externally, and distinguish production from impaired removal. A mutation that increases peroxide accumulation may have disrupted a scavenger rather than increased electron leakage. A change in oxygen consumption may change ROS production, but it is not a direct measurement of ROS production. Scientific inference requires the chemical and physiological observations to support one another.

4. From oxidative chemistry to cellular dysfunction

The consequences of oxidative stress depend on which molecules are modified. An enzyme is not simply a chain of amino acids. Its function may depend on a precisely arranged active site, a metal ion or a cluster of metal and sulfur atoms. Modification of a small but essential part of the enzyme can abolish activity even though most of its structure remains intact.

Iron–sulfur clusters are especially instructive. A number of enzymes use these clusters for electron transfer or catalysis. In some enzymes an exposed iron atom in a cluster participates directly in binding or transforming a substrate. Superoxide and hydrogen peroxide can oxidize susceptible clusters and cause loss of an iron atom. The damaged cluster then fails to support the reaction. If the affected enzyme is required to synthesize an amino acid or process a carbon compound, the cell may experience an apparent nutrient deficiency despite possessing the relevant genes.

This mechanism connects oxidative injury to growth limitation. Imagine a cell that can normally synthesize an essential amino acid from a simpler precursor. Oxidative inactivation of one enzyme in that pathway blocks the route. Translation becomes limited by the amino acid. Growth may resume when the missing amino acid is supplied, provided the rest of the cell remains functional. Supplying the product bypasses the damaged reaction but does not itself remove superoxide or restore the iron–sulfur cluster. The experiment distinguishes a metabolic bottleneck from complete cell death.

Iron is useful because it can exchange electrons, but this property creates another risk. Ferrous iron, Fe²⁺, can react with peroxide in the Fenton reaction: Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH. Charge is conserved: the left side carries two positive charges and the right side also carries a net charge of two. One electron from Fe²⁺ contributes to peroxide cleavage. The hydroxyl radical generated can attack a nearby biomolecule. The risk depends not only on peroxide concentration but also on availability of redox-active iron. Total cellular iron includes metal held tightly in proteins and storage complexes, so it is not identical to the chemically accessible iron pool.

Oxidative disruption of an iron–sulfur cluster can therefore have two consequences. The enzyme loses function, and released iron may contribute to further oxidative chemistry if it enters an accessible pool. The cell must coordinate iron acquisition, storage, incorporation into enzymes and repair. Simply eliminating all iron would prevent essential metabolism; simply importing more iron might worsen peroxide-dependent injury. Homeostasis involves maintaining a usable supply while limiting inappropriate reactions.

Proteins contain other chemically susceptible sites. Cysteine side chains contain thiol groups, which can be oxidized to forms that alter catalytic activity or form disulfide bonds. Some reversible thiol modifications function in signalling; others inhibit enzymes or alter protein folding. More severe oxidation may result in persistent structural damage, and the cell then requires protein quality control, including chaperones that assist folding and proteases that remove irreversibly damaged proteins. A chaperone does not reverse every chemical modification. Its contribution depends on the nature of the injury.

DNA can be damaged when reactive chemistry occurs near the genetic material. Oxidation of guanine can generate 8-oxoguanine, a modified base that may contribute to mutation if it escapes correction and is copied inappropriately. Oxidative reactions can also damage the deoxyribose backbone or produce breaks. It is necessary to distinguish damage from mutation. Damage is a chemical alteration; a mutation is a stable change in genetic sequence. A damaged base may be corrected before replication, the cell may die, or a mutation may become fixed. The complete molecular machinery of DNA repair is a separate topic, but this distinction is essential for explaining why oxidative stress is not equivalent to inheritable change.

Membrane injury can impair selective permeability and energy conservation. Lipid peroxidation is a chain process in which an initiating reaction produces a lipid radical, which reacts with oxygen and can propagate damage to another lipid. The vulnerability depends strongly on lipid composition. Polyunsaturated fatty acids are particularly susceptible; many bacteria have membranes with relatively few polyunsaturated chains, so lipid peroxidation should not automatically be described as the dominant lesion in every bacterium. Oxidation of membrane proteins can also compromise transport or respiratory activity without widespread lipid peroxidation.

The physiological result may be loss of ATP production, failure of biosynthetic pathways, disruption of ion gradients, or increased demand for repair. These effects can reinforce each other. A damaged respiratory protein may reduce energy conservation; inadequate energy then limits restoration of proteins and membranes. On the other hand, slowing growth may reduce the demand for an affected biosynthetic pathway, allowing maintenance reactions to continue. The final outcome is determined by lesion type, rate of injury, protective capacity and opportunities for recovery rather than by a single universal ROS concentration.

5. Superoxide dismutase: removing a radical by disproportionation

Superoxide is removed efficiently by the enzyme superoxide dismutase, abbreviated SOD. The word dismutation, or disproportionation, refers to a reaction in which molecules of the same species undergo opposite redox changes. The net reaction is 2O₂•⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. One superoxide loses an electron and becomes molecular oxygen; the other gains an electron and, together with protons, becomes hydrogen peroxide. The result is not the immediate elimination of all ROS. Instead, two superoxide molecules are converted into one peroxide molecule and one molecule of oxygen.

A metal-containing SOD uses changes in oxidation state of its catalytic metal to facilitate the two electron-transfer steps. In a schematic metal cycle, the oxidized metal accepts an electron from one superoxide, producing O₂ and a reduced metal. The reduced metal transfers an electron to a second superoxide, and proton participation yields H₂O₂ while the oxidized metal is regenerated. The precise metal and coordination environment differ among SOD families, but the net stoichiometry is the same. Because the enzyme returns to its original catalytic state, the metal is not consumed once per pair of superoxide molecules.

Microbes possess several types of SOD. Iron- and manganese-containing enzymes occur in many bacteria; copper/zinc and nickel forms occur in particular groups or compartments. E. coli has cytosolic iron and manganese SODs and can also express a periplasmic copper/zinc enzyme. The existence of more than one form does not imply that each has the same regulation or subcellular location. The physiological benefit depends on whether the enzyme intercepts superoxide near vulnerable targets, whether the correct metal is available, and whether peroxide produced downstream can be removed.

A classical way to infer enzyme function uses a reaction in which superoxide reduces an indicator. If the addition of a protein decreases indicator reduction, the protein may compete with the indicator for superoxide. McCord and Fridovich established the enzymatic activity of superoxide dismutase using such biochemical reasoning. A lower indicator signal alone is insufficient to identify the enzyme's mechanism, since another substance could interfere with the indicator or its source. The inference becomes stronger when the product chemistry, enzyme purification and catalytic properties are consistent with dismutation.

A separate question is whether SOD matters inside living microbes. Carlioz and Touati studied E. coli mutants defective in the cytosolic SOD genes sodA and sodB. Loss of both functions prevented aerobic growth on a minimal glucose medium, although the double mutant could grow aerobically under richer nutritional conditions. This result shows why medium composition is an indispensable experimental variable. If enzymes required for biosynthesis are particularly vulnerable to superoxide, providing their products can partially bypass the consequence of injury. The rich-medium phenotype does not establish that oxidative stress has disappeared.

The genetic experiment illustrates the value of overlapping defences. Losing one SOD may have a smaller effect than losing both when the remaining form can partly compensate. A double mutant can reveal the combined importance of functions that appear relatively dispensable when examined separately. A rigorous comparison would use matched genetic backgrounds and evaluate growth and survival separately. Restoring the missing gene can help determine whether a phenotype is actually attributable to the targeted function rather than an unrelated change.

An elementary stoichiometric problem reinforces the chemistry. If 80 nanomoles of superoxide undergo dismutation, the ideal reaction produces 40 nanomoles of hydrogen peroxide and 40 nanomoles of oxygen and consumes 80 nanomoles of protons. The ratio follows directly from the balanced equation. In a real cell, peroxide may be removed as soon as it is formed, and other reactions compete for superoxide. Thus the calculated 40 nanomoles is the amount produced by the specified reaction, not a prediction of the final measurable peroxide pool.

SOD and peroxide-removing enzymes are linked. A cell with a highly active SOD but inadequate peroxide clearance can still suffer peroxide-dependent injury. Conversely, increasing catalase does not replace an enzyme that directly prevents superoxide from attacking a vulnerable iron–sulfur cluster. Each enzyme changes the chemical species present, and their contributions must be interpreted as a sequence of reactions rather than as a collection of interchangeable antioxidant labels.

6. Catalase, peroxidases and the demand for reducing power

Hydrogen peroxide is removed through several enzymatic strategies. Catalase catalyses the net reaction 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. In this disproportionation one peroxide molecule is oxidized to oxygen and another is reduced to water. The reaction does not require the net expenditure of a separate electron donor for each pair of peroxide molecules. Many catalases use a heme group to carry out the sequential reactions, although manganese-containing catalases also occur. The high turnover of catalase can be advantageous when peroxide exposure is substantial.

In a simplified heme-catalase cycle, peroxide reacts with the resting enzyme to form water and an oxidized enzyme intermediate. A second peroxide molecule reduces this intermediate, yielding oxygen and another water molecule. The enzyme returns to its resting catalytic state. This explanation is enough to understand the overall reaction without memorizing every heme electronic state. The essential feature is that the two substrate molecules perform complementary redox roles.

Peroxidases use a different net strategy. They reduce peroxide to water using electrons supplied by a reducing partner. The generic balanced half-reaction for peroxide reduction is H₂O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2H₂O. A corresponding oxidation of an electron donor supplies those two electrons. The identity of the donor depends on the enzyme and cellular system. For instance, bacterial alkyl hydroperoxide reductase AhpC can use a catalytic cysteine to react with peroxide and an associated reducing system to restore the enzyme to its active state. In the classical E. coli AhpCF system, reducing equivalents from NADH can reach AhpC through AhpF.

This distinction has a physiological consequence. A peroxidase may function effectively at low peroxide concentrations, but continuous operation requires a supply of reducing equivalents. NADH and NADPH are not unlimited resources. They are generated and consumed by metabolism, and starvation may reduce their availability. Catalase, in contrast, does not consume a separate reductant in its net reaction. The comparison does not mean that catalase needs no cellular investment: the enzyme must be synthesized, folded and provided with its cofactors, and its abundance and activity may be regulated.

In E. coli, Ahp-based removal is particularly important for low endogenous peroxide fluxes, while catalases become especially significant under larger peroxide loads. This pattern is not a universal concentration threshold applicable to all microorganisms. It depends on enzyme abundance, kinetic properties, electron donor availability and environmental conditions. Catalase–peroxidases, such as KatG, can exhibit both activities. A catalase-negative organism may still possess alternative peroxide defences. The presence or absence of visible oxygen evolution is therefore not a comprehensive test of oxidative survival.

Consider a stoichiometric comparison. Dismutation of 100 nanomoles of superoxide generates 50 nanomoles of peroxide. If all of this peroxide is removed by catalase, it produces an additional 25 nanomoles of oxygen and 50 nanomoles of water. The oxygen released by SOD itself is 50 nanomoles, giving a total of 75 nanomoles from the two specified reactions. If peroxide is instead reduced by a peroxidase, it requires 100 nanomoles of electrons, equivalent in principle to 50 nanomoles of a two-electron donor, and gives 100 nanomoles of water in the reduction half-reaction with the necessary protons. It does not evolve molecular oxygen in that half-reaction.

An observed gas signal can be understood from these equations but should not be overinterpreted. Catalase activity often gives oxygen evolution after exposure to peroxide, whereas a peroxidase can remove peroxide without oxygen evolution. A strong gas signal can reflect substantial catalase capacity, but it does not show that intracellular peroxide was always low or that cells remained viable. A cell may possess antioxidant enzyme activity after losing its ability to reproduce. Measuring the reaction and measuring survival answer different biological questions.

The removal system must also compete with damaging reactions. A peroxide molecule may be intercepted by a scavenger, diffuse away, or react with an accessible metal. Increasing scavenger activity can lower the probability of damage, but its benefit depends on its location and the rate at which it reaches peroxide. Efficient interception before Fenton chemistry is particularly valuable because the hydroxyl radical is too reactive to be managed by an equivalent universal scavenging enzyme. Prevention of its production is therefore a central strategy.

The coordinated reactions of SOD, catalase and peroxidases illustrate a general principle of microbial homeostasis. A defence that changes one dangerous intermediate into another is useful only in the context of downstream removal and repair. Protecting one enzyme or one molecular class may be insufficient if another essential cellular function becomes the limiting factor.

7. Induction, metal homeostasis and repair of oxidative injury

Maintaining antioxidant enzymes constitutively has a benefit: some protection is available at the instant of exposure. Yet synthesizing high amounts of every protective protein when they are unnecessary would consume energy and materials. Many microbes therefore combine baseline protection with induced responses. A stress-responsive regulator senses an aspect of the perturbation and changes expression of a collection of genes. This collection is called a regulon when its members are coordinated by a shared regulatory system.

OxyR provides a well-studied example in E. coli. The regulator contains a sensitive cysteine that can react with hydrogen peroxide. Oxidation leads to a conformational change involving a disulfide bond, altering the regulator's action on particular promoters. Increased expression of peroxide-removing and protective functions helps restore cellular redox balance. When exposure decreases, reducing systems can return OxyR toward its reduced state. The sequence is chemically meaningful: peroxide changes a sensory residue; the regulator changes transcription; newly synthesized proteins modify the capacity of the cell to manage peroxide. It is not an instantaneous elimination of the oxidant by OxyR itself.

SoxR and SoxS illustrate a distinct arrangement. SoxR contains an iron–sulfur sensory cluster that can be oxidized in response to certain redox-active compounds. In E. coli, activated SoxR promotes expression of SoxS, which regulates additional protective genes. The response may include superoxide defence as well as functions that reduce the burden caused by the compounds. Although textbooks sometimes introduce SoxRS as a superoxide stress system, evidence indicates that redox-active compounds themselves can activate SoxR effectively. It is not accurate to treat SoxR as a direct universal measurement of superoxide concentration. Other bacteria have different arrangements, so the E. coli two-regulator model is an example rather than a rule for all microbes.

PerR is a peroxide-responsive repressor found in organisms such as Bacillus subtilis. Its regulation involves a metal-dependent oxidation mechanism that can prevent normal repression of protective genes. The regulatory logic differs from simply oxidizing a cysteine on OxyR. Both systems show how exposure to a chemical can change the activity of a transcriptional regulator, but the molecules involved and the direction of regulatory changes are not interchangeable.

Induction takes time. If exposure is sufficiently severe, essential functions may fail before protective proteins accumulate. An earlier mild stress can sometimes induce protection that benefits cells during a subsequent larger exposure. Such a response depends on timing, the initial condition of the cell and the ability to synthesize the needed proteins. Basal enzymes and induced enzymes therefore contribute differently during the first moments and later stages of stress. Measuring only a late expression response can conceal why early survival differs between two populations.

Iron regulation is inseparable from peroxide management. Storage and sequestration of iron can reduce the accessible pool that contributes to Fenton chemistry, while appropriate iron–sulfur cluster assembly restores damaged enzymes. Dps, a DNA-protecting protein prominent in stationary-phase E. coli, combines DNA-associated protection with iron management. The cell is not protected simply because Dps binds DNA; lowering inappropriate iron-dependent chemistry is also important. Different microbes use different sets of iron storage and protection proteins. The general reasoning is to distinguish usable iron from uncontrolled redox-active iron.

Repair is the response after damage has occurred. Reassembly of susceptible enzyme cofactors may restore metabolic pathways. Protein degradation and resynthesis may replace an oxidized enzyme. DNA repair can remove or correct particular lesions before they become inherited mutations. Protection and repair can be distinguished experimentally by asking whether the initial level of injury is lower or whether recovery is faster after the same injury. A mutant that suffers the same initial damage as a normal cell but recovers poorly may be defective in repair rather than initial detoxification.

It is possible for a cell to show increased antioxidant gene expression and poor survival at the same time. Strong induction may indicate that the cell is experiencing a severe challenge. The expression change is evidence of sensing or regulation, not proof of successful protection. A defensible mechanistic explanation compares the timing of induction, enzyme activity, a relevant damage measurement and the eventual outcome under defined recovery conditions. This multi-level approach prepares us for starvation, where regulation may be particularly important even when no immediate oxidant exposure occurs.

8. Quantifying oxidant balance and testing antioxidant function

Chemical equations explain what antioxidant enzymes do, but the physiological question is how fast their reactions occur relative to damage. Imagine a well-mixed intracellular compartment whose peroxide concentration is represented by H, measured in micromoles per litre. Let P represent the rate at which peroxide enters or is produced in that compartment, in micromoles per litre per minute. In the simplest approximation, removal occurs at rate kH, where k is a first-order effective removal constant with units of inverse minutes. This approximation is reasonable over a limited concentration range when the relevant removal processes respond approximately proportionally to peroxide concentration.

The change in peroxide concentration is then dH/dt = P − kH. This equation is a mass balance for the chosen compartment. Production is positive because it adds peroxide; removal is negative because it subtracts peroxide. At a steady state, the concentration is not changing, so dH/dt = 0. Setting the right-hand side to zero gives P = kH, or H = P/k. The units confirm the result: dividing micromoles per litre per minute by inverse minutes leaves micromoles per litre. At steady state the production and removal fluxes are equal, not zero. A functioning cell can continuously generate peroxide while maintaining a relatively low concentration.

For a hypothetical cell with P = 0.60 micromolar per minute and k = 3.0 per minute, the steady concentration is 0.20 micromolar. If an impairment of scavenging reduces k to 0.75 per minute without changing production, the concentration becomes 0.80 micromolar, a fourfold increase. If instead the production rate quadruples while k remains unchanged, the same concentration is obtained. Measuring concentration alone cannot distinguish these causes. A separate measurement of production or clearance, or a perturbation affecting one process specifically, is required.

After production has ceased, the model simplifies to dH/dt = −kH. Dividing by H and integrating from H₀ at time zero to H at time t gives ln(H/H₀) = −kt, so H = H₀ exp(−kt). The half-time is the time at which H/H₀ = 1/2. Taking logarithms gives −kt = −ln 2, and the half-time is ln 2/k. With k = 0.75 per minute, this is approximately 0.924 minutes. With k = 3.0 per minute, it is about 0.231 minutes. These are invented teaching values, not measured bacterial constants. They show how a change in scavenging capacity affects both a sustained concentration and the speed of recovery after exposure.

This model is not valid across every peroxide concentration. An enzyme may become saturated, lose activity or run short of its reducing substrate. If scavenging follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics, the removal rate approaches a maximum instead of increasing without limit. At concentrations far below the apparent Michaelis constant, the rate VmaxH/(Km + H) is approximately (Vmax/Km)H, which explains the first-order approximation. Here Vmax has units of concentration per time and Km has units of concentration. At high concentrations the approximation fails. The cell may also induce additional antioxidant enzymes, so k may change with time. Compartmentalization and diffusion may make the well-mixed assumption inappropriate.

In a comparative example, suppose scavenger A has an apparent Km of 2 micromolar and Vmax of 12 micromolar per minute, whereas scavenger B has Km of 100 micromolar and Vmax of 100 micromolar per minute. At H = 1 micromolar, A removes 12/3 = 4 micromolar per minute and B removes 100/101 ≈ 0.99 micromolar per minute. At H = 100 micromolar, A removes 1200/102 ≈ 11.76 micromolar per minute and B removes 10000/200 = 50 micromolar per minute. The hypothetical example demonstrates why the dominant removal route can change with concentration. It does not assign these numerical constants to catalase or AhpC, nor does it assume that the different mechanisms obey identical kinetics under all conditions.

Another useful model compares peroxide removal with injury. Suppose a peroxide molecule can enter one of two competing first-order pathways: detoxification with effective rate constant kd and reaction at a vulnerable site with effective rate constant ki. If these constants remain fixed and the molecule has no other fate in the model, the probability that it enters the damaging pathway is ki/(kd + ki). For kd = 9 per minute and ki = 1 per minute, the probability is 0.10. Increasing kd to 19 per minute reduces the probability to 0.05. The point is not that actual intracellular reactions have only two possible outcomes, but that the presence of a scavenging enzyme can reduce the likelihood of injury by intercepting a shared chemical intermediate. Sequestering accessible iron can instead reduce ki. Both interventions can protect, through distinct mechanisms.

How would a scientist decide whether a protective mutation changes peroxide removal rather than tolerance of peroxide injury? A strong comparison would involve matched strains, comparable starting physiological states and an appropriate control without peroxide. One can compare disappearance of peroxide, the activity of a relevant target enzyme and subsequent colony formation. If a mutant clears peroxide normally but retains less activity in a susceptible enzyme, its problem may lie downstream of scavenging. If restoring the mutated gene restores the phenotype, the causal inference is stronger. Even then, an altered expression pattern or a general growth defect could influence the observations, so the untreated control remains necessary.

Fluorescent redox-sensitive indicators and biochemical enzyme assays report different aspects of the system. An indicator may respond to more than one oxidant, its signal can depend on cellular uptake or metabolic state, and the indicator itself can participate in chemical reactions. Therefore an increase in generic fluorescence is not sufficient evidence that a particular ROS increased. A change in an oxidant-sensitive enzyme provides information about the activity of that target but may not distinguish oxidation from other forms of inactivation. A viability measurement tests recovery rather than oxidant identity. Converging observations are much more informative than a single signal.

The same logic applies when comparing antioxidant activity between exponential and stationary phase. A higher total enzyme activity in one culture might merely reflect more biomass. Activity per milligram of protein, or another justified normalization, can help distinguish abundance from cell number. When comparing survival fractions, equal initial viable counts or a clearly specified normalization are important. These are general principles of experimental interpretation, not a requirement to perform advanced laboratory methods. They enable students to recognize which variable has actually been measured and to avoid confusing a change in biochemical flux with a change in the number of survivors.

9. Nutrient limitation, starvation and the maintenance economy

A nutrient becomes limiting when the amount or rate at which it is supplied prevents the population from achieving a higher rate of growth or biomass production under the specified conditions. This may occur even when other nutrients are plentiful. Starvation is a more severe situation in which a required resource is absent or available in insufficient quantity to support ordinary cellular demand. The transition is not necessarily sharp. Some environments supply a trickle of nutrient that permits maintenance but little measurable growth; others remove the relevant nutrient almost completely. A microorganism must respond to the actual resource constraints rather than to a universal condition called starvation.

The first step is to identify which resource is missing. Carbon is needed for constructing organic molecules and may also be used as an energy source. Nitrogen is essential for amino acids and nucleotides. Phosphorus is needed for nucleic acids, phospholipids and phosphate-containing metabolites. Sulfur is needed for certain amino acids and cofactors. Iron supports electron-transfer proteins and many catalytic reactions. A shortage of one resource can affect pathways that appear unrelated when viewed in isolation because the construction and operation of the cell require these resources together.

A carbon-starved heterotrophic bacterium may have too little substrate to make ATP and reducing equivalents. It may also have too little carbon skeleton for biosynthesis. By contrast, when nitrogen becomes limiting while an oxidizable carbon substrate remains available, energy generation may continue for a time even though protein and nucleotide synthesis cannot sustain previous growth. In a phosphorus-limited environment, the synthesis of phosphorus-rich cellular material becomes constrained; the organism may alter nutrient acquisition or mobilize internal phosphorus reserves. These examples explain why an identical decrease in population growth can arise from different biochemical causes.

Resource conservation is not the same as stopping all reactions. A living cell maintains electrochemical gradients, keeps potentially harmful compounds under control, repairs molecular damage and preserves the integrity of its genetic information. These activities can require ATP or reducing power even when there is no net production of new biomass. The energy expenditure associated with such functions is often described in elementary terms as maintenance demand. The demand is not a universal constant: temperature, damage, membrane leakage and physiological state may change it.

Suppose a culture receives an energy source equivalent to 80 arbitrary energy units per hour and requires 20 units per hour for maintenance. At most 60 units per hour remain for other processes in this simplified budget. If the supply falls to 25 units while maintenance remains 20, only five units are left. If supply falls below maintenance demand, the cell cannot indefinitely maintain the same set of activities at their previous rates. It may lower expenditure, use stored compounds, alter membrane properties or eventually lose viability. This is a qualitative budget model; energy allocation is not calculated from the growth yield formula without additional assumptions.

Internal reserves can delay the consequences of an external nutrient shortage. Some bacteria accumulate glycogen, polyhydroxyalkanoates or other storage compounds when conditions favour their synthesis. These stores may supply carbon or energy later, depending on the organism and the missing nutrient. Polyphosphate can serve several physiological functions, including a reservoir of phosphate in many microbes. A reserve does not solve every shortage. Degrading glycogen cannot provide nitrogen if nitrogen is the only missing element; a cell needs the right resource, not simply any stored material. Furthermore, obtaining energy from reserves requires functional enzymes and suitable metabolic pathways.

Recycling differs from reserve mobilization. A storage compound is accumulated partly for later use, whereas an ordinary cellular component may be dismantled and its constituents reused. During starvation a cell may degrade selected proteins or RNA and recover amino acids or nucleotides. This can provide resources for making protective proteins while reducing the burden of maintaining unused growth machinery. Bacterial proteolysis and RNA turnover are not identical to eukaryotic lysosomal autophagy, although both address the general problem of intracellular resource recovery. The bacterial cell does not possess a lysosome merely because it recycles macromolecules.

A change in cellular size can accompany starvation. Some bacteria undergo reductive divisions, producing smaller cells with little increase in total biomass, whereas others mainly cease division or change cell shape. A decrease in average cell size may reduce the amount of material that must be maintained, but it does not prove that every cell is dormant. Optical density can change when average size, shape or refractive properties change. Thus a direct count, viable count and biomass measurement may tell different stories, exactly as established in the earlier microbial growth chapters.

Nutrient acquisition also changes. A cell exposed to low concentrations of a substrate may increase high-affinity transport or induce enzymes that use an alternative resource. These adjustments have a cost. A transport system can concentrate a nutrient only if the necessary energy and driving forces are available. A biosynthetic pathway can replace a missing organic nutrient only if its precursors are available and the required enzymes remain functional. Physiological plasticity cannot violate mass conservation or energy conservation.

The transition from growth to maintenance is important because growth machinery itself is expensive. Ribosomal RNA and ribosomal proteins represent a substantial investment in rapidly growing bacteria. When the supply of amino acids is inadequate, continued production of ribosomes cannot by itself restore translation. Rather than maintain the previous pattern of resource allocation, many bacteria activate regulatory systems that reduce investment in growth and increase investment in survival. The stringent response provides a classic example of this reorganization.

10. The stringent response: discovering a starvation signal

Early investigations of bacterial physiology established that the synthesis of stable RNA is closely connected with nutrient availability. In a rapidly growing bacterial cell, ribosomal RNA, or rRNA, forms the structural and catalytic core of ribosomes. Transfer RNA, or tRNA, supplies amino acids during translation. Together these stable RNAs support the high protein synthesis capacity needed for rapid growth. If a required amino acid becomes unavailable, a ribosome cannot complete its normal task merely because additional rRNA is produced. The coordination of RNA synthesis with amino acid supply is therefore an important regulatory problem.

Classical experiments with E. coli showed that amino acid starvation could cause a pronounced reduction in stable RNA synthesis. Certain mutant strains did not show the usual regulation and were historically called relaxed mutants, in contrast to strains displaying stringent control. The distinction revealed that the change in RNA synthesis was not merely a passive result of missing substrates for RNA polymerization. Amino acid shortage and RNA synthesis involve different immediate substrates. A regulatory link was needed to explain why the rate of stable RNA synthesis responded so strongly to the supply of an amino acid.

Cashel and Gallant investigated the nucleotide composition of starved cells and described unusual spots appearing during chromatographic separation of cellular nucleotides. These historically termed magic spots were associated with guanosine polyphosphates later recognized as ppGpp and pppGpp. The notation refers to nucleotide molecules with additional phosphate groups. Guanosine tetraphosphate, ppGpp, has two phosphates at the 5′ position and two at the 3′ position of the ribose. Guanosine pentaphosphate, pppGpp, has three at the 5′ position and two at the 3′ position. The combined notation (p)ppGpp refers to both compounds.

The significance of these nucleotides is regulatory rather than structural. GTP is used in translation and many other cellular processes. Converting a fraction of the guanine nucleotide pool into (p)ppGpp produces an intracellular signal capable of changing the operation of several processes. Such a stress-associated signal is called an alarmone. It does not convey information between different cells merely because it is called a signal; here the key event is a change in the intracellular nucleotide pool.

A useful experimental contrast concerns timing. Suppose starvation is imposed on two matched populations. One shows a prompt increase in (p)ppGpp and a reduction in stable RNA synthesis. The other lacks the relevant synthetase function and does not show the usual nucleotide increase or RNA response. This pattern supports a role for the alarmone in coordinating RNA synthesis with starvation. It is still necessary to distinguish correlation from causation. Restoring alarmone production, or independently changing the regulatory nucleotide concentration, can strengthen the causal argument. The history of the stringent response illustrates how physiological observation, genetic differences and chemical identification together reveal a regulatory mechanism.

The response was originally characterized through amino acid starvation and stable RNA synthesis, but its significance is broader. In many bacteria (p)ppGpp influences transcription, translation, nucleotide metabolism and other growth-associated activities. Its effects vary among species, and the details of the molecular mechanism cannot be generalized from E. coli to every bacterium. Nevertheless, the shared biological theme is coordination of growth capacity with resource availability.

The term stringent response should not be used as a synonym for any decrease in growth. A cell could grow slowly because temperature reduces catalytic rates, because it lacks an essential enzyme, or because an external compound directly inhibits translation. Not all such situations require the same change in alarmone concentration. Similarly, activation of the stringent response does not mean every biosynthetic reaction stops. Some biosynthetic activities, particularly those that help overcome a particular nutrient limitation, may increase. The important feature is a selective change in resource allocation, rather than universal transcriptional or metabolic shutdown.

11. From uncharged tRNA to (p)ppGpp: the RelA and SpoT system

To understand how a bacterium can recognize amino acid shortage, recall the mechanism by which tRNA carries amino acids to a ribosome. An aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase links an amino acid to its corresponding tRNA using energy from ATP. The resulting aminoacyl-tRNA is called charged tRNA. During translation the amino acid is incorporated into a polypeptide and the tRNA becomes deacylated, or uncharged. A fresh amino acid must be attached before that tRNA can contribute another amino acid to a growing protein.

If one amino acid becomes scarce, the fraction of its corresponding tRNA that is charged can decrease. At a ribosome translating a codon that requires the missing amino acid, the availability of suitable charged tRNA is reduced. In E. coli, deacylated tRNA associated with a starved ribosomal A site can activate the alarmone synthetase RelA. The A site is the aminoacyl-tRNA-binding site of the ribosome. This is an informative location for a sensor because it reports the supply of amino acids at the very process that consumes them for protein production.

Activated RelA transfers a pyrophosphate group from ATP to a guanine nucleotide. With GDP as the acceptor, the idealized reaction is ATP + GDP → AMP + ppGpp. With GTP as the acceptor, ATP + GTP → AMP + pppGpp. Each reaction transfers two phosphates together from ATP to the 3′ position of the acceptor nucleotide; the original 5′ phosphates remain. The products contain the correct total number of phosphate groups: five on both sides in the GDP reaction and six on both sides in the GTP reaction. Detailed acid-base species are omitted from these biochemical shorthand equations.

It is useful to understand why a cell expends ATP to make a signal at a time when resources are scarce. A relatively small signalling pool can coordinate much larger changes in cellular activity. Producing an alarmone consumes chemical energy, but reducing unnecessary production of growth machinery can save a greater investment. The signal therefore represents an allocation decision carried out by molecular interactions, not a claim that the bacterium consciously chooses what to do.

In E. coli, SpoT is also central to control of the alarmone pool. Unlike RelA, which is primarily a synthetase in this organism, SpoT has both synthesis and hydrolysis capabilities, with its hydrolytic activity particularly important for keeping (p)ppGpp levels appropriately controlled. SpoT integrates several non-amino-acid stresses, including signals associated with fatty acid and other nutrient limitations. Hydrolysis removes the added 3′ pyrophosphate moiety, regenerating the corresponding guanine nucleotide. A further enzyme, GppA, can convert pppGpp to ppGpp in E. coli. These reactions demonstrate that a measured alarmone concentration depends on synthesis, interconversion and removal.

A cell need not increase synthesis in order to increase the concentration of a regulatory molecule if removal is reduced. Conversely, recovery of a previous nutrient supply does not instantly eliminate every alarmone molecule. Hydrolysis and turnover return the pool toward the growth-associated state. This is another instance of a balance between rates rather than a simple switch. An exam problem may describe a mutation affecting alarmone hydrolysis and ask why the growth-associated state is difficult to restore. The appropriate prediction is that the alarmone could remain elevated, subject to its other routes of turnover and synthesis.

The names RelA and SpoT are derived from bacterial systems studied in particular organisms. Many other bacteria possess a long bifunctional Rel protein, and some possess additional small alarmone synthetases. These arrangements should be recognized as variations of the general principle rather than memorized as a universal two-gene system. Some microbes lack the bacterial system entirely, and eukaryotic nutrient sensing must be considered in its own molecular context. The Academy scope here is the bacterial physiological principle, not a catalogue of all Rel homologues.

A frequently encountered misconception is that a starved cell produces (p)ppGpp because it has abundant free uncharged tRNA everywhere in the cytoplasm, irrespective of the status of translation. The classical RelA response is linked specifically to a starved ribosome. This distinction matters because a direct inhibitor of a translation step can change ribosome occupancy and therefore affect alarmone production in ways not predictable from the statement that translation has slowed. The causal chain begins with the particular nutrient shortage, continues through reduced charging and the ribosomal signal, and leads to regulation of the alarmone pool.

The effect of alarmone synthesis is not simply explained by the loss of GTP consumed in making it. The alarmone can interact with regulatory targets, and changes in guanine nucleotide metabolism can themselves influence the physiology of some organisms. The relative contributions vary. This distinction prepares us to examine transcriptional reprogramming, where the same small molecule can have different effects at different promoters.

12. How the stringent response redirects cellular resources

A rapidly growing E. coli cell invests strongly in ribosome production because protein synthesis capacity supports a high rate of biomass accumulation. RNA polymerase is responsible for making RNA molecules from DNA templates. Its activity is distributed across many promoters, each of which is a region of DNA that helps determine transcription initiation. During starvation, redistribution of transcriptional activity is more useful than indiscriminately stopping RNA synthesis. The cell needs fewer new ribosomes but may need more nutrient acquisition functions, amino acid biosynthetic enzymes and protective proteins.

In E. coli, ppGpp can interact with RNA polymerase and act together with the regulatory protein DksA. The effect depends on properties of individual promoters and the steps involved in forming a productive transcription initiation complex. At many ribosomal RNA promoters, the interaction reduces transcription initiation. Other promoters, including those associated with amino acid biosynthesis or stress adaptation, can be favoured under the changed regulatory conditions. This is why ppGpp is not accurately described as a general transcriptional inhibitor. Its physiological purpose is achieved through differential effects on the expression of different genes.

The reduction in ribosome production is particularly significant because rRNA molecules are long and ribosome assembly also requires numerous proteins. Continuing to synthesize large quantities of ribosomal components during severe nutrient shortage would consume nucleotides, amino acids and energy without necessarily increasing productive protein synthesis. By reducing growth-associated transcription, the cell can spare these resources and redirect available transcriptional capacity. This does not mean that every molecule saved from rRNA synthesis goes directly to an amino acid biosynthetic pathway. Rather, a changed distribution of metabolic investment alters the options available to the cell.

Translation itself can also be affected by the alarmone and by the nutrient shortage that triggered it. GTP-dependent steps in translation are sensitive to changes in nucleotide availability and, in particular contexts, to interactions with regulatory nucleotides. The cell therefore reduces some of the demand imposed by protein production. It must nevertheless retain enough translation capacity to synthesize protective and adaptive proteins when resources permit. A severe lack of amino acids can constrain these protective reactions as well. The stringent response cannot make missing atoms available.

DNA replication and cell division are growth-associated activities whose coordination with nutrient supply is essential. If resources are inadequate for completing chromosome duplication or supporting daughter cells, reducing initiation of new rounds of replication can prevent excessive demand. Alarmone-dependent regulation can influence replication-related processes, with the mechanism differing among organisms. It would be incorrect to conclude that all ongoing DNA replication stops instantaneously whenever ppGpp rises. Initiation, elongation, repair and division are separable events. A physiological response may affect one or more of these without acting as a universal block on every DNA polymerase.

In many Gram-positive bacteria, regulation through changes in GTP availability can be particularly important. For example, Bacillus subtilis uses alarmone-dependent control of guanine nucleotide metabolism to alter cellular physiology. This differs in important details from the direct RNA polymerase-centered model commonly taught for E. coli. For examination purposes, the safest general statement is that the stringent response coordinates gene expression and growth-related processes with nutrient and stress conditions through (p)ppGpp-dependent regulation. A specific molecular target should be attributed to the organism in which it is established rather than assumed to exist universally.

The distinction between active regulation and passive substrate shortage can be tested by comparing a strain unable to mount the normal alarmone response with a matched normal strain. Both may encounter the same external shortage and experience reduced charging of an affected tRNA, yet their allocation of transcriptional activity may differ. The mutant may continue relatively high stable RNA synthesis despite its inability to sustain the earlier rate of translation. Such a mismatch can waste resources or compromise stress adaptation. Differences in survival still require direct measurement; a transcriptional phenotype alone does not quantify long-term viability.

Recovery is also regulated. When the missing nutrient becomes available, aminoacylation can resume, ribosome-associated starvation signals can decrease, and alarmone turnover helps restore the growth-associated state. A delay before growth restarts may reflect replenishment of depleted pools, restoration of functional ribosomes, repair of damaged components and reorganization of metabolic flux. This delay is a form of lag and should not automatically be interpreted as a lower eventual exponential growth rate. A population can resume growth late but subsequently divide as rapidly as a population that experienced no starvation.

It is possible to examine this recovery quantitatively without introducing specialist mathematics. Suppose two hypothetical cultures are transferred to fresh medium with identical viable inocula of 10⁵ colony-forming units per millilitre. Culture A resumes exponential growth after one hour, and culture B after three hours. Both subsequently double every 30 minutes. At hour five, culture A has completed eight doublings, giving 2.56 × 10⁷ CFU per millilitre, and culture B has completed four doublings, giving 1.6 × 10⁶. Culture A has sixteen times as many colony-forming units at that time, even though their post-lag generation times are identical. This example assumes no loss of viability during lag, no density limitation and an abrupt transition into exponential growth. A single final count cannot distinguish differences in lag from differences in exponential growth rate or starting viability.

The stringent response demonstrates that bacterial growth and bacterial survival share a regulatory connection. When growth is appropriate, resources flow into building and division. When resources become scarce, the cell changes the pattern of that flow. The next stage of the story is stationary-phase physiology, where many such adjustments interact over longer periods and the behaviour of individual cells may become increasingly diverse.

13. Stationary phase and the general stress response

In a closed batch culture, the exponential phase cannot continue indefinitely. As biomass increases, the culture consumes nutrients and releases products into the environment. Oxygen transfer may become limiting, acidity may change, and compounds that were initially present at low concentrations may accumulate. Eventually the net increase in population size slows. A stationary phase is defined by the approximate plateau in a measured population or biomass curve, not by a universal molecular program. Different cultures can arrive at a plateau through different combinations of nutrient shortage and physiological stress.

The first important consequence is that stationary phase is not necessarily identical to a pure starvation experiment. A culture deprived of one nutrient in a controlled setting may activate a particular response. An aging batch culture can encounter multiple shortages and stresses simultaneously. Its observed phenotype may include responses to acidity, oxidative damage and accumulated metabolites as well as starvation. Thus a comparison of young and old cultures cannot identify the causal contribution of one factor without additional controls.

E. coli provides a classic model of a general stress response associated with the alternative sigma factor RpoS, also called sigma S. A sigma factor helps bacterial RNA polymerase recognize particular promoter classes. The principal housekeeping sigma factor supports much of the gene expression associated with ordinary growth. When RpoS becomes more influential, transcription can be redirected toward functions that help the cell tolerate environmental challenges. The switch is not achieved by replacing every RNA polymerase molecule. Sigma factors compete for access to the transcription machinery, and promoter recognition and regulatory interactions determine the resulting pattern.

RpoS abundance and activity are subject to several forms of regulation, including changes in synthesis and protein stability. Starvation-related regulatory signals, including (p)ppGpp in relevant contexts, can influence the general stress response. It is therefore useful to connect the stringent response with RpoS without equating them. The alarmone system directly coordinates diverse growth-associated processes with stress and nutrient supply; RpoS is a transcriptional regulator of a protective gene expression program. Either system can have effects that depend on other regulators and environmental conditions.

Examples of RpoS-associated protective functions in E. coli include the stationary-phase catalase KatE and Dps-mediated protection of cellular material. These functions illustrate why an older culture may tolerate an oxidative challenge differently from an exponentially growing culture even if both have identical DNA sequences. The cells have different amounts of proteins, different metabolic activity and potentially different iron accessibility. Their physiological history has changed the initial conditions of the challenge.

The general stress response is not organized around RpoS in all bacteria. Bacillus subtilis and other bacteria may use sigma B or other regulators for overlapping protective purposes. The exact targets differ, but the basic problem remains the same: the cell must increase suitable protective functions while continuing essential maintenance. When an examination describes an unfamiliar organism, one should reason from the stated molecular roles rather than assume that an E. coli-specific gene must be present.

Stationary-phase survival can involve changes in the envelope, reduction of growth-associated processes, increased scavenging of scarce nutrients, protein quality control and changes in DNA protection. The membrane remains important because maintenance of ionic gradients and selective permeability can prevent excessive loss of cellular resources. Protective proteins can reduce the probability of new damage. Turnover of damaged components can release reusable materials. These processes are connected rather than independent: a compromised membrane can increase energy demand, while insufficient energy can limit repair.

A population plateau does not establish a precise equality between cell division and death in every stationary culture. If the actual number of viable individuals were measured and if births and irreversible deaths were the only processes affecting that number, a zero net change would imply equal aggregate rates over the observation interval, possibly both close to zero. In practice, optical density, CFU and direct counts have different meanings. Some cells can lose culturability or recover it, aggregates can separate, and debris can contribute to optical density. The appearance of a plateau depends partly on the chosen measurement.

Consider a hypothetical culture containing 10⁸ CFU per millilitre on two successive days. The observation is compatible with most cells remaining intact and dividing very little. It is also compatible with turnover in which loss of some cells is offset by division of others. The two interpretations cannot be distinguished from the two CFU measurements alone. A measurement of DNA synthesis, single-cell behaviour or appropriately interpreted biomass change could provide additional information. The central lesson is that population stability does not imply identical individual behaviour.

As starvation continues, maintenance requirements may consume internal reserves and some cells may accumulate damage. Viable counts can decline even while turbidity remains high. A decrease in colony-forming capacity can result from irreversible death or from a physiological condition that prevents recovery in the chosen medium and observation window. The term death phase is a useful description of a typical growth curve, but individual survival must be interpreted with attention to the recovery assay.

Stationary-phase physiology therefore links nutrient sensing, protective protein expression and heterogeneity. Some cells may retain enough activity to use nutrients released by damaged neighbours, while others remain in low-growth states. The balance can change over time. A culture is not a homogeneous container filled with copies of an average cell. This observation is a foundation for understanding dormancy and persistence.
