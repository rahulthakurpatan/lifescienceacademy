1. The oxygen transport problem

A cell consumes oxygen at the end of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. The reduction of oxygen to water allows electrons to continue moving through the respiratory complexes, which sustain the proton gradient used for ATP synthesis. Although an animal obtains oxygen from its environment at a respiratory surface, that surface is usually far from many of the mitochondria that need oxygen. An active muscle, for example, cannot depend on oxygen diffusing across several centimetres of tissue from the skin. Diffusion works effectively over short distances because the mean distance travelled by a diffusing molecule increases approximately with the square root of elapsed time. To move material over long distances, a large animal generally requires bulk flow or a specialised system that brings the environment close to the cells.

The previous chapter followed oxygen across the pulmonary blood–air barrier and explained why alveolar gas composition and pulmonary blood flow must be coordinated. The present question begins after oxygen has entered blood. How can a liquid that dissolves relatively little oxygen carry enough of it to maintain an active body? A mammal at rest consumes roughly a few hundred millilitres of oxygen gas equivalent per minute, although the exact rate varies with body size, metabolic state and temperature. A litre of water-like fluid equilibrated with a physiologically ordinary oxygen pressure would contain only a few millilitres of oxygen. If the cardiovascular system depended on dissolved oxygen alone, the necessary flow would greatly exceed its usual pumping capacity.

Respiratory pigments solve this problem by reversibly binding oxygen. A pigment is a molecule or molecular complex that absorbs visible light in a wavelength-dependent manner; in respiratory physiology its colour is less important than its capacity to bind oxygen in a form that can be released again. The relevant properties are the number of binding sites, their affinity for oxygen, their interaction with other sites and molecules, and the concentration of pigment in the transporting fluid. A pigment that binds oxygen irreversibly would be a poor transporter, however impressive its initial loading capacity. Equally, a protein that binds only at pressures higher than those available at the respiratory surface would be of little use.

A useful transporter must work in two very different environments. In the lung, the pressure of dissolved oxygen in blood rises as oxygen diffuses from alveolar gas. Binding to haemoglobin draws part of this dissolved oxygen into a bound reservoir without directly contributing to the partial pressure. In a systemic capillary, neighbouring tissue consumes oxygen and thereby establishes a gradient out of the blood. Dissolved oxygen moves out first, and some of the bound oxygen dissociates to replenish it. Binding and unbinding are reversible reactions operating throughout this process, rather than a single loading event in the lung followed by an irreversible chemical conversion in tissue.

It is tempting to regard haemoglobin as a vehicle that actively transports oxygen across membranes. This analogy obscures a critical distinction. The heart produces bulk blood flow, whereas oxygen exchanges with haemoglobin according to chemical equilibrium and binding kinetics. Oxygen crosses the relevant lipid membranes predominantly by diffusion. The pigment neither pumps oxygen with ATP nor dictates the direction of diffusion independently of the local free oxygen concentration. The red cell is the vehicle in the moving blood; haemoglobin increases the cargo available within that vehicle.

Not every animal has solved oxygen distribution in the same way. Insects typically deliver air through tracheal tubes close to cells, reducing their dependence on respiratory pigment in circulating haemolymph. Many molluscs and arthropods use copper-containing haemocyanin. Certain marine invertebrates use non-haem iron proteins or extracellular haem proteins with distinctive prosthetic groups. These alternative designs reveal that the general transport problem is older and broader than the specific mammalian haemoglobin system. A comparison should relate the pigment to the animal's respiratory surface, circulating fluid and oxygen demand rather than assign an evolutionary rank based on blood colour.

The chapters on blood and cardiac output established the other two major components of oxygen carriage: erythrocytes provide a high intracellular haemoglobin concentration, and cardiac output distributes blood to tissues. Together with the respiratory surface, they form a series of linked processes. Failure of one stage cannot always be compensated by improvement at another. Increasing oxygen uptake in the lungs, for instance, has limited effect if most haemoglobin sites are already occupied but the concentration of haemoglobin is low. This distinction becomes clear once the three different measures of oxygen in blood are separated.

2. Three measures of oxygen in blood

Consider a sealed chamber containing a blood sample and a gas mixture at equilibrium. Oxygen molecules constantly enter and leave the liquid, yet at equilibrium their net transfer is zero. The oxygen partial pressure, written PO₂, describes the equilibrium oxygen tension of the dissolved gas; it is expressed in units such as millimetres of mercury or kilopascals. It reflects the chemical availability of freely dissolved oxygen, not the number of oxygen molecules currently bound to a protein. When two regions have different free oxygen activities, oxygen tends to move down that gradient. For physiologically similar aqueous compartments, the difference in oxygen partial pressure is a convenient representation of that driving force.

Henry's law provides a first quantitative connection between pressure and concentration. At a fixed temperature and for a specified solvent in the dilute range, the concentration of dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure. For oxygen in blood near human body temperature, an approximate conventional solubility coefficient is 0.003 mL oxygen gas equivalent per decilitre of blood per mmHg. Consequently a PO₂ of 100 mmHg corresponds to roughly 0.30 mL of dissolved oxygen per decilitre. A PO₂ of 40 mmHg corresponds to roughly 0.12 mL per decilitre. These values refer to oxygen volumes expressed under a consistent gas-volume convention, not to bubbles circulating through the blood.

Haemoglobin binds oxygen through haem groups. In the predominant adult human haemoglobin, HbA, the protein has four globin chains and four haem groups. Each functional ferrous haem site can bind one oxygen molecule reversibly. It follows that a fully oxygenated tetramer carries four oxygen molecules. Oxygenation of a functional haem group must not be confused with irreversible oxidation of the iron to the ferric state. Ferric methaemoglobin cannot bind oxygen at the oxidised site in the ordinary reversible fashion. The earlier erythrocyte chapter explains the maintenance of the functional iron state and synthesis of haem; the present emphasis is on how occupancy affects carriage.

Oxygen saturation measures the fraction of available haemoglobin oxygen-binding sites occupied by oxygen. For an ordinary sample without appreciable abnormal haemoglobin derivatives, saturation of 0.97 means approximately ninety-seven out of every hundred sites are occupied on average. It does not imply that ninety-seven percent of the haemoglobin tetramers each carry exactly four oxygen molecules. Different occupancy states coexist in equilibrium. Saturation is dimensionless and can be written either as a fraction or as a percentage. A value of 97% must be entered as 0.97 in the ordinary oxygen content equation, not as 97.

Oxygen content is the quantity of oxygen actually carried per unit volume of blood, including both dissolved and pigment-bound oxygen. In human respiratory physiology this is commonly reported in mL oxygen per dL blood. It depends on the abundance of pigment as well as the fraction of sites occupied. Two samples may have identical PO₂ and saturation yet different oxygen contents if they have different haemoglobin concentrations. A sample containing half the haemoglobin carries approximately half as much bound oxygen at the same saturation. The dissolved contribution remains approximately unchanged at the same pressure, temperature and solubility.

The converse distinction is equally important. Suppose two samples contain the same haemoglobin concentration and PO₂ but differ in oxygen affinity because the pH differs. At equilibrium the lower-affinity sample can have lower saturation and lower total oxygen content despite having the same dissolved oxygen pressure. Partial pressure does not uniquely determine saturation unless the binding relationship and environmental conditions are specified. In a graph of saturation against PO₂, a change in pressure moves the sample along its existing curve; a change in affinity moves the curve itself. Changing only the amount of ordinary haemoglobin alters capacity but does not necessarily alter the fractional saturation curve.

One way to understand the distinction is to imagine a container of free oxygen molecules in contact with a reversible population of binding sites. Adding more identical binding sites at constant imposed free oxygen pressure increases the amount bound but does not change the fractional occupancy of those sites if they do not interact with the new molecules. This experiment requires an oxygen reservoir to maintain the pressure. In a closed sample with a fixed total amount of oxygen, adding more sites could instead lower the free oxygen pressure and redistribute the available oxygen. Exam questions sometimes switch silently between a fixed-pressure experiment and a fixed-total-oxygen experiment; the predicted outcome then differs.

Measurements reflect these definitions. A blood gas electrode can determine dissolved oxygen tension, while optical measurement of haemoglobin species can estimate their relative abundance using distinct absorption spectra. Pulse oximetry estimates arterial oxygen saturation from pulsatile optical signals under assumptions about the haemoglobin species and signal quality; it does not directly measure haemoglobin concentration, cardiac output or oxygen content. A measured saturation is therefore not a direct demonstration of sufficient oxygen delivery. When abnormal haemoglobin derivatives are appreciable, functional saturation and fractional oxyhaemoglobin are different definitions, and the choice of denominator must be checked.

3. The oxygen content equation and its derivation

The amount of oxygen that a gram of haemoglobin can carry at complete saturation is its oxygen-binding capacity. A simple molecular calculation begins with the approximate molar mass of adult haemoglobin, 64,500 g per mole of tetramers. One mole of tetramers contains four moles of oxygen-binding sites. At standard temperature and pressure, one mole of an ideal gas occupies approximately 22.4 litres. Therefore four moles of oxygen correspond to about 89.6 litres, or 89,600 mL, of oxygen gas equivalent. Dividing by 64,500 g gives approximately 1.39 mL of oxygen per gram of fully functional haemoglobin. This theoretical value follows directly from the assumed molar mass and binding stoichiometry.

For ordinary blood calculations a conventional empirical coefficient of 1.34 mL oxygen per gram haemoglobin is widely used. The measured effective capacity can be slightly smaller than the ideal stoichiometric prediction because the haemoglobin measured in blood is not always entirely available for oxygen carriage and because measurement conventions differ. Values such as 1.34 or 1.39 may therefore appear in different textbooks. Use the coefficient supplied in a question, state the convention if it is not supplied, and do not combine coefficients representing incompatible definitions of haemoglobin or saturation.

Let Hb denote haemoglobin concentration in g/dL, S the fractional saturation of the relevant functional sites, and P the oxygen tension in mmHg. For normal blood and the conventional coefficients just described, the content C in mL oxygen/dL can be written C = 1.34 × Hb × S + 0.003 × P. The first term is bound oxygen; the second represents dissolved oxygen. Multiplying g/dL by mL/g and by a dimensionless fraction produces mL/dL. Multiplying the solubility coefficient by mmHg gives the same units. Addition is valid because both terms describe the quantity of the same substance per volume of blood.

The equation is often written specifically for arterial blood as CaO₂ = 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂. A corresponding expression uses venous saturation and venous PO₂ for CvO₂. The letter a in a subscript identifies arterial blood, while a capital A frequently identifies alveolar gas. The distinction is important in respiratory equations because alveolar and arterial oxygen pressures need not be identical. The content equation itself describes blood, not alveolar gas.

For a teaching example consider Hb = 15 g/dL, arterial saturation 98% and PaO₂ = 100 mmHg. The bound term is 1.34 × 15 × 0.98 = 19.698 mL/dL. The dissolved term is 0.003 × 100 = 0.30 mL/dL, giving 19.998 mL/dL or approximately 20.0 mL/dL. About 98.5% of the oxygen content in this particular example is pigment-bound. The percentages change when oxygen pressure or haemoglobin concentration changes. It is therefore more useful to derive the proportion for the given conditions than to memorise a supposedly universal dissolved fraction.

Now lower haemoglobin concentration to 7.5 g/dL while keeping the imposed arterial pressure and saturation unchanged. The bound content becomes 9.849 mL/dL and the dissolved content remains 0.30 mL/dL. Total content is 10.149 mL/dL. The sample now carries almost half the oxygen per unit volume despite its apparently normal PaO₂ and saturation. This is the defining reasoning pattern for anaemic reduction in oxygen carriage, not a claim that every anaemic individual necessarily maintains these same pressures or saturations.

A third hypothetical sample has Hb = 15 g/dL, SaO₂ = 0.85 and PaO₂ = 50 mmHg. Its total arterial oxygen content is 1.34 × 15 × 0.85 + 0.003 × 50 = 17.085 + 0.15 = 17.235 mL/dL. In this sample the oxygen tension is lower and saturation is reduced, but the abundant haemoglobin still provides more oxygen content than the low-haemoglobin sample above. Ranking oxygen delivery by arterial pressure alone would rank these two samples incorrectly if their flows were equal.

The relative sizes of the two terms explain why a modest increase in dissolved oxygen pressure has a relatively small direct effect on content after haemoglobin is nearly saturated. If pressure rises from 100 to 200 mmHg and saturation rises from 0.98 to 1.00 at Hb = 15 g/dL, the bound contribution increases by 0.402 mL/dL and the dissolved contribution by 0.30 mL/dL. This idealised comparison adds about 0.70 mL/dL, much less than doubling the haemoglobin concentration would add at the original saturation. At very high oxygen tensions the dissolved term becomes more significant; the approximation that dissolved oxygen is negligible has a domain of validity, not the status of a biological law.

An equation is useful only when its assumptions match the measurement. If substantial carboxyhaemoglobin or methaemoglobin is present, multiplying total haemoglobin by functional saturation without accounting for unavailable sites can overstate oxygen content. One can instead use a suitably defined fractional oxyhaemoglobin measurement with a compatible capacity coefficient or explicitly estimate the functional haemoglobin pool. Similarly, the dissolved coefficient is temperature-dependent and should not be applied without qualification to arbitrary fluids, temperatures or nonhuman blood. The equation is a well-defined model of oxygen content under ordinary mammalian physiological conditions.

4. Why haemoglobin binding is cooperative

A binding equilibrium describes the balance between the association and dissociation of a ligand. For a single independent oxygen-binding site, write M + O₂ ⇌ MO₂, where M is the unoccupied binding molecule. At equilibrium the dissociation constant Kd is the product of free M and free oxygen concentrations divided by the concentration of the occupied complex. If the total concentration of M sites equals free M plus MO₂, then the fraction occupied is Y = [O₂]/(Kd + [O₂]). When oxygen concentration equals Kd, the fractional occupancy is one half. At low concentration the occupancy rises approximately proportionally with concentration; at high concentration it approaches one asymptotically.

Because dissolved oxygen concentration is proportional to oxygen tension at fixed conditions, the equation can be written Y = P/(P50 + P), where P50 is the pressure for half occupancy. This describes a hyperbola. Myoglobin is a biologically important example of an oxygen-binding protein with a single haem site, and its equilibrium binding curve can be represented in this way. If a hypothetical tetramer contained four strictly independent identical sites, its average fraction occupied would obey the same hyperbolic relationship. Four sites alone do not establish cooperativity.

The observed oxygen saturation curve of ordinary adult haemoglobin is instead sigmoidal. Its slope at very low oxygen tension is smaller than that of a strongly binding single-site protein, then increases over an intermediate range before decreasing as the finite set of sites becomes occupied. The shape indicates that occupation of one site changes the binding behaviour of other sites. Oxygen is a homotropic allosteric ligand: binding of oxygen alters the tendency of the same ligand to bind elsewhere in the protein. Positive cooperativity means that early binding events favour further binding.

A molecular explanation is the coupling between local changes at the haem and the quaternary organisation of the tetramer. In deoxygenated haemoglobin, the haem iron lies displaced from the mean plane of the porphyrin toward the proximal histidine. Oxygen binding alters this arrangement and draws the iron toward the porphyrin plane. Movement is transmitted through the proximal histidine and its helix to other parts of the globin structure. Changes at subunit contacts modify the stability of alternative conformations and thereby alter oxygen affinity at sites that have not yet become occupied.

The traditional T and R state description provides a useful framework. T denotes a relatively low-affinity tense ensemble favoured in deoxygenated conditions, while R denotes a relatively high-affinity relaxed ensemble favoured by oxygen binding. Both names refer to conformational states rather than to literal stretching and relaxation of the protein by muscle-like force. Oxygen can bind to a molecule in the low-affinity ensemble, and an R-like molecule need not be fully saturated. It is the probability of the conformations and their respective affinities that change with ligand occupancy.

In a concerted allosteric model, the tetramer is represented as an equilibrium between two conformational states, each with a characteristic affinity. Oxygen stabilises the high-affinity population by binding preferentially to it. In sequential descriptions, binding at one subunit produces a conformational change that influences adjacent subunits. Actual haemoglobin behaviour is richer than any elementary two-state cartoon; these frameworks explain why the population shifts and why the resulting curve is not equivalent to four independent binding sites. They should not be interpreted as direct observations of one unique trajectory for every oxygenation event.

A convenient empirical representation of cooperative saturation is the Hill equation Y = Pⁿ/(P50ⁿ + Pⁿ). The parameter n is an effective cooperativity coefficient in this representation. If n = 1, the equation reduces to the single-site hyperbola; n greater than one yields an increasingly sigmoidal curve. Rearrangement gives Y/(1 − Y) = (P/P50)ⁿ. Taking logarithms produces log[Y/(1 − Y)] = n log P − n log P50. A plot of log odds of saturation against log pressure has slope n in the ideal Hill model. Real haemoglobin data do not maintain a perfectly constant slope over all pressures, so a fitted coefficient is an empirical descriptor, often near 2.7 in the central region for adult human haemoglobin under ordinary conditions.

The Hill coefficient is not the number of occupied sites, nor does a value of 2.7 mean that a tetramer can bind a fractional number of oxygen molecules. A single tetramer binds an integer number from zero through four, while fractional saturation refers to a population average. The empirical slope reports the sensitivity of occupancy to changing ligand pressure. In the limits of very low or very high occupation, observed slopes can depart from the central value. The Hill equation is useful for approximate calculations but cannot supply individual microscopic association constants for all four binding steps.

The physiological consequence of cooperative binding is easiest to see by comparing the upper and middle parts of the curve. At high pressures near pulmonary arterialisation, the curve is relatively flat. A modest reduction in oxygen pressure may therefore have a limited effect on loading as long as the blood remains on the plateau. At intermediate pressures typical of many systemic capillary beds, the curve is steeper: a reduction in oxygen tension accompanying increased consumption produces a much larger reduction in saturation. Oxygen is released where metabolism lowers local oxygen availability. This coupling of molecular binding to tissue demand is one reason the sigmoid is functionally advantageous.

5. Reading the dissociation curve and P50

The oxygen dissociation curve is an equilibrium relationship between the tension of freely dissolved oxygen and the fractional occupancy of haemoglobin oxygen-binding sites. The term dissociation curve does not require the sample to be progressively deoxygenated in a particular apparatus. Provided the sample reaches equilibrium and its environmental conditions remain constant, loading and unloading should approach the same equilibrium relationship. A discrepancy between loading and unloading measurements could result from inadequate equilibration, altered pH, chemical modification or other changes during measurement. Such a discrepancy should not be interpreted automatically as ordinary physiological cooperativity.

The partial pressure at which the fractional saturation equals one half is called P50. This pressure provides a simple operational measure of affinity. A low P50 means that relatively little dissolved oxygen is needed to occupy half the available sites, indicating higher apparent affinity. A high P50 means that a larger pressure is needed for the same occupancy. Typical adult human haemoglobin in blood under approximately standard physiological conditions has a P50 near 26–27 mmHg. The value is not a constant of all haemoglobins, nor even a universal constant of human blood. Temperature, acid–base state, organic phosphates and the particular haemoglobin species must be specified for precise comparisons.

A convenient graphical exercise begins by drawing a horizontal line at 50% saturation. Where the line intersects the curve, project vertically down to the pressure axis. If the intersection occurs at 20 mmHg rather than 27 mmHg, the curve has moved to the left around its midpoint. At a pressure of 27 mmHg, the higher-affinity sample will ordinarily have saturation above 50% if its curve retains a similar shape. Conversely, a sample with P50 of 35 mmHg will ordinarily have saturation below 50% at 27 mmHg. The terms right shift and left shift refer to the relative location of the saturation-versus-pressure relationship, not to the direction in which oxygen is physically flowing.

The Hill approximation allows the magnitude of such a change to be estimated. For P50 = 27 mmHg and n = 2.7, at P = 40 mmHg the ratio P/P50 is about 1.48. Raising it to the power 2.7 gives approximately 2.89. Saturation is the ratio of that number to one plus itself, which gives about 0.743. At P = 100 mmHg, the ratio is about 3.70 and its 2.7th power is about 34.3; the predicted saturation is about 0.972. This model therefore places 100 mmHg on the plateau and 40 mmHg on the steeper portion of the relationship. The values are illustrative calculations, not experimentally measured blood data.

Consider a lower-affinity curve with P50 = 32 mmHg but the same empirical exponent. At 40 mmHg, the pressure ratio is 1.25 and its 2.7th power is approximately 1.83. Fractional saturation is about 0.646. At 100 mmHg the ratio is 3.125 and predicted saturation is roughly 0.956. The loss of saturation due to the shift is much larger at 40 than at 100 mmHg. This example illustrates why a modest reduction in affinity can increase the difference between pulmonary and tissue occupancy while causing only a small decrease in pulmonary loading when alveolar pressure is high enough.

This benefit is conditional. If pulmonary end-capillary oxygen pressure itself becomes very low, moving the entire saturation curve toward higher pressures may substantially compromise loading. An oxygen carrier is not judged solely by its ability to release oxygen at the tissues. It must first acquire a sufficient quantity at the respiratory surface. The best affinity for a given organism depends on its loading pressure, unloading pressure, blood flow, oxygen capacity and metabolic requirements. If the loading and unloading pressures both lie in a very low pressure range, a leftward curve may be favourable even though oxygen release at a fixed pressure would be reduced by increased affinity.

The distinction between affinity and oxygen capacity can be demonstrated graphically by plotting two different vertical variables. A saturation plot uses percentage of available sites; a content plot uses mL oxygen per dL of blood. If two samples contain different amounts of otherwise identical haemoglobin and are equilibrated under the same conditions, their saturation curves can overlap while their content curves separate. Their maximum bound content is different because the number of sites differs. P50 is read from half of the functional saturation scale, not half of some arbitrary maximum content borrowed from another blood sample.

At high pressure the bound content approaches its capacity asymptotically, but the dissolved contribution continues to increase approximately linearly under Henry's law. Consequently the total content curve does not possess a perfectly horizontal upper asymptote in the same sense as the saturation curve. At normal physiological pressures this distinction may be difficult to see because the dissolved term is small. A graph extending far beyond the usual arterial range can reveal the additional oxygen carried in solution. Identifying the ordinate before interpreting a curve prevents several common errors.

A second caution concerns comparisons between different proteins. Two curves can have the same P50 and different slopes because their cooperativity differs. Conversely they can have different P50 values but cross at some pressure if their shapes differ substantially. It is therefore unsafe to infer the complete binding behaviour of a protein from one midpoint. P50 is an interpretable summary, not a substitute for the full curve. In an examination involving a graph, calculate or read the actual saturations at the stated loading and unloading pressures whenever possible rather than assume that a single affinity measure determines both.

6. The Bohr effect: linking metabolism to oxygen release

A working tissue consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. The metabolic environment can also become more acidic as the balance of acid production, buffering and removal changes. The capillary blood passing through such a tissue is exposed to a combination of lower oxygen tension, higher carbon dioxide tension and often a lower pH than blood leaving the lungs. These changes can promote haemoglobin unloading by two distinct mechanisms. The fall in oxygen tension drives movement along the dissociation curve. The altered acid–base environment can reduce haemoglobin's affinity, shifting its dissociation curve. Understanding the distinction between the two mechanisms makes it possible to predict the effect of changing only one variable in a controlled experiment.

The Bohr effect is the dependence of oxygen affinity on hydrogen ion activity and carbon dioxide. In ordinary adult mammalian blood, increased hydrogen ion concentration, corresponding to lower pH, generally reduces oxygen affinity. Increased carbon dioxide often produces this response partly because carbon dioxide participates in reversible hydration reactions that generate hydrogen ions. Carbon dioxide can also interact directly with haemoglobin through carbamate formation at amino groups. The oxygen affinity changes arise through linked equilibria; neither a hydrogen ion nor carbon dioxide simply displaces oxygen from its haem site by competing for that identical binding position.

In the deoxygenated conformational ensemble, certain amino acid groups have proton-binding properties that differ from their properties in oxygenated conformations. Protonation can favour electrostatic interactions and stabilise lower-affinity configurations of the tetramer. In a simplified model, the binding of hydrogen ions preferentially stabilises T-like haemoglobin. The consequence is a population shift: at the same dissolved oxygen pressure, fewer oxygen sites are occupied than would be occupied at a higher pH. The exact residues and magnitude of the effect depend on the protein and solution conditions; a single salt bridge is not a complete explanation of every haemoglobin's behaviour.

In a systemic capillary the resulting lower affinity can facilitate release of oxygen into the dissolved pool, which is continually depleted by diffusion toward metabolising cells. In the pulmonary capillary, carbon dioxide diffuses toward alveolar gas. Its removal is associated with changes in the acid–base equilibrium that favour a higher oxygen affinity, helping haemoglobin acquire oxygen. These processes need not occur as discrete successive stages. Gas diffusion, acid–base reactions, haemoglobin oxygenation and changing blood composition interact as blood moves through the capillary.

The name Bohr effect is historically associated with Christian Bohr, Karl Hasselbalch and August Krogh, whose work in 1904 demonstrated the influence of carbon dioxide on oxygen equilibrium curves. Their observations established that oxygen binding cannot be understood from oxygen pressure alone. In the simplest modern reconstruction of the reasoning, one equilibrates comparable haemoglobin samples with different gas mixtures, measures saturation over a range of oxygen pressures and compares the curves. If the higher carbon dioxide condition yields lower saturation at the same oxygen pressure, it supports the existence of linked regulation. It does not by itself identify which proportion of the effect arises through hydrogen ion binding rather than direct carbamate chemistry.

A more discriminating teaching experiment employs three series of samples. In the first, oxygen pressure changes while pH, temperature and carbon dioxide conditions are standardised. In the second, pH is adjusted using an appropriate buffer while other controllable variables are held comparable. In the third, carbon dioxide is varied while pH is held as nearly constant as the buffer system permits. A shift in the second series demonstrates a pH dependence independently of the ordinary coupled increase in carbon dioxide. A remaining shift in the third series is consistent with an additional carbon dioxide effect, provided ionic composition, temperature and other potentially influential factors have also been controlled. This is an experimental logic exercise, not a reproduction of the exact historical protocol.

The magnitude of a pH-dependent shift may be described by a Bohr coefficient, commonly expressed as the slope of log P50 against pH under defined conditions. For ordinary mammalian blood this slope is generally negative: raising pH lowers P50, while lowering pH raises P50. Suppose an illustrative experiment finds a P50 of 27 mmHg at pH 7.40 and 31 mmHg at pH 7.20. The base-ten logarithm of the pressure ratio is log₁₀(31/27), approximately 0.060. The pH change is −0.20, so the approximate slope is −0.30. This number describes the particular teaching dataset rather than establishing a universal coefficient. Different solutions, pigments and experimental conventions produce different values.

A recurring confusion is between the Bohr and Haldane effects. The Bohr effect asks how carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions influence oxygen binding. The Haldane effect concerns how oxygenation influences the carriage of carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions. The two are thermodynamically related through linked binding equilibria but are not identical experimental questions. In a tissue, deoxygenated haemoglobin can bind hydrogen ions and facilitate carbon dioxide carriage, whereas oxygenation in the lung promotes release of hydrogen ions and carbon dioxide. The detailed mechanisms of bicarbonate formation, chloride exchange and the Haldane effect belong to the following chapter. The key examination task here is to identify which ligand or condition was changed and which binding outcome was measured.

The Bohr effect is not an absolute rule applying with identical magnitude and direction to every respiratory pigment. Comparative physiology includes diverse haemoglobins and other oxygen-binding proteins with different pH dependencies. Some fish haemoglobins exhibit a pronounced reduction in oxygen-carrying capacity at low pH, the Root effect, rather than merely a change in pressure required for half saturation of a fixed functional capacity. In specialised contexts such as oxygen secretion into a swim bladder, the ability to release oxygen against a high local oxygen pressure has a particular biological role. An ordinary Bohr shift in human haemoglobin should not be described as eliminating binding sites or making full saturation impossible under all pressures.

7. Temperature and organic phosphates as regulators of affinity

Temperature changes the equilibrium between bound and unbound oxygen. Oxygen binding by ordinary adult mammalian haemoglobin is generally favoured by lower temperature under otherwise comparable conditions, so raising temperature shifts the saturation relationship toward higher oxygen pressures. A working muscle produces heat along with carbon dioxide. The rise in local temperature can therefore reinforce acid-related reductions in oxygen affinity. This does not mean the blood must be heated before oxygen can dissociate. Oxygen unloading is continuously possible whenever oxygen activity in the surrounding tissue is sufficiently low.

It is useful to distinguish a change in binding affinity from a change in gas solubility. Temperature affects the solubility coefficient for dissolved oxygen as well as the haemoglobin equilibrium. When an experiment holds PO₂ constant across temperatures, it does not necessarily hold dissolved oxygen concentration exactly constant. When an experiment holds dissolved concentration constant, the corresponding partial pressure may change. In physiological curve comparisons, a specified PO₂ is the conventional independent variable, and affinity is represented by the resulting saturation. If an examination problem provides a temperature-specific solubility coefficient, it should be used rather than silently assuming the coefficient for blood at 37°C.

A major regulator inside human red cells is 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, abbreviated 2,3-BPG. The older name 2,3-diphosphoglycerate, or 2,3-DPG, refers to the same molecule in this context. Erythrocytes obtain ATP primarily from glycolysis, and an intermediate of that pathway, 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, can enter a side pathway through bisphosphoglycerate mutase. The resulting 2,3-BPG can subsequently be hydrolysed to 3-phosphoglycerate. This bypass diverts material around the ATP-producing phosphoglycerate kinase reaction. The cell thus sacrifices an opportunity to produce ATP in order to maintain a metabolite with an important oxygen transport role.

The bypass is often called the Rapoport–Luebering shunt. Its importance becomes clear in a cell whose primary function is carrying oxygen rather than consuming it in mitochondria. A mature mammalian erythrocyte lacks mitochondria and relies on glycolytic metabolism to maintain membrane integrity and ion gradients. Metabolic regulation of 2,3-BPG allows the same cell to alter the binding behaviour of the protein it carries. The pathway is not an oxygen-pumping mechanism, nor does 2,3-BPG bind oxygen directly as a storage molecule. Its action is exerted through the haemoglobin tetramer.

In deoxygenated adult haemoglobin a central cavity between the beta chains is accessible to the negatively charged organic phosphate. Electrostatic interactions with positively charged groups favour its binding in the relatively low-affinity conformational ensemble. As oxygenation favours a change in quaternary structure, the cavity narrows and binding of 2,3-BPG becomes less favourable. One central 2,3-BPG molecule can influence the affinity of the four haem sites by shifting the conformational equilibrium of the tetramer. It is therefore a heterotropic allosteric effector: unlike oxygen, it is not the ligand whose binding is being regulated.

Increasing the concentration of 2,3-BPG generally decreases oxygen affinity and raises P50 in adult human blood. Removing organic phosphate from purified haemoglobin can produce a higher-affinity preparation than haemoglobin studied in its normal erythrocyte environment. For this reason the preparation method is scientifically significant when interpreting oxygen-binding curves. A difference between intact blood and isolated haemoglobin does not necessarily indicate that protein structure was destroyed during purification. It may result from removal of a reversible physiological effector.

In 1967 the work of Benesch and Benesch and the independent observations of Chanutin and Curnish established the role of organic phosphate in regulating oxygen affinity. Subsequent studies of reciprocal oxygen and diphosphoglycerate binding clarified the nature of the allosteric linkage. A useful conceptual test compares purified haemoglobin without added organic phosphate with the same preparation after adding known concentrations of 2,3-BPG. If the added compound raises P50 without reducing the number of functional haem sites, the most direct interpretation is an altered binding equilibrium rather than destruction of haem. A graded response and reversible removal of the effector provide stronger evidence than a single comparison.

To distinguish the effect from an artefact, control the haemoglobin concentration, pH, temperature, ionic strength and oxidation state. Organic phosphates carry multiple charges, so addition of an effector can also alter the chemical environment if the experiment is not adequately controlled. A suitable control can compare the effect of a salt adjustment without the same specific ligand interaction. An observation of stronger binding by deoxygenated haemoglobin than by oxygenated haemoglobin is consistent with preferential stabilisation of the low-affinity form, but a complete structural interpretation requires complementary evidence about where the ligand binds. Such reasoning links biochemical binding experiments to physiological curves without treating either as proof of every molecular detail.

Changes in erythrocyte 2,3-BPG concentration generally occur over longer time scales than immediate shifts in local temperature and pH as blood passes through a capillary. Sustained hypoxic exposure and some forms of anaemia can be associated with changes in red cell organic phosphate metabolism that facilitate unloading. The direction and magnitude of whole-blood affinity changes depend on the simultaneous acid–base state and other factors. At altitude, for instance, hyperventilation can promote alkalosis and favour a left shift, while altered red cell 2,3-BPG can oppose that influence. The net curve cannot always be predicted from one regulatory variable alone. Detailed environmental acclimatisation belongs to the later stress and adaptation subunit.

A useful examination comparison is between two samples with identical haemoglobin molecules but different intracellular organic phosphate concentrations. If their haemoglobin concentrations, pH and temperatures are held constant, the sample with greater 2,3-BPG should generally require a higher oxygen pressure to reach a given intermediate saturation. In contrast, an increase in the concentration of haemoglobin molecules alone increases the total oxygen-binding capacity rather than necessarily changing the P50. A question that asks about oxygen unloading at fixed pressure tests affinity; a question asking how many millilitres can be carried at complete saturation tests capacity. Both changes may happen in a living animal, but the two variables must first be distinguished.

8. Foetal haemoglobin and the placental loading problem

A developing mammalian foetus does not ventilate its lungs with atmospheric air. Instead, oxygen passes from the maternal circulation across the placenta into foetal blood. The exchange occurs between distinct maternal and foetal blood compartments separated by placental tissues; the blood supplies do not ordinarily mix freely. The relevant loading pressure in foetal blood is much lower than the arterial oxygen pressure achieved in an adult ventilating healthy lungs. A haemoglobin suited to these conditions must acquire substantial oxygen occupancy at relatively modest oxygen tension.

Human foetal haemoglobin, HbF, is composed of two alpha and two gamma globin chains, in contrast to the two alpha and two beta chains of predominant adult HbA. Both tetramers contain four haem groups and can bind four oxygen molecules. The functional difference therefore does not arise because HbF possesses additional oxygen sites. A major contribution to the difference in oxygen affinity is the weaker interaction of 2,3-BPG with HbF compared with HbA. Differences in the residues forming the central cavity reduce the favourable electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged ligand. The organic phosphate consequently stabilises the low-affinity state less strongly.

The whole-blood oxygen equilibrium curve associated with HbF is generally shifted to the left of that of adult blood under comparable physiological conditions. Typical teaching values for P50 are approximately 19 mmHg for HbF-rich blood and 26–27 mmHg for HbA-rich adult blood, with actual values depending on preparation and physiological conditions. A lower P50 allows greater oxygen occupancy at the same low pressure. In a simple Hill model with n = 2.7 and P50 = 19 mmHg, saturation at 25 mmHg is approximately 0.677; using P50 = 27 mmHg gives approximately 0.448. The calculation is a comparison of models at identical pressure, not a measurement of actual maternal and foetal blood within the placenta.

The partial pressure gradient, not the difference in binding affinity by itself, drives diffusion across the placental exchange barrier. HbF binds incoming dissolved oxygen and thereby helps maintain a gradient as oxygen enters foetal blood. At the same oxygen pressure, the foetal blood can retain a higher saturation than a comparable adult-haemoglobin sample. The total transfer nevertheless also depends on maternal and foetal flows, exchange area, diffusion distance, local oxygen consumption and oxygen capacity. High HbF affinity cannot generate oxygen if maternal oxygen supply is absent.

A further physiological contribution comes from carbon dioxide exchange. Foetal carbon dioxide diffuses toward maternal blood, tending to reduce carbon dioxide in the foetal compartment and increase it in the maternal compartment. The associated changes in acid–base state can favour oxygen binding in foetal blood and oxygen unloading in maternal blood. This paired phenomenon is often called the double Bohr effect. It assists transfer under appropriate conditions, but the size of the contribution depends on buffering and the actual exchange environment. It is not necessary to assume identical pH changes on both sides of the placenta or that oxygen and carbon dioxide have precisely reciprocal pressure gradients.

A higher affinity also has a potential cost: at a fixed systemic tissue oxygen tension, a high-affinity protein tends to retain more oxygen. Foetal physiology compensates through the combined properties of the entire transport system, including haemoglobin concentration, blood flow and tissue oxygen extraction. The functional value of HbF is best understood as an adaptation to the available loading environment rather than a universally superior oxygen carrier. Following birth, pulmonary oxygen uptake changes the loading conditions and globin gene expression gradually shifts toward adult haemoglobin. The developmental control of globin expression is considered in the relevant gene expression and blood chapters.

A direct experimental comparison should separate the effect of globin composition from the effect of the red cell's organic phosphate concentration. One can compare purified HbA and HbF in a defined buffer before and after adding the same concentration of 2,3-BPG. If the HbA curve shifts further toward higher pressure, the comparison supports a difference in sensitivity to the organic phosphate. Whole blood from two developmental stages cannot alone identify the protein-level cause because it may differ simultaneously in cell metabolism, pH, haemoglobin composition and other environmental factors. Control of those variables turns a physiological observation into a testable mechanistic inference.

An important species qualification follows from comparative physiology. The factors causing developmental changes in oxygen affinity are not necessarily the same in every mammal. A foetus may express a different globin form, alter its erythrocyte organic phosphate concentrations, or combine these changes in different ways. A human HbF mechanism must not be generalised as the universal explanation for every vertebrate developmental transition. The conserved problem is acquiring and delivering oxygen under the pressures imposed by the developmental environment; the molecular solutions can differ.

9. Myoglobin and intracellular oxygen transfer

Myoglobin is an oxygen-binding protein found especially in cardiac and skeletal muscle. Unlike circulating mammalian haemoglobin, it is primarily intracellular and contains one globin chain with one haem group. Its function is not to increase the oxygen content of blood. It contributes to oxygen availability within muscle, where oxygen must travel from the capillary through interstitial fluid and the cell membrane toward the mitochondria. Myoglobin can hold oxygen reversibly and may facilitate intracellular diffusion of oxygen under appropriate conditions, in addition to acting as an oxygen reserve.

The equilibrium saturation curve of an isolated single-site myoglobin molecule is hyperbolic rather than sigmoidal. There are no additional sites within that monomer to produce the ordinary homotropic cooperativity of a haemoglobin tetramer. A representative myoglobin P50 is only a few mmHg, although precise values depend on species and conditions. At the oxygen tensions corresponding to many systemic capillaries, myoglobin therefore tends to be highly saturated while circulating haemoglobin can already release a substantial fraction of its oxygen.

The contrast does not mean myoglobin actively pulls oxygen from haemoglobin by physical contact. Most circulating haemoglobin is confined to erythrocytes, whereas myoglobin is located in muscle cells. Oxygen dissociates into the dissolved pool, diffuses through intervening compartments, and can bind myoglobin. The differences in binding equilibria allow oxygen to be partitioned differently between the two pigments at the pressures encountered in blood and muscle. The net direction of transfer still requires a free oxygen activity gradient maintained by metabolism.

At low intracellular oxygen tension, myoglobin releases an increasing fraction of its stored oxygen. For a model with P50 = 3 mmHg, the fractional saturation at 10 mmHg is 10/(10 + 3), about 0.769. At 3 mmHg, it is 0.50. At 1 mmHg, it is 0.25. A decrease from 10 to 1 mmHg could thus release about 52% of the total myoglobin oxygen-binding capacity if equilibrium is approached and the local pool has sufficient time to exchange. The absolute quantity released cannot be determined without knowing how much myoglobin is present in the tissue.

In terrestrial mammals, this oxygen reserve can support transient changes in muscle oxygen availability, but its importance is particularly evident in diving mammals with exceptionally high muscle myoglobin concentrations. During a dive, ventilation is interrupted and oxygen must be drawn from stores in lungs, blood and tissues. The ability to store substantial oxygen inside muscle reduces immediate dependence on continuous delivery from blood to every working region. High concentrations of myoglobin are therefore relevant to the integrated oxygen budget of diving animals. Such an example illustrates the biological importance of oxygen storage rather than implying that myoglobin is absent or unimportant in nondriving mammals.

A model of facilitated intracellular diffusion requires more than reversible binding alone. If an oxygen-binding molecule can move within the cytoplasm, the movement of oxygenated protein can contribute to the net movement of oxygen equivalents along a concentration gradient, while deoxygenated protein can return. The contribution depends on protein mobility, concentration, local gradients and rates of oxygenation and deoxygenation. It is not equivalent to an energy-driven membrane transporter and may be small in some physiological conditions. A stationary binding protein would still provide buffering capacity but would not carry its bound oxygen across a distance by protein diffusion.

The distinction between oxygen storage and oxygen delivery can be illustrated through a transient exercise problem. Imagine oxygen consumption rises suddenly, but the local blood flow takes time to increase. The initial additional demand can draw on dissolved oxygen and some previously bound intracellular oxygen. Once a new steady state is established, a finite store cannot support continuing consumption indefinitely unless oxygen is replenished. Myoglobin can buffer a temporary mismatch, whereas sustained aerobic metabolism requires continuing oxygen input. This distinction between a stock and a rate will be used again when comparing oxygen content with oxygen delivery.

Myoglobin also provides a valuable experimental control for evaluating the meaning of a Hill plot. A single-site protein can have very high affinity but no positive homotropic cooperativity. A haemoglobin tetramer can have lower average affinity and still show strong positive cooperativity. Thus a curve's position and shape encode different features. High affinity is indicated by low P50; positive cooperativity is indicated by the steepening of the curve relative to the single-site relationship in the relevant range. Conflating the two features leads to incorrect predictions when comparing oxygen storage and transport proteins.

10. Comparative respiratory pigments and their biological roles

The fact that haemoglobin dominates oxygen carriage in vertebrate blood can make it appear to be the only biologically effective oxygen transporter. In reality, several chemically distinct proteins perform related tasks in animal respiratory systems. Comparing them requires more than memorising a table of metals and colours. The central questions are how each molecule reversibly binds oxygen, where the molecule is located, whether it exhibits interactions among binding sites, and how its binding properties fit the oxygen availability and circulation of the animal. Metal identity is useful for classification, but it is not sufficient to predict oxygen affinity or respiratory performance.

Haemoglobin uses an iron-containing porphyrin group called haem. The functional oxygen-binding centre includes a ferrous iron ion in coordination with the porphyrin nitrogen atoms and a ligand supplied by the protein. The surrounding globin fold establishes a chemical environment in which oxygen can bind and dissociate reversibly. The colour change accompanying oxygenation is related to changes in the electronic absorption spectrum of this complex. Oxygenated mammalian haemoglobin is bright red, whereas deoxygenated haemoglobin is darker red. The often repeated statement that deoxygenated human blood is blue is incorrect. Veins can appear bluish through skin because the optical properties of tissue and blood affect the light reaching the observer.

In vertebrates, the bulk of the haemoglobin is packaged inside circulating erythrocytes. This arrangement permits a high intracellular concentration of protein without making all of it freely dissolved in plasma. A consequence is that oxygen has to cross the erythrocyte membrane as part of its exchange with the plasma, although this transfer is normally very rapid. Not all invertebrate haemoglobins are packaged in cells. Some are present as large extracellular complexes dissolved in the circulating fluid. The molecular organisation of such complexes can limit their loss from the circulation and influence their allosteric behaviour. An examination question identifying haemoglobin in an invertebrate should therefore not automatically assume the presence of a mammalian-type erythrocyte.

Haemocyanin is an oxygen transporter found in many molluscs and arthropods. Its oxygen-binding centre contains a pair of copper ions rather than the iron porphyrin characteristic of haemoglobin. In the deoxygenated state the copper ions are predominantly in the cuprous state, while oxygen binding produces a coupled dicopper–oxygen complex with the copper centres in a more oxidised state. The resulting absorption spectrum gives oxygenated haemocyanin a blue colour. Deoxygenated haemocyanin is much less strongly coloured. This reversible oxygenation chemistry differs from the haem chemistry of vertebrate haemoglobin; it should not be described as a blue type of haemoglobin.

Haemocyanin is commonly present as a large extracellular protein in haemolymph. The large assemblies of molluscan and arthropod haemocyanins differ in their structural organisation, and the affinities and cooperative responses vary among species. Oxygen binding to individual centres may be influenced by interactions between subunits, just as binding to haemoglobin can be altered by changes elsewhere in its structure. However, the existence of a multimeric organisation does not by itself determine how large the Hill coefficient will be. The behaviour must be measured under appropriate conditions. The particular copper-binding chemistry and the extracellular location can be learned as representative features without assuming that all haemocyanins share a single numerical P50.

A crab using haemocyanin and a fish using haemoglobin both encounter the general problem that oxygen is less available in many aquatic environments than in air. Their gills establish gradients between ventilated water and circulating fluid, and reversible pigment binding raises the quantity of oxygen that can be transported at the pressure attained in the gill. A comparison of their oxygen delivery would require measurements of pigment concentration, the effective oxygen capacity per volume of circulating fluid, saturation at the gill and tissue pressures, and flow. It would be inappropriate to conclude that one animal carries more oxygen simply because the metal in its pigment is different.

Haemerythrin represents another solution. Despite its name, its oxygen-binding centre does not contain a haem group. The protein uses a binuclear non-haem iron centre, where oxygenation involves reversible chemistry at the paired iron ions. Oxygenated haemerythrin is often described as violet or pinkish violet, while the deoxygenated form is comparatively pale. Representative haemerythrins occur in certain marine invertebrates, including sipunculans and some other groups. They may be found in circulating cells, and related intracellular proteins can also serve oxygen storage functions. The distinction between haemerythrin and haemoglobin illustrates why the prefix haem in a biological name is not reliable evidence for a porphyrin cofactor.

A haemerythrin binding centre can bind one oxygen molecule per functional diiron site. It would be wrong to infer that the presence of two iron ions automatically provides two independent oxygen sites. The two metal centres cooperate chemically in binding the oxygen molecule. Likewise, the two copper ions of a haemocyanin site participate in a single oxygen-binding centre. The relationship between the number of metal atoms and oxygen stoichiometry therefore depends on molecular architecture. When a quantitative problem specifies the mass or molar concentration of a pigment, the number of oxygen molecules bound per functional unit must be stated before capacity can be calculated.

Chlorocruorin is a haem-based oxygen-binding protein found in some annelids, particularly certain polychaetes. It contains a modified haem group and occurs in large extracellular complexes. Its unusual greenish appearance in dilute solution contrasts with the appearance of ordinary haemoglobin; colour can vary with concentration and oxygenation state, so the name is not a universal prediction of the appearance of an animal's entire circulation. Chlorocruorin belongs with haem-containing respiratory proteins, not with copper-based haemocyanin or non-haem iron-based haemerythrin. The structural modification affects the optical and binding properties of the protein, but the details vary and should not be reduced to the claim that all green blood has high or low affinity.

Erythrocruorin is a useful related example of giant extracellular haemoglobin complexes in annelids. A single large complex contains many oxygen-binding subunits assembled into an ordered architecture. Its large size distinguishes it from the typical small vertebrate haemoglobin tetramer enclosed in a red cell. Yet the underlying oxygen-binding chemistry is still haem based. Comparing erythrocruorin with chlorocruorin illustrates that the localisation and higher-order structure of respiratory proteins can evolve independently of the identity of their basic oxygen-binding cofactor. Not every animal with haemoglobin uses blood cells, and not every iron-containing respiratory pigment contains haem.

The alternative to circulating pigments is not always the absence of any oxygen-binding proteins. The insect tracheal system, introduced in comparative respiration, brings gas directly to many tissues through branching tubes and fine tracheoles. The circulating haemolymph generally plays a smaller role in oxygen distribution than mammalian blood does. Nevertheless, some insects express haemoglobin, especially in particular physiological or environmental contexts, such as oxygen-poor habitats. Certain chironomid larvae possess haemoglobins that help oxygen availability under low environmental oxygen. Such exceptions demonstrate why statements about an entire phylum should be qualified. The relative contributions of direct gas delivery and pigment-assisted transport depend on the animal's architecture and environment.

Respiratory pigment affinity must be evaluated in the context of both loading and unloading. For a species living where water oxygen tension varies substantially, increased affinity may improve oxygen uptake at the gill when environmental oxygen is scarce. Yet increased affinity could also reduce oxygen release at a fixed tissue pressure. Organisms can resolve this trade-off by changing blood flow, pigment concentration, tissue oxygen gradients, cooperative behaviour or the response to local allosteric conditions. Other species may have pigments whose affinity changes with pH in a direction or magnitude different from human haemoglobin. Applying the mammalian Bohr rule to every invertebrate without checking the experimental data would be an unwarranted generalisation.

A useful comparative experiment might measure oxygen-binding curves of a molluscan haemocyanin at two temperatures and pH values. The first step is to define the maximum oxygen-binding capacity of the sample and use that value to express fractional occupancy. The second is to equilibrate samples at a series of oxygen tensions while measuring the oxygen-bound fraction by a method appropriate to the pigment. The third is to compare the curves at matched pressure, rather than compare raw optical absorbance values of two chemically different pigments. The absorbance change per bound oxygen molecule can differ among proteins, and comparisons of uncalibrated optical signals can therefore be misleading.

If one pigment reaches half saturation at 12 mmHg and another at 30 mmHg under the same stated conditions, the former has higher apparent affinity around its midpoint. However, its carrying capacity per litre of blood may be greater, equal or smaller. The curves may also differ in shape, making comparisons at the actual loading and unloading pressures necessary. To predict oxygen transport by either species, measure or specify the concentration of active binding sites and the volume of circulating fluid passing through the respiratory organ per unit time. These requirements connect comparative physiology directly with the quantitative analysis developed in the following sections.

The variety of respiratory pigments provides an instructive example of convergent biological function. Different protein folds and metal chemistries can solve the same general transport problem. The distribution of a pigment among animals is shaped by evolutionary history as well as ecological and physiological constraints. Blood colour is a consequence of molecular electronic properties, not an independent measure of the quality of respiratory adaptation. A student who understands the chemical centre, location, capacity and binding curve can reason about an unfamiliar pigment from experimental information without having memorised every taxonomic occurrence.

11. Oxygen delivery: linking the respiratory system to the cardiac pump

The oxygen content equation states how much oxygen is present in a given volume of blood. It does not tell how much oxygen reaches a tissue in one minute. A litre of highly oxygenated blood remaining stationary in the circulation supplies no continuing inflow to a distant tissue. The movement of the oxygen-bearing fluid is therefore a second essential component of oxygen transport. In earlier cardiovascular chapters, cardiac output was defined as the volume of blood pumped by a ventricle per unit time. When systemic arterial blood enters the organs, the quantity of oxygen that enters with it is the product of flow and arterial oxygen content.

For the whole systemic circulation, oxygen delivery is denoted DO₂ and is commonly expressed in mL oxygen per minute. If cardiac output Q is expressed in L/min and arterial oxygen content CaO₂ in mL/dL, the equation is DO₂ = 10 × Q × CaO₂. The factor ten is not a biological parameter. It converts litres of blood to decilitres, because one litre contains ten decilitres. If blood flow is already expressed in dL/min, the conversion factor is unnecessary. In SI units using oxygen concentration in mmol/L and flow in L/min, the corresponding product directly yields mmol/min.

For an illustrative resting adult with cardiac output 5 L/min and CaO₂ 20 mL/dL, delivery is 10 × 5 × 20 = 1,000 mL oxygen/min. This value is much greater than typical resting whole-body oxygen consumption because tissues do not normally remove every oxygen molecule from the blood on each passage. Delivery is the amount entering the systemic circulation per minute, whereas oxygen consumption is the amount removed and used by tissues per minute in the steady state. The remaining oxygen returns through the systemic veins and passes through the pulmonary circulation again.

Regional delivery is calculated with the same principle. A muscle bed receiving 0.30 L blood/min at an arterial content of 20 mL/dL receives 60 mL oxygen/min. A second bed receiving 0.10 L/min at the same content receives 20 mL/min. These values are independent of how much each tissue ultimately extracts. If a vasoactive response raises the first bed's flow without changing arterial content, the oxygen delivered to that region increases proportionately. Redistribution of a fixed cardiac output can therefore alter local oxygen delivery even when total systemic delivery is unchanged.

This distinction is physiologically important because cardiac output supplies many organs simultaneously. The heart and brain require continued oxygen availability, while active muscle can demand a dramatic increase relative to its resting state. Local regulation of perfusion helps allocate the available blood according to regional metabolic requirements. The relevant earlier cardiovascular mechanisms are changes in arteriolar resistance, autoregulation and adjustments in the cardiac pump. Here the important consequence is that a normal or high arterial saturation cannot guarantee adequate regional oxygen delivery when local blood flow is impaired.

A useful comparison involves two hypothetical animals with identical blood oxygen content but different cardiac outputs. Animal A pumps 4 L/min at 18 mL/dL, producing a delivery of 720 mL/min. Animal B pumps 6 L/min at the same content, producing 1,080 mL/min. If the animals have different body masses and metabolic rates, the larger delivery need not mean that animal B is better matched to its demands. An adequate comparison should relate supply to consumption and, when relevant, to body mass or tissue mass. In a small isolated organ, a flow-normalised oxygen delivery can be more informative than an unscaled whole-body value.

Changes in haemoglobin concentration can be partly offset by changes in flow. Consider the illustrative sample with Hb = 7.5 g/dL, SaO₂ = 98% and PaO₂ = 100 mmHg from the earlier calculation. Its CaO₂ is approximately 10.15 mL/dL. To deliver the same 1,000 mL/min as blood with a content of 20 mL/dL at 5 L/min, cardiac output would have to be 1,000 divided by (10 × 10.15), or approximately 9.85 L/min. This is nearly double the initial output. Such compensation is not guaranteed: it requires sufficient pumping capacity, circulating volume and appropriate distribution of flow. The numerical exercise explains why a disturbance of oxygen capacity can impose additional cardiovascular demands.

Increasing the haematocrit or haemoglobin concentration also has potential costs. The increased number of oxygen-binding sites can raise arterial oxygen content, but higher red cell volume fraction can increase the effective viscosity of blood and alter vascular resistance. If cardiac output falls sufficiently, the net oxygen delivery may fail to increase in proportion to content. Conversely, a reduction in blood viscosity accompanying lower red cell concentration may sometimes facilitate flow, but the resulting oxygen delivery still depends on the product of content and flow. A complete physiological prediction should account for both factors rather than assume a linear benefit from increasing haemoglobin.

The difference between delivery and oxygen stores is important during rapidly changing conditions. Cardiac output multiplied by arterial content describes the oxygen entering the circulation or organ per unit time at the instant considered. If blood or tissue oxygen stores are changing, the relationship between arterial inflow, venous outflow and cellular utilisation also includes the rate of change of stored oxygen. At steady state, storage no longer changes and a simple mass balance applies. During brief transitions after exercise begins or ventilation stops, the steady-state approximation should be recognised as a limitation.

A quantitative examination problem can be approached by listing the independently controlled determinants. First calculate CaO₂ from haemoglobin, saturation and dissolved oxygen tension. Then identify whether the supplied flow refers to the entire circulation or a particular organ, and convert its units. Multiply to obtain delivered oxygen per minute. Only after obtaining this rate should consumption or extraction be calculated. If a question changes one determinant while holding another constant, the new delivery follows directly from the new product. If the question does not specify flow, it may be impossible to rank delivery using content alone.

The oxygen delivery equation is deliberately simple because it is a conservation-based definition. It does not incorporate the kinetics of unloading, capillary recruitment, diffusion distance or mitochondrial function explicitly. These processes determine how much of the delivered oxygen can actually reach and be consumed by the cells. A tissue supplied with blood that has high oxygen content and adequate flow may nevertheless experience limited oxygen utilisation if oxygen cannot diffuse through the tissue or if oxidative metabolism is impaired. Delivery is therefore necessary but not sufficient for sustained aerobic ATP production.

12. Extraction, tissue unloading and the Fick principle

When blood flows through a systemic tissue, it usually leaves with lower oxygen content than it had on entry. The difference between arterial content CaO₂ and venous content CvO₂ represents the amount of oxygen removed from each decilitre of blood. For a defined vascular bed in steady state, oxygen consumption equals the oxygen entering in arterial blood minus the oxygen leaving in venous blood. This statement follows from conservation of mass and does not require any particular mathematical model of the haemoglobin dissociation curve.

If Q is flow in L/min, oxygen consumption V̇O₂ = 10 × Q × (CaO₂ − CvO₂), provided the contents are given in mL/dL. This is an application of the Fick principle. For the whole body the venous content must represent genuinely mixed systemic venous blood, not simply a convenient sample from a single peripheral vein. Different organs extract different amounts of oxygen; blood returning from them is mixed in proportion to their flows. Substituting a venous sample from an unusually active or inactive tissue into a whole-body calculation can produce a misleading result.

The Fick principle for organ metabolism must be distinguished from Fick's law of diffusion, which relates diffusive flux to concentration or pressure gradients and the physical properties of the diffusion path. Both are named after Adolf Fick, but they address different levels of physiological organisation. The diffusion law concerns oxygen crossing a barrier; the Fick principle relates net exchange of oxygen by an organ or organism to the difference between inflow and outflow. The previous chapter discussed diffusion and membrane conductance. The present mass balance can be applied even when the detailed geometry of oxygen diffusion within a tissue is unknown.

Suppose arterial blood contains 20 mL/dL and mixed venous blood contains 15 mL/dL. The arteriovenous difference is 5 mL/dL. With a cardiac output of 5 L/min, oxygen consumption is 10 × 5 × 5 = 250 mL/min. The oxygen extraction ratio, sometimes written OER, is the fraction of delivered oxygen removed by the tissues: OER = (CaO₂ − CvO₂)/CaO₂. Here the ratio is 5/20 = 0.25, or 25%. Thus delivery is 1,000 mL/min and consumption is one quarter of that value. The ratio should be expressed as a fraction or percentage, not as a flow or concentration.

Venous saturation can provide a rough indication of extraction when arterial saturation is high, haemoglobin concentration is stable and dissolved oxygen contributions are small. Under those assumptions, arterial and venous contents are approximately proportional to their respective haemoglobin saturations. If arterial saturation is 98% and venous saturation is 73%, the approximate extraction fraction is (0.98 − 0.73)/0.98, or 25.5%. It is not exactly the difference of 25 percentage points unless arterial saturation is 100%. The full content calculation is preferable when the dissolved contribution or abnormalities of haemoglobin are important.

The same mass balance can be rearranged to estimate flow. If whole-body oxygen consumption has been measured at 240 mL/min, arterial content is 19.5 mL/dL and mixed venous content is 14.7 mL/dL, the content difference is 4.8 mL/dL. Cardiac output is 240/(10 × 4.8) = 5.0 L/min. This calculation illustrates the classic use of the Fick principle for determining cardiac output. An independently measured oxygen consumption is required. Merely assuming consumption from a typical textbook value would make the calculated flow an estimate rather than a direct measurement.

The principle also predicts how venous content must change if supply and demand are altered. Consider a tissue with a flow of 0.20 L/min, arterial content 20 mL/dL and oxygen consumption 20 mL/min. It removes 20/(10 × 0.20) = 10 mL/dL, so its venous content is 10 mL/dL and extraction is 50%. If flow increases to 0.40 L/min while consumption remains unchanged, the required difference falls to 5 mL/dL and venous content rises to 15 mL/dL. A higher venous oxygen content in this example indicates that less oxygen was extracted from each unit of blood, not necessarily that the tissue consumed less oxygen per minute.

The converse can be considered at fixed flow. If a tissue initially extracts 25% of its oxygen supply and its metabolic demand increases, it can increase the arteriovenous content difference by removing more oxygen from each unit of blood. This tends to lower venous oxygen tension and saturation. As venous oxygen tension falls, the steeper part of the haemoglobin curve can facilitate additional unloading. Nevertheless extraction cannot increase indefinitely: oxygen content cannot become negative, and some residual oxygen is required to maintain gradients through tissue and to accommodate spatial variation in diffusion distances.

At a sufficiently low supply, oxygen consumption becomes limited by the delivery and extraction capacities of the tissue. Over a range of deliveries above this limitation, metabolism may maintain a relatively constant oxygen consumption by changing extraction. Below that range, further reductions in delivery can reduce aerobic consumption. The transition is not fixed at a universal numerical delivery value. It depends on organ type, metabolic activity, oxygen diffusional conductance, capillary distribution and the properties of the oxygen carrier. A plot of consumption against delivery is therefore a useful conceptual model but should not be presented as an identical quantitative law for every organ.

The supply limitation can be demonstrated with a simple teaching model. Let metabolic demand be 100 mL/min and suppose the maximal achievable extraction ratio under the model conditions is 0.60. At a delivery of 250 mL/min, the required extraction is 0.40 and the demand can be satisfied. At delivery of 180 mL/min, required extraction is approximately 0.56 and the model still permits adequate consumption. At delivery of 140 mL/min, even 60% extraction supplies only 84 mL/min. Aerobic consumption cannot meet the stipulated demand under those assumptions. The critical delivery for this illustrative model is 100/0.60, approximately 167 mL/min. The chosen extraction limit is hypothetical and must not be interpreted as a clinical threshold.

During exercise, increases in oxygen consumption can be supported by both increased cardiac output and greater extraction in active muscle. It is instructive to consider two successive states: at rest, flow is 5 L/min and the mixed arteriovenous content difference is 5 mL/dL, yielding consumption 250 mL/min. During an illustrative activity, flow rises to 12 L/min and the difference rises to 10 mL/dL, yielding consumption 1,200 mL/min. Neither determinant alone accounts for the full increase. The combined product has increased by a factor of 4.8, while flow has increased by a factor of 2.4 and extraction difference by a factor of two. This example shows why cardiovascular and respiratory adaptations must be analysed together.

Regional oxygen extraction is also related to capillary transit time. When flow increases through a fixed capillary volume, mean residence time decreases, potentially reducing the time available for oxygen transfer. In living tissues, recruitment of additional perfused capillaries and changes in exchange surface area can alter the relationship between flow and transit time. Increased flow therefore does not necessarily cause the same fractional extraction change in every tissue. The Fick balance remains true regardless of the microscopic pattern; what changes is the attainable venous content and the resulting consumption.

A student should distinguish the rate of oxygen transfer from the saturation of venous blood. A high venous saturation can arise because delivery exceeds demand, because tissues are extracting little oxygen, or because some flow bypasses exchange regions. A low venous saturation can arise because demand is high relative to flow or arterial content. Neither measurement alone establishes the cause. The correct reasoning starts with the complete mass balance and then uses additional measurements of flow, content, tissue function and distribution to distinguish the alternatives.

Finally, mixed venous content is a weighted average, not a simple average of venous saturations from organs with unequal flows. If one region receives 1 L/min and returns blood with content 8 mL/dL while another receives 4 L/min and returns blood with content 16 mL/dL, the combined venous content is (1 × 8 + 4 × 16)/(1 + 4) = 14.4 mL/dL. The unweighted mean of 12 mL/dL is incorrect. The more highly perfused region contributes a larger fraction of the returning blood. This mixing principle is also important for the interpretation of pulmonary shunt and ventilation–perfusion mismatch in the preceding chapter.

13. Hypoxaemia, anaemia and tissue oxygen availability

Several disturbances can limit oxygen supply to cells, but they need not produce the same changes in arterial measurements. Hypoxaemia refers to abnormally low oxygen tension in arterial blood. Tissue hypoxia refers to inadequate oxygen availability or utilisation relative to tissue needs. Although hypoxaemia can lead to hypoxia, the two concepts are not identical. A low haemoglobin concentration can reduce oxygen delivery without causing arterial hypoxaemia, while a modest reduction in arterial tension may be partly compensated by increased flow or extraction. The following examples are physiological comparisons rather than diagnostic or treatment instructions.

In a hypoxaemic state, impaired ventilation, diffusion, ventilation–perfusion relationships or shunting can reduce the oxygen pressure attained in arterial blood. Once oxygen pressure moves below the plateau of the haemoglobin curve, saturation may decline substantially. At a fixed haemoglobin concentration, this reduces bound oxygen content. The dissolved contribution also decreases but is usually much smaller in ordinary blood. The cause of low arterial pressure belongs to the gas exchange and ventilation chapters; the consequence examined here is its effect on the amount of oxygen available for systemic transport.

In anaemia, the total concentration of haemoglobin is reduced. If gas exchange remains effective and the haemoglobin that is present has ordinary binding properties, arterial oxygen tension and fractional saturation may remain close to normal. The major change is the reduction in oxygen capacity per volume of blood. An optical saturation measurement may therefore look reassuring even while the content is substantially lower than normal. This is why saturation and haemoglobin concentration are distinct observations in a physiological assessment. The numerical examples presented earlier demonstrate the magnitude of the difference without requiring any assumption of reduced pulmonary oxygen transfer.

A third category involves insufficient tissue blood flow. When perfusion is reduced, arterial content and pressure entering the tissue may be normal but delivery per minute falls. Tissues may compensate by extracting a larger fraction of the available oxygen, so the venous content becomes lower. If flow falls sufficiently, extraction cannot compensate indefinitely. This situation differs mechanistically from low haemoglobin: the amount carried in each decilitre can be normal, but too few decilitres arrive per minute. It illustrates why the measurement of blood oxygen content does not establish the rate of supply.

A further distinction arises when tissue utilisation of oxygen is impaired. If oxidative metabolism cannot use oxygen effectively, blood may leave a tissue with relatively high oxygen content despite impaired aerobic energy production. In that case a high venous oxygen saturation does not necessarily demonstrate adequate cellular metabolism. The mass balance describes the amount removed from blood; it does not independently establish that the removed oxygen has supported normal ATP synthesis. The mitochondrial mechanisms themselves belong to the biomolecules and bioenergetics curriculum, but their relationship to supply is necessary for interpreting whole-body oxygen measurements.

Carbon monoxide provides another instructive example of the difference between oxygen tension and content. It binds strongly to haemoglobin and occupies sites that would otherwise be available to oxygen. It also increases the oxygen affinity of remaining available sites, impairing unloading. In an idealised comparison at the same arterial oxygen tension, the dissolved oxygen measured by a blood gas electrode can be near normal while the functional oxygen capacity is reduced. The remaining oxygen-binding sites may retain oxygen more strongly. Thus carbon monoxide can produce a combined defect in capacity and release rather than merely reproduce the behaviour of blood containing a lower concentration of otherwise normal haemoglobin.

Methaemoglobin illustrates a chemically different loss of capacity. When iron at a haem site is oxidised to the ferric state, that site cannot bind oxygen in the usual reversible manner. Changes in the behaviour of remaining sites can also affect the overall oxygen-binding curve. The ordinary content equation is not adequate if it uses a total haemoglobin measurement and a functional saturation that excludes unavailable sites without correction. This is a case in which the definition of the measured variables is as important as the mathematical operation. It is also a reason to avoid inferring functional oxygen capacity from a single optical saturation reading when abnormal pigments are present.

A comparison of four hypothetical samples can organise these concepts. Sample A has Hb 15 g/dL, SaO₂ 0.98, PaO₂ 100 mmHg and cardiac output 5 L/min; its content is approximately 20.0 mL/dL and delivery 1,000 mL/min. Sample B has Hb 8 g/dL with the same saturation, pressure and flow; its content is 10.806 mL/dL and delivery 540.3 mL/min. Sample C has Hb 15 g/dL, SaO₂ 0.80, PaO₂ 45 mmHg and flow 5 L/min; content is 16.215 mL/dL and delivery 810.75 mL/min. Sample D has the same blood composition as A but flow 2.5 L/min, so content remains about 20.0 mL/dL while delivery is approximately 500 mL/min.

These data deliberately hold other factors constant to isolate each disturbance. Sample B has reduced capacity, C has reduced arterial oxygen tension and saturation, and D has reduced bulk flow. All three have lower delivery than A, yet only C is stipulated to be hypoxaemic. A ranking based on arterial oxygen tension would identify C as the most impaired by that measurement, whereas the calculated delivery is lowest in D among the three. A ranking based on saturation alone would fail to distinguish A, B and D. This comparison is an example of why physiological reasoning must identify the quantity of interest before ranking conditions.

The relative impairment of oxygen delivery does not automatically establish the degree of tissue hypoxia. One individual may have a lower oxygen demand, a greater extraction reserve, a different pattern of regional flow or a different effective diffusional conductance. A model supplying only arterial variables and total cardiac output cannot establish oxygenation of every cell. The numerical values provide a basis for comparing supply, while additional evidence is required to evaluate metabolic adequacy. An examination that asks for delivery has a precise numerical answer; an examination asking whether tissues are adequately oxygenated may require further assumptions.

Compensation can also introduce a distinction between an acute disturbance and a long-term response. A sustained reduction in oxygen capacity may stimulate changes in erythropoiesis and alter the properties of circulating blood over time. A rapid drop in perfusion can evoke cardiovascular adjustments before the composition of red cells changes. Changes in breathing may affect arterial oxygen tension but may not correct a defect in haemoglobin capacity. These regulatory responses operate on different timescales and target different components of the transport chain. Understanding the original disturbance helps explain why a compensatory response may be appropriate in one situation but insufficient in another.

The central examination lesson is that oxygen tension, capacity, flow and utilisation are separate variables connected by physiological processes. Partial pressure governs diffusion; the pigment increases the amount carried at that pressure; circulation distributes the amount; tissue exchange and metabolism determine how much is extracted. Disturbances at different stages may produce a similar final shortage of cellular oxygen yet require different causal explanations. A complete model follows oxygen from the respiratory surface to the mitochondrial sink rather than treating the arterial saturation as a surrogate for every stage.
