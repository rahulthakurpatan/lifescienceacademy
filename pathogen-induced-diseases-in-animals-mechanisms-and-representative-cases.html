1. From microbial encounter to animal disease

A living animal maintains a complicated exchange with the environment. Its intestinal surface takes up nutrients and water while keeping much of the luminal microbial community outside the internal tissues. Its respiratory tract moves air across a thin exchange surface without allowing inhaled particles to accumulate indefinitely. The cells accomplishing these tasks have receptors, transport proteins, cytoskeletal machinery and signaling pathways that evolved for normal physiological functions. A pathogen can exploit these same features. To understand infectious disease at the level of cell biology, it is necessary to follow the consequences of that exploitation across successive levels of organization. The first molecular interaction matters because it changes a cellular process; the cellular process matters because many cells cooperate to sustain a tissue function; and a tissue disturbance becomes disease when normal physiological performance is compromised.

Exposure describes contact between a potential pathogen and a host. Colonization describes establishment at a site, often accompanied by growth, without necessarily implying destruction of that site. Infection implies establishment of an infectious agent within or on a host in a biologically meaningful interaction. Disease refers to the disturbance of the host's normal structure or function resulting from the interaction. These distinctions prevent a common error in experimental reasoning: counting bacteria on the surface of an epithelial cell does not directly measure the cell's functional injury. A tissue may contain many microorganisms yet retain substantial physiological capacity, whereas a comparatively small source of a secreted molecule may alter a large area of epithelium. The terms are useful conceptual distinctions rather than rigid thresholds; infection can occur without recognizable symptoms and colonization can precede infection.

A virulence factor is a microbial feature that contributes to disease-causing capacity in a given host context. Adhesion molecules can allow residence at a susceptible site, secretion proteins can redirect host signaling, and surface molecules can provoke inflammatory responses. A factor need not directly destroy a cell to be important. For example, retention at a rapidly flushed epithelial surface can be a necessary upstream event even when the eventual lesion is caused by a different molecule. Conversely, a factor may cause a physiological disturbance in an isolated cell but make little contribution to whole-organism disease if the pathogen cannot reach the relevant tissue. Virulence is therefore a relationship among microbial properties, host properties and the environment rather than a simple list of molecules.

A useful distinction is between a host cell that is susceptible and a host cell that is permissive. Susceptibility in viral infection often means that the cell supports attachment and entry; permissiveness means that intracellular conditions allow productive infection. A receptor may be present without the necessary intracellular support. For bacteria, adhesion and intracellular survival can likewise be separated as biological stages. An animal can develop a particular lesion only when the relevant upstream conditions have been satisfied, or when products originating elsewhere reach the target tissue. In this chapter the distinction is particularly important for cholera, in which a bacterial product enters epithelial cells even though the characteristic disease does not require the bacterium itself to invade them.

Pathogenesis is the causal account linking microbial events to disease. A useful account specifies the anatomical compartment, the host target, the altered process, the resulting tissue lesion and the physiological consequence. Merely naming a receptor or a toxin is not enough. The sequence also contains several points where the host can interrupt the process: physical clearance, antimicrobial activity, removal of infected cells, replacement of injured cells and compensation by other regions of an organ. Some of these responses may themselves cause harm. Removal of infected epithelial cells can restrict microbial spread while temporarily weakening the barrier; recruitment of defensive leukocytes may protect internal tissues while impairing local function. Thus the final condition is a joint outcome, not an exact readout of pathogen number.

The three representative diseases in this chapter exemplify different dominant mechanisms rather than three mutually exclusive categories. Cholera illustrates a largely functional secretory disturbance produced by a microbial molecule. Salmonella enteritis illustrates invasive infection associated with an inflammatory alteration of the intestinal surface. Influenza illustrates viral multiplication in susceptible respiratory epithelial cells together with direct cellular damage and inflammatory injury. In each case, the identity and organization of the affected tissue explain why similar cellular changes may yield different organism-level signs. The intestinal epithelium regulates movement of solutes and water; airway epithelium coordinates defense and air passage; alveolar epithelium forms a thin gas exchange barrier. Comparing them requires knowing what healthy cells normally do.

2. Epithelial architecture and the meaning of tissue dysfunction

An epithelium forms a coherent layer of cells that separates two spaces. In the intestine, the apical surface faces the lumen and the basolateral surface faces neighboring cells and underlying tissue. These surfaces have different sets of membrane proteins. Polarization makes directed transport possible, because solutes can enter through one side of the cell and leave through the other. The tissue is not merely a row of independent membrane vesicles. Tight junctions control movement between adjacent cells and help preserve the membrane domains. Adherens junctions mechanically connect cells through proteins linked to the cytoskeleton. A supporting basement membrane provides attachment and helps maintain tissue organization. Changes in any of these elements can alter the overall physiological performance of the sheet.

Two broad routes carry material across the epithelium. The transcellular route crosses the apical and basolateral membranes of epithelial cells. The paracellular route passes between adjacent cells through a pathway regulated by junctional proteins. Movement along either route depends on the relevant concentration differences, electrical potential differences and permeability. Different solutes encounter different barriers. A tight junction is therefore neither an absolutely impermeable cement nor a freely open gap. Selectivity matters when interpreting disease: increased passage of a small ion need not imply that an intact bacterium can cross, and a change in macromolecular permeability does not identify which membrane transporter has changed activity.

The intestinal surface both absorbs and secretes fluid. It must maintain enough luminal water for contents to move while recovering much of the fluid entering the digestive tract. Absorptive and secretory processes coexist, and their relative rates differ along the intestine and with cell differentiation. Many intestinal transport processes depend indirectly on the basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase. This pump uses ATP to maintain low cytosolic sodium relative to the extracellular fluid. The resulting electrochemical gradient provides energy for secondary active transport, such as coupled sodium and glucose uptake across the apical membrane. Ion channels, by contrast, provide selective routes through which ions can move down their electrochemical gradients. A channel can increase transport enormously without generating the underlying gradient by itself.

A respiratory epithelial cell has a different set of physiological responsibilities. In much of the conducting airway, a layer of mucus traps particles and coordinated ciliary motion carries mucus toward clearance. Secretory cells help maintain the mucus layer and hydration. Tight junctions help separate inhaled material from deeper tissue. Disturbing a cilium or epithelial junction may therefore reduce clearance without immediately stopping gas exchange. In the alveoli, gas transfer depends instead on a very thin epithelial and capillary barrier and on the maintenance of open air spaces. The consequences of injury thus depend on position: an equally severe lesion in two anatomical regions need not have the same physiological effect.

Functional loss can precede structural destruction. An intestinal epithelial cell may remain attached to its neighbors and retain a nucleus and most metabolic activities while a transport pathway has become abnormally active. In contrast, the death or detachment of an airway epithelial cell can leave a physical gap, and failure of neighboring cells to repair the gap may amplify the initial disturbance. Histological appearance is valuable evidence, but neither normal morphology nor visible damage tells the entire story. Functional assays such as directed ion transport, barrier resistance or ciliary motion provide complementary information. The investigator should choose the readout according to the biological role under examination rather than assuming that all epithelial injury is best measured by cell death.

3. Evidence that a microbe can cause disease through different routes

The early recognition of infectious agents answered an important question: microorganisms can be causes of disease rather than simply organisms appearing in damaged tissue. It did not automatically establish which biological action produced each symptom. A particularly revealing distinction emerged in work on cholera. In 1959, Sambhu Nath De reported that a bacteria-free culture filtrate from Vibrio cholerae could cause fluid accumulation in rabbit intestinal tissue. The important conceptual result was that the effect did not require intact bacteria to be present in the affected compartment. The finding supported a secreted factor capable of acting at a distance from its microbial source. It also changed the question from whether bacteria could be detected in injured tissue to which product was responsible for altered physiology.

The finding illustrates the difference between sufficiency and necessity. If a cell-free product can cause a defined effect in a model, it is sufficient for that effect under those model conditions. It does not establish that the product is the only contributor to disease in a naturally infected animal. A whole organism must still encounter the bacterium, allow it to persist at the relevant site and expose its tissues to a biologically effective product. Nor does the result by itself establish a precise intracellular target. Subsequent work on toxin chemistry and epithelial transport was required to connect the observed fluid movement with particular cellular events. Historical demonstrations are best understood as steps that exclude alternative explanations, not as single experiments that reveal every aspect of a pathway.

The recognition of influenza virus likewise separated causation from mere association. Bacterial organisms had previously been suspected as the primary cause of influenza, but in 1933 Wilson Smith, Christopher Andrewes and Patrick Laidlaw identified a human influenza virus using a susceptible animal model. The historical significance is the distinction between an infectious viral agent and bacteria that may accompany respiratory illness. A secondary bacterial infection can complicate a primary viral disease without contradicting the viral origin of that disease. Evidence about etiology must therefore be interpreted in the context of infection stage and tissue damage. Detecting a bacterium late in respiratory illness does not necessarily establish that it initiated the epithelial injury.

Modern cellular studies build upon these distinctions by asking narrower questions. Does a microbial product alter a host signaling pathway before epithelial permeability changes? Is an inflammatory response associated with bacterial entry, with soluble bacterial products, or with both? Does a viral infection reduce tissue function even when the number of viable cells remains relatively high? An association between two measurements is a starting point, not a complete causal demonstration. A convincing explanation requires independent readouts of the proposed upstream event and downstream consequence, comparison to appropriate controls, and consideration of alternative mechanisms. In an examination, an answer that distinguishes these levels of inference is generally more defensible than one that simply repeats the accepted pathway name.

An instructive example from the history of invasive bacterial cell biology is the observation of localized membrane ruffles at sites of Salmonella interaction with host cells. A 1993 study by Jones, Paterson, Hall and Falkow examined signaling associated with such ruffling and distinguished it from the superficially similar response elicited by growth factors. The importance of the comparison is that the same morphological endpoint can be generated by different upstream processes. A ruffled cell membrane is a visible consequence of changes in the cortical cytoskeleton; it is not a molecular identification of the original signal. Later work on bacterial effectors and host signaling provided a more detailed mechanistic account. For this chapter, the historical observation supports the concept of pathogen-induced remodeling and provides a bridge from cellular entry to tissue-level disease. It does not establish that every enteric pathogen uses that particular mechanism, or that all inflammatory injury is caused directly by membrane ruffling.

4. Cholera: noninvasive colonization and secretory dysfunction

The characteristic intestinal disease caused by toxigenic Vibrio cholerae illustrates how a microbe can disrupt an organ while remaining largely outside host cells. The bacterial population occupies the intestinal environment, where successful association with the small intestinal surface allows a secreted toxin to influence nearby epithelium. The site is physiologically important because the small intestine handles a substantial flow of water and electrolytes. The normal balance between secretion and absorption can be reversed without widespread physical destruction of the epithelial sheet. Understanding the disease therefore requires an explanation of transport and cellular signaling rather than an assumption that fluid loss must arise from epithelial lysis.

Cholera toxin belongs to the AB5 family of toxins. Its B component is an assembly of five receptor-binding subunits. Its A component supplies the catalytic activity and includes regions associated with the binding assembly. The host receptor class includes GM1 ganglioside, a glycolipid with a carbohydrate-containing head group exposed at the cell surface. Recognition depends on molecular complementarity between the binding component and the accessible glycolipid. The two parts of the toxin solve different biological problems: contact with the host surface and alteration of an intracellular target. Knowing this structural separation helps explain why detection of toxin binding alone cannot demonstrate that downstream signaling has occurred.

After uptake, a fraction of internalized toxin reaches intracellular compartments along a retrograde transport route toward the endoplasmic reticulum. The catalytic A1 portion ultimately reaches the cytosol, where it encounters its target. This transport sequence is distinct from bacterial invasion. It is the product that crosses cellular barriers, not necessarily the bacterial cell that secreted it. Ordinary epithelial trafficking machinery normally sorts and distributes host material; the toxin illustrates how a foreign molecule can exploit the existence of this machinery. An epithelial cell with bound toxin may not yet display a secretory response if delivery to the cytosolic target has not occurred.

The critical intracellular target is the stimulatory alpha subunit of a heterotrimeric G protein, usually written Gs alpha. In the normal signaling cycle, an activated receptor promotes nucleotide exchange, allowing GTP to replace GDP on the alpha subunit. The GTP-bound state can stimulate adenylyl cyclase. The alpha subunit's own GTP hydrolysis activity normally helps terminate this state, so signaling is transient unless further input sustains it. This arrangement permits a cell to respond to an extracellular signal without making the response permanent. Signal termination is as important as activation because epithelial ion transport must adjust to changing physiological requirements.

The catalytic portion of cholera toxin transfers an ADP-ribose group from NAD to Gs alpha. The modification interferes with normal GTP hydrolysis and consequently prolongs the active signaling state. Adenylyl cyclase remains stimulated and intracellular cyclic AMP rises. Cyclic AMP is a second messenger synthesized from ATP; its concentration reflects both its generation and its degradation by phosphodiesterases. The toxin does not simply provide cyclic AMP from outside or act as a substitute for an extracellular hormone. It changes the duration of activity of a host signaling protein. The resulting persistent signal is transmitted through the cell's own downstream regulatory machinery.

Protein kinase A responds to cyclic AMP and phosphorylates appropriate targets. In intestinal secretory cells, an important downstream target is CFTR, an apical anion channel. Increased channel activity favors secretion of chloride and, depending on context, bicarbonate. The electrochemical driving force for chloride movement is maintained through transport processes on the basolateral side, including the sodium-potassium ATPase and transporters that accumulate chloride. CFTR itself is an ATP-regulated channel, not an ATP-driven chloride pump. ATP binding and hydrolysis influence its opening cycle, but net ionic flux through the open pore follows electrochemical forces.

The increased anion movement changes luminal solute content and associated electrical conditions. Sodium can accompany anion secretion through available epithelial pathways, including paracellular routes. Water moves in response to the resulting osmotic conditions. At the same time elevated cyclic AMP can reduce aspects of sodium absorption. Both an increase in secretion and a decrease in absorption can move the net balance toward luminal accumulation. In a healthy intestinal tract these fluxes are coordinated and compensatory recovery occurs in downstream regions; in severe secretory dysfunction that compensatory capacity is exceeded. The physiological consequence is a large loss of fluid and salts from the animal's extracellular fluid compartment.

This chain is instructive because the affected transport proteins are ordinary host molecules. The pathogen does not need to construct its own epithelial ion channel or pump water into the gut directly. Its toxin changes the control signal that governs host transport. Amplification occurs because an enzymatic change in a signaling component affects production of many second-messenger molecules and because sustained channel activity can allow continued ionic flux. The amount of water lost need not correspond in a simple one-to-one ratio with the amount of bacterial material present. The relationship depends on the spatial extent, duration and intensity of epithelial signaling, together with intestinal absorptive reserve.

5. The physiology behind secretory diarrhea

To make sense of toxin-mediated intestinal disease, consider a small region of normal polarized epithelium. The word secretion describes net transfer from the tissue side toward the intestinal lumen. Absorption describes net transfer in the opposite direction. Individual ions and water molecules move in both directions at the same time, and it is the difference between the opposing flows that determines the net result. A large gross absorptive rate can coexist with a substantial gross secretory rate. A disturbance of one rate may therefore be physiologically important even if the other rate remains unchanged. A student who looks only at the direction of a single transporter may miss the contribution of parallel pathways in determining net fluid movement across the whole surface.

Consider sodium and chloride separately. The concentration of sodium is normally lower inside many animal cells than in extracellular fluid. This difference is maintained by the sodium-potassium ATPase, which exchanges three intracellular sodium ions for two extracellular potassium ions per catalytic cycle. The pump maintains an electrochemical gradient, not merely a concentration gradient. The inside of many epithelial cells is electrically negative relative to the extracellular compartment. Both electrical and chemical contributions influence the entry of positively charged sodium. Potassium permeability and other conductances contribute to the membrane potential, so a complete numerical prediction of epithelial voltage requires information about several transport pathways. The exam-level principle is that a gradient maintained by active transport can support other processes that do not themselves directly hydrolyze ATP.

A common basolateral transport route in secretory epithelial cells is sodium-potassium-two chloride cotransport. The cotransporter moves these ions together, with a stoichiometry that has no net transported electrical charge per completed cycle. Because the transport process couples the movements, the energetic favorability of one ion's gradient can compensate for the energetically unfavorable movement of another. Under appropriate physiological conditions, chloride accumulates within the secretory cell above the concentration expected from its passive equilibrium alone. Basolateral potassium conductances help maintain suitable electrical conditions. The arrangement creates a store of electrochemical potential that can be dissipated when apical chloride channels open. This explains why the direction of chloride movement cannot be inferred from the negative charge of chloride alone.

An open ion channel provides a route, not a predetermined direction of movement. Its current depends on both conductance and the difference between membrane voltage and the ion's equilibrium potential. A change in channel opening probability can therefore alter ion movement even when ion concentrations remain initially constant. On a longer timescale, movement of ions changes the gradients that originally drove the movement, and other transport proteins respond to maintain or re-establish them. The immediate cellular response and the steady physiological response are distinct. The continuous supply of ions from the basolateral side helps explain how sustained activation of an apical secretory conductance can continue to influence luminal fluid rather than ending immediately after a small intracellular store is depleted.

Water movement is coupled to solute movement because the chemical potential of water changes with the concentration of dissolved substances. In a simplified setting, water tends to move toward the side with higher effective osmotic concentration, provided the barrier is sufficiently permeable to water. The adjective effective matters because a solute that crosses a membrane freely may not sustain a persistent osmotic gradient across it. An epithelium is not an ideal semipermeable membrane, and its water permeability and junctional selectivity vary along its length. Hydrostatic pressure differences can also contribute to net movement. Nevertheless, when solutes accumulate on the luminal side and the epithelial routes allow water movement, secretion of electrolytes can be accompanied by substantial water secretion.

One might imagine that the lumen must become extremely hypertonic if the toxin drives a large volume of fluid into it. That conclusion is not required. Water can follow solutes rapidly, producing a large increase in fluid volume without a correspondingly large persistent difference in osmolarity. The relevant distinction is between the rate at which solutes and water are transported and the concentrations observed after water movement has partially equilibrated the system. A concentration measurement taken at a single time cannot by itself quantify cumulative fluid flux. A large volume of nearly isotonic secretion may remove a considerable quantity of dissolved salt and water from the extracellular compartment despite having an osmolarity similar to that of the originating fluid.

The continued presence of absorptive mechanisms is another essential detail. Sodium-glucose cotransport couples entry of glucose and sodium across the absorptive apical membrane. The sodium gradient supplies a driving force, and glucose can subsequently exit toward the tissue side through facilitative transporters. Water absorption is favored when solute transfer produces appropriate osmotic conditions. The mechanism is distinct from cyclic AMP-dependent chloride secretion, even though both processes are influenced by the general cellular ionic environment. Therefore a high secretory rate does not logically imply that every absorptive process has ceased. The simultaneous existence of secretion and absorption is the cell-biological principle behind the physiological observation that absorptive function can persist in secretory intestinal disease. The point here is the independence of transport routes, not a clinical treatment regimen.

At the level of the animal, extracellular fluid contains plasma and interstitial fluid. Sustained fluid transfer from tissue into the intestinal lumen, followed by loss from the organism, reduces the extracellular fluid reservoir if replacement does not match loss. Circulating blood volume and tissue perfusion may then fall. Electrolyte changes can disturb other organ functions because membrane potentials, cellular volume regulation and biochemical reactions depend on ion concentrations. These secondary effects do not require bacterial cells to colonize the organs that exhibit physiological disturbances. They follow from the dependence of the entire organism on a shared internal fluid environment. Cholera is a particularly clear example of a localized initiating interaction with potentially extensive systemic consequences.

A quantitative balance clarifies why compensation may initially conceal injury. Let S denote gross secretion into a defined intestinal compartment in liters per hour and A denote gross absorption from that compartment in the same units. If other inputs and outputs are ignored for the moment, the rate of change of its fluid volume is S minus A. If the two rates are equal, volume remains constant even though substantial exchange takes place. When secretion exceeds absorption by a small amount, accumulation is slow; when the difference increases, accumulation is rapid. The model deliberately treats each rate as constant over a selected interval, although in real physiology both may vary with volume, luminal composition and intestinal position. It is useful for interpreting controlled teaching datasets, not for estimating disease severity from a single patient's symptoms.

The distinction between gross and net movement also applies to measured ion current. Suppose a cell secretes more chloride while absorbing less sodium, but a second cell type simultaneously increases an oppositely directed transport process. An electrical measurement across the tissue may not show the full magnitude of either change separately because currents can partially offset each other. Direct ion flux measurements and electrophysiological measurements answer related but nonidentical questions. Electroneutral transport can also change salt and water movement without contributing directly to net electrical current. A correct mechanistic interpretation therefore avoids equating a single measured current with the complete rate of intestinal fluid secretion.

The relationship between membrane voltage and ion concentration can be stated more formally. Define membrane potential V as the electrical potential inside a cell minus the potential outside. For an ion of charge number z, the electrochemical free-energy change associated with moving one mole of that ion from the inside to the outside is RT ln(Cout/Cin) − zFV, where R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, F is Faraday's constant, and C denotes the relevant ion concentration in an ideal dilute solution. A negative value favors that direction of passive movement. At equilibrium, the expression is zero. Rearranging produces the Nernst equilibrium potential E = (RT/zF) ln(Cout/Cin). The sign of z is crucial for an anion such as chloride. The equilibrium potential is a property of an ion's concentration ratio under these assumptions, not a measurement of the actual membrane voltage in a cell containing several permeable ionic species.

For a simplified channel with approximately linear current-voltage behavior, the electrical current can be expressed as I = g(V − E), using the usual convention in which outward positive-charge current is positive. A chloride flux and its conventional electrical current have opposite directional signs because chloride carries negative charge. Students should therefore specify whether a graph represents ionic particle movement or conventional current. The equation also distinguishes a change in conductance, g, from a change in driving force, V − E. If channel activity increases while the driving force remains briefly unchanged, the magnitude of the ionic current increases. In a living secretory cell, sustained transport can alter concentrations and membrane potential, so the assumption of a fixed driving force is most defensible over a limited comparison interval. This is why a current measurement must be interpreted in its physiological setting rather than as an invariant measure of channel number.

A further consequence of polarized transport is that a change in one membrane can depend on compensating activity at another membrane. The apical exit of chloride cannot maintain a stable intracellular chloride concentration indefinitely unless uptake and other losses balance it. A high apical conductance may therefore produce different sustained responses in cells with different basolateral transport capacities. This principle is not specific to infection; it is a conservation requirement for any transporting epithelial cell. The same reasoning explains why a model of an isolated membrane cannot fully predict net transepithelial transport. It may accurately characterize the channel while omitting the processes that supply its ions, establish voltage and determine the behavior of neighboring cells. Physiological models should preserve the distinction between a membrane-level flux and the net flux across an organized tissue.

6. Signal duration, biochemical specificity and regulation

The toxin-mediated signaling lesion provides a general lesson about signal transduction: a protein can be abnormal even when its concentration remains normal. Consider the G protein activation cycle. In the absence of a sustained activating stimulus, GDP-bound Gs alpha is associated with its partner subunits in a state that does not stimulate its principal effector strongly. Receptor activation favors nucleotide exchange. GTP binding changes the conformation and interaction properties of the alpha subunit. Its activated state is terminated through hydrolysis of GTP to GDP and inorganic phosphate. The energy of hydrolysis is linked to a molecular switch that changes the lifetime of the signal. The cycle allows repeated brief activation events and preserves the capacity for future stimulation.

When a covalent modification changes the rate of termination of an active signaling state, the cell's response may persist after the original extracellular event has ended. In the cholera model, Gs alpha is modified, so the change is not simply another round of transient receptor stimulation. The relevant comparison is between switching on a normal pathway and impairing its switch-off mechanism. An observation of elevated cyclic AMP cannot distinguish these possibilities without additional evidence. Excessive receptor activation, excessive cyclase activity, defective cyclic AMP degradation and abnormal G protein termination could all increase the abundance of the same second messenger. The identity of the primary lesion must be inferred from the ordered relationships among these molecular events, not from cyclic AMP concentration alone.

The role of cyclic AMP is to link an upstream regulatory protein with downstream cellular targets. Adenylyl cyclase forms cyclic AMP from ATP, while phosphodiesterases hydrolyze cyclic AMP to noncyclic products. The cellular concentration of the messenger represents a dynamic balance between synthesis and removal. A simple analogy is the volume in a container with an inlet and an outlet: even if the inlet remains open at a fixed rate, the level can eventually become constant when output matches input. At steady state, production has not stopped. The equality of rates is why a constant elevated cyclic AMP concentration can coexist with sustained stimulation. In a real cell, compartmentalization and local enzyme activity complicate the simple well-mixed model, but the balance principle remains applicable.

The action of protein kinase A introduces another layer of regulation. Cyclic AMP binds regulatory components of the kinase system and permits catalytic activity toward appropriate substrates. Phosphorylation can influence protein conformation, interactions, localization or transport activity. It does not imply that all phosphorylated proteins become more active; the effect depends on the substrate and regulatory site. CFTR provides an example where phosphorylation promotes channel activity in conjunction with nucleotide-dependent gating. Thus the molecular chain has multiple distinct kinds of biochemical event: ligand binding, vesicular trafficking, covalent modification of a signaling protein, production of a second messenger, phosphorylation and regulated channel opening. Collapsing these into the single statement that a toxin opens a channel obscures the actual causal sequence.

It is valuable to separate binding specificity from catalytic specificity. The B component of cholera toxin recognizes accessible cell-surface glycans, while the catalytic component has biochemical specificity for a host signaling target. A binding event brings the molecule into a cellular context in which catalytic action may occur. The existence of a receptor therefore does not guarantee the complete downstream response. Target availability, intracellular delivery, and the physiological role of the affected pathway matter. In a cell lacking an appropriate apical secretory channel, elevated cyclic AMP could have other effects but would not automatically generate the same chloride secretory phenotype. This is one reason why studying signaling in a nonpolarized cell line does not fully substitute for studying the function of a polarized intestinal epithelial sheet.

The toxin response also illustrates amplification without requiring a new signaling pathway. A catalytic reaction can affect regulatory proteins; a signaling protein can influence the formation of many messenger molecules over its active lifetime; and a regulated conductance can permit movement of many ions. At every stage the amount of downstream material need not equal the amount of upstream material. The amplification is not infinite, because each stage has a finite capacity. The number of target molecules is limited, enzyme rates are constrained, and transport gradients can be altered by sustained flux. Feedback regulation, protein turnover, trafficking and messenger degradation can all influence how long the effect persists. A conceptual model should therefore predict strong amplification without implying an unlimited linear response to toxin concentration.

An experiment that measures normal CFTR abundance after toxin exposure is entirely compatible with increased chloride secretion. Protein abundance and protein activity answer different questions. A similar distinction holds for Gs alpha: a measurement of total protein does not establish its nucleotide state or rate of hydrolysis. An examination may present a table in which total protein levels remain unchanged while cyclic AMP and epithelial current increase. The appropriate inference is that activity or regulatory state can change without altered abundance. It would be unjustified to conclude that gene expression never changes during infection; the narrower conclusion is that a transcriptional increase is not required to explain the particular observed response. This distinction helps students connect rapid post-translational regulation with the slower changes associated with tissue adaptation.

Because the toxin-mediated lesion involves normal host signaling, there is also a boundary between cellular physiology and a disease-specific mechanism. Cyclic AMP is not intrinsically pathological. It participates in numerous ordinary physiological responses in many tissues. CFTR is not a microbial protein and its normal activity contributes to epithelial function. Disease emerges when the timing, magnitude and localization of their activity no longer match physiological demand. This is a general theme of host manipulation: a pathogen may alter the control of a healthy process rather than destroy the machinery itself. It also explains why a molecular signal can have different biological meanings in different tissues, depending on the downstream proteins and tissue architecture present.

7. Salmonella enteritis: epithelial invasion and inflammation

An invasive intestinal pathogen can affect the same organ by a different route. Nontyphoidal Salmonella enterica, represented here by the serovar Typhimurium, can interact with intestinal epithelial cells and underlying immune cells. Gastroenteritis is a major disease pattern, but outcomes vary with serovar and host conditions; the same broad bacterial group includes agents associated with distinct systemic syndromes. The present discussion concerns the inflammatory intestinal mechanism, not a classification of clinical infections. The useful contrast with cholera is that disruption of epithelial integrity and recruitment of inflammatory cells participate prominently in the lesion.

Invasion involves crossing the boundary between the extracellular environment and a host cell. An epithelial cell is not normally a professional phagocyte, yet Salmonella can induce cytoskeletal rearrangements that facilitate uptake. A type III secretion system can transfer bacterial effector proteins into host cells, where the effectors influence regulators of actin dynamics. The epithelial plasma membrane changes shape as cortical actin is reorganized. This illustrates the trigger model of uptake introduced in the preceding chapters. The distinction from simple attachment is that membrane remodeling now provides a pathway to an intracellular compartment. An attached bacterium is therefore not necessarily an internalized bacterium, even if microscopy shows intimate contact with the membrane.

Once internalized, Salmonella is commonly associated with a membrane-bound compartment, the Salmonella-containing vacuole. The existence of the vacuole separates uptake from subsequent intracellular survival. A cell may internalize a bacterium successfully yet eliminate it later; conversely, failure of intracellular clearance may prolong a local infectious stimulus. Bacterial effectors and host trafficking pathways can influence the compartment's fate. The cellular consequences of infection depend on these interactions and on the cell type. A macrophage is already equipped to engulf particles and kill microbes, whereas an epithelial cell has a major role in barrier and transport physiology. The significance of intracellular residence differs accordingly.

Microbial molecules and epithelial disturbances can activate host inflammatory responses. Epithelial cells produce signals that recruit leukocytes from underlying tissue. Neutrophils, among other cells, can accumulate and move toward the affected epithelial surface. Their activity can restrain bacteria, but released enzymes, reactive species and changes accompanying cell migration can also affect tissue integrity. Inflammation changes vascular permeability and tissue fluid handling. Epithelial barrier function and solute transport may be altered, contributing to intestinal fluid accumulation. Therefore the characteristic lesion is not explained exclusively by mechanical entry of bacteria or exclusively by a single secreted toxin.

This is a situation in which defensive function and physiological harm must be distinguished. Neutrophil recruitment can limit microbial spread and may nonetheless worsen local epithelial disturbance. Reduced inflammation is not automatically a successful outcome if it permits a larger microbial burden later. Similarly, intense inflammation is not automatically evidence of effective protection if tissue integrity is severely impaired. A student analyzing an experimental comparison should examine bacterial localization, viable burden, inflammatory indicators and epithelial functional readouts together. Changes in one variable can have different meanings at different times in infection.

The intestinal manifestation depends on both local injury and compensatory capacity. Pain can accompany inflammatory changes in tissue, and fever can arise from systemic signaling even when most viable bacteria remain in the gastrointestinal tract. Systemic symptoms must not be confused with systemic dissemination. The latter implies spread of infectious organisms to sites beyond the primary compartment. An intestinal infection can produce signaling molecules that act at distant sites without bacteria being recoverable from those sites. Conversely, actual dissemination can occur in particular host and microbial contexts. A causal explanation needs evidence about both location and physiological consequences.

8. The intestinal inflammatory lesion in greater depth

The epithelial lining of the intestine is exposed to abundant microbial molecules even in health. This fact creates a special problem for host defense. An indiscriminate response to everything present in the lumen would interfere with normal digestion and epithelial function. The host therefore maintains boundaries that limit access of microorganisms to internal tissue and regulate the conditions under which inflammatory responses occur. Mucus, epithelial polarity, junctions and controlled communication with underlying cells are important parts of this organization. When considering an invasive pathogen, the starting point is not a sterile intestinal lumen. It is a spatially organized tissue in which contact, internalization and access to deeper compartments have different meanings.

One useful anatomical term is lamina propria, the supporting connective tissue beneath an epithelial layer. It contains vessels and immune cells and participates in exchanges between epithelium and the rest of the organism. A microorganism on the luminal surface is separated from this compartment by an epithelial barrier. Crossing that barrier can expose microbial molecules to cells that were previously shielded from them. Consequently, the host response may change qualitatively even if the total number of microbial particles in the organ changes little. Measuring total material in an intestinal specimen does not specify whether it came from luminal contents, epithelial cells or the tissue beneath the epithelium. Spatial information is essential in explaining the significance of a bacterial interaction.

Specialized epithelial regions complicate the picture. Microfold cells, often called M cells, participate in transport of material from the intestinal lumen toward underlying lymphoid tissue. Their normal role is related to sampling, not to indiscriminate destruction of the epithelial barrier. In discussions of Salmonella, such sites illustrate that different epithelial cell types do not present equivalent routes or consequences of contact. Ordinary absorptive cells, specialized sampling cells and professional phagocytes differ in morphology, trafficking and biological function. A simplified diagram of a uniform intestinal cell sheet is useful for introducing epithelial polarity, but it cannot be interpreted as evidence that every cell in the intestine behaves in the same way during infection.

Inflammation involves changes in local tissue signaling and the distribution and activity of defensive cells. Epithelial cells can signal the presence of microbial disturbance to surrounding cells, and cells in supporting tissue can contribute further signals. Soluble mediators can attract leukocytes or change the behavior of nearby vascular cells. Chemotaxis is directed cell movement in response to chemical cues. It is distinct from a simple increase in random motility. In a tissue, directed recruitment involves multiple steps, including movement through vascular boundaries and migration within tissue. These processes allow leukocytes to accumulate in the region where the disturbance occurs, making local defensive activity greater than it would be if the cells remained distributed uniformly.

Recruitment has both benefits and costs. A neutrophil can eliminate bacteria through several mechanisms, and a recruited population can help contain an infection. However, some of the molecules and physical events associated with defensive activity can also affect host structures. When leukocytes move through an epithelial layer, their passage occurs in the same tissue whose normal function depends on controlled permeability. If injury is extensive, junctional organization and transport become disturbed. The presence of neutrophils in a histological section therefore supports an inflammatory component of disease but cannot establish, without further evidence, how much of the functional damage came from bacteria themselves and how much came from the host response.

The tissue fluid environment is also altered in inflammation. Increases in vascular permeability allow plasma components and fluid to enter supporting tissue more readily. The consequences include tissue swelling and changes in the chemical conditions around cells. At the epithelial surface, altered junctional selectivity and transport processes can contribute to movement of fluid into the lumen. Intestinal inflammation may also change motility, limiting the time available for recovery of fluid and electrolytes. These effects are distinct from a purely secretory mechanism even though both types of disease can result in diarrhea. The final symptom is shared because both routes disrupt the net handling of fluid, not because their initiating molecular lesions are identical.

A useful histological comparison begins with the question of what a tissue section can reveal. It can show the organization of cells, the location of inflammatory infiltrates and regions where normal tissue architecture is disrupted. Depending on the staining method, it may show loss of epithelial continuity, altered cell shape or the presence of particular microbial or host molecules. Histology is less direct as a measure of transport activity. A cell with a recognizable apical surface may still have abnormal ion flux. A tissue with focal lesions may retain substantial overall absorption. The functional importance of a lesion depends on its extent, distribution and the ability of surviving regions to compensate. Therefore even an accurate morphological description should be connected with an appropriate physiological measurement before strong claims about organ function are made.

In the classical comparison between secretory diarrhea and inflammatory diarrhea, secretory disease is associated mainly with altered epithelial transport, whereas inflammatory disease includes disturbance of the epithelial barrier and immune-cell recruitment. This contrast is useful but not absolute. Infection-associated inflammation can alter secretory pathways, and a predominantly toxin-mediated disease need not be completely free of all host inflammatory responses. The classification identifies a dominant explanatory mechanism. It is not a definition based solely on the appearance of symptoms. The presence of diarrhea by itself does not distinguish a transport lesion from an inflammatory lesion; that distinction requires evidence about how the tissue has changed.

The distinction has consequences for the interpretation of a laboratory dataset. Consider a set of hypothetical tissue observations in which bacterial material is localized mainly to the epithelial surface, intracellular cyclic AMP is increased, fluid secretion is substantial, and inflammatory-cell accumulation is modest. This pattern is consistent with a dominant transport disturbance. In a different specimen, abundant inflammatory cells and disrupted epithelial continuity are observed with a relatively modest change in cyclic AMP. An invasive or inflammatory mechanism is more consistent with that second pattern, but the observations do not alone identify a particular bacterial species. Many different agents can perturb an epithelial barrier. The evidence supports a class of mechanism, which is a narrower and more defensible conclusion than naming an etiological agent without appropriate identification.

The outcome of enteritis also depends on epithelial renewal. Intestinal epithelial cells are continuously replaced from proliferative compartments. Newly generated cells differentiate and acquire the characteristic membrane proteins and junctional properties required for normal intestinal function. If injury outpaces repair, exposed areas and dysfunctional regions can persist even when the initiating microbial stimulus is decreasing. Conversely, rapid restoration of tissue continuity can reduce the functional consequences of a focal lesion. The time dependence of damage and repair helps explain why measurements made at different stages of an infection can appear inconsistent when interpreted without attention to the biological sequence.

The terms pathogen-associated and damage-associated molecular patterns distinguish the origins of signals that can contribute to inflammation. Microbial molecules can indicate the presence of an organism, while molecules released or exposed during host cellular damage can indicate disruption of normal tissue integrity. Recognition pathways for these signals are treated in detail in immunology; the important connection here is that inflammatory activity can arise from both the initiating microbial encounter and the injury that follows. As a result, the strength of an inflammatory signal need not vary in direct proportion to viable bacterial number. Cellular injury may create additional stimuli even while the original infectious burden is falling. This provides a biological reason to distinguish amplification of a host response from multiplication of the microorganism. It also provides an alternative interpretation when an injury marker and a microbial marker do not follow identical time courses.

A distinction is also needed between a cellular pathway that is necessary in one model and a pathway that is universally necessary in natural disease. A particular epithelial response may be a major contributor in an experimental system that contains only epithelial cells. In an animal, several resident and recruited cell populations may produce overlapping signals. An observation showing that one host signaling route is absent in a simplified preparation does not establish that all inflammation disappears in the organism. Functional redundancy can preserve one output even when one contributing route is interrupted. For NET-level reasoning, a question that explicitly assumes a single linear pathway permits a direct prediction, whereas a question involving an intact tissue must be interpreted with attention to parallel inputs. This is a biological limitation of simple pathway diagrams, not a reason to abandon their use.

9. Influenza: respiratory epithelial infection and cytopathic injury

Influenza virus illustrates disease in a tissue whose major role is not nutrient absorption. The conducting respiratory tract continually receives particles from inhaled air. The airway surface liquid, mucus and motile cilia constitute an important clearance system. Infection of cells in this system can alter physical removal of material, expose deeper tissue and change local communication among epithelial and immune cells. In more severe disease, involvement of distal air spaces can compromise the alveolar exchange surface. The consequences of viral infection therefore reflect the location of affected cells and the physiological reserve of the respiratory tract.

The influenza virion is surrounded by a lipid envelope carrying viral glycoproteins. Hemagglutinin recognizes appropriate sialylated host surface glycans and participates in membrane fusion after entry by endocytosis. The presence of a compatible attachment structure is only one condition for infection. Intracellular conditions also affect whether the incoming genome can support production of progeny. Viral multiplication involves recruitment of host cellular resources and changes in cellular activity. The emphasis here is on the resulting epithelial lesion rather than a complete viral replication cycle.

A cytopathic effect is a recognizable change in a cell associated with viral infection. In respiratory epithelium, infected cells can become dysfunctional, undergo cell death or detach from the epithelial layer. Changes may also occur in cells that are not themselves productively infected, because local inflammatory signals and the altered tissue environment affect neighboring cells. Loss of ciliated cells or impairment of their movement reduces mucociliary transport. This weakens a clearance mechanism that normally protects against many inhaled microbes, not just influenza. Secondary bacterial disease may therefore arise from a respiratory tract already altered by viral infection. The concept is a sequential interaction between infections, not a claim that influenza virus is a bacterium.

The respiratory immune response adds another layer of causation. Host defenses restrict viral spread and help eliminate infected cells, while inflammatory signaling and cellular recruitment may also disturb epithelial and vascular barriers. The relative contribution of direct viral injury and host-mediated injury varies by time, location and host context. For this reason, a measured viral load does not uniquely determine disease severity. A tissue may remain impaired after infectious viral particles decline, because lost cells, edema and altered tissue structure need time to resolve. Conversely, an early infection can have substantial viral replication before extensive inflammatory injury is apparent.

Gas exchange illustrates a direct link from microscopic structure to organ function. In an alveolus, oxygen passes from air across a thin tissue barrier toward blood. Fluid accumulation in air spaces can reduce the area effectively exposed to air and increase the distance across which oxygen must diffuse. Inflammatory injury may also disturb the coordinated function of alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial cells. Disturbance of the airway or exchange surface is not equivalent to a simple blockage of a molecular receptor. It is a change in an integrated physiological system. A useful examination answer connects the relevant epithelial lesion to its regional function and distinguishes gas exchange impairment from mere detection of viral molecules.

Repair is part of the disease trajectory. Epithelial progenitor populations and surviving neighboring cells contribute to re-establishment of tissue continuity, but the restoration of a specialized function can lag behind closure of a physical gap. Newly covering cells may not yet have normal differentiation, junctions or coordinated ciliary function. Therefore recovery of one readout cannot be assumed to represent complete recovery of all other readouts. The interval between microbial control and restoration of organ function is a general principle shared by many infectious diseases.

10. Respiratory injury from cilia to gas exchange

The respiratory tract illustrates how an infection can damage a tissue that relies on highly specialized epithelial differentiation. A motile cilium is a cellular projection supported by an organized microtubule-based axoneme. Its motion depends on coordinated molecular motor activity and an appropriate extracellular environment. Individual cilia generate directed movement at the tissue surface because their orientation and beating patterns are coordinated across many cells. The transport of mucus is an emergent property of the tissue rather than the action of one isolated cilium. Consequently, damage affecting a fraction of the epithelial cells can impair clearance through disruption of continuity and coordination even when the remaining individual cilia are structurally normal.

Mucus is not simply a layer of unwanted material. Its properties allow particles to be trapped while the ciliated surface transports the material away. Efficient transport depends on hydration, viscosity, epithelial surface geometry and coordinated motion. A change in mucus volume or consistency can therefore alter clearance even without loss of cilia. Similarly, a change in ciliary function can impair clearance without a large change in mucus production. In a viral disease, these variables may change together. If a study reports slower movement of a particle on an infected epithelial culture, several components of the clearance apparatus could have contributed. Measurement of ciliary beat frequency alone cannot completely explain transport through the tissue.

Respiratory epithelial cells have junctional structures that maintain the boundary between luminal and underlying compartments. Loss of one cell can create a local discontinuity, but neighboring cells may temporarily rearrange to cover the exposed region. The permeability of the tissue can therefore change dynamically. A microscopic image of detached cells at one time may indicate damage but cannot establish whether the barrier will recover rapidly or whether deeper tissue has already been affected. Examination questions sometimes use two separate readouts, one for viability and another for transepithelial barrier function. The reason is that a viable sheet with altered junctions and a partly denuded sheet may both be permeable, even though the underlying biological mechanisms differ.

Influenza-associated cellular injury occurs within this specialized structure. Virus-related changes in infected cells may impair their normal activities before they die. Some host processes are redirected toward viral reproduction, and stress signals may activate pathways associated with programmed cell death. A cell that undergoes apoptosis may be eliminated in a regulated manner, whereas other forms of injury can involve loss of membrane integrity and release of intracellular material. The detailed molecular classification of cell death belongs to earlier cell biology and immunity chapters; here the important point is that elimination of an infected cell can both remove a site of viral production and create a temporary deficit in tissue function. Cell death has different consequences when it affects a replaceable epithelial cell than when it is widespread throughout a thin exchange surface.

The term cytopathic effect refers to changes associated with infection, commonly observed in cellular systems. It is not equivalent to a direct measurement of infectious particles. Cell rounding, altered morphology, detachment or loss of normal specialized function may accompany infection, but each of these changes can also have nonviral causes. Conversely, production of viral particles can occur before dramatic morphological change is evident. If an examination asks whether the absence of visible cytopathic changes proves that infection has not occurred, the correct response is no. The inference depends on what was measured and the time of observation. Independent evidence of viral replication or viral material may be required, with careful distinction between noninfectious remnants and infectious particles.

The immune response in the respiratory tract can extend the zone of physiological disturbance beyond the initially infected cells. Soluble signaling molecules affect nearby cells and blood vessels. Recruited immune cells can remove infected material while changing the local extracellular environment. Some neighboring cells may become dysfunctional even without containing viral replication products. This is an important distinction between a cell-autonomous effect, arising within the infected cell itself, and a non-cell-autonomous effect mediated by surrounding cells or soluble signals. Both contribute to tissue-level disease. Observing a lesion in an uninfected cell does not necessarily contradict a viral cause of the disease, because the viral infection may have initiated an indirect causal sequence.

The consequences of lower respiratory involvement differ from those in the conducting airway. Alveoli are small air spaces that collectively provide a large exchange area. Their epithelial lining includes thin type I alveolar cells and type II cells, which contribute to surfactant production and epithelial repair. The epithelial barrier is closely associated with the capillary circulation. Under normal conditions, the organization minimizes the distance for diffusion while keeping air and liquid compartments separate. A substantial inflammatory disturbance in this region can alter permeability, allow fluid to accumulate and reduce the amount of area available for effective exchange. Injury to cells involved in surfactant maintenance can further disturb the stability of air spaces. These structural consequences can matter even when the molecular event that initiated infection is no longer occurring at the same intensity.

Surfactant reduces surface tension at the air-liquid interface. This function is important because the alveolar surface contains a thin liquid lining. If surface tension is excessive, maintenance of open air spaces becomes more difficult. The relationship between surfactant and tissue mechanics is more complex than the behavior of a single spherical bubble, because alveoli are interconnected structures supported by surrounding tissue. Nevertheless, the principle helps explain why damage to a specialized secretory cell population can influence gas exchange through a physical change in the organ. It is an example of indirect dysfunction: the virus need not bind oxygen or chemically block its diffusion to impair delivery of oxygen to the blood.

The respiratory system also relies on matching airflow to blood flow. Regions that receive air but little blood and regions that receive blood but little air contribute differently to gas exchange. A local lesion can therefore have a systemic consequence that depends on the distribution of ventilation and perfusion, not just the total number of affected epithelial cells. When considering hypothetical diagrams of normal and injured lung, students should identify which physical limitation has changed: diffusion distance, effective surface area, airflow, perfusion or some combination. Such interpretation avoids attributing every reduction in oxygen exchange exclusively to the molecular process of viral entry.

A second bacterial infection can arise after viral disease because the normal protective environment has changed. Impaired mucociliary clearance is one plausible contributor, and epithelial disruption or altered host defensive function may also play roles. The initial respiratory infection and the subsequent bacterial complication are distinct events. Demonstrating bacteria in a later lesion does not establish that the original illness was caused by those bacteria, nor does prior detection of influenza virus prove that all later damage is caused exclusively by viral replication. The temporal order of observations, anatomical localization and evidence of active infection are needed to separate initiating and secondary processes. This example reinforces why causal reasoning in infection is stronger when it accounts for changes in the host tissue over time.

11. Comparing lesion types, local effects and systemic consequences

The distinction between local and systemic disease is equally important. Local injury originates within a restricted tissue or anatomical compartment. Systemic consequences are changes in the organism beyond that compartment. Intestinal fluid loss can lower circulating volume and alter electrolyte balance without microbial invasion of the bloodstream. Locally produced inflammatory signals may alter distant physiological processes. Respiratory damage can reduce oxygen availability to tissues that are not infected. Thus a distant physiological effect is not proof of distant microbial localization. Demonstrating dissemination requires evidence about the infectious agent itself in the additional compartment.

A related distinction is between tissue tropism and tissue vulnerability. Tropism concerns the types of tissues or cells in which an infectious agent can establish relevant interactions. Vulnerability concerns how much physiological loss results when those tissues are disturbed. A cell population may be highly susceptible yet replaceable, whereas damage to a more restricted population can produce disproportionately large functional consequences. The thin alveolar exchange barrier, for example, has little tolerance for widespread accumulation of fluid. Conversely, intestinal fluid handling can compensate for modest shifts in transport until capacity is exceeded. The relationship between cellular damage and organism-level dysfunction need not be linear.

In comparative animal biology, the underlying signaling and membrane transport principles are broadly conserved among vertebrates, but receptor accessibility, tissue architecture, immune responses and physiological reserve can differ. Consequently, a lesion described in an animal model should not be treated as identical to a human disease in all respects. Model systems are useful because they isolate or represent parts of a causal sequence. Their limitations become important when a result is extrapolated beyond the specific tissue and host in which it was observed. This is why a convincing examination answer often states both what a study establishes and what it does not establish.

12. Tissue specificity, physiological reserve and animal variation

The concept of tropism is often introduced using a receptor-ligand interaction. That is a useful foundation, but an animal is not simply a collection of cells expressing different receptor genes. The accessibility of a receptor depends on the anatomical organization of a tissue and the polarity of its cells. A molecule on a basolateral surface may not be readily accessible from the lumen when junctions are intact. A surface glycan may occur in several cellular environments, yet the consequences of recognition can differ because the cells have distinct intracellular components. The relevant context includes where contact occurs, whether entry is supported and what happens after the first interaction. This distinction applies to both the bacterially produced toxin and the viral attachment system, while the specific molecular events remain different.

Another feature of tropism is that the site of microbial residence and the site of physiological action may not be identical. In toxin-mediated disease, the producer and target can occupy different compartments. In invasive disease, the primary site of bacterial interaction may be accompanied by inflammatory changes in neighboring tissue. In respiratory viral disease, the distribution of injured cells may exceed the distribution of cells supporting productive infection. Consequently, one must distinguish a map of microorganism localization, a map of microbial product distribution and a map of tissue injury. These maps may overlap, but there is no general requirement that they be identical.

Physiological reserve refers to the ability of a system to sustain function despite disturbance. Organs often have parallel pathways, spatial redundancy and homeostatic compensation. A change in a limited intestinal region can be partly compensated by transport in other regions. An airway may remain sufficiently open despite a small focal epithelial lesion. Such compensation can make early cellular injury difficult to infer from whole-organism measurements. However, reserve is finite. Once the extent or intensity of injury exceeds compensatory capacity, organ-level function may decline more rapidly. This produces a nonlinear relationship between the fraction of cells affected and the severity of a physiological outcome.

The nonlinear relationship also explains why equal relative changes at the molecular level cannot be assumed to cause equal organism-level changes. A twofold increase in epithelial cyclic AMP does not necessarily double net fluid loss, because downstream responses can saturate and because absorption continues. A twofold increase in an inflammatory marker does not establish a twofold increase in tissue damage, because inflammatory signaling is not the only determinant of injury. A twofold increase in viral RNA abundance does not establish a twofold change in gas exchange, because replication, localization, epithelial injury and respiratory mechanics intervene. A quantitative relationship is useful only if the variables and assumptions are clearly defined.

The three cases also demonstrate different relationships between energy supply and disease. Intestinal transport depends on ATP-dependent maintenance of ionic gradients. The toxin-mediated secretory response exploits those gradients rather than creating water movement independently of host energetics. In inflammatory enteritis, defensive cells and epithelial repair require energy and biosynthetic resources, and tissue disruption can alter access to nutrients and oxygen. In influenza, the infected cell's biochemical machinery participates in viral production, while the respiratory tissue's physiological role is to provide conditions for oxygen delivery throughout the organism. Although all living systems require energy, each case involves a different link between metabolism, tissue architecture and disease. The comparison is informative only when the particular biological connection is specified.

Animal models support the study of conserved mechanisms, but the meaning of an experimental outcome depends on species and tissue. An epithelial receptor may vary in abundance or accessibility across hosts. The relative contributions of different immune cells may differ, and the dimensions and organization of a respiratory tract influence the consequences of a lesion. Thus an observation in a model can support the existence of a mechanism without establishing the exact magnitude of disease in every animal. The same caution applies to isolated cells: they can demonstrate molecular events under defined conditions but lack the full arrangement of neighboring cells, circulation and tissue repair. A mechanistic conclusion becomes stronger when different levels of observation support the same causal sequence without requiring each model to reproduce every feature of natural disease.

From an evolutionary perspective, pathogens and hosts have different immediate biological interests. The host benefits from maintenance of its own tissues and control of infection. A pathogen's continued propagation depends on access to appropriate resources and opportunities for persistence or transmission. Tissue injury may accompany that propagation, but injury itself need not be the direct target of selection in every case. A microorganism can cause harm as a by-product of exploiting a cellular process. Host defensive activity can also contribute to harm while having evolved because it is protective on average. This interpretation avoids the misleading assumption that pathogens intentionally seek to maximize host damage or that every injurious host response must be a biological mistake. The outcome arises from interacting systems with constraints, not from a single universal rule about virulence.

13. Quantitative models of flux, balance and injury

An important examination skill is the ability to convert a mechanistic statement into a limited quantitative prediction. Begin with a fluid-balance example in which an intestinal compartment receives fluid at rate I, secretes additional fluid at rate S, absorbs fluid at rate A and loses luminal contents downstream at rate O. Let V represent the volume of fluid in the compartment. Conservation of volume, assuming constant density and no other routes, gives dV/dt = I + S − A − O. The units of each term are volume divided by time. The equation does not identify any transporter; it simply expresses the fact that volume change equals the sum of incoming flows minus outgoing flows. Biological mechanisms determine the values of the terms. The equation provides a framework within which those mechanisms can be interpreted.

In a hypothetical isolated intestinal segment, assume the incoming and outgoing luminal flows are both zero during a short observation interval. Suppose secretion is 18 milliliters per hour and absorption is 12 milliliters per hour. Net accumulation is 6 milliliters per hour. Over two hours, constant rates would yield 12 milliliters of additional fluid. If secretion remains 18 but absorption decreases to 7 milliliters per hour, net accumulation becomes 11 milliliters per hour and the corresponding two-hour accumulation is 22 milliliters. The increase in accumulation is therefore 10 milliliters, even though secretion did not change. A result of this kind would demonstrate that the reduction in absorption is sufficient to account for the difference within the simplified balance. It would not prove that secretion and absorption are independent in a real epithelium.

A second model concerns the abundance of a signaling messenger. Let C be cyclic AMP concentration, P its production rate expressed in concentration per unit time, and k a first-order removal coefficient expressed in inverse time. Assume removal is proportional to concentration over the observed range. The balance becomes dC/dt = P − kC. At steady state, the derivative equals zero, so C equals P divided by k. This equation shows that increasing production or decreasing the removal coefficient can both raise the steady-state concentration. For example, if P is 2 micromolar per minute and k is 0.5 per minute, the predicted steady-state concentration is 4 micromolar. If P rises to 6 with the same k, the predicted value is 12 micromolar. If instead P stays at 2 and k falls to one sixth per minute, the same steady-state concentration is predicted. Identical final concentrations are therefore compatible with different regulatory lesions.

The messenger model is deliberately simplified. It assumes a single well-mixed compartment, constant production and a removal rate proportional to concentration. It does not explicitly represent enzyme saturation, subcellular cyclic AMP gradients, dynamic feedback or the finite number of signaling proteins. The inferred production and removal rates are also not directly measured by a single concentration reading. A time course or an independent measurement of the component processes is necessary to distinguish changes in P from changes in k. In an examination question, a graph showing the same final concentration but different rates of approach to it may provide additional evidence about turnover. A model is useful precisely because it states what has been assumed and what additional observations would be required to discriminate possible explanations.

A third calculation concerns epithelial electrical measurements. Conductance describes the ease with which an electrical current passes through a pathway, while resistance describes opposition to current. For a simple linear circuit, conductance is the reciprocal of resistance. Transepithelial electrical resistance, commonly abbreviated TEER, is often normalized to area so that measurements can be compared between epithelial cultures of different sizes. In a hypothetical experiment, a control epithelial sheet has a resistance of 500 ohm square centimeters, while an affected sheet has a resistance of 250 ohm square centimeters. The reciprocal conductance per area doubles from 0.002 to 0.004 siemens per square centimeter. This indicates an increase in net electrical conductance under the conditions of the measurement. It does not on its own establish whether the change arises from junctions, channels or cell loss.

The latter qualification is particularly important when comparing cholera and invasive enteritis. An increase in transcellular ion conductance can lower measured epithelial resistance even if the physical junctional barrier remains largely intact. Loss of junctional integrity can also lower resistance. The two mechanisms are not interchangeable, and a single TEER value cannot discriminate them. A marker of paracellular passage and an independent assessment of cell viability or tissue morphology can help refine the interpretation. Even a marker must be chosen carefully: the permeability of the epithelium to a small ion is not identical to its permeability to a larger solute. Thus an analytical question that provides multiple readouts should be answered by integrating them rather than selecting one as an infallible indicator of damage.

The respiratory tract provides a different quantitative relationship. A simplified form of Fick's diffusion law states that the rate of gas transfer is proportional to exchange area and the concentration difference across the barrier, and inversely proportional to diffusion distance. Let A represent effective area, L the effective thickness, D an effective diffusion coefficient and ΔC the concentration difference. Under steady conditions in a simplified uniform barrier, the rate is D A ΔC/L. Consider relative values rather than absolute physiological units. If effective area falls to 75 percent of baseline and effective thickness doubles, while D and ΔC remain unchanged, transfer becomes 0.75 divided by 2, or 37.5 percent of baseline. This hypothetical calculation explains why alveolar injury and fluid accumulation can combine to impair gas exchange more than either change alone.

The diffusion calculation has limitations. An actual lung is a heterogeneous organ; blood flow, ventilation, gas binding to hemoglobin and spatial variation in injury influence whole-organism oxygen exchange. The thickness of an injured barrier cannot be substituted into a one-compartment model to predict a patient's oxygen saturation directly. Nonetheless, the direction of the predicted effects is informative: loss of available area and an increased diffusion distance both oppose transfer when other variables are held constant. The examination-level objective is to connect structural change to a physical principle and identify conditions under which that principle is valid. The example also reinforces that different organs require different functional readouts of infectious disease.

14. Experimental reasoning: locating the cause of a tissue lesion

A mechanistic hypothesis is more useful than a descriptive association because it generates predictions about observations that should follow from a proposed causal relationship. Suppose a physiological disturbance is attributed to an epithelial signaling change. The proposed cause should occur in the relevant cells, should precede the physiological response in the causal sequence, and should be compatible with known transport mechanisms. If an independent observation shows that the proposed downstream pathway is absent while the disturbance still occurs, the original hypothesis may be incomplete or incorrect. Such reasoning is central to CSIR-NET Part C, where unfamiliar biological data frequently accompany familiar molecular concepts. The task is not to recognize a disease name immediately but to identify which relationships the observations actually support.

An important experimental distinction concerns the difference between a negative observation and the absence of a biological event. If bacterial cells are not observed inside epithelial cells, several interpretations are possible. The cells may truly be absent from that compartment, the sampling method may have missed the affected region, or the detection method may lack sufficient sensitivity. A negative finding has stronger meaning when the relevant compartment was examined using a method that could reliably detect the expected object. Similarly, failure to detect a particular inflammatory marker does not establish the complete absence of an inflammatory response. The marker may represent only one component of a broader response or may have been measured at an inappropriate time. Scientific conclusions depend on both the result and the quality of the measurement.

The historical cholera filtrate observation provides a particularly clear example of a causal distinction. The separation of a soluble material from intact bacterial cells addresses whether the bacterial cell itself is necessary for a particular immediate tissue response. When intestinal fluid accumulation can be attributed to a cell-free component, the response is compatible with a soluble mediator. Additional biochemical evidence is needed to identify the mediator and the specific intracellular target. The logic differs from demonstrating that a microorganism is found in an injured tissue. The latter establishes spatial association, while the former distinguishes possible forms of causal action. This distinction also explains why a soluble microbial product can be studied as a model of a host pathway without equating that simplified model with the full disease.

A transport-focused epithelial model can represent the two sides of a polarized tissue and permit independent observations of the luminal and tissue-facing compartments. The value of this model is its ability to distinguish directional transport from nonspecific leakage. When material moves preferentially in one direction, the polarity and activity of epithelial pathways may explain the result. When a normally excluded marker appears across the tissue, a loss of barrier selectivity may be relevant. However, directional flow alone is not evidence for a particular transport protein, because several different pathways can contribute to it. The interpretation becomes stronger if the measured transport change is accompanied by an appropriate change in the predicted host signaling state and if the structural barrier remains sufficiently intact for the proposed model to apply.

A comparison between cyclic AMP and epithelial current illustrates the distinction between upstream and downstream readouts. An increased messenger concentration establishes an altered balance of production and removal in the measured system. A change in current establishes altered net movement of electrical charge under the measurement conditions. If both increase together, the result is compatible with a signaling-dependent change in ionic transport. It does not by itself establish the identity of every intermediate step. If current changes without a measurable rise in total cyclic AMP, a cyclic AMP-independent pathway is one possibility, but a localized messenger change that is diluted in a whole-cell measurement is another. Examination questions usually specify simplified assumptions, and a student should use those assumptions while recognizing that real systems can contain additional routes.

Distinguishing epithelial transport from physical leakage can require observations at different scales. A change in electrical resistance is sensitive to the conductance of available ionic pathways. A tracer measurement can report passage of a molecule of a particular size and chemical nature. A viability measurement indicates whether cells remain alive under a specified definition of viability. Morphological observations can show tissue continuity and localization of injury. Taken together, these readouts can support a more specific interpretation than any one alone. For example, increased ionic conductance with largely preserved morphology is compatible with regulated changes in channel activity, whereas loss of continuity accompanied by increased passage of otherwise excluded macromolecules supports a more substantial barrier disturbance. Neither pattern by itself identifies the microorganism responsible.

The difference between intracellular localization and infectious activity is equally important for viral disease. A measurement of viral nucleic acid can indicate the presence of viral genetic material, but it may include material that is no longer associated with infectious particles. A measurement of infectious activity addresses a distinct question: whether material can initiate an infection in a suitable system under the conditions of the test. A microscopic image may show viral components in specific cells but does not necessarily quantify their ability to produce infectious progeny. The three measurements answer different biological questions and need not decline at identical rates during recovery. A student should avoid treating the words viral load, viral RNA and infectious viral burden as interchangeable unless the problem explicitly defines them that way.

In inflammatory enteritis, spatial and temporal evidence is especially useful. A measurement of bacterial material associated with a tissue does not specify whether bacteria are attached to the luminal surface, enclosed within epithelial cells, associated with defensive cells or located in an underlying compartment. A rise in an inflammatory readout may follow epithelial disturbance or may arise in parallel from recognition of microbial molecules. The presence of both phenomena at the same observation time does not resolve their order. Distinguishing these possibilities requires knowledge of what the measurement represents and whether the observations establish an ordering in time. An analytical interpretation should state the strongest conclusion supported without inventing a missing temporal or spatial measurement.

Control observations provide the baseline for interpreting a proposed cause. A suitable negative comparison accounts for the underlying tissue behavior in the absence of the specific disease-associated change. A positive comparison establishes that the measurement can report a known type of response in the model. Neither comparison needs to reproduce every property of the natural disease. Its value depends on the question being asked. For example, a measurement intended to detect changes in epithelial permeability requires a baseline that accounts for the integrity of the epithelial sheet. A comparison of samples with different amounts of viable tissue cannot automatically be interpreted as a difference in signaling activity per cell. The investigator must consider what is being normalized and why that denominator is biologically relevant.

A final distinction is between a necessary intermediate and a correlated marker. If a proposed mechanism requires a specific downstream event, the mechanism predicts that the corresponding physiological effect cannot follow through that route when the intermediate is absent. A marker, by contrast, may accompany the effect without being responsible for it. An inflammatory mediator could rise alongside injury because both respond to an earlier event; its presence would then be informative without proving that it caused the injury. In a teaching question where a particular host pathway has been independently established, reasoning about an abstract interruption of that pathway can distinguish upstream and downstream consequences. These predictions are conceptual statements about causation; they are not instructions for modifying a pathogen.

The value of combining different models is that each makes some observations easier while sacrificing others. An isolated epithelial system can clarify polarity and transport but cannot fully represent circulation or recruited immune cells. A tissue section provides spatial information but ordinarily captures a limited moment. Whole-animal physiological observations incorporate compensation and interacting organs but may not identify the initial molecular lesion. If results from these different levels are consistent, they support an integrated account. If they differ, the difference may indicate that a process present in one system is missing in another. It is not automatically a contradiction. A rigorous answer identifies the scale of the observation and avoids conclusions that require unmeasured variables.

15. Time-dependent damage, repair and the interpretation of graphs

Disease is a process rather than a static state. Even when a particular mechanism dominates, the relevant variables may peak at different times. At the onset of infection, the pathogen may interact with only a limited area of tissue. As the interaction progresses, physiological dysfunction may increase because more cells are affected, a signaling disturbance persists or inflammatory responses accumulate. At a later stage, microbial activity can decrease while tissue damage remains substantial. Recovery occurs when the remaining functional cells and repair processes restore sufficient tissue performance. This sequence is not identical in every disease, but it explains why a single observation often gives an incomplete view of pathogenesis.

For toxin-mediated secretory disease, the important time variables include the period of epithelial exposure, the lifetime of the altered signaling state and the subsequent restoration of transport regulation. Removal of an extracellular stimulus does not guarantee instantaneous normalization of an intracellular protein that has undergone covalent modification. The host must restore normal control through processes that affect the modified protein, messenger concentrations and downstream transport. In addition, the animal's fluid reservoir may remain depleted after the net rate of intestinal loss decreases. The molecular lesion and its organism-level consequence therefore have different timescales. A graph of luminal secretion rate and a graph of circulating volume may show related but nonidentical patterns, because circulating volume depends on cumulative loss and replacement.

In invasive intestinal disease, inflammatory activity and microbial burden may show an apparent delay relative to one another. Recruitment of cells involves communication, movement and accumulation, so a strong cellular response may develop after the initiating microbial interaction. When the initiating stimulus decreases, recruited cells and altered tissue structure do not disappear immediately. The persistence of an inflammatory lesion after a decline in microbial burden is therefore compatible with the biological mechanisms described earlier. It does not prove that the infection has been completely eliminated, and it does not establish that continued tissue injury is due exclusively to a particular immune cell population. The temporal pattern supplies evidence for a lag but requires additional observations to identify its cause.

In influenza, a similar distinction can be drawn between viral replication, epithelial integrity and specialized clearance function. Imagine a hypothetical graph in which an infectious viral burden rises early, reaches a peak and then decreases. Epithelial barrier function falls somewhat later, and ciliary transport returns to its initial value only after barrier function has improved. This graph is biologically plausible because injury accumulates through both direct and indirect routes, and repair of a continuous epithelial sheet can precede restoration of fully differentiated ciliated cells. The graph should not be presented as a universal measured time course for influenza. It is a teaching model that makes the difference between microbial control and functional recovery explicit.

Quantitative reasoning about time also involves the distinction between a rate and an accumulated amount. Suppose a variable represents the rate of fluid loss in milliliters per hour. The total volume lost during a specified interval is the area under the rate-time curve, provided units and signs are handled consistently. A high rate lasting a short time can produce a smaller accumulated loss than a moderate rate lasting a long time. Similarly, if an injury score is measured repeatedly, the area under its curve may summarize exposure to the injury over time, although the biological interpretation depends on how that score is defined. One must not calculate an area under an ordinal score and then treat it as a direct physical quantity without justification.

A hypothetical rate dataset illustrates the principle. During the first hour, a compartment loses fluid at 8 milliliters per hour; during the second and third hours, it loses fluid at 5 milliliters per hour; during the fourth hour, its net loss is 2 milliliters per hour. Assuming each rate is constant over its stated interval and no replacement occurs, cumulative loss is 8 plus 10 plus 2, or 20 milliliters. The peak loss rate was 8 milliliters per hour, which is not the same as the cumulative loss. If another compartment loses 6 milliliters per hour for four hours, its peak rate is lower but cumulative loss is 24 milliliters. This simple example is designed to prevent an inference that disease burden is always proportional to the maximum measured rate.

When comparing time courses, the units and the baseline of each variable require attention. It is possible to express a tissue function as a percentage of its uninfected baseline while expressing microbial material in absolute units. The curves may appear visually similar on a graph whose vertical axes have different scales. Visual similarity is not proof of a fixed mathematical relationship. A normalized curve can show relative change but may conceal large differences in absolute magnitude between tissues or measurements. Therefore a careful interpretation identifies both the direction of change and the quantity being represented. Where uncertainty intervals or variability among samples are supplied, they should be considered before drawing conclusions about the timing of peaks or differences between groups.

Repair depends on more than the number of surviving cells. Cells must occupy the correct locations, establish junctions, polarize their membranes and acquire the differentiated properties required for local function. A regenerated intestinal surface with incomplete transporter localization may not yet reproduce its normal absorptive behavior. A regenerated respiratory surface with incomplete ciliary differentiation may not yet clear mucus normally. Thus a count of epithelial nuclei may return to baseline while a functional readout remains abnormal. This discrepancy need not mean that the cell count is inaccurate; it can indicate that structural repopulation and functional maturation are separate stages.

An integrative interpretation of disease progression should therefore distinguish at least three processes: the persistence or removal of the infectious stimulus, the generation and accumulation of injury, and the restoration of function. Some processes overlap and influence one another, but they do not reduce to one variable. This perspective allows students to evaluate experimental graphs in which a tissue function worsens after microbial burden begins to decline or improves before all microbial material disappears. The correct inference depends on the specific definitions and observations, rather than a general rule that improvement in one variable necessarily means improvement in every other variable.

16. Bringing molecular pathogenesis and examination reasoning together

The most reliable way to reason from an unfamiliar pathogen-associated lesion is to reconstruct the normal host process before interpreting its disturbance. If the lesion is described as excessive epithelial secretion, identify the direction of fluid movement, the transport machinery and the source of the driving gradients. If the lesion is described as inflammatory damage, identify the compartment where microbial recognition occurs, the epithelial boundary and the relevant tissue functions. If the lesion concerns viral respiratory disease, determine whether the question concerns conducting airways, alveolar exchange, infected cells or the broader tissue response. This preliminary reconstruction prevents a student from attaching an established mechanism to an inappropriate anatomical location.

Next, distinguish the level of the initiating cause from the level of the measured outcome. A toxin may alter a host enzyme or signaling protein, but the observed outcome can be measured as epithelial current, fluid movement or a systemic physiological change. Each is separated from the initial cause by intermediate processes. A viral infection may be measured at the level of intracellular genetic material while its physiological effect appears as reduced mucus transport or gas exchange. An invasive bacterial interaction may be detected microscopically while the dominant clinical pattern reflects inflammatory disturbance of the tissue. When a question asks for the most immediate effect of a molecular change, the answer should identify the nearest relevant downstream event rather than jumping directly to a final organism-level outcome.

The distinction between association and causation is particularly useful in multiple-statement questions. A statement that a marker is increased during disease may be supported by a dataset, while a second statement that the marker is responsible for every aspect of tissue injury may go beyond the evidence. A statement that a pathogen is detected in one tissue may be correct, while an assertion that distant physiological symptoms prove infection of distant tissue may be false. Reading each statement at its stated level of certainty is essential. Words such as always, exclusively, sufficient, necessary and directly can change the scientific meaning of a proposition even when most of its vocabulary is familiar.

Biological applications of the mechanisms can be understood at the level of principle without moving into specialist treatment or experimental protocols. Cholera toxin studies helped illuminate how G proteins regulate signaling and how epithelial ion transport controls fluid movement. Research on invasive enteritis has clarified the interaction between cellular boundaries, cytoskeletal dynamics and inflammatory tissue responses. Influenza pathogenesis provides a model of the consequences of infection for a highly specialized epithelial barrier and of the difference between pathogen control and tissue repair. These connections show that the study of disease is not separate from normal cell biology. Disease can reveal the function of a cellular system by showing what happens when its control or organization is disrupted.

There are limits to this integrative approach. Not every animal pathogen acts predominantly through the three patterns emphasized here, and a single infectious disease can combine several types of damage. Some diseases involve other host organs or persistent interactions that are not represented by these examples. Those possibilities do not invalidate the examples; they clarify why the framework must remain conditional on anatomical and mechanistic evidence. The next curriculum topic shifts to plant hosts, whose rigid cell walls and tissue organization impose different constraints on microbial entry and spread. The central habit of tracing microbial interaction to altered host function remains useful, while the specific mechanisms must be reconsidered in the context of plant anatomy rather than transferred unchanged from animal cells.

In a hypothetical host system, a receptor-binding observation and a downstream physiological observation can be separated by a defect in the host response itself. Imagine that a cell displays normal binding of a microbial product but fails to show the expected change in epithelial transport. The result is compatible with a problem in intracellular processing, a signaling intermediate, a transport protein or the relevant electrochemical gradient. It does not prove that the binding observation was false. If instead a downstream signaling readout is demonstrably normal but transport is absent, the plausible location of the defect becomes more restricted: the measurements are compatible with a problem at or after the step that connects signaling to transport. This reasoning uses the order of the pathway to interpret a hypothetical loss of function, without assuming that a particular mutation has been identified. It is especially useful when the question gives several readouts and asks which level of the host pathway is affected.

Q1. Which event distinguishes toxin-mediated cholera from a mechanism requiring bacterial invasion of intestinal epithelial cells?

Answer and explanation

B. A microbial product acts on a host cell while its producer can remain extracellular.

Cholera toxin can alter epithelial signaling without bacterial internalization. Intracellular bacterial growth, nuclear loss and systemic dissemination are not required for this characteristic secretory mechanism.

Q2. Which molecular structure is recognized by the B subunit assembly of cholera toxin in the classical model?

  1. Gs alpha
  2. Adenylyl cyclase
  3. CFTR pore
  4. GM1 ganglioside
Answer and explanation

D. GM1 ganglioside

GM1 provides an accessible glycolipid receptor. Gs alpha is a cytosolic catalytic target, cyclase is a downstream effector and CFTR is a downstream regulated channel.

Q3. What directly explains prolonged stimulation of adenylyl cyclase in the classical cholera toxin pathway?

  1. Impaired GTP hydrolysis by modified Gs alpha.
  2. Suppression of phosphodiesterase as the primary catalytic action of the toxin.
  3. Inhibition of GDP release from the stimulatory G protein.
  4. Accelerated hydrolysis of GTP by Gs alpha.
Answer and explanation

A. Impaired GTP hydrolysis by modified Gs alpha.

The toxin modifies Gs alpha, prolonging its active signaling state. Reduced cAMP removal can also elevate messenger concentration but is not the classical primary toxin lesion. Inhibition of GDP release or accelerated GTP hydrolysis would oppose prolonged Gs activation.

Q4. Which statement best describes the transport role of CFTR?

  1. An ATP-driven chloride transporter that moves anions independently of their electrochemical gradient.
  2. A basolateral sodium-glucose cotransporter.
  3. A regulated anion channel permitting flux according to electrochemical driving forces.
  4. A basolateral electroneutral chloride-accumulating cotransporter.
Answer and explanation

C. A regulated anion channel permitting flux according to electrochemical driving forces.

CFTR is a host anion channel with phosphorylation- and nucleotide-dependent regulation. Its gating differs from active pumping, and water responds to tissue-level osmotic conditions.

Q5. The basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase of an intestinal epithelial cell primarily contributes to which condition?

  1. Direct transport of glucose across the apical membrane.
  2. Direct activation of apical CFTR by phosphorylation.
  3. Maintenance of ionic gradients supporting secondary transport.
  4. Establishment of an inward sodium gradient by passive diffusion alone.
Answer and explanation

C. Maintenance of ionic gradients supporting secondary transport.

The pump establishes sodium and potassium gradients using ATP. Other transporters exploit the gradients; glucose uptake is not the pump's own transported reaction.

Q6. Why can large fluid losses occur even when intestinal luminal fluid is approximately isotonic?

  1. Water follows net solute transfer, so cumulative volume can increase without extreme persistent hypertonicity.

  4. Isotonicity establishes that gross secretion and absorption are equal.
Answer and explanation

A. Water follows net solute transfer, so cumulative volume can increase without extreme persistent hypertonicity.

Osmolarity is a concentration, not a cumulative flux measurement. Water and solutes may move together, generating substantial volume loss without a large sustained concentration difference.

The relevant cellular response involves membrane and cytoskeletal remodeling. Attachment and post-entry survival are distinct stages; intact bacteria cannot simply diffuse through the lipid bilayer.

  4. The lag alone identifies delayed repair as the exclusive explanation.
Answer and explanation

A. The timing is compatible with accumulated direct injury, host responses or delayed repair.

A time lag is compatible with several causal processes. It is insufficient to identify one immune mediator or to exclude an initiating viral cause.

Q38. A hypothetical intestinal fluid-loss rate is 10 mL/h for 1 h and 4 mL/h for 3 h. What is cumulative loss, assuming no replacement?

  1. 14 mL
  2. 40 mL
  3. 6 mL
  4. 22 mL
Answer and explanation

D. 22 mL

Integrating the piecewise constant rates gives 10×1 + 4×3 = 22 mL. Peak rate and accumulated volume are different quantities.

Q39. A localized intestinal secretory disturbance is accompanied by low circulating fluid volume. Which explanation requires no microbial dissemination?

  1. Bacteria necessarily colonize every blood vessel.
  2. Net intestinal fluid loss draws on the extracellular fluid reservoir.
  3. The circulating-volume change proves direct toxin action on blood vessels.
  4. Circulating volume is independent of epithelial water movement.
Answer and explanation

B. Net intestinal fluid loss draws on the extracellular fluid reservoir.

An organism's shared fluid environment links a local intestinal lesion to systemic perfusion. The effect alone does not locate the microorganism in the circulation.

Q40. A tissue has unchanged total channel protein but increased channel-mediated current under otherwise comparable gradients. What may have changed?

  1. The total channel amount must have doubled.
  2. All channels must have become impermeable.
  3. Channel open probability or other activity-related properties.
  4. The increased current establishes that epithelial barrier permeability to macromolecules is increased.
Answer and explanation

C. Channel open probability or other activity-related properties.

Regulated channels may change activity independently of abundance. The measured current reflects ionic flux and cannot be interpreted as a direct microbial count.

Q41. A specimen from an animal model shows a lesion similar to one observed in humans. Which conclusion is appropriately limited?

  1. Every species must show identical receptor distribution and tissue reserve.
  2. Similar histology demonstrates equal organ-level functional impairment.
  3. No useful biological conclusion can ever be drawn from animal models.
  4. The model supports a potentially shared mechanism but does not establish identical disease magnitude in both species.
Answer and explanation

D. The model supports a potentially shared mechanism but does not establish identical disease magnitude in both species.

Conserved mechanisms and species-specific context can coexist. Model findings support bounded causal claims rather than unqualified equivalence.

Q42. A tissue assay detects an inflammatory marker that rises alongside epithelial injury. What does the association establish?

  1. The marker is necessary and sufficient for all injury.
  2. An association excludes contributions from other host-cell processes.
  3. The two variables are associated under the observed conditions; the marker's causal role is not yet proved.
  4. The association rules out any common upstream cause.
Answer and explanation

C. The two variables are associated under the observed conditions; the marker's causal role is not yet proved.

Correlation permits multiple causal structures, including common causes. A causal claim requires additional evidence beyond simultaneous increase.

Q43. Assertion: Cholera can produce profound fluid loss without widespread epithelial destruction. Reason: epithelial ion transport can become dysregulated in structurally viable cells.

  1. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both statements are true, but the reason is unrelated.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

A. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

A transport lesion can have a large functional consequence despite substantial epithelial viability; the reason supplies the relevant causal link.

Q44. Assertion: Influenza-associated epithelial injury can increase susceptibility to a secondary bacterial disease. Reason: mucociliary clearance is a host barrier that can be impaired during viral disease.

  1. Both statements are true, but the reason is unrelated.
  2. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

B. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Compromised clearance is one contributing mechanism. This does not imply that every influenza infection is complicated by bacterial infection.

Q45. Assertion: A lower TEER value alone identifies a specific tight-junction protein defect. Reason: TEER reflects combined ionic conductance pathways.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion.
  2. Assertion false, reason true.
  3. Both true but reason unrelated.
  4. Assertion true, reason false.
Answer and explanation

B. Assertion false, reason true.

TEER measures the combined electrical behavior of the epithelial layer and does not identify a particular molecular defect. The stated reason explains why the assertion is too strong.

Q46. Which pair is correctly matched?

  1. Cholera — primary destructive intestinal invasion; influenza — predominantly secretory intestinal dysfunction.
  2. Salmonella enteritis — exclusively noninvasive signaling; influenza — exclusively virus-independent inflammation.
  3. Influenza — airway injury independent of host responses; cholera — mandatory destruction of intestinal epithelial cells.
  4. Cholera — altered epithelial secretion; influenza — respiratory epithelial injury.
Answer and explanation

D. Cholera — altered epithelial secretion; influenza — respiratory epithelial injury.

The correct match identifies the representative dominant mechanisms. The alternatives either require a lesion that is not obligatory or wrongly make one contributing mechanism exclusive.

Q47. Consider statements I: Local infection can cause systemic physiological changes. II: Systemic symptoms necessarily establish microbial dissemination. III: Repair can lag behind microbial control. Which are correct?

  1. I and II only.
  2. II and III only.
  3. I and III only.
  4. I, II and III.
Answer and explanation

C. I and III only.

Local signals or fluid losses can affect distant systems without dissemination, and repair has its own time course. Statement II incorrectly equates effects with organism localization.

Q48. Consider statements I: CFTR conductance depends on activity and driving force. II: CFTR is an ATP-driven chloride pump. III: Anion transport can influence water movement. Which are correct?

  1. I and III only.
  2. I and II only.
  3. II and III only.
  4. II only.
Answer and explanation

A. I and III only.

CFTR is a regulated ion channel, not an active chloride pump. Solute movement can influence water movement by changing osmotic conditions.

Q51. Which ordering is most appropriate for a classical toxin-mediated epithelial mechanism?

  1. Host recognition → altered intracellular signaling → regulated ion flux → altered fluid balance.
  2. Altered fluid balance → increased ion conductance → host recognition → intracellular signaling.
  3. Host recognition → ion flux → Gs activation → messenger production.
  4. Host recognition → systemic bacterial spread → obligatory epithelial lysis → fluid loss.
Answer and explanation

A. Host recognition → altered intracellular signaling → regulated ion flux → altered fluid balance.

The sequence follows the established cause-and-effect relationship. The other choices reverse causal order or require unnecessary dissemination and epithelial destruction.

Q52. A student argues that a lack of visible cellular damage excludes disease caused by a microbial product. Which correction is best?

  1. Only histology measures cellular function.
  2. All symptoms require cell death.
  3. Physiological dysfunction can occur before or without obvious structural injury.
  4. A microbial product cannot alter a living cell.
Answer and explanation

C. Physiological dysfunction can occur before or without obvious structural injury.

Living polarized epithelial cells can undergo marked changes in signaling and transport. A morphological observation alone cannot exclude a functional lesion.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Infection is always disease.

Correction: Infection and host dysfunction are distinguishable; some infections are asymptomatic. Why it is confused: The words are often used together in everyday speech. Examination distinction: Ask whether a positive microbial measurement establishes organ dysfunction.

2. All pathogenic bacteria invade host cells.

Correction: Extracellular bacteria can cause disease through products acting on host cells. Why it is confused: Entry is emphasized in many cell biology diagrams. Examination distinction: Separate entry of the toxin from entry of the bacterium.

3. Cholera toxin binding and its catalytic action occur at the same host target.

Correction: GM1 recognition and cytosolic Gs modification are distinct events. Why it is confused: The word receptor is mistaken for any downstream target. Examination distinction: Match GM1, Gs and CFTR to different stages.

4. Cholera toxin directly pumps water into the intestine.

Correction: Host ion transport changes effective osmotic conditions; water follows net gradients. Why it is confused: The final symptom is mistaken for the molecular action. Examination distinction: Trace from signaling to epithelial flux.

5. CFTR hydrolyzes ATP to pump chloride uphill.

Correction: ATP-dependent gating is not equivalent to ATP-driven transport of chloride. Why it is confused: ATP-binding proteins are often assumed to be pumps. Examination distinction: Classify channel versus primary active transporter.

6. High cAMP proves increased cyclase activity.

Correction: Messenger abundance depends on synthesis and removal. Why it is confused: Students infer a rate from a concentration. Examination distinction: Compare P and k in the steady-state model.

7. Isotonic fluid cannot be secreted in large amounts.

Correction: Concentration and cumulative transported volume are distinct. Why it is confused: Osmolarity is confused with flux. Examination distinction: Interpret an isotonic but high-volume secretion dataset.

8. All absorptive transport stops in secretory disease.

Correction: Independent or partly independent absorption routes may remain active. Why it is confused: Net secretion is mistaken for absence of absorption. Examination distinction: Compare gross and net fluid rates.

9. Attached bacteria are necessarily intracellular.

Correction: Attachment and cellular entry are distinct. Why it is confused: Surface-associated fluorescence can lack depth information. Examination distinction: Interpret compartment-specific measurements.

10. Inflammation is exclusively damaging.

Correction: It can support microbial control while contributing to injury. Why it is confused: Visible injury makes protective functions easy to overlook. Examination distinction: Compare burden and injury as separate outcomes.

11. Diarrhea alone identifies cholera.

Correction: Multiple mechanisms alter intestinal fluid balance. Why it is confused: Shared symptoms are treated as disease-specific. Examination distinction: Use cellular and molecular readouts.

12. Reduced TEER proves a particular junctional defect.

Correction: Changes in transcellular or paracellular conductance can contribute. Why it is confused: Resistance is used as shorthand for barrier integrity. Examination distinction: Interpret TEER together with tracer permeability.

13. Viral attachment proves productive infection.

Correction: Entry and intracellular permissiveness are different requirements. Why it is confused: Binding diagrams end before replication. Examination distinction: Compare binding and productive infection measurements.

14. Viral RNA equals infectious virus count.

Correction: Nucleic acid and infectious activity are distinct readouts. Why it is confused: Both are loosely called viral load. Examination distinction: Interpret late RNA persistence with low infectious activity.

15. Systemic symptoms imply dissemination.

Correction: Local injury and soluble host signals can have distant effects. Why it is confused: The geographic extent of symptoms is confused with microbial location. Examination distinction: Distinguish physiological effects and agent localization.

16. Continuous airway coverage proves full ciliary recovery.

Correction: Specialized differentiation and coordination can lag behind closure. Why it is confused: Morphology is mistaken for complete function. Examination distinction: Compare epithelial coverage and mucus movement.

17. The largest instantaneous loss rate means the greatest total loss.

Correction: Accumulated loss depends on duration and the integral of rate. Why it is confused: Students compare peak values only. Examination distinction: Compute piecewise rate-time area.

18. Every animal model exactly reproduces human disease.

Correction: Model interpretation depends on species, tissue and variables studied. Why it is confused: Conservation is mistaken for identity. Examination distinction: State the limits of extrapolation.

Revision and student mastery

Connect microbial location, molecular target, altered host process, tissue lesion and systemic consequence. Cholera illustrates a predominantly secretory lesion; Salmonella enteritis illustrates inflammatory intestinal dysfunction; influenza illustrates respiratory epithelial injury and impaired clearance or exchange. These mechanisms may overlap, and the observed lesion depends on timing, host context and physiological reserve.

Essential relationships

  • Net compartment volume change = inputs + secretion − absorption − outputs.
  • At steady state in a linear messenger model: C = P/k.
  • Area-normalized conductance = 1/TEER.
  • In a simplified diffusion model: rate ∝ area × concentration difference / thickness.
  • Cumulative fluid loss is the integral of net loss rate over time.
