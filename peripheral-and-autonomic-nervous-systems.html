1. Peripheral communication and the problem of homeostasis

A mammal that stands after lying down must simultaneously activate skeletal muscles, prevent an excessive fall in arterial pressure, and continue exchanging gases and moving food through the intestine. A single motor pathway cannot accomplish all these tasks in the same way. Skeletal muscles need commands that can change rapidly with intended movement, while the heart and blood vessels need adjustments that take place even when the animal is asleep. The peripheral nervous system provides the communication routes through which the central nervous system receives information and sends commands to tissues. Its autonomic component specialises in regulating internal organs, coordinating processes that support the organism rather than directly producing locomotion.

The distinction between central and peripheral is primarily anatomical. The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system, whereas most nerves, peripheral ganglia and nerve endings belong to the peripheral nervous system. A nerve consists of axons together with supporting cells and connective tissue; it is not a single neuron. Some of its axons may originate in the central nervous system, so the fact that a nerve is peripheral does not mean that every cell body associated with it lies outside the brain or spinal cord. A lower motor neuron provides a particularly clear example: the cell body is within the spinal cord, but much of its axon travels through a peripheral nerve to skeletal muscle.

The distinction between sensory and motor is functional. An afferent pathway carries information towards the central nervous system; an efferent pathway carries commands away from it. In a typical spinal sensory neuron, a stimulus changes the electrical activity of a peripheral ending, and action potentials travel along an axon toward the cord. An efferent neuron may instead begin within the cord and conduct action potentials outwards. These terms describe the usual direction of information transfer in a functioning circuit, not an intrinsic property of action potentials that makes propagation in the opposite direction physically impossible. Artificial electrical stimulation of the middle of an axon can generate impulses in both directions.

Somatic and visceral provide another functional classification. Somatic sensation includes signals from skin, joints and skeletal muscles; visceral sensation samples conditions in internal organs, such as distension of a hollow organ or deformation of an arterial wall. Somatic motor output supplies skeletal muscle. Visceral motor output supplies cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands and is generally called autonomic motor output. An autonomic reflex requires visceral sensory input in many circumstances, but sensory neurons must not be mistaken for the postganglionic motor neurons that execute the response. A mixed nerve may carry both afferent and efferent traffic, including somatic and visceral components.

The word autonomic therefore does not mean that the system is completely independent of the brain. Autonomic output is closely regulated by the brainstem, spinal cord and hypothalamus. Emotional state, exercise, body temperature, eating and sleep can all alter autonomic activity. Some processes, especially enteric reflexes, have extensive local circuitry, but the activity of these circuits can still be adjusted by central signals. This combination of local operation and central coordination makes the physiological response both flexible and robust.

Information flow, sensory endings and controlled variables

An intact sensory pathway begins with a physical or chemical event in the environment of a receptor. The receptor converts that event into a change in membrane current. Depending on the sensory system, the transduction occurs in a neuronal ending or in a specialised receptor cell that communicates with an afferent neuron. For the baroreceptor example, deformation of the terminal membrane changes the activity of mechanosensitive channels. The electrical response controls the frequency of action potentials delivered to central circuits. These impulses do not contain a tiny copy of the arterial pressure wave; rather, their timing and frequency provide an encoded representation of changes in wall deformation. Central neurons interpret that signal in combination with other sensory input.

It is important to separate sensing from the actual physiological quantity being sensed. Blood pressure is a mechanical force per unit area, but the sensory ending in an arterial wall responds primarily to the deformation produced in that wall. If an artery becomes less distensible, the relationship between pressure and deformation can change. Thus an identical arterial pressure need not give an identical sensory discharge in every mechanical condition. The physiological response depends not only on the pressure, but also on the conversion of pressure into receptor deformation and the conversion of deformation into an electrical signal. This illustrates a general principle that applies to many visceral afferents: the input measured by a neuron is related to, but is not necessarily identical to, the homeostatic variable of interest.

Visceral sensory input may reach consciousness, as when intestinal distension produces discomfort, but much of it is processed without a consciously perceived sensation. Signals from lungs, blood vessels and digestive organs participate in reflex responses that occur continuously. The word sensory does not imply that a stimulus will always be consciously experienced. Conscious perception requires additional processing in central pathways, whereas a reflex can be organised by networks that do not require conscious cortical interpretation. This distinction prevents a common error in which the autonomic nervous system is described as entirely motor simply because the word autonomic is associated with involuntary behaviour. The motor system is autonomic; the reflex loop in which it operates has a sensory limb as well.

Internal sensory pathways may travel alongside motor pathways because organs require both incoming and outgoing connections. A named nerve can therefore be anatomically common to two limbs of a reflex without functioning as a single bidirectional neuron. Separate axons carry the two messages. If the vagus is electrically stimulated without regard to fibre direction, both sensory fibres and motor fibres may be activated. Some effects observed in an intact animal can therefore result from a centrally mediated response to stimulated sensory fibres, rather than a direct action of the stimulated efferent fibres on a target. Isolating the direction of conduction or stimulating a severed nerve end while monitoring the relevant organ can help separate these contributions.

A useful homeostatic circuit can be analysed at four successive levels: the perturbation, the sensory representation, the neural output and the effector response. Suppose circulating volume decreases acutely. The perturbation affects the filling of the heart and arterial pressure. Receptors respond to associated changes in stretch. Central circuits adjust several efferent pathways. The heart and vessels then change their activity, modifying the original perturbation. If one measures only the last step, several upstream mechanisms may be consistent with the observation. If one measures the sensory discharge and the efferent discharge as well, the possible locations of failure become much more restricted. This is why the language of circuit organisation is useful for experimental physiology and not merely descriptive anatomy.

2. Cranial nerves, spinal nerves and peripheral ganglia

A peripheral nerve is organised for both conduction and protection. Individual nerve fibres lie within delicate connective tissue called endoneurium. Groups of fibres form fascicles bounded by perineurium, which helps maintain the local chemical environment around the fibres. Epineurium surrounds and connects fascicles and carries larger blood vessels. Schwann cells support peripheral axons, forming myelin around many larger fibres and enclosing groups of smaller unmyelinated fibres. These arrangements allow different axons to conduct their own signals within a common cable without electrically merging into one continuous pathway.

In humans, twelve pairs of cranial nerves are conventionally distinguished. The olfactory and optic pathways carry sensory information related to smell and vision respectively; the optic nerve is developmentally a central tract despite its traditional cranial nerve designation. The oculomotor, trochlear and abducens nerves control extraocular muscles, with the oculomotor nerve additionally carrying parasympathetic fibres for pupillary constriction and accommodation. The trigeminal nerve is the principal somatic sensory pathway for the face and also supplies muscles of mastication. The facial nerve controls facial expression, conveys taste from much of the anterior tongue and carries parasympathetic fibres associated with lacrimal and selected salivary glands. The vestibulocochlear nerve carries information about hearing and balance. The glossopharyngeal nerve contributes taste and pharyngeal sensation, carries important cardiovascular sensory fibres and supplies the parasympathetic pathway to the parotid gland. The vagus has extensive visceral sensory and motor connections and supplies parasympathetic pathways to thoracic and much of the abdominal viscera. The accessory and hypoglossal nerves supply selected neck and tongue muscles respectively.

The useful way to learn this overview is not to memorise twelve disconnected names. Classify the fibres by the direction and kind of information they carry, and ask which effectors or receptors are linked to the brain. For example, the vagus nerve carries sensory information from internal organs as well as motor commands to them. A change recorded in an intact vagus cannot automatically be called a change in parasympathetic efferent discharge. A recording method or anatomical preparation must distinguish the fibre populations.

There are thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves in humans: eight cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five sacral and one coccygeal. Each is connected to the spinal cord by dorsal and ventral roots. The dorsal root conveys sensory fibres and has an associated dorsal root ganglion containing the cell bodies of sensory neurons. Most spinal sensory neurons are pseudounipolar, with a single process that branches into peripheral and central processes. The ventral root conveys motor axons, including somatic motor axons and, at appropriate spinal levels, preganglionic autonomic axons. The roots join to form a mixed spinal nerve that divides into dorsal and ventral rami. Both rami generally contain sensory and motor fibres. Thus dorsal ramus and dorsal root are not interchangeable anatomical terms.

A peripheral ganglion is a collection of neuronal cell bodies outside the central nervous system. The dorsal root ganglion differs fundamentally from an autonomic motor ganglion. In the former, the sensory neuron does not ordinarily need to communicate with a second neuron to transmit its signal into the spinal cord. Its cell body is attached to the conducting axonal branch. In an autonomic ganglion, a preganglionic neuron communicates chemically with a postganglionic neuron, creating a genuine relay in the motor pathway. Consequently, a drug that blocks transmission at ganglionic nicotinic receptors is expected to inhibit conventional autonomic motor relays, not to interrupt all sensory signals passing through dorsal root ganglia.

A plexus is a network in which nerve fibres are redistributed into different named branches. The brachial plexus, for instance, distributes fibres from several spinal levels to the upper limb. Redistribution means that a lesion of one spinal root has a different anatomical pattern from a lesion of one distal peripheral nerve. This is an important experimental distinction: impairment of sensation and contraction in the same region may indicate disruption of a mixed nerve rather than independent disorders of sensory receptors and muscle.

Fibre populations and selective interruption

Anatomically mixed nerves contain a heterogeneous population of fibres. Large myelinated somatic motor axons and many proprioceptive afferents conduct relatively quickly; smaller myelinated fibres and unmyelinated fibres generally conduct more slowly. Preganglionic autonomic axons are commonly small myelinated fibres, whereas many postganglionic autonomic axons are unmyelinated. These distributions are useful associations, not absolute identities. The same class of conduction speed can contain fibres with different sensory or motor functions, and conduction velocity varies with temperature, fibre diameter and myelination.

In a nerve recording, electrical signals from many fibres can sum to produce a compound action potential. A brief stimulus delivered to the nerve activates a population of axons; electrodes positioned at a distance record the arrival of their impulses. If the fibres have different velocities, the recording can exhibit early and late components. Increasing the distance between stimulation and recording increases the time separation between fast and slow components, provided the signals remain detectable. This permits an experimenter to infer heterogeneity of conduction even before establishing the function of every individual fibre.

The amplitude of a compound action potential differs conceptually from the amplitude of a single neuronal action potential. The latter is commonly treated as all-or-none under stable conditions, whereas the compound response can become larger as more fibres are recruited. It is therefore possible to increase stimulus strength and increase the recorded compound amplitude without increasing the amplitude of each individual impulse. Recruitment during electrical stimulation is also not necessarily identical to recruitment during normal behaviour. Electrical current applied to a nerve bundle may activate large nearby fibres at lower thresholds than smaller distant fibres, whereas physiological recruitment is determined by synaptic input to neuronal cell bodies.

The ability to conduct an impulse and the ability to evoke an effector response are two distinct properties. Damage to myelin can alter impulse timing or conduction reliability without initially destroying the axonal terminal. Conversely, a defect in transmitter release or postsynaptic receptors can prevent the target from responding even when impulses reach the nerve ending normally. An experimental design that monitors both the compound nerve response and target activity can distinguish these cases. If the nerve response disappears before the target response is lost, conduction failure becomes plausible. If nerve impulses persist but the target fails, transmission or effector responsiveness must be examined.

Consider the consequences of interrupting a dorsal root proximal to the dorsal root ganglion. Peripheral sensory endings and their cell bodies may remain present, yet their normal central pathway is interrupted. Information generated by these receptors cannot reach the cord along the severed central process. A ventral root interruption instead blocks motor output from the relevant central neurons. The sensory input can still reach spinal circuits, but the corresponding motor command cannot travel through the damaged root. Interruption of a mixed spinal nerve can affect both categories. The anatomical extent of a functional deficit depends on the degree of overlap between neighbouring segmental innervation and on the completeness of the interruption.

Peripheral ganglia should also be compared according to their connections rather than merely their shape. A sensory ganglion receives information generated at the peripheral processes of its own neurons. The ganglion is a location for somata, satellite glial cells and axonal branches. An autonomic ganglion contains neurons that receive synaptic inputs from central preganglionic cells and send their own axons to target tissues. Enteric ganglia include multiple interconnected neuronal types and are capable of participating in local circuit computations. These three arrangements can all be recognised as clusters of cells in histological sections, but they are not functionally interchangeable.

Many peripheral neurons arise from embryonic neural crest, including dorsal root sensory neurons and numerous autonomic and enteric neurons. Schwann cells also arise largely from neural crest lineages. Developmental origin helps explain why peripheral sensory and motor ganglia have common structural characteristics, while differences in cell specification and circuit connectivity produce distinct physiological roles. Cranial sensory ganglia have more varied contributions, including placodal lineages. Developmental relatedness is not a sufficient basis for predicting transmitter identity or sensory modality; mature function requires information about both neuronal differentiation and its anatomical connections.

3. Somatic output versus the autonomic relay

The simplest somatic motor pathway is formed by a lower motor neuron with its cell body in the brainstem or ventral horn of the spinal cord and its axon ending directly on skeletal muscle fibres. An action potential arrives at the nerve terminal, calcium enters, and acetylcholine is released. Acetylcholine activates muscle-type nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction. The resulting depolarisation normally triggers a muscle action potential. Later chapters explain how this electrical event recruits calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and initiates force production. Here the important principle is that the peripheral efferent route contains one motor neuron rather than a chain of two.

Most autonomic motor routes contain two neurons arranged in series. A preganglionic neuron has its cell body in the central nervous system and sends its axon to a peripheral autonomic ganglion. There acetylcholine released from the preganglionic ending activates neuronal nicotinic receptors on a postganglionic neuron. If the postsynaptic cell reaches threshold, it sends action potentials through its own axon to the effector. The transmitter released at the effector depends on the division and particular tissue. Cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glandular cells respond according to their receptor complement and intracellular machinery.

The relay introduces an opportunity for convergence and divergence. Several preganglionic neurons may influence the same postganglionic neuron, whereas a single preganglionic axon can branch to influence several ganglion cells. Divergence can coordinate a response across a region of tissue without requiring a distinct central axon for each effector cell. Convergence permits several central signals to influence the likelihood of postganglionic firing. It would nevertheless be incorrect to assume that all ganglia amplify every incoming signal in a fixed ratio. Synaptic strength, timing and the excitability of ganglion cells determine the actual transformation of input into output.

The difference between a somatic junction and an autonomic neuroeffector junction is also spatial. A skeletal muscle fibre usually receives its motor input at a highly organised end plate. Many postganglionic autonomic axons, by contrast, contain varicosities, swellings distributed along branches. Transmitter released from these sites can act over short distances on multiple effector cells rather than across a single narrowly defined synaptic cleft. In electrically coupled smooth muscle, the response may spread between neighbouring cells. Local transmitter diffusion, uptake, receptor abundance and tissue coupling therefore shape the spatial reach of autonomic commands.

There is no universal rule that somatic responses are voluntary and autonomic responses unconscious. The withdrawal reflex is an involuntary somatic motor response; the effector is skeletal muscle. Changes in autonomic activity may follow voluntary behaviour, such as exercise or controlled breathing, despite the fact that the visceral efferent pathway remains autonomic. The criterion that resolves ambiguous examples is the type of effector and motor organisation, not whether a conscious decision preceded the response.

Ganglionic integration and distributed effector contacts

The postsynaptic response in an autonomic ganglion need not be represented as a switch that is always closed whenever a preganglionic action potential arrives. Fast nicotinic transmission can produce a depolarising postsynaptic potential whose size depends on the amount of transmitter released, the number and state of receptors, and the membrane properties of the target neuron. If the depolarisation crosses threshold, an action potential is generated in the postganglionic neuron. A subthreshold response may instead combine with other inputs. The ganglion therefore retains basic principles of neural integration studied in synaptic physiology even though its role is to relay commands to an organ.

Suppose two converging preganglionic fibres each produce a subthreshold postsynaptic response. If activated almost simultaneously, their depolarisations may overlap and produce firing. If the same inputs occur far apart in time, each response may decay before the next begins and the neuron may remain below threshold. In a real preparation, the exact sum depends on membrane conductance and the spatial distribution of inputs, and the synaptic response is not necessarily a simple linear addition. Nonetheless, timing of inputs offers a straightforward explanation of why identical numbers of preganglionic impulses need not produce identical numbers of postganglionic impulses.

Autonomic ganglia can also exhibit slower modulatory responses involving metabotropic receptors. Such responses alter the responsiveness of the cell over longer times than the principal fast nicotinic event. A slow response can change the likelihood that subsequent fast excitation will reach threshold, without serving as the immediate trigger of every postganglionic impulse. This distinction is important when interpreting pharmacological experiments: suppression of fast ganglionic transmission does not establish that all receptor types in the ganglion are nicotinic, and detection of muscarinic responses in a ganglion does not overturn the basic rule about fast transmission.

The effector-side arrangement likewise influences the temporal structure of the response. At a distributed autonomic neuroeffector junction, successive action potentials may release transmitter from numerous varicosities. When stimulation frequency increases, transmitter concentration near responsive cells may rise because the interval available for clearance becomes shorter. A slowly responding smooth muscle integrates this changing chemical signal over time. If force is recorded during a train of stimuli, the trace may rise smoothly rather than showing a distinct contraction after every single neural impulse. This behaviour is compatible with discrete all-or-none impulses in the supplying neurons.

Many visceral tissues can maintain activity without neural input because they possess their own electrically or chemically regulated mechanisms. The absence of autonomic action potentials is not equivalent to the absence of all organ activity. Cardiac pacemakers can continue generating rhythm, smooth muscle can respond to stretch or circulating signals, and glands may respond to hormones. Autonomic nerves modulate these intrinsic processes. By contrast, an isolated skeletal muscle normally depends on somatic motor impulses for neurally evoked contraction, though direct electrical stimulation of the muscle can bypass the junction. An experiment must specify whether the measured activity is spontaneous, evoked neurally or evoked directly.

Signal distribution at the neuroeffector junction should not be confused with systemic endocrine signalling. A transmitter released at a varicosity can diffuse to neighbouring target cells, but its concentration falls with distance and clearance. Hormones released into the circulation reach potentially distant tissues through blood flow and are filtered by their receptor expression and local metabolism. The two modes interact because neural activity can control hormonal secretion and circulating hormones can alter excitability of neurons and effectors. The sympathoadrenal system is a particularly clear example of this interaction.

4. Thoracolumbar sympathetic organisation

In the conventional mammalian description, sympathetic preganglionic cell bodies occupy the intermediolateral region of the thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord, approximately T1 to L2 in humans. The name thoracolumbar describes where the central neurons originate rather than limiting the geographical distribution of sympathetic targets. Sympathetic fibres reach tissues throughout the head, trunk and limbs. The paired sympathetic trunks, which are chains of paravertebral ganglia connected by axons, make this extensive distribution possible.

A preganglionic axon leaves the spinal cord via a ventral root, joins the spinal nerve briefly and then enters the sympathetic trunk through a white ramus communicans. The pale appearance of white rami reflects the myelination of many fibres. In the trunk, it may synapse on a ganglion cell at the same level, travel upwards or downwards to a ganglion at a different level, or pass through the trunk without synapsing. These alternatives explain why tracing the spinal segment from which a nerve first emerges is not sufficient to locate its peripheral ganglion.

Postganglionic fibres destined for the body wall commonly return to a spinal nerve through a gray ramus communicans. They distribute with peripheral nerves to structures such as eccrine sweat glands, arrector pili and blood vessels. Gray rami contain predominantly unmyelinated postganglionic axons and occur at spinal levels beyond the restricted region of white rami. The distinction between white and gray rami is especially useful in examination problems involving selective interruption of sympathetic pathways.

Some sympathetic preganglionic fibres pass through the sympathetic trunk into abdominal splanchnic nerves and synapse in prevertebral ganglia, for example those associated with the coeliac or mesenteric arteries. Postganglionic fibres then reach abdominal viscera, often travelling with blood vessels. This arrangement places the relay closer to particular organ territories than a paravertebral route would. Not every splanchnic nerve contains the same kind of fibre: cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves commonly carry postganglionic sympathetic fibres, whereas pelvic splanchnic nerves carry sacral parasympathetic preganglionic fibres. Names alone are insufficient to infer neurotransmission.

An important route goes to chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla. These cells share developmental and functional characteristics with sympathetic postganglionic neurons. Preganglionic acetylcholine activates nicotinic receptors on chromaffin cells, leading to secretion of catecholamines into the bloodstream. Thus the relay does not continue through a conventional postganglionic axon to each individual target. Circulating epinephrine and norepinephrine can reach receptor-bearing cells at a distance. Their temporal distribution differs from transmitter released at local nerve terminals: secretion, blood transport and clearance determine the endocrine response.

The anatomical distribution of sympathetic ganglia provides a basis for coordinated responses but does not imply that the whole sympathetic system always fires as a unit. During thermal stress, sympathetic activity to sweat glands can change differently from activity to vascular smooth muscle in another territory. During exercise, sympathetic support of circulation is combined with local metabolic vasodilation in active tissues. The relevant physiological question is which population of sympathetic neurons is being recruited, at which target, and against which background of local and endocrine regulation.

Tracing representative sympathetic routes

The upper thoracic origin of sympathetic fibres to the head creates an informative anatomical prediction. A preganglionic neuron supplying the pupillary dilator does not need to originate at a spinal level adjacent to the eye. Its axon leaves the upper thoracic spinal cord and ascends in the sympathetic trunk, reaching a cervical ganglion. The relevant postganglionic neurons send fibres toward the head, including fibres that accompany arterial routes. A disruption of the ascending trunk can therefore reduce sympathetic input to the head even when the oculomotor nerve and ocular muscles remain intact. This illustrates why the distribution of symptoms can reflect the route of a pathway rather than the location of its effector.

The fibres that supply a forearm sweat gland require a different postganglionic distribution. Central axons enter the trunk at the available thoracolumbar levels. Following their appropriate relay, postganglionic fibres join peripheral nerve pathways that reach the limb and skin. A distal peripheral nerve can contain motor axons to skeletal muscles, sensory axons from the skin and sympathetic postganglionic fibres to cutaneous structures. Interruption of such a nerve can therefore have consequences for local sweating or vascular regulation in addition to movement and sensation. The autonomic component is not a separate anatomical cable running independently to every skin region.

Abdominal prevertebral ganglia illustrate a different relationship between organ position and spinal organisation. Preganglionic axons can traverse the paravertebral chain and pass towards ganglia located near arterial branches supplying the viscera. Because the ganglia lie near these vascular territories, postganglionic fibres can follow distribution routes into organ walls. A transection performed between the chain and the prevertebral ganglion interrupts fibres upstream from the ganglionic synapse. A transection performed on the organ side of the ganglion interrupts fibres downstream from it. Identical reductions in nerve-evoked organ response could result, but the two interruptions can be distinguished by stimulating the appropriate side of the ganglion.

The adrenal medulla adds a route in which the target cell of the preganglionic neuron is secretory rather than a conventional axon-bearing relay neuron. Depolarisation of the chromaffin cell increases its intracellular calcium and stimulates exocytosis of secretory granules. Epinephrine synthesis from norepinephrine uses phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase in relevant chromaffin cells, and the local adrenal cortical hormonal environment supports this synthetic capacity. Once secreted into blood, catecholamines can affect vascular, cardiac and metabolic targets according to their receptor distribution. The nervous system has therefore converted an electrical command into an endocrine signal with a wider spatial distribution.

The secretion of epinephrine should not be represented as a universal requirement for every sympathetic response. Local nerve terminals can release norepinephrine at a target independently of an acute increase in circulating epinephrine. The relative contribution of these two signals varies with the situation. A selective loss of adrenal medullary secretion does not remove every sympathetic nerve terminal, just as removal of one organ's postganglionic supply does not remove all circulating catecholamines. In a tissue preparation, local nerve stimulation and application of circulating-hormone analogues are therefore distinct experimental interventions.

Spatially differentiated sympathetic discharge is important for understanding thermoregulation. If core temperature increases, neural output that promotes sweating can increase, while the regulation of cutaneous blood flow may favour heat transfer from deeper tissues to the body surface. These coordinated changes cannot be inferred from a blanket assumption that sympathetic activation constricts every vessel and suppresses every secretory gland. At the same time, blood flow to other territories must be adjusted to preserve circulation. The central command selects output patterns according to multiple physiological needs, and each tissue translates that command through its own receptors and local environment.

5. Craniosacral parasympathetic organisation

Parasympathetic preganglionic neurons are conventionally described as forming a craniosacral outflow. Cranial components arise from the brainstem and travel in cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X. Sacral components arise from the sacral spinal cord, particularly S2 to S4 in the conventional human description, and travel in pelvic splanchnic nerves. Most parasympathetic ganglia are close to or within the target organ. Therefore the preganglionic axon is often long and the postganglionic axon short, in contrast to many sympathetic pathways. These are typical anatomical relationships rather than mathematical definitions of the divisions.

The oculomotor parasympathetic pathway relays through the ciliary ganglion. Its postganglionic fibres influence the circular sphincter of the iris and ciliary muscle. Contraction of the sphincter narrows the pupil, changing the quantity of light entering the eye. The facial nerve carries pathways associated with the pterygopalatine and submandibular ganglia, whereas the glossopharyngeal nerve carries the pathway relaying in the otic ganglion to the parotid gland. These connections show that parasympathetic control is not confined to the digestive tract and heart.

The vagus connects the brainstem to the heart, respiratory tract and substantial portions of the gastrointestinal system. It is a mixed cranial nerve, with many sensory fibres and several kinds of motor fibres. The vagal parasympathetic output to the heart influences pacemaker rate and atrioventricular conduction, while gastrointestinal vagal output frequently acts through enteric neural networks. The sacral parasympathetic outflow contributes to regulation of the distal bowel, urinary bladder and reproductive structures. No inference about the exact target should be made merely because a fibre is called vagal or pelvic.

A near-organ ganglion can organise responses in a specific region rather than distributing a message across the entire body. This local selectivity is particularly useful when one digestive segment needs adjustment without identical responses in every organ. The same anatomical principle explains why parasympathetic denervation of a particular target need not abolish parasympathetic activity elsewhere.

Cranial and sacral parasympathetic pathways are described together because of their conventional anatomical and physiological organisation. They share the common use of cholinergic preganglionic transmission and predominantly cholinergic postganglionic transmission. Their functions are not identical, however. The effect of salivary parasympathetic activity differs from the effect of cardiac vagal activity because the gland and heart express different receptor subtypes and downstream mechanisms. The next section therefore follows the transmitter from the neural ending to the receiving tissue.

Visceral afferents, brainstem output and organ selectivity

When food enters the stomach, the digestive system requires a response that accounts for both the local contents and the state of other organs. Distension can alter the activity of vagal sensory endings; the corresponding information reaches the brainstem and can influence autonomic motor pathways returning to the digestive tract. The incoming sensory neuron and outgoing preganglionic motor neuron are different cells. A peripheral ganglion associated with the sensory pathway contains sensory neuronal somata, whereas the motor pathway uses a near-organ ganglionic relay. Confusing these two ganglia would create an anatomically incorrect model of a vagovagal reflex.

A large part of the functional diversity of vagal signalling arises because the nerve does not act on a single organ or cell type. Cardiac vagal pathways influence pacemaker and conduction tissues. Gastrointestinal vagal pathways can influence excitatory and inhibitory enteric circuits. Sensory fibres convey information about mechanical and chemical changes in several viscera. A single experimental measure of total vagal activity cannot establish that all these components changed in the same direction. Functional studies therefore often need recordings from identified branches or measurements of specific organ responses.

Pupillary control offers a relatively simple example of selective parasympathetic activation. An increase in retinal illumination is conveyed by the visual pathway to central circuits that recruit parasympathetic output to the sphincter of the iris. The postganglionic cells of the ciliary ganglion release acetylcholine, activating muscarinic receptors on the sphincter muscle. Contraction reduces pupil diameter. This response combines a sensory pathway carried by one cranial nerve with an efferent pathway carried by another. A defect of retinal afferent signalling and a defect of the sphincter motor pathway can both reduce the normal light response, but the expected pattern differs when light is delivered to the two eyes.

To understand that prediction, distinguish direct constriction of the illuminated pupil from consensual constriction of the other pupil. Central light-reflex circuits normally distribute information to both sides. If one eye's afferent pathway fails completely, illumination of that eye alone provides little input to either efferent pathway, whereas illumination of the other eye can still cause constriction on both sides if the motor pathways are intact. If one eye's efferent parasympathetic pathway fails, the affected pupil cannot constrict normally in response to illumination of either eye, while the other pupil can respond to light entering either intact afferent pathway. These idealised patterns assume no other lesion, adequate stimulation and complete pathway failure. They demonstrate how bilateral output enables a physiological localisation problem.

Salivary secretion also illustrates organ selectivity. Parasympathetic pathways associated with cranial nerves VII and IX reach different glandular targets. The resulting postganglionic release of acetylcholine activates secretory and associated vascular responses. Changes in salivary output depend on which gland is observed and on the composition of its secretion, so total fluid volume and protein content are not equivalent measures of neural influence. A comparison of both variables can reveal distinctions that would be hidden by a single total-volume measurement.

Sacral parasympathetic output supports coordinated responses in pelvic organs. Its preganglionic fibres leave the spinal cord and travel toward ganglia associated with distal bowel and urinary and reproductive structures. In many instances the final response involves local nerve networks and other motor systems. This arrangement means that a sacral autonomic signal can facilitate an organ response without alone determining the whole behaviour. Successful voiding, for example, requires outlet coordination as well as detrusor activation. A prediction based only on detrusor contraction neglects an important mechanical component of the system.

6. Chemical transmission and receptor-dependent responses

Acetylcholine is the characteristic fast transmitter at both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia. It is synthesised from choline and acetyl coenzyme A by choline acetyltransferase and loaded into vesicles. A presynaptic action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels; calcium entry promotes vesicle fusion. At the ganglion, acetylcholine binds neuronal nicotinic receptors, which are ligand-gated cation channels. Opening of these channels usually depolarises the receiving cell because the combined reversal potential lies above its resting membrane potential. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses extracellular acetylcholine, restricting the duration of the signal, and choline can be taken back up for reuse.

Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons also release acetylcholine, but the characteristic receptors at the visceral effector are muscarinic receptors. These belong to the G protein-coupled receptor family rather than the ionotropic nicotinic family. The distinction has a powerful experimental implication: blocking a muscarinic receptor can suppress a response at a target organ while leaving fast nicotinic transmission through the corresponding ganglion functional. Conversely, a ganglionic nicotinic blocker interrupts the upstream relay even if muscarinic receptors remain fully capable of responding to acetylcholine applied directly to the organ.

Two major muscarinic examples illustrate why the identity of a transmitter cannot predict the sign of a physiological response. M2 receptors in the heart couple principally to Gi/o proteins, reducing adenylyl cyclase activity and cyclic AMP signalling. G protein subunits also activate a potassium conductance in relevant cardiac cells. Together these actions reduce pacemaker depolarisation and can hyperpolarise the membrane, increasing the time required to reach threshold. M3 receptors on many glandular and smooth muscle cells instead couple principally to Gq/11 proteins. Phospholipase C activation generates inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol. The increase in intracellular calcium promotes processes such as secretion or contraction in appropriate tissues. Both responses begin with acetylcholine, yet one slows a pacemaker and the other stimulates a cellular activity.

Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine. Its synthesis starts with tyrosine, proceeds through DOPA and dopamine, and culminates in norepinephrine production through dopamine beta-hydroxylase in secretory vesicles. Released norepinephrine activates adrenergic receptors. Removal from the vicinity of nerve endings occurs importantly through neuronal uptake; intracellular metabolism and other clearance pathways also contribute. This termination mechanism differs from the rapid extracellular enzymatic hydrolysis of acetylcholine.

Adrenergic alpha and beta receptors are metabotropic. Alpha1 receptors generally couple to Gq/11, increasing phospholipase C signalling and cytosolic calcium. In vascular smooth muscle this tends to increase contractile activity. Alpha2 receptors typically couple to Gi/o; presynaptic alpha2 receptors can restrain additional norepinephrine release, providing local negative feedback. Beta receptors generally couple to Gs and promote cyclic AMP formation. Beta1 activation in cardiac pacemaker and contractile cells increases the rate or strength of cardiac activity. Beta2 activation in selected smooth muscles often promotes relaxation, partly through cyclic AMP-dependent regulation of contractile machinery. The same rise in cyclic AMP therefore does not imply the same mechanical response in every tissue.

The beta2 receptor is especially important for understanding the difference between sympathetic nerve activity and circulating catecholamine activity. Bronchial smooth muscle can respond to beta2 stimulation with relaxation, and circulating epinephrine is an important physiological adrenergic signal. Direct sympathetic innervation of human airway smooth muscle is relatively sparse, so a simplistic diagram showing abundant sympathetic nerve endings directly dilating every human airway would be misleading.

An exception to the usual sympathetic adrenergic rule is the innervation of most human eccrine sweat glands. The postganglionic neurons are sympathetic by their anatomical origin, but release acetylcholine acting chiefly on muscarinic receptors to stimulate sweating. The adrenal medulla represents a different exception: the preganglionic neuron releases acetylcholine onto chromaffin cells, which secrete catecholamines into blood. Neither exception changes the definition of sympathetic outflow. Division identity follows anatomy and circuit organisation, not the transmitter found at every terminal.

Autonomic terminals may also release cotransmitters. ATP, neuropeptide Y, vasoactive intestinal peptide and nitric oxide-associated pathways provide examples in appropriate tissues. Cotransmission can modify the amplitude, duration or character of a response, particularly when stimulation frequency changes. A simple acetylcholine versus norepinephrine classification remains a useful starting framework, but it is not an exhaustive description of each autonomic synapse. In experimental interpretation, a residual response after receptor blockade may reflect parallel transmitters, incomplete blockade or local effector activity; these possibilities need to be distinguished before assigning a mechanism.

Dissecting receptor mechanisms through physiological predictions

In the simplest description, acetylcholine binding to a nicotinic receptor opens a pore through which several cations can pass. At negative resting membrane potentials, the net current through the open channel is usually inward. A positive shift in membrane potential brings a ganglion cell closer to threshold. As the membrane approaches the channel's reversal potential, the net driving force becomes smaller. This is why the magnitude and sign of synaptic current cannot be inferred solely from the fact that a ligand is present. The reversal potential and the membrane potential are both relevant, as described in membrane physiology.

A muscarinic receptor instead initiates a biochemical sequence through associated G proteins. An activated G protein can influence an enzyme or channel, and the resulting intracellular signals can persist beyond the short interval during which acetylcholine remains at high concentration near the receptor. Because several steps intervene between binding and final response, the effect of a muscarinic receptor may be slower than the fast nicotinic ganglionic response. The slower response can be advantageous at visceral effectors that regulate secretion or sustained mechanical tone rather than needing a muscle action potential tightly locked to a single presynaptic impulse.

At cardiac M2 receptors, reduction in cyclic AMP alters pacemaker function, including the behaviour of cyclic nucleotide-sensitive channels. Activation of acetylcholine-sensitive inwardly rectifying potassium channels by G protein subunits can shift the membrane towards a more negative potential. Both effects can extend the interval before the next spontaneous action potential. A change in heart rate may therefore occur through a change in the slope or starting level of diastolic depolarisation. It is not necessary for acetylcholine to change the amplitude of every action potential to alter the rhythm.

Cardiac beta1 receptors provide a contrasting prediction. Gs-mediated stimulation of adenylyl cyclase increases cyclic AMP. Cyclic AMP-dependent signalling increases the activity of mechanisms involved in pacemaking and calcium handling. In a pacemaker cell, the rate of spontaneous depolarisation can increase; in working myocardium, calcium entry and intracellular calcium cycling can increase contractile force. These two effects are related through receptor signalling but are not synonymous. An experiment measuring only ventricular force cannot determine the magnitude of the change in sinoatrial rate, and an experiment measuring only rate cannot establish the change in force.

In vascular smooth muscle, alpha1 receptor activation increases pathways that promote calcium-dependent contraction. Calcium binds calmodulin, activating myosin light-chain kinase and increasing phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chains. This enables cross-bridge cycling and force development. Additional pathways can alter the sensitivity of the contractile apparatus to calcium. Thus the amount of contraction depends both on cytosolic calcium and on the state of enzymes regulating the contractile proteins. A force response is not necessarily a direct linear measure of calcium concentration.

Beta2 receptor activation in appropriate smooth muscles produces a different mechanical outcome. Cyclic AMP-dependent signalling can reduce the ability of calcium-dependent mechanisms to sustain contraction, promoting relaxation. This contrasts with the positive inotropic effect of beta1 signalling in cardiac muscle. The apparent contradiction disappears when one recognises that cardiac and smooth muscle cells use distinct arrangements of contractile regulation and calcium handling. A second messenger is not labelled excitatory or inhibitory in isolation; its effect depends on the molecular targets present in the receiving cell.

Presynaptic alpha2 receptors illustrate regulation at the sending side rather than the receiving organ. When norepinephrine is released from a sympathetic terminal, some molecules can activate these receptors on the terminal itself. Their signalling can reduce further transmitter release, including by influencing calcium entry or the release apparatus. This autoreceptor feedback limits the increase in chemical output during ongoing activity. Blocking such a receptor can increase transmitter release under some conditions, even though the blocked receptor belongs to the adrenergic family. A drug effect therefore depends on whether the receptor is presynaptic, postsynaptic or present at both locations.

The classification of adrenergic receptors also helps interpret differences between norepinephrine and epinephrine. These ligands can act on overlapping receptor families but do not have identical affinities or efficacies at all subtypes. The relative importance of a receptor in a particular tissue depends on ligand concentration, access to the tissue, receptor expression and competing signals. In a preparation expressing both contractile alpha receptors and relaxant beta2 receptors, a change in catecholamine concentration or a selective blocker can alter the net response. The mechanism is a balance of simultaneous actions rather than a universal one-ligand, one-effect rule.

An antagonist can be used to test whether a receptor contributes to an observed response. If a selective beta1 antagonist reduces the acceleration of the heart following sympathetic stimulation, the result supports a beta1-mediated component. It does not by itself prove that the remaining response is mediated by one particular receptor. Residual responses can arise from incomplete antagonist occupancy, other transmitter systems or altered intrinsic activity. Conversely, failure to observe an effect of a blocker is not proof that a receptor is absent. The receptor could be present but inactive under the conditions tested, or the measured endpoint might be insensitive to its action. Proper interpretation requires suitable controls and a response that depends on the proposed mechanism.

7. Autonomic tone and organ-specific integration

An organ's resting activity is rarely identical to its activity in the complete absence of neural input. Autonomic tone refers to ongoing neural activity that adjusts the operating state of a target. If an autonomic pathway continuously causes a partial contraction or slowing, its withdrawal can produce a response in the opposite direction without activation of an antagonistic pathway. Many systemic resistance vessels have ongoing sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone. Reducing that tone allows vascular smooth muscle to relax, even though the vessel may receive little or no direct parasympathetic innervation.

The sinoatrial node generates spontaneous activity through intrinsic pacemaker mechanisms. Both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves adjust this activity. At rest in many healthy adult humans, vagal influence contributes to a heart rate below the intrinsic rate measured when both autonomic influences are blocked. An increase in heart rate during the onset of exercise can involve removal of vagal inhibition before large increases in sympathetic drive develop. At greater workloads, increased sympathetic activity adds to the chronotropic and inotropic response. This illustrates how reciprocal and independent changes in two pathways can produce a graded physiological adjustment.

Dual innervation does not guarantee an antagonistic effect at every target. Both divisions can promote salivary secretion, but the amount and composition of secretion depend on the receptors and glandular responses involved. In the iris, the sympathetic pathway promotes contraction of radial dilator muscle, whereas parasympathetic activity contracts circular sphincter muscle. In this case antagonism at the organ level arises because two distinct muscles act mechanically in opposite directions. It is not necessary for sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons to act on the same individual cell.

The urinary bladder provides an example in which several effector systems must be coordinated. During storage, sympathetic pathways favour detrusor relaxation and outlet resistance, while somatic pudendal motor activity supports the external urethral sphincter. During voiding, sacral parasympathetic output promotes detrusor contraction and coordinated changes in outlet activity permit flow. The somatic sphincter must relax appropriately. A strong detrusor contraction without a corresponding decrease in outlet resistance does not necessarily yield effective voiding. This functional interpretation is more useful than memorising one autonomic division as excitatory and the other inhibitory.

During an acute fall in arterial pressure, a reduction in stretch of arterial baroreceptors decreases their afferent discharge. Central cardiovascular circuits respond by increasing sympathetic drive and reducing cardiac parasympathetic drive. The resulting changes in heart activity, venous capacitance and arteriolar resistance help restore pressure. The efferent mechanism has to be distinguished from the sensory event that initiates it. A damaged afferent pathway could prevent an appropriate response even if all peripheral autonomic motor axons and receptors remain functional.

The familiar labels fight or flight and rest and digest are useful summaries of some coordinated patterns, not laws of simultaneous activation and inactivity. A resting individual still requires sympathetic vascular tone, and an exercising individual still retains autonomic regulation of multiple organ systems. The patterns change with the challenge, the tissue and the time course. An examination question that specifies the organ and receptor should be answered from those mechanisms rather than from the slogan alone.

Feedback through medullary and hypothalamic circuits

The brainstem contains networks that integrate visceral input with the signals required to control circulation, breathing and other internal functions. The nucleus of the solitary tract receives substantial visceral sensory input, including cardiovascular information travelling through glossopharyngeal and vagal afferents. Its output influences medullary circuits that adjust sympathetic activity and cardiac parasympathetic activity. The hypothalamus integrates autonomic control with temperature regulation, energy balance, endocrine activity and behaviour. The system therefore has several levels of control rather than a single anatomical centre from which every autonomic signal emerges.

A cardiovascular reflex can be followed through an explicit sequence. A rise in arterial pressure increases wall stretch at appropriately functioning arterial baroreceptors, raising their discharge. Central processing increases cardiac parasympathetic activity and reduces sympathetic vasoconstrictor and cardiac drive. Heart rate, contractility and vascular resistance can decrease, tending to reduce arterial pressure toward its prior operating value. The feedback response opposes the original disturbance; that opposition is the defining characteristic of negative feedback. If a response instead reinforced the initial pressure rise, it would not constitute the usual compensatory baroreflex.

The reverse sequence during a pressure fall is equally instructive. A fall in receptor discharge is not simply an absence of useful information. Because the afferents have ongoing activity in the normal operating range, a decrease carries information about the direction of change. Central networks can respond to this decrease with disinhibition of sympathetic vasomotor pathways and reduced vagal cardiac restraint. The existence of a baseline firing rate thus allows sensory and motor pathways to represent changes in both directions. A nerve that were completely silent at its operating point could signal an increase in activity but could not encode a further decrease by reducing its firing rate.

Autonomic reflexes operate over different time scales. Rapid cardiac responses can follow changes in parasympathetic influence within a small number of beats. Sympathetic changes in vascular tone and cardiac contractility often evolve over a longer interval. Humoral and renal adjustments can contribute to longer-term regulation. The observed time course of a pressure disturbance is therefore the combined result of several processes. A short recording may reveal the fast neural response while missing slower compensation; a later measurement may show pressure restored despite continuing alterations in underlying neural activity.

Feedback regulation does not require a variable to be held at exactly one immutable numerical value. Different behavioural conditions can change the range in which the system operates and the relative contribution of its components. During dynamic exercise, central and sensory signals allow cardiovascular regulation to support the increased metabolic demand even though heart rate and arterial pressure may differ from their resting values. A baroreflex operating around an exercise-associated state can still buffer perturbations rather than forcing pressure back to its pre-exercise value. The appropriate interpretation is a flexible regulated operating condition, not the complete absence of feedback whenever the resting value changes.

An important experimental distinction is the difference between blocking a compensatory pathway and imposing the disturbance that normally activates it. If a vascular bed loses sympathetic tone, vascular resistance may decrease and arterial pressure may fall. Other intact reflex pathways may respond to the fall by changing cardiac activity or constricting vessels elsewhere. Consequently, the final pressure measured after the intervention may not reveal the direct size of its effect on the blocked pathway. The direct effect and secondary compensation can be separated more clearly by examining time course, recording local variables and controlling the systemic conditions when possible.

Central signals can also anticipate changes rather than waiting until a controlled variable has deviated. At the initiation of voluntary movement, coordinated central command recruits motor and cardiorespiratory adjustments. The anticipatory component reduces the extent to which a subsequent metabolic or mechanical disturbance must be corrected. Feedback remains necessary because anticipated demand and actual demand are not always identical. Integration of feedforward and feedback control explains why an autonomic response can begin before a measurable change in arterial gas concentrations or arterial pressure.

Temperature regulation is another example of a response selected by the physiological state. Thermal sensory information and central temperature-sensitive networks influence sympathetic pathways to cutaneous vessels and sweat glands. The thermal response must be coordinated with blood pressure regulation: promoting heat loss through the skin can increase the circulatory demand for maintaining perfusion. During exercise in a warm environment, the requirements for muscle blood flow, skin heat transfer and arterial pressure maintenance interact. The final pattern of neural activity cannot be assigned to one organ in isolation, because several regulatory objectives are being satisfied simultaneously within the available physiological capacity.

Central autonomic coordination is also apparent during feeding. Sensory information associated with food can promote salivation and digestive preparations before nutrients are absorbed. Once food enters the gut, distension and chemical composition activate additional local and central circuits. As absorption proceeds, endocrine and metabolic signals contribute to the adjustment of organ function. These stages demonstrate that autonomic responses are embedded in a larger coordinated behavioural sequence. The distinction between neural, local and endocrine contributions is useful for analysis, but in an intact organism they interact rather than acting in separate chronological blocks.

Comparing autonomic organ responses without misleading generalisations

The effect of autonomic innervation on a blood vessel depends in part on the location of its smooth muscle and its receptor complement. Contraction of a resistance arteriole tends to reduce its radius and increase resistance to flow. Contraction of a capacitance vessel can reduce the volume held in that region and favour the redistribution of blood toward the heart. Both changes may involve sympathetic adrenergic signalling, but they influence circulation through different mechanical variables. A student who labels both effects only as vasoconstriction misses their distinct contributions to arterial pressure and cardiac filling.

At the heart, the term chronotropic refers to an effect on rate, while inotropic refers to an effect on contractile force under defined conditions. Dromotropic effects concern conduction, particularly through specialised conducting tissue. These properties can change together during sympathetic activation, yet an intervention may influence one more strongly than another. Parasympathetic influence is especially prominent in pacemaker and atrioventricular tissues; direct parasympathetic effects on ventricular contractility are generally less prominent than sympathetic effects in the conventional account. A slower heart rate after vagal stimulation should therefore not be interpreted automatically as an identical percentage decrease in ventricular contractility.

The bronchial tree shows why neural control must be interpreted alongside circulating and local signals. Parasympathetic cholinergic stimulation can promote airway smooth muscle contraction and secretion. Beta2 adrenergic signalling can relax bronchial smooth muscle. Human airway smooth muscle has relatively limited direct sympathetic innervation, making circulating catecholamines and receptor-targeted stimulation important when discussing adrenergic relaxation. Airway calibre in a living animal also depends on mechanical conditions and local mediators. An observed increase in airway resistance is compatible with several causes and cannot, without additional evidence, be attributed exclusively to increased parasympathetic nerve activity.

Control of breathing is sometimes incorrectly classified entirely as autonomic because it usually does not require conscious attention. Respiratory skeletal muscles receive somatic motor innervation. Their pattern of activity can be generated automatically by brainstem networks, while airway smooth muscle and glands are subject to autonomic regulation. The automatic origin of a command does not determine the category of its peripheral motor pathway. This distinction connects the respiratory and nervous-system chapters: brainstem respiratory rhythm provides the command pattern, somatic motor neurons execute the skeletal muscle component, and autonomic pathways contribute to the regulation of the airways and circulation.

Digestive smooth muscle provides another case in which a shorthand description can conceal the actual neural organisation. Parasympathetic drive may stimulate a digestive process through an enteric motor circuit rather than by directly causing contraction of every muscle cell contacted by the incoming fibre. Activation of inhibitory enteric motor neurons can be essential to an overall increase in efficient propulsion. In this setting, a local relaxation can contribute to the function commonly described as stimulation of digestion. Conversely, sympathetic activity may reduce propulsion by inhibiting neural transmission or changing sphincter activity, without requiring every region of gut smooth muscle to relax in parallel.

Autonomic effects on the reproductive system also illustrate sequential cooperation rather than universal antagonism. Parasympathetic pathways contribute to mechanisms of genital vasodilation, including activation of local nitric oxide-associated pathways. Sympathetic pathways participate in emission and other aspects of reproductive responses, and somatic motor pathways contribute to contractions of relevant skeletal muscles. These functional associations are best understood as examples of different components acting in a coordinated behaviour, not as comprehensive clinical accounts or absolute assignments of every phase to one division.

An additional important example is glandular secretion. The secretory response of a gland can involve water and electrolyte movement, release of stored macromolecules and changes in regional blood supply. Different neural stimuli may alter these components in different proportions. If two experimental groups show the same mass of a secreted substance but different fluid volumes, the concentration differs even though the total mass released is unchanged. This principle helps explain why sympathetic and parasympathetic influences on salivary glands cannot always be reduced to a simple opposing pair of increased versus decreased secretion. The measured endpoint determines which aspect of regulation is being described.

8. Enteric networks and local visceral reflexes

The gastrointestinal wall contains an extensive nervous system organised into ganglia and connecting fibres. The myenteric plexus lies between the main circular and longitudinal smooth muscle layers, while the submucosal plexus is located closer to the mucosal surface. The myenteric network contributes especially to the organisation of motility; the submucosal network contributes especially to secretion and local regulation associated with the mucosa. These are predominant functional associations, not impermeable boundaries between two independent networks.

An enteric circuit can contain sensory neurons, interneurons and excitatory or inhibitory motor neurons. This organisation allows a local stimulus to be converted into an organised response without first travelling to the brain. Consider distension caused by a bolus in a segment of intestine. Local mechanosensitive neural elements detect deformation, and enteric circuitry can organise contraction on the oral side and relaxation on the anal side. Excitatory motor pathways commonly use acetylcholine and tachykinins, whereas inhibitory motor pathways can use nitric oxide and other transmitters. The resulting gradient of pressure and resistance helps propel luminal contents in the aboral direction.

Relaxation ahead of a bolus is not passive absence of neural activity in all cases. Inhibitory motor neurons can actively reduce smooth muscle contraction. If an experimental manipulation suppresses inhibitory transmission while leaving excitation intact, the segment ahead may fail to relax normally. Contraction may still occur behind the bolus, yet coordinated propulsion can be impaired. Measuring total force alone could therefore lead to the wrong conclusion about the effectiveness of motility.

Interstitial cells of Cajal contribute to rhythmic electrical activity and coordination of gut smooth muscle. They are not a substitute for enteric neurons: a basic electrical rhythm does not by itself establish the full spatial organisation of a peristaltic response. Smooth muscle excitability, pacemaker activity and neural excitation or inhibition interact to determine whether a region contracts, relaxes or remains relatively quiescent. The distinction matters in isolated-organ experiments, where neural and myogenic components can survive removal of central connections.

Extrinsic sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways modulate enteric function. Parasympathetic activity often supports digestive motility and secretion through enteric circuits, while sympathetic activity often suppresses these functions and adjusts vascular supply. The precise action depends on the digestive region and physiological state. An isolated segment of intestine can retain local reflex activity when its extrinsic nerves are severed, but this does not mean the intact animal's digestive regulation is independent of central signals, hormones or luminal conditions.

A short visceral reflex is completed locally, whereas a long reflex involves the central nervous system. A vagovagal reflex, for example, can send sensory information from a digestive organ to the brainstem and return motor commands through vagal pathways. Both the local and central routes may operate during a meal. The local network rapidly adjusts regional responses while the long reflex coordinates distant regions and whole-body state. The broader digestive consequences belong to digestive physiology; the central lesson here is that the peripheral nervous system contains not only cables and relays but also integrative circuits.

Local circuit design and separating neural from myogenic mechanisms

To understand enteric organisation, imagine following a signal from the site of a local intestinal deformation. An intrinsic sensory element is excited by deformation directly or through associated cellular signalling. Information is conveyed within a network containing interneurons that can distribute signals longitudinally along the gut. Excitatory motor neurons increase the activity of smooth muscle in one region, while inhibitory motor neurons reduce tone in another. Because the two outputs act at different positions, a local stimulus can generate a spatially asymmetric response. Propulsion requires such organisation: equal contraction on both sides of a bolus would not create the same favourable directional mechanical conditions.

The distinction between ascending and descending enteric pathways refers to their direction along the intestinal axis, not ascent and descent along the spinal cord. For a given segment, the oral direction is toward the mouth and the anal direction is toward the terminal end of the digestive tract. Ascending excitatory and descending inhibitory pathways are a useful conventional model of the peristaltic reflex. Actual motor patterns vary between regions and with luminal conditions. The model is therefore a statement about a representative polarised reflex, not a claim that every movement of every digestive organ has exactly that arrangement.

An isolated bowel segment provides a way to test the necessity of local neural mechanisms. Suppose a distending stimulus reproducibly produces increased force on its oral side and reduced force on its anal side. If extrinsic nerves have been interrupted before the test, the observations establish that ongoing communication with the central nervous system is not essential for this particular pattern. If the response is abolished by suppression of local action-potential-dependent neural transmission while direct stimulation of the smooth muscle still evokes contraction, the results support a local neural contribution. The direct muscle test is critical because suppression of the pattern would otherwise be compatible with damage to the muscle itself.

Not all electrical activity recorded from a gastrointestinal preparation originates in neurons. Interstitial cells of Cajal are important elements in gastrointestinal pacemaker networks and contribute to the generation or propagation of slow waves. Smooth muscle cells receive these electrical influences and may generate additional calcium-dependent electrical events associated with contraction. The presence of periodic changes in membrane potential is therefore not, by itself, evidence that enteric motor neurons are rhythmically firing. Neural recordings, pharmacological distinctions and measurements of muscle activity can provide complementary evidence about the source and consequences of the rhythm.

The interaction between electrical rhythm and neural modulation can be understood by considering excitability. A depolarising input can increase the likelihood that a smooth muscle cell reaches the voltage range at which calcium entry becomes substantial during a slow wave. An inhibitory input can move the membrane potential away from that range or influence intracellular signalling that reduces contraction. The underlying rhythmic electrical activity may continue even while the mechanical response changes. This creates a situation in which a neural intervention changes the amount of force without necessarily abolishing the pacemaker rhythm.

Inhibitory enteric transmission often includes nitric oxide as an important mediator. Nitric oxide is generated enzymatically and can diffuse to nearby target cells. In relevant smooth muscle, activation of soluble guanylyl cyclase increases cyclic GMP signalling and contributes to relaxation. Unlike a conventional small-molecule transmitter stored in synaptic vesicles, nitric oxide is produced on demand. This difference illustrates the broader principle that neural communication can employ multiple chemical mechanisms, even within one organ system. It is not necessary to treat nitric oxide as an exception that invalidates the general concept of neurotransmission; rather, the mode of synthesis, release and receptor action needs to be specified.

The submucosal network is positioned to influence the interface between luminal contents, epithelial transport and the local circulation. Sensory signals associated with mucosal stimulation can recruit secretory pathways, and the resulting secretion changes the environment through which nutrients and other material pass. A local reflex may therefore regulate fluid movement as well as mechanical propulsion. A mucosal stimulus need not cause the same motor pattern as a distending stimulus in the muscular wall. The location and nature of the sensory input determine which circuit is recruited.

The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic sensory pathways is especially important in interpreting experiments on the gut-brain axis. An intrinsic enteric neuron may initiate a local response within the bowel wall, whereas an extrinsic afferent can transmit information from that wall to the central nervous system. Both pathways can respond to related mechanical or chemical events. Removal of extrinsic input can alter centrally mediated coordination while leaving local reflexes operational. Conversely, disruption of local enteric circuitry can impair regional motor organisation even when the central nervous system and the extrinsic nerve trunks remain intact. The two forms of regulation are complementary rather than redundant.

Evidence from research on intestinal sensory transduction and peristalsis shows that distension-evoked motor patterns can involve neural elements located within the myenteric and muscular layers. This does not imply that the mucosa is unimportant for all intestinal reflexes. Mucosal stimuli recruit additional signalling routes, and distinct preparations may preserve different components of the bowel wall. A strong experimental conclusion specifies which layer was retained, what stimulus was applied and what endpoint was measured. The presence of a response after removing one layer establishes that the removed layer was not essential under those conditions, not that it has no function in the intact gastrointestinal tract.

9. Evidence: chemical transmission and experimental localisation

Otto Loewi's 1921 frog-heart experiments provided a classical way to distinguish direct electrical transmission from a transferable chemical signal. Stimulation of the vagus attached to one beating heart slowed that heart. Fluid from the first preparation was then transferred to a second heart, which also slowed despite not receiving the original electrical stimulation. Loewi's Nobel lecture describes this transferable activity and the subsequent evidence linking it to acetylcholine. The observation is especially significant because electrical continuity between the nerves of the two hearts was unnecessary.

The logic of the experiment can be expressed as a comparison between two hypotheses. If the first heart's slowing depended entirely on an electrical signal that could only travel through the stimulated nerve, transfer of the bathing fluid should not reproduce the response in a separate heart. If stimulation instead released a biologically active chemical into the fluid, the recipient heart might respond. A modern teaching control would transfer fluid obtained without nerve stimulation and compare the recipient response under matched conditions of temperature, volume and ionic composition. The transfer observation supports chemical mediation; identifying the molecule requires additional chemical and pharmacological evidence.

A modern isolated preparation can distinguish receptor location from pathway identity. Imagine stimulating a preganglionic sympathetic fibre supplying a vascular preparation and measuring smooth muscle force. Blocking neuronal nicotinic receptors at the ganglion can suppress the response to preganglionic stimulation. If the postganglionic fibre is then stimulated downstream from the ganglion, the vascular response may return. The downstream stimulation bypasses the blocked synapse. Direct application of an adrenergic agonist to the vascular preparation provides a further test of effector receptor function.

The reverse experiment helps distinguish a ganglionic defect from an effector receptor defect. Blocking alpha1 receptors on smooth muscle may reduce the contraction elicited by either preganglionic or postganglionic sympathetic stimulation. Direct alpha1 agonist stimulation is also impaired if the relevant receptors remain blocked. An investigator should nevertheless consider whether alternative receptors, cotransmission or incomplete drug selectivity contribute to the remaining signal. Measuring the electrical firing of the postganglionic axon independently of the contractile response separates conduction from tissue activation.

This experimental framework applies equally to the parasympathetic pathway. A muscarinic antagonist acting at the heart can prevent vagal slowing without abolishing all action potentials conducted in the vagus. A ganglionic nicotinic antagonist, by contrast, interferes with the relay from preganglionic to postganglionic neurons. Because these interventions affect different sites, the pattern of responses to stimulation at several levels carries more information than a single response to a drug.

The enteric system provides another form of causal evidence. If a bowel segment still produces organised responses to local distension after isolation from extrinsic nerves, central input is not necessary for that particular response under the tested conditions. If the response disappears when local neural transmission is suppressed, this supports a role for local circuits rather than a purely mechanical effect of the bolus. Neither result proves that extrinsic innervation is irrelevant in an intact animal, because an isolated tissue cannot reproduce all endocrine, behavioural and circulatory interactions. The strength of the conclusion must match the experimental preparation.

Experimental design, pathway direction and alternative explanations

One way to determine whether a response to nerve stimulation originates in a peripheral motor pathway or in a centrally mediated reflex is to compare stimulation of the two ends of a divided mixed nerve. If the peripheral end remains connected to the organ and the central end remains connected to the brain or spinal cord, stimulating the peripheral end can recruit efferent fibres toward the organ without requiring a return command through the severed nerve. Stimulation of the central end can recruit afferent signals that influence central circuits, producing responses through other intact output routes. The interpretation depends on which connections remain available; an effector response following central-end stimulation is not necessarily a direct action of the severed motor fibres.

This approach requires care because electrical stimulation does not intrinsically respect the physiological direction of information transfer. Impulses can propagate in both directions from a stimulated site if the axon is excitable and continuous. At a peripheral cut end, impulses in sensory fibres may travel toward sensory endings as well as impulses in motor fibres toward their targets. Such activity can sometimes influence local tissues. Therefore, a conclusion that a peripheral-end response is exclusively due to the expected motor pathway should be supported by additional evidence, such as receptor blockade, known fibre organisation or recording of relevant motor activity.

A different strategy compares the response to indirect nerve stimulation with the response to direct application of a transmitter or agonist. Suppose a preparation shows a weak response to stimulation of its nerve, but a near-normal response when an appropriate agonist is applied to the target. The target's ability to respond is preserved under the conditions of direct application. This favours an upstream defect involving conduction, transmitter synthesis or transmitter release. However, an unusually high agonist concentration may overcome reduced receptor availability, so a single near-maximal response cannot fully establish normal receptor sensitivity. Comparing concentration-response relationships and recording nerve activity would help distinguish these possibilities.

A transmitter assay offers a complementary approach. If a stimulus releases a chemical into a collection medium, the amount measured is determined by release and by removal during the observation interval. A higher extracellular concentration can result from increased exocytosis, reduced uptake or slower degradation. The concentration alone is therefore not a direct measurement of release probability. The distinction becomes particularly important when an intervention affects a transporter or degradative enzyme. Changes in the chemical concentration and changes in target force should be interpreted together with knowledge of transmitter clearance.

The principle of necessity and sufficiency is useful in evaluating physiological mechanisms. If blocking a ganglionic nicotinic receptor abolishes a response to preganglionic nerve stimulation under well-controlled conditions, normal function of that receptor is necessary for the tested pathway to generate its response. If direct activation of a muscarinic receptor evokes a response at the target even when the ganglion is bypassed, that activation is sufficient to produce the measured effect under the experimental conditions. Neither observation alone proves that the receptor is the sole mechanism used by the organ in all physiological situations.

Controls should match the type of claim being made. A vehicle control tests whether the solvent or handling used to apply a substance affects the measured endpoint. A time control tests whether responsiveness changes simply because the preparation has been maintained for longer. A direct effector stimulation control helps determine whether the tissue remains functional after neural blockade. Repeated baseline measurements help establish whether the preparation was stable before the intervention. These controls do not guarantee that every interpretation is correct, but they eliminate common alternative explanations for a changed response.

Receptor selectivity should be interpreted as relative rather than absolute. A substance that preferentially affects one receptor subtype may interact with additional targets at sufficiently high concentrations. Furthermore, receptor blockade can alter baseline physiological activity and thereby change the available range of the measured response. If a preparation is already maximally contracted, an additional contractile stimulus may appear ineffective even when its receptor and signalling pathway are intact. An experimenter can reduce such ceiling effects by choosing a stable intermediate baseline and comparing complete response patterns rather than only maximal values.

Historical experiments and modern selective interventions answer different scientific questions. Loewi's fluid-transfer experiment established that stimulation of a nerve could generate transferable chemical activity affecting a separate organ. Receptor-selective experiments address the identity and location of the receiving mechanisms. Electrophysiological recordings address the timing of neural events, while force and secretion measurements describe the response of the effector. These methods are not interchangeable; each contributes a different piece of the causal chain. A comprehensive interpretation connects the observations without pretending that any one technique independently establishes every step.

10. Quantitative reasoning and illustrative data

The length and conduction velocity of peripheral fibres influence the timing of autonomic responses. For an axon of length L measured in metres and a constant conduction speed v measured in metres per second, the idealised propagation time is L divided by v, in seconds. In a two-neuron pathway the total latency includes propagation along both axons, a ganglionic synaptic delay and the time required for transmission and response at the effector. An estimate based only on axonal velocity ignores these additional processes. It also assumes that the fibres conduct at approximately constant speed under the conditions examined.

Consider an illustrative sympathetic pathway with a preganglionic length of 0.10 m and a conduction speed of 10 m/s. Its propagation time is 0.010 s, or 10 ms. A postganglionic length of 0.30 m and speed of 1 m/s give a propagation time of 0.30 s, or 300 ms. If a ganglionic delay of 2 ms is assumed, the estimated arrival at the final nerve ending is 312 ms after initiation of the preganglionic impulse. This is not a measured human reflex latency. It omits sensory conduction, central processing and the kinetics of the neuroeffector response.

A second illustrative calculation concerns heart rate. The interval between successive beats is approximately 60 divided by heart rate in beats per minute, when expressed in seconds. A rate of 75 beats/min corresponds to a cycle length of 0.80 s. Slowing to 60 beats/min extends the interval to 1.00 s, an increase of 0.20 s. The two values describe a change in rhythmic timing but do not by themselves establish whether the cause was increased vagal activity, reduced sympathetic activity, intrinsic pacemaker changes or an alteration in conduction. To determine the mechanism, experiments must measure or manipulate relevant pathways.

Receptor occupancy offers a simple connection between chemical signals and responses. In an ideal equilibrium model for a single receptor population, fractional occupancy is C divided by the sum of C and KD, where C is the free ligand concentration and KD is the equilibrium dissociation constant in the same concentration units. If C equals KD, half the receptors are occupied. If C is nine times KD, occupancy is 0.90. This relationship assumes an independent binding population at equilibrium, without cooperativity, and describes binding rather than physiological effect. Receptor reserve, signal amplification, desensitisation and competing pathways can cause the concentration-response curve to differ from the occupancy curve. Students should not equate fifty percent receptor occupancy with exactly fifty percent change in heart rate or smooth muscle force.

These three examples show why a numerical result is most useful when accompanied by a statement of what has been measured or modelled. A conduction delay describes arrival of a nerve signal, not necessarily onset of an organ response. A heart-rate change describes the organ, not the division responsible. An occupancy calculation describes binding, not the full downstream biological response. The distinction between these levels is central to interpreting autonomic experiments.

Model-based interpretation of autonomic responses

A simplified model of cardiac autonomic control can describe the change in measured rate as the sum of a sympathetic contribution and a vagal contribution. Suppose a teaching preparation has an intrinsic rate of 96 beats/min under combined blockade, and separate measurements suggest that sympathetic input adds 12 beats/min while vagal input subtracts 30 beats/min. A purely additive approximation predicts a net rate of 78 beats/min. Removing only the vagal contribution would predict 108 beats/min if the sympathetic contribution remained unchanged; removing only sympathetic influence would predict 66 beats/min if vagal influence remained unchanged.

This calculation is useful for understanding the direction of change, but it relies on an important approximation. The two pathways may interact at the level of receptors, second messengers and neural terminals, and changes in heart rate can alter other physiological variables that feed back onto the controller. In an intact animal, blocking one pathway may therefore cause changes in the other pathway. The numerical subtraction cannot automatically be applied to real measurements of resting heart rate and intrinsic heart rate to calculate exact sympathetic and parasympathetic firing rates. Those rates are distinct variables that would require appropriate neural measurements.

Resistance provides another useful connection between neural output and organ function. In an ideal cylindrical tube with steady laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid, resistance is proportional to the inverse fourth power of radius if viscosity and length remain constant. An illustrative reduction in radius from 1.00 to 0.90 in relative units would increase resistance by a factor of 1 divided by 0.90 to the fourth power, approximately 1.52. At constant pressure difference, flow would fall to about 0.656 of its original value. This model shows why modest changes in the calibre of resistance vessels can have substantial effects on flow.

Real circulation is more complex than a fixed-pressure rigid tube. Blood viscosity can vary with vessel diameter and flow conditions, vessels are compliant, vascular beds have multiple branching pathways, and local autoregulation can alter the response. A neural signal that constricts one vascular region may change the pressure difference across it as well as its radius. The fourth-power relationship is therefore useful for directional and approximate quantitative reasoning when its assumptions are given, not a complete prediction of whole-organ perfusion in an intact animal.

A simple receptor-binding example can also illustrate the effect of a competitive antagonist. Under an ideal equilibrium model, a reversible competitive antagonist shifts the concentration of agonist required to produce a given receptor occupancy. If the antagonist concentration is equal to three times its inhibition constant, the apparent agonist dissociation constant is increased by a factor of four. At an agonist concentration equal to the original dissociation constant, the predicted agonist occupancy in this simplified model is then one fifth rather than one half. Increasing agonist concentration sufficiently can overcome the competition at equilibrium. The constants and concentrations must use the same units, and the result depends on competition for a common binding site without additional allosteric or kinetic effects.

A concentration-response shift should not be confused with a shift in the anatomical pathway. A muscarinic antagonist acting at an effector can shift the response to externally applied acetylcholine without changing the identity of the ganglionic transmitter. Similarly, a neuronal nicotinic receptor antagonist may reduce the response to preganglionic stimulation without changing the sensitivity of the effector's adrenergic receptors. Comparing the two stimulus-response relationships provides a way to infer the site at which a physiological signal has been interrupted.

A second type of quantitative reasoning concerns the relative timing of fast and slow nerve components. Consider two recording electrodes 0.12 m apart along the same nerve. A reproducible fast component arrives at the second electrode 2 ms after passing the first, while a slower component requires 60 ms. The calculated velocities are 0.12 divided by 0.002, or 60 m/s, and 0.12 divided by 0.060, or 2 m/s. The results are consistent with a fast myelinated population and a much slower population, but velocity alone does not identify whether the slower fibres are sensory or autonomic motor axons. Further identification is required. The advantage of recording at two points is that the propagation estimate need not include the time required for stimulus initiation or target-organ activation.

Suppose an experiment records both postganglionic discharge and force generated by a vascular smooth muscle preparation. Before an intervention, nerve stimulation produces 20 impulses per second in the recorded postganglionic neuron and a force increase of 4 mN. Afterwards, the recorded firing rate remains 20 impulses per second, but the force increase is only 1 mN. The result establishes that the measured decrease in force is not accompanied by a reduction in the measured firing rate. It is consistent with an alteration of transmitter release per impulse, target receptor response or contractile function. It does not exclude a change in other unrecorded neurons. A transmitter assay and direct agonist stimulation would provide information about release and effector responsiveness, respectively.

In another illustrative preparation, local neural blockade changes the pressure required to propel a bolus through a segment of gut. If the pressure behind the bolus rises from 5 to 8 arbitrary pressure units while the pressure ahead rises from 2 to 7, the difference decreases from 3 to 1 unit. Greater pressure behind the bolus is thus compatible with a smaller driving difference across it. The result illustrates why a stronger contraction at one location does not necessarily improve propulsion when the downstream segment fails to relax. This simple pressure comparison omits friction, geometry and wall elasticity, but identifies the direction of the change in the driving pressure.

Autonomic reasoning often requires combining a numerical measurement with anatomical and chemical constraints. A delayed organ response could result from slower axonal conduction or from slower intracellular signalling. A reduced contractile response with preserved discharge could arise from impaired transmitter release or impaired receptor function. A drug-induced increase in transmitter concentration could arise from reduced clearance rather than increased neuronal firing. In each case, the number itself is informative only when the level of the circuit being measured is specified. The aim is to infer a causal mechanism using multiple observations, while recognising the limitations of simplified models and the possibility of alternative explanations.

12. Misconceptions and examination traps

All nerves contain only motor fibres

Mixed nerves contain both sensory and motor fibres. Classify individual axons, not the nerve by one endpoint. A sensory loss and motor deficit after one interruption can have a common anatomical cause.

Dorsal ramus is equivalent to dorsal root

A dorsal root is afferent; a dorsal ramus is generally mixed. Exams may replace the word root with ramus to test whether the distinction is understood.

Every peripheral ganglion is a synaptic relay

A dorsal root ganglion houses sensory cell bodies; a typical sensory impulse does not require a synapse there. A ganglionic receptor blocker therefore does not block every sensory ganglion.

Every autonomic pathway is a two-neuron axonal chain

The adrenal medulla uses a preganglionic synapse onto chromaffin cells with endocrine release. Classify by pathway anatomy rather than imposing a conventional postganglionic axon.

All sympathetic terminals release norepinephrine

Most eccrine sweat gland sympathetic fibres release ACh. Identify outflow before transmitter identity, particularly when an exam asks whether sweating is parasympathetic.

Every cholinergic receptor is muscarinic

Ganglionic and skeletal muscle nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated channels; muscarinic receptors are GPCRs. Distinguish receptor type and location.

ACh is always excitatory

Cardiac M2 signalling slows pacemaker activity, while nicotinic transmission depolarises ganglia. Identify the receptor and downstream target before assigning the response.

All beta receptors increase contraction

Beta2 stimulation may relax smooth muscle despite increasing cAMP. The contractile mechanism determines the result, not simply the direction of a second messenger.

Sympathetic fibres leave the cord at cervical levels to reach the head

Thoracolumbar preganglionic fibres may ascend the sympathetic trunk. Trace origin and relay separately.

All splanchnic nerves carry sympathetic preganglionic fibres

Pelvic splanchnic nerves carry sacral parasympathetic fibres; cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves commonly carry sympathetic postganglionic fibres. Names do not specify a universal fibre class.

Every organ has opposing dual innervation

Many vessels and sweat glands mainly receive sympathetic innervation. Reduced sympathetic tone can cause relaxation without parasympathetic activation.

Resting means sympathetic activity is absent

Basal vasoconstrictor tone can persist at rest. Interpret withdrawal of a tonic pathway as an active physiological change.

Vagal activity is entirely parasympathetic motor traffic

Vagus contains extensive afferent as well as motor components. An intact-nerve recording does not identify fibre direction.

Autonomic means every involuntary movement is visceral

Skeletal respiratory muscles and withdrawal reflexes use somatic motor neurons. The effector and peripheral pathway define the category.

Enteric activity ceases when the vagus is cut

Local enteric circuits can retain reflexes without CNS input. Extrinsic modulation and local generation are distinct.

A stronger gut contraction always produces faster propulsion

Downstream relaxation is essential for many propulsive patterns. Increased pressure on both sides can reduce the driving difference.

A drug-induced reduction of organ response proves that nerve conduction stopped

Effector receptors and transmitter release can fail despite preserved impulses. Combine neural recordings with direct agonist stimulation.

Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Mixed sensory and motor

The dorsal root contributes sensory fibres and the ventral root contributes motor fibres. Their union forms a mixed nerve. A ganglion is a collection of cell bodies rather than this bundle of axons.

  2. Dorsal root ganglion
  3. Sympathetic chain ganglion
  4. Neuromuscular end plate
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Dorsal root ganglion

The pseudounipolar spinal sensory cells have somata in the dorsal root ganglion. The ventral horn contains somatic motor cells, whereas the sympathetic chain is a visceral motor relay.

  2. Parasympathetic pathway to the heart
  3. Somatic pathway to skeletal muscle
  4. Parasympathetic pathway to a salivary gland
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Somatic pathway to skeletal muscle

The lower somatic motor neuron projects directly to skeletal muscle; the ordinary autonomic pathways have a preganglionic and a postganglionic neuron.

4. The fast excitatory transmitter-receptor pair at a conventional autonomic ganglion is:

  2. Acetylcholine–neuronal nicotinic
  3. Acetylcholine–M2
  4. Dopamine–D2

Correct answer: B. Acetylcholine–neuronal nicotinic

ACh activates ligand-gated neuronal nicotinic receptors at the fast synapse in both major autonomic divisions. M2 and adrenergic receptors typically mediate metabotropic modulation.

  2. Thoracolumbar

Correct answer: B. Thoracolumbar

The main source is the thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord, approximately T1–L2. The trunk distributes output to distant levels, including the head.

The III, VII, IX and X pathways have parasympathetic components; the other groupings contain predominantly sensory or somatic motor nerves.

  2. Cranial parasympathetic preganglionic fibres

  4. Optic afferents

Correct answer: A. Postganglionic sympathetic fibres

Gray rami return predominantly unmyelinated sympathetic postganglionic fibres to spinal nerves. White rami are associated with entry of preganglionic sympathetic fibres at thoracolumbar levels.

  1. Ventricular myocardium
  2. Most human eccrine sweat glands
  3. Pupillary radial dilator muscle
  4. Most resistance arterioles

Correct answer: B. Most human eccrine sweat glands

Sympathetic eccrine gland fibres typically release ACh onto muscarinic receptors. The other listed sympathetic targets commonly use adrenergic signalling.

  2. A postganglionic parasympathetic axon releasing dopamine
  3. Preganglionic sympathetic ACh acting at nicotinic receptors
  4. A direct optic nerve ending
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Preganglionic sympathetic ACh acting at nicotinic receptors

Chromaffin cells receive cholinergic preganglionic sympathetic input and release catecholamines into blood. They function as modified sympathetic effector cells rather than somatic muscle.

  2. Gi/o
  3. Gq/11 exclusively
  4. G12/13 exclusively
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Gi/o

  2. M2
  3. α1
  4. Nicotinic Nm
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. α1

Alpha1 receptors activate PLC-associated pathways that support calcium-dependent contraction. Beta2 signalling can instead relax appropriate smooth muscle.

  2. Feedback inhibition of further transmitter release
  3. Direct skeletal muscle contraction
  4. Elimination of ganglionic ACh

  4. Nm

Beta2 signalling promotes airway smooth muscle relaxation. M3-mediated cholinergic action commonly supports bronchoconstriction; Nm is the skeletal neuromuscular receptor.

  3. Myenteric plexus

  4. Keratin

Nitric oxide generated by inhibitory enteric neurons can activate soluble guanylyl cyclase and promote relaxation. The other choices do not constitute this neural transmitter mechanism.

  2. Ongoing neural influence on a target's operating state

Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Ongoing neural influence on a target's operating state

Tonic activity permits increases and decreases around a baseline. Withdrawal of vasoconstrictor tone, for example, can produce dilation.

  4. Becomes identical to cardiac motor activity

Increased arterial wall stretch normally increases receptor discharge, which initiates compensatory autonomic responses. Afferent activity is distinct from motor discharge.

Original practice questions. These are not previous-year examination questions.

1. A dorsal root is completely interrupted while its ventral root remains intact. Which prediction is best for the affected segment?

  1. Afferent entry is impaired, but efferent conduction in the intact ventral root can persist
  2. All ventral horn cells necessarily die immediately
  3. Only autonomic ganglia lose nicotinic receptors
  4. Both roots remain functionally normal
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Afferent entry is impaired, but efferent conduction in the intact ventral root can persist

The dorsal root carries central sensory processes. Interruption impairs entry of those signals, while the intact ventral root can still conduct motor output. The resulting reflex can be lost because its sensory limb is interrupted, not because the motor axon is necessarily damaged.

  1. Vascular α1 receptor
  2. Ganglionic nicotinic synapse or upstream preganglionic conduction
  3. Smooth muscle contractile apparatus
  4. All postganglionic axons
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Ganglionic nicotinic synapse or upstream preganglionic conduction

Both downstream tests remain effective, excluding complete failure of the final effector and postganglionic route. The defect lies before the downstream stimulus site; ganglionic blockade is one possibility but not uniquely proved without a preganglionic conduction recording.

3. An intact parasympathetic cardiac pathway is tested after selective muscarinic blockade at the heart. Which combination is expected?

  1. Ganglionic fast transmission abolished, cardiac response retained
  2. Ganglionic fast transmission retained, vagally evoked slowing reduced
  3. Both neuronal nicotinic current and cardiac M2 response necessarily enhanced
  4. Vagus becomes a sympathetic nerve
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Ganglionic fast transmission retained, vagally evoked slowing reduced

Fast ganglionic transmission depends primarily on neuronal nicotinic receptors, whereas the heart's slowing depends importantly on M2 signalling. A selective target blocker separates these two synaptic levels.

4. A selective α1 antagonist is applied to a vascular effector. The postganglionic firing rate is unchanged. What is the best prediction?

  1. Neural firing must cease
  2. The α1-mediated contraction decreases despite retained neural activity
  3. The effector becomes skeletal muscle
  4. Preganglionic ACh release is necessarily eliminated
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The α1-mediated contraction decreases despite retained neural activity

The antagonist acts downstream from conduction and release. Preserved discharge does not guarantee preserved receptor activation. Any remaining contraction may involve incomplete blockade or other mechanisms.

5. An isolated gut segment shows a polarised response after extrinsic nerves are cut. Local neural blockade removes it, but direct muscle stimulation still works. What follows?

  1. The tested pattern requires local neural activity, not ongoing extrinsic input
  2. All gastrointestinal regulation is independent of the brain in vivo
  3. Smooth muscle is irreversibly damaged
  4. The local pattern must be due only to circulating adrenaline
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The tested pattern requires local neural activity, not ongoing extrinsic input

The preparation isolates the local circuitry from central input and the direct muscle test excludes total loss of contractility. The result establishes a local neural contribution under the tested conditions, not universal independence of the intact digestive system.

6. A gut segment contracts more strongly oral to a bolus after inhibition is blocked, but pressure ahead also rises. Which outcome is possible?

  1. Propulsion must improve in proportion to oral contraction
  2. Propulsion worsens because downstream relaxation is impaired
  3. Pressure gradients become irrelevant
  4. All neural responses cease
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Propulsion worsens because downstream relaxation is impaired

Efficient propulsion depends on coordinated contraction behind and low resistance ahead. Increased force at one site is insufficient when inhibitory relaxation is lost; local force and net transport are distinct endpoints.

  1. 23 ms
  2. 110 ms
  3. 213 ms
  4. 310 ms

Correct answer: C. 213 ms

Preganglionic time = 0.15/15 = 0.010 s = 10 ms. Postganglionic time = 0.18/0.9 = 0.20 s = 200 ms. Add 3 ms to obtain 213 ms, excluding terminal and effector kinetics.

  1. Decrease by 0.20 s
  2. Increase by 0.20 s
  3. Increase by 0.80 s
  4. No change
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Increase by 0.20 s

Initial interval = 60/100 = 0.60 s; final interval = 60/75 = 0.80 s. The increase is 0.20 s. A rate decrease cannot be interpreted as an equal linear decrease in interval.

  4. Velocity is 0.04 m/s

10. If ligand concentration is 3KD in a single-site equilibrium model, fractional receptor occupancy is:

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.75
  4. 1.00
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 0.75

Occupancy = C/(C + KD) = 3/(3+1) = 0.75. The calculation describes receptor binding, not necessarily 75% of the maximal organ response.

  1. 0.5 μM
  2. 2 μM
  3. 6 μM
  4. 8 μM

Correct answer: D. 8 μM

The apparent KD is KD(1 + [I]/Ki) = 2 × (1+3) = 8 μM. Half occupancy occurs at the apparent KD in this model. This assumes reversible competition and matched concentration units.

  2. All α receptors are postsynaptic

The central circuit receives no effective signal from the left eye but receives the right-eye input and distributes it to both intact efferents. This distinguishes afferent interruption from a unilateral motor defect.

  2. The heart cannot beat without autonomic input

  4. Remove all physiological fluid from the recipient
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Transfer matched donor fluid without nerve stimulation

An unstimulated fluid-transfer control matches the handling and solution conditions while omitting the proposed trigger. A response specific to stimulated fluid supports transferable chemical activity, with further tests needed to identify its molecule.

21. A drug reduces nerve-evoked smooth muscle force, but a high concentration of direct agonist still evokes maximal force. Which additional test best distinguishes reduced receptor sensitivity from reduced neurotransmitter release?

  1. Compare a full direct agonist concentration-response curve and measure evoked transmitter
  2. Count the number of unrelated cranial nerves
  3. Measure only maximal agonist force again
  4. Assume normal receptor sensitivity because maximal force persists
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Compare a full direct agonist concentration-response curve and measure evoked transmitter

A maximal response may persist despite a shift in sensitivity. A full curve tests effector responsiveness over concentrations; an evoked transmitter assay helps identify release or clearance changes.

22. A sympathetic nerve to skin is interrupted distal to its ganglion, but the sweat glands remain viable. Which combination is expected?

  1. Local nerve-evoked sweating decreases; direct muscarinic stimulation may still work
  2. Sweating increases necessarily because all sympathetic fibres are inhibitory
  3. All parasympathetic ganglia fail
  4. Direct muscarinic stimulation becomes impossible by definition
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Local nerve-evoked sweating decreases; direct muscarinic stimulation may still work

Eccrine glands receive predominantly cholinergic sympathetic motor input. Distal denervation disrupts nerve-mediated secretion while a viable gland may respond to a directly applied muscarinic agonist.

15. Original SET and integrated practice

Original practice questions. These are not previous-year examination questions.

1. Which statement pair is correct? I. ACh is released at conventional sympathetic ganglia. II. Most sympathetic eccrine gland terminals release ACh.

  1. I only
  2. II only
  3. Both I and II
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Both I and II

Both are correct. Ganglionic ACh is the common preganglionic signal; sweat glands are a standard exception to the usual postganglionic adrenergic pattern.

2. Which statement pair is correct? I. All dorsal rami are exclusively sensory. II. Dorsal roots carry sensory fibres.

  1. I only
  2. II only
  3. Both
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. II only

Dorsal rami are generally mixed; dorsal roots convey afferents. The difference is their position relative to the union of roots.

3. Assertion: Most human eccrine sweat glands are sympathetically innervated. Reason: Their postganglionic nerves predominantly release ACh onto muscarinic receptors.

  1. Both true and reason establishes sympathetic identity
  2. Both true but reason does not establish sympathetic identity
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Both true but reason does not establish sympathetic identity

Both statements are true, but sympathetic identity follows anatomical origin and routing. Cholinergic signalling alone cannot establish division identity, because parasympathetic pathways also use ACh.

4. Assertion: Enteric short reflexes can occur without CNS input. Reason: Enteric networks contain intrinsic sensory, interneuronal and motor elements.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Both true and reason explains assertion

A local circuit can receive a stimulus, process it and execute an output because the necessary neuronal elements are present within the organ wall.

5. Assertion: Reduced sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone can dilate many resistance vessels. Reason: Every resistance vessel receives a direct antagonistic parasympathetic supply.

  1. Both true and explanatory
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Assertion true, reason false

Withdrawal of ongoing constrictor input permits relaxation. Many systemic vessels do not have substantial direct parasympathetic innervation.

6. Match the receptor to its typical effector action: M2, α1, β2.

  1. Cardiac slowing, vascular contraction, bronchial relaxation
  2. Vascular contraction, bronchial relaxation, cardiac slowing
  3. Skeletal end plate, cardiac slowing, ganglionic excitation
  4. Bronchial relaxation, skeletal contraction, retinal sensation
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Cardiac slowing, vascular contraction, bronchial relaxation

M2 contributes to cardiac slowing; alpha1 promotes vascular contraction and beta2 can relax bronchial smooth muscle. Receptor location and coupling provide the mechanism.

7. Which pathway bypasses the usual autonomic postganglionic axon?

  1. Motor supply to an ordinary arteriole
  2. Adrenal medullary pathway
  3. Cardiac vagal pathway
  4. Iris sphincter pathway
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Adrenal medullary pathway

The preganglionic sympathetic fibre activates chromaffin cells, which secrete catecholamines into the blood. The other examples ordinarily use ganglionic postganglionic neurons.

8. A 0.06 m nerve segment conducts at 3 m/s. How long does propagation alone take?

  1. 2 ms
  2. 20 ms
  3. 50 ms
  4. 180 ms
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 20 ms

0.06/3 = 0.02 s = 20 ms. Do not add synaptic or tissue latency unless the question provides those terms.

Revision and mastery

Reconstruct the pathway before predicting a response. Identify afferent versus efferent, central origin, ganglion, transmitter, receptor, target and any ongoing tone. Use receptor coupling and effector physiology to explain the direction of change.

Key terms: afferent, efferent, mixed nerve, sensory ganglion, preganglionic, postganglionic, sympathetic trunk, white and gray rami, terminal ganglion, nicotinic, muscarinic, adrenergic, autonomic tone, enteric plexus, visceral reflex.

Key quantitative relations: propagation time = L/v; cardiac interval = 60/heart rate in seconds; simple equilibrium occupancy = C/(C + KD); ideal vessel resistance ∝ 1/r⁴. Each requires its own stated assumptions.
