1. Why cells need a separate oxidative compartment

A liver cell handles a remarkable variety of organic molecules. Among them are fatty acids that are too long to be efficiently handled by the usual mitochondrial pathways. At the same time, a newly germinating oilseed must make carbohydrates from its stored lipids before the developing shoot can sustain itself by photosynthesis. A mature green leaf has a different problem: it must recover carbon from a compound generated when Rubisco reacts with oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. These apparently unrelated problems share an organelle. In each case, a peroxisome provides a place for specific metabolic reactions that involve oxidation, especially reactions in which oxygen can be converted into hydrogen peroxide.

The underlying problem is chemical rather than anatomical. Oxidation means loss of electrons, not necessarily addition of oxygen. When a substrate loses electrons, some other molecule must accept them. Cells can use NAD⁺, flavins and molecular oxygen as parts of electron-transfer reactions. In certain reactions, oxygen itself accepts two electrons and two protons and forms hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Hydrogen peroxide is useful in controlled amounts, but a persistent excess can damage cellular constituents. The cell therefore faces a design problem: how can it exploit oxidative enzymes without exposing every other cellular reaction to their reactive products?

Recall the lysosome and vacuole. Their membranes help create specialized environments for digestion or storage. A peroxisome follows the same general principle of compartmentalization, but the chemistry is different. Its defining reactions are not the acid hydrolysis of macromolecules. Instead, it brings together selected oxidative enzymes and protective systems, especially catalase. The same compartment may also support reactions that make valuable lipids or recover carbon in plant metabolism. Compartmentalization makes the intermediates available to the next enzyme and helps regulate their access to the rest of the cell.

A second principle is just as important: a compartment does not have to perform a whole metabolic pathway by itself. Products made by one organelle may be processed by another. In a mammalian cell, the peroxisome can shorten a fatty acid and the mitochondrion can continue its oxidation. In a leaf, reactions distributed across chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria together recover carbon. The organelles are distinct, yet their physiological functions are connected.

By the end of this chapter, it should be possible to infer what an unfamiliar peroxisomal enzyme does from its substrates and products, predict the effect of removing catalase or disrupting protein import, follow the movement of carbon between organelles, and distinguish experimentally a peroxisome from another small membrane-bounded organelle. The emphasis is on explaining the organization that makes these activities possible, rather than memorizing a list of enzyme names.

2. Structure: a single membrane around a specialized matrix

Under an electron microscope, a typical peroxisome appears as a small rounded or elongated structure enclosed by one membrane. Many are approximately 0.1–1 micrometre across, although size and shape depend on the organism, tissue, developmental stage and nutritional conditions. The older descriptive term microbody was applied to certain structures of this kind before their biochemical identities were clear. Peroxisomes occur in many eukaryotic cells, including animal, fungal and plant cells, but their abundance and enzyme content vary greatly. Mature mammalian red blood cells, which have lost their usual membrane-bound organelles, are an obvious exception to the idea that every eukaryotic cell contains them.

The limiting membrane is a single lipid bilayer, not a single layer of lipid molecules. Its cytosolic face contacts the cytoplasm; the opposite face contacts the peroxisomal matrix. The matrix contains a concentrated mixture of soluble enzymes. Some peroxisomes display an electron-dense crystalline or paracrystalline inclusion. In certain mammalian species, a crystalloid of urate oxidase is an example. Such an inclusion is not a nucleoid or a ribosome. Human beings lack functional urate oxidase, so the presence of a urate oxidase crystalloid cannot be a universal criterion for human peroxisomes. Many ordinary peroxisomes have no conspicuous crystalline core.

Unlike mitochondria and plastids, peroxisomes do not contain their own genome or ribosomes. Their proteins are encoded by nuclear genes and made on cytosolic ribosomes. A visible organelle may contain numerous copies of a particular enzyme without being able to synthesize that enzyme internally. It must obtain new proteins through a selective targeting system, which will be examined after their physiological roles.

The membrane is neither an absolute barrier to all small molecules nor a freely permeable bag. Metabolic substrates and products have to enter and leave; membrane proteins support selective exchange, including transport of fatty-acid substrates and smaller metabolites. Large soluble matrix enzymes require dedicated import machinery. The membrane also provides a surface for the insertion of new membrane proteins and for growth and division of the organelle. Unlike the mitochondrial inner membrane, it does not possess a respiratory electron-transport chain coupled to ATP synthase.

A useful distinction is between structural identity and functional specialization. Two peroxisomes can possess similar membranes and import machinery yet contain different sets of enzymes. A leaf peroxisome contains machinery for photorespiration; a specialized peroxisome in an oil-rich seed contains machinery for lipid mobilization and the glyoxylate cycle. They are not fundamentally different classes of membrane-bound organelles. The identity of an organelle is maintained by its membrane and targeting system, while the activities of an individual organelle reflect the proteins imported into it.

At the cellular scale, the small size creates a relatively high membrane area per volume. For a spherical model with radius r, surface area is 4πr² and volume is 4πr³/3, so the area-to-volume ratio is 3/r. A peroxisome of diameter 0.50 µm has radius 0.25 µm and an area-to-volume ratio of 12 µm⁻¹. A sphere of diameter 1.0 µm has a ratio of 6 µm⁻¹. This explains geometrically why small compartments can provide more interface for exchange per unit volume. It does not prove that a smaller peroxisome exchanges every substrate twice as fast: actual transport also depends on protein density, concentration gradients and membrane permeability. The geometrical result is a useful prerequisite for understanding organelle growth and division.

3. Discovery and experimental identification of peroxisomes

Microscopy initially identified small single-membrane structures, but appearance alone did not reveal their purpose. A cell contains many rounded vesicles. If a biologist sees a small body near a mitochondrion, neither its location nor its diameter is sufficient to establish its identity. Biochemical evidence was essential. In the 1950s microbodies were described in electron micrographs. Christian de Duve and Pierre Baudhuin subsequently synthesized evidence that these particles housed enzymes of peroxide metabolism; their 1966 review helped establish the peroxisome as a distinct organelle.

The reasoning behind cell fractionation is worth understanding. When cells are broken gently, many organelles remain intact. Centrifugation separates particles partly according to their size, shape and density. A first low-speed step removes much nuclear material; higher forces collect progressively smaller components. Peroxisomes, lysosomes and mitochondria can overlap in a crude particulate fraction. A density gradient can improve the separation by allowing particles to settle in regions matching their buoyant density. It is not correct to imagine that every organelle can always be collected as a perfectly pure pellet at a unique centrifugation speed.

Scientists can identify fractions by measuring marker enzyme activities. Catalase is a useful peroxisomal marker in many cell types. Acid phosphatase is a traditional lysosomal marker, whereas succinate dehydrogenase is associated with the mitochondrial inner membrane. If a fraction contains much catalase and an oxidase but very little acid phosphatase or succinate dehydrogenase, the biochemical pattern supports the existence of a distinct oxidative compartment. If all markers appear together, poor separation is a plausible explanation. A marker alone does not prove complete purity because some proteins can be found in multiple locations or released during cell disruption.

Consider the illustrative fractionation results below. All three columns show specific activity, in arbitrary units per milligram of fraction protein. Fraction B is enriched in catalase compared with the other fractions, while A is rich in mitochondrial marker activity and C in lysosomal marker activity. Relative enrichment is not the same as total recovery. A fraction can contain very little total protein and have high activity per milligram while recovering only a small fraction of the original enzyme. To calculate recovery, multiply specific activity by protein mass and compare the total activity with that in the starting homogenate.

Another approach is enzyme cytochemistry. A reaction that produces an electron-dense deposit where catalase is active can reveal the location of enzyme-containing structures under the electron microscope. Antibodies against catalase or peroxisomal membrane proteins offer an independent way to localize them. Today, fluorescent proteins bearing a peroxisomal targeting signal can reveal small fluorescent puncta inside living cells. The important idea is not the details of these techniques but the convergence of structural, biochemical and localization evidence. Punctate fluorescence is not automatically proof of peroxisomes: aggregates and other organelles also appear as puncta. Co-localization with an established marker and a targeting-signal control make the interpretation stronger.

The discovery thus illustrates a general principle of experimental cell biology. A membrane identifies a physical boundary; enzymes identify biochemical capacity; a targeting experiment identifies the mechanism that maintains the capacity. A strong conclusion combines them. This is the basis for using catalase and oxidases in exam problems that distinguish peroxisomes from lysosomes and mitochondria.

4. Oxidases, oxygen and the formation of hydrogen peroxide

An oxidase is an enzyme that transfers electrons from a substrate to molecular oxygen. Not all oxidases produce the same oxygen-reduction product. Some reduce oxygen to water; a number of characteristic peroxisomal oxidases instead reduce it to hydrogen peroxide. It is therefore the chemistry of a specified enzyme, rather than its name alone, that determines what is formed.

For a simplified two-electron reaction, use RH₂ to represent a reduced organic substrate and R to represent the corresponding oxidized product. The substrate half-reaction is RH₂ → R + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. The oxygen half-reaction is O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O₂. Adding them cancels the protons and electrons and gives the net equation RH₂ + O₂ → R + H₂O₂. The equation is a general bookkeeping model for this type of oxidase reaction, not a universal equation for every enzyme called an oxidase. In the combined reaction one mole of substrate supplies the electrons required to make one mole of peroxide.

Many peroxisomal oxidases use a flavin cofactor such as FAD or FMN. In a schematic FAD-dependent reaction, electrons removed from the organic substrate first reduce enzyme-bound FAD to FADH₂. The reduced cofactor is subsequently reoxidized by oxygen, producing H₂O₂. The enzyme's cofactor is regenerated so it can participate in another catalytic turnover. The flavin is not consumed in a one-to-one ratio with substrate. The exact enzyme chemistry and cofactor differ among substrates. For example, acyl-CoA oxidase initiates fatty-acid β-oxidation inside peroxisomes, whereas glycolate oxidase in green leaves oxidizes glycolate to glyoxylate.

This chemistry immediately distinguishes the peroxisome from the mitochondrial respiratory pathway. In mitochondria, electrons from many reactions pass through carriers to the respiratory chain. Their transfer is linked to generation of a proton-motive force across the inner membrane and ultimately to ATP synthesis. In the characteristic peroxide-producing peroxisomal reaction, electrons pass to oxygen without entering such a chain in the peroxisome. Oxygen consumption by a particle is therefore not sufficient evidence for oxidative phosphorylation. One must determine where the electrons go, whether a proton gradient is made and whether ATP synthase is present.

Hydrogen peroxide belongs to the broad family of reactive oxygen species (ROS). It is not itself a free radical: unlike superoxide (O₂•⁻) and the hydroxyl radical (•OH), H₂O₂ has no unpaired electron. Nevertheless, its relative persistence makes it biologically significant. In the presence of suitable transition metal ions it can contribute to the production of very reactive radicals; it can also modify susceptible protein groups in regulated redox signaling. A simplified Fenton reaction is Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH. The hydroxyl radical is highly reactive and can attack nearby molecules. This explains why peroxide management matters even though peroxide is not a radical.

The organelle does not convert every oxidative reaction into a harmless event. Peroxide may cross biological membranes to varying degrees and may influence neighboring regions. Cellular protection therefore requires active removal by enzymes, not merely placement of a lipid bilayer around an oxidase. The next question is how the peroxisome matches its oxidizing capacity with its capacity to consume the peroxide.

5. Catalase: two chemical solutions to peroxide removal

Catalase is an enzyme containing a heme group, in which iron is coordinated by an organic ring. The heme participates in reversible changes of oxidation state that allow the enzyme to react rapidly with hydrogen peroxide. Catalase is abundant in many peroxisomes, and its exceptionally high catalytic capacity is especially useful when the peroxide-producing reactions are operating at substantial rates. Its activity is not equivalent to physically trapping peroxide in the organelle.

The familiar catalatic reaction is 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. It is called a disproportionation because molecules of the same chemical species undergo both oxidation and reduction. Oxygen has oxidation number −1 in H₂O₂, −2 in H₂O and 0 in O₂. One peroxide molecule is reduced while the other is oxidized. The iron-containing catalytic intermediate is first formed when peroxide reacts with the enzyme; a second peroxide molecule restores the resting enzyme and releases oxygen. The enzyme itself is regenerated. The detailed electronic structure of this intermediate is not required to reason about the net reaction, but the two stages explain why two peroxide molecules are used for every oxygen molecule released.

A second possible mode is the peroxidatic reaction. A suitable reduced electron donor, represented as AH₂, is oxidized while peroxide is reduced: H₂O₂ + AH₂ → 2H₂O + A. The donor rather than a second peroxide supplies the reducing equivalents. In a classical example, ethanol can act as a donor and is converted into acetaldehyde: CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O₂ → CH₃CHO + 2H₂O. This demonstrates the chemical principle of coupled oxidation; it does not establish peroxisomal catalase as the main route of alcohol metabolism in the human liver. In that tissue alcohol dehydrogenase is generally the major route under ordinary conditions. Which catalase activity predominates depends on the substrates, peroxide availability and conditions.

The difference between the two modes is observable. During catalatic turnover, oxygen is a product. During the idealized peroxidatic turnover, peroxide consumption is coupled to donor oxidation and no oxygen is released by that reaction. Both reactions remove peroxide, but counting oxygen bubbles alone therefore measures only part of peroxide removal if both modes operate. Other peroxide-removing enzymes, such as peroxidases and peroxiredoxins, also occur in cells; some peroxisomes possess additional antioxidant systems. A catalase-deficient cell may retain some peroxide-scavenging capacity, so complete loss of antioxidant function is not the automatic conclusion.

To see how stoichiometry predicts a real measurement, imagine a closed reaction containing 6.0 µmol of hydrogen peroxide and sufficient catalase. If the peroxide is completely decomposed only by the catalatic reaction, oxygen production is 6.0/2 = 3.0 µmol and water production is 6.0 µmol. If 2.0 µmol of peroxide instead goes through the peroxidatic route with a suitable donor, only 4.0 µmol remains for catalatic decomposition; the oxygen yield becomes 2.0 µmol. In an actual gas measurement one must allow for dissolved oxygen, gas exchange and other oxygen-consuming reactions. The theoretical yield is a stoichiometric maximum under the stated assumptions, not a guarantee of measured gas volume.

Catalase may be detected by adding peroxide to an enzyme-containing preparation and observing oxygen production, or by measuring disappearance of peroxide spectrophotometrically. In a simplified optical assay at 240 nm, peroxide contributes to absorbance, so a falling signal can indicate its removal. Proper controls are important because other substances can absorb light and peroxide can decompose without enzyme. A blank lacking enzyme and a control containing heat-inactivated enzyme help identify the enzyme-dependent contribution. These experimental observations provide a bridge from the chemical equation to a cell-level prediction: decreased catalase activity can raise peroxide exposure even when oxidase activity has not increased.

6. Peroxide homeostasis: reaction rates and compartmentalization

It is tempting to say that a peroxisome contains catalase and is therefore always protected against oxidative stress. The correct description is a dynamic balance between the rate at which peroxide is produced, its enzymatic consumption and its movement out of the organelle. A high concentration of catalase helps but does not by itself guarantee a low peroxide concentration. The oxidase flux may increase when more fatty-acid substrate arrives; catalase activity may change; membrane exchange may change. A steady state exists only when the combined rates of removal equal production.

Let H represent the amount of peroxide in a peroxisomal population, in nmol, and let P be its production rate in nmol min⁻¹. Let C(H) be the rate at which catalase and other internal reactions remove peroxide, and E(H) its net outward transfer rate, both in nmol min⁻¹. Conservation of matter gives dH/dt = P − C(H) − E(H). This is a mass balance, not a full kinetic description of catalase. It is valid as written for the chosen population if all important sources and losses are included. If there is net inward peroxide movement, the transfer term can become negative. When P equals C + E, H remains constant, but individual molecules may still be produced and removed continuously.

A particularly simple model assumes that the combined removal rate is proportional to H over a limited range: C + E = kH, where k is a first-order rate constant in min⁻¹. Then dH/dt = P − kH. Setting the derivative to zero gives H* = P/k, the steady-state amount. If P = 12 nmol min⁻¹ and k = 3 min⁻¹, H* is 4 nmol. If a reduction in removal capacity lowers k to 1.5 min⁻¹ without changing P, the new steady state is 8 nmol. The amount doubles. The inference is a consequence of the linear model and fixed production; real antioxidant enzymes may become saturated and their regulation may change in a stressed cell.

If peroxide production stops, the same model becomes dH/dt = −kH. Separation and integration give ln(H/H₀) = −kt, or H = H₀e⁻ᵏᵗ. The half-time t½ is ln(2)/k. For k = 3 min⁻¹, this is 0.231 min, about 13.9 seconds. For k = 1.5 min⁻¹ it is 0.462 min, about 27.7 seconds. The time constant tells us how quickly the system responds, not just how much peroxide is present. This is why two compartments with the same measured peroxide amount can respond very differently to a sudden increase in substrate.

A separate stoichiometric balance explains oxygen measurements in intact peroxisomes. Suppose a peroxide-producing oxidase turns over at 10 µmol min⁻¹, consuming one oxygen and producing one peroxide per substrate molecule. If all peroxide undergoes catalatic decomposition, 5 µmol oxygen per minute is regenerated. The net oxygen consumption is therefore 5 µmol min⁻¹, although the oxidase actually consumes 10 µmol min⁻¹. If peroxide is removed entirely by a peroxidatic pathway instead, there is no oxygen regeneration from that route and net consumption becomes 10 µmol min⁻¹. An oxygen electrode records the net rate; it cannot by itself identify the individual enzyme fluxes. These predictions exclude other oxygen-consuming or oxygen-producing reactions.

Compartmentalization makes removal efficient by bringing peroxide-producing and peroxide-consuming enzymes into the same small environment. However, it does not require every peroxide molecule to meet catalase before leaving. At modest levels peroxide can participate in signaling, whereas excessive exposure can oxidize proteins and lipids or contribute to radical chemistry. Oxidative stress means that production and protective capacity are out of balance sufficiently to disturb cellular function. It does not mean that the cell contains any ROS at all. This distinction becomes particularly useful when interpreting loss-of-function experiments: reducing oxidase activity may reduce peroxide production but can simultaneously impair the useful metabolic pathway that originally required the oxidase.

7. Fatty-acid oxidation: why two organelles cooperate

Fatty acids store considerable chemical energy in their hydrocarbon chains, but cells also need to control the molecular composition of their lipids. A fatty acid that is unusually long or otherwise poorly suited to the conventional mitochondrial pathway can require prior processing. In mammals, peroxisomes are especially important in shortening very-long-chain fatty acids (often defined as fatty acids with at least 22 carbons) and processing certain branched or unusual fatty acids. Mitochondria are major sites of energy-yielding oxidation of many common fatty acids. This division of labour is not absolute: substrate preferences overlap, and the relative contribution varies among tissues and organisms.

Before a fatty acid enters a typical β-oxidation cycle, it is present as a fatty acyl-CoA, a thioester of coenzyme A. The name β-oxidation refers to the chemistry at the β-carbon, the second carbon beyond the carbonyl carbon in the acyl chain. Oxidation prepares the chain for cleavage between the α- and β-carbons. A standard cycle releases a two-carbon acetyl-CoA unit and produces an acyl-CoA shortened by two carbons. The shortened molecule may enter another cycle, allowing progressive processing rather than cleavage of the entire chain in one reaction.

Four transformations make up a usual peroxisomal β-oxidation cycle. First, acyl-CoA oxidase removes two hydrogen equivalents from the chain, introducing a double bond between the α- and β-carbons. Enzyme-bound FAD accepts the electrons and transfers them to oxygen, forming peroxide. Second, enoyl-CoA hydration adds water across the double bond, forming a β-hydroxyacyl-CoA. Third, a dehydrogenase oxidizes the hydroxyl group to a keto group while reducing NAD⁺ to NADH. Fourth, a thiolase uses CoA to cleave the β-ketoacyl-CoA, releasing acetyl-CoA and the shorter acyl-CoA. In mammalian peroxisomes some of these enzyme functions are grouped in multifunctional proteins. The four transformations nevertheless remain chemically distinguishable.

The mitochondrial pathway has a broadly similar carbon-cleavage logic, but its first oxidation uses acyl-CoA dehydrogenases whose electrons are transferred through electron-transferring flavoprotein and associated machinery toward the respiratory chain. In peroxisomes the first step sends electrons to oxygen, and the energy associated with that transfer is not conserved as ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. Peroxisomes lack the respiratory chain and ATP synthase that would be necessary for this local conversion. They can still generate acetyl-CoA and NADH, which are valuable metabolic products. Reducing equivalents and carbon can be exchanged with other compartments in appropriate forms. It would therefore be equally incorrect to say that peroxisomal metabolism contributes no usable energy to the cell or that the peroxisome itself makes ATP from the first oxidation step.

Exchange is required throughout the process. Long-chain acyl substrates are handled by peroxisomal membrane transport systems, including ABC transporters in mammals; the precise transport and activation details vary with substrate and organism. CoA, NAD⁺ and other cofactors must be available to the relevant enzymes, and reduced cofactors must be reoxidized so that the cycle can continue. NADH cannot simply accumulate indefinitely. Metabolite shuttle reactions help transfer reducing equivalents between compartments. Shortened fatty-acid products and acetyl units may leave in forms appropriate for onward metabolism, including carnitine derivatives in mammalian cells. The peroxisome thus participates in an interdependent metabolic network rather than acting as a miniature autonomous mitochondrion.

Consider a straight saturated C24 fatty acyl chain shortened to C16 in a peroxisome. Each ordinary cycle removes two carbon atoms. The required number of cycles is (24 − 16)/2 = 4. Four acetyl-CoA units are released, with eight carbons total; the remaining chain has sixteen carbons, so carbon is conserved. The four initial oxidase reactions produce four peroxide equivalents, and the four NAD⁺-dependent reactions produce four NADH equivalents in this simplified scheme. If all peroxide is decomposed catalatically, two oxygen equivalents are released from it. The calculation deliberately stops at C16, rather than assigning all mitochondrial ATP yields to the peroxisome. It also assumes an unbranched saturated substrate and excludes additional activation or auxiliary reactions.

For a fully processed straight even-carbon saturated chain containing n carbons, the familiar theoretical count is n/2 acetyl units and n/2 − 1 cycles, because the final four-carbon intermediate yields two acetyl units on its last cleavage. This general carbon-counting relationship is useful but should not be confused with the usual physiological endpoint of mammalian peroxisomal chain shortening. In a question that specifies an intermediate chain length, use the difference in lengths, not the full-oxidation formula.

Plants and many fungi arrange this metabolic division differently. In plants fatty-acid β-oxidation takes place in peroxisomes rather than mitochondria. In budding yeast, peroxisomes likewise provide the β-oxidation site; growth on fatty acids can lead to greater peroxisomal abundance. That does not mean that these organisms lack mitochondrial respiration. It means that the initial fatty-acid degradation and the later energy-yielding processing are distributed differently across compartments. The most useful way to compare cells is to follow the carbon and electron products, not to assume that every eukaryote uses the mammalian arrangement.

8. Additional mammalian roles and functional specialization

The prominence of fatty-acid shortening and peroxide metabolism can make peroxisomes appear to be purely degradative. They also contribute to lipid synthesis. A well-established example is the early steps in the synthesis of ether phospholipids, including plasmalogens. These lipids have an ether linkage in place of the usual ester linkage at one position of the glycerol backbone; plasmalogens possess a vinyl ether at that position. They are especially important components of certain membranes, including those in nervous tissue and heart. Early biosynthetic reactions take place in peroxisomes and later steps in the endoplasmic reticulum. A defect in peroxisomal function may consequently alter membrane composition as well as fatty-acid degradation.

Peroxisomes also contribute to the shortening of particular intermediates in bile-acid synthesis in the liver. The purpose of mentioning this example is not to memorize the entire steroid pathway but to recognize that β-oxidation chemistry can modify a molecule for biosynthesis rather than simply burn it for energy. Similarly, selected branched-chain fatty acids require specialized processing. Phytanic acid, whose methyl branch interferes with ordinary β-oxidation, is an example of a substrate that first undergoes α-oxidation to permit subsequent degradation. These examples explain why a mitochondrial pathway cannot always substitute for the peroxisomal one merely because both contain lipid-oxidizing enzymes.

A useful prediction follows from this variety. Suppose a particular cell loses the capacity to import most peroxisomal matrix proteins, while mitochondrial respiration remains intact. The expected phenotype may combine accumulation of substrates normally processed by peroxisomes, decreased synthesis of certain products, and altered peroxide handling. Measuring ATP concentration alone might miss the defect. Conversely, a cell with defective mitochondrial ATP production need not have defective peroxisomal protein targeting. Different biochemical tests are required to distinguish these possibilities.

The number and enzyme composition of peroxisomes respond to metabolic needs. Liver and kidney cells have substantial oxidative and detoxification responsibilities and often contain prominent populations. Cells growing under conditions that favour fatty-acid utilization may increase organelle abundance or enzyme expression. More peroxisomes do not necessarily mean more oxidative stress: their removal capacity and substrate demand may also have increased. A microscope provides a count, but a functional conclusion requires additional information about enzyme activities and metabolic fluxes.

Peroxisomal activity is also relevant to disease. A broad conceptual example is Zellweger spectrum disorder, in which defects in peroxisome biogenesis genes can disrupt multiple matrix protein functions. A different category is X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, involving the ABCD1 peroxisomal membrane transporter and altered handling of very-long-chain fatty acids. These examples distinguish a general assembly or import defect from a defect involving a selected membrane transport function. They are not interchangeable mechanisms, and neither should be reduced to a simple absence of catalase. Detailed clinical diagnosis and genetic variants are not required for this organelle chapter. The essential lesson is that perturbing a transport protein, an import receptor or one metabolic enzyme can produce different patterns of cellular dysfunction.

Another important boundary is the distinction between detoxification in peroxisomes and in smooth ER. Peroxisomal catalase consumes peroxide and can participate in certain peroxide-dependent oxidations. Smooth ER contains other detoxification systems, notably cytochrome P450 enzymes in liver cells, and also contributes to lipid metabolism. Both compartments have oxidative enzymes, but they have different membrane organization, enzyme locations and reaction mechanisms. Therefore, the word detoxification alone cannot identify an organelle. Look for peroxide-producing oxidases plus catalase when the question points toward peroxisomes.

9. Glyoxysomes: mobilizing stored oil during seed germination

An oil-rich seed starts its development with abundant stored triacylglycerols. These reserve molecules contain fatty acids that can support the developing embryo. A newly germinating seed has not yet established effective photosynthesis, but it requires sugars for growth, cell wall production and respiration. The problem is not simply how to obtain energy from fat; the seed must also retain enough carbon to build carbohydrate. Specialized peroxisomes known as glyoxysomes help solve this problem.

Oil bodies, also called lipid droplets, store neutral lipids inside the cell. Their surface is a phospholipid monolayer, whereas the glyoxysome is enclosed by a bilayer. The two structures are distinct even though they often lie close together in germinating tissues. Lipase activity releases fatty acids from stored triacylglycerols. The fatty acids are made available to peroxisomal β-oxidation, which produces acetyl-CoA. The associated oxidases produce peroxide, so catalase remains important in this specialized compartment. The release of fatty acids and their subsequent oxidation must be distinguished: lipid hydrolysis first makes the substrate available, and β-oxidation then cleaves the carbon chain.

Why cannot ordinary mitochondrial oxidation of acetyl-CoA by itself provide the required net carbohydrate carbon? In the usual citric acid cycle, two-carbon acetyl units enter by condensation with oxaloacetate, and two carbons are released as carbon dioxide per cycle. Oxaloacetate is regenerated. Although isotope labeling can show redistribution of particular carbon atoms into many intermediates, the ordinary cycle by itself does not produce a net gain of four-carbon intermediate from acetyl-CoA. The carbon balance, rather than the identity of individual labeled atoms on the first turn, is the key limitation. Mammals lack the classical glyoxylate-cycle bypass and generally cannot make net glucose from even-chain fatty-acid acetyl-CoA. Glycerol released from triacylglycerol and propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids are important exceptions to an overly broad statement that no component of fat can contribute to glucose.

The glyoxylate cycle provides a carbon-conserving alternative. Citrate is formed from oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA and rearranged to isocitrate. Rather than sending isocitrate through the decarboxylating part of the citric acid cycle, isocitrate lyase cleaves it into succinate (four carbons) and glyoxylate (two carbons). Malate synthase then combines glyoxylate with a second acetyl-CoA to make malate (four carbons). Malate can support regeneration of oxaloacetate, permitting the cycle to continue. The distinctive enzymes to remember are isocitrate lyase and malate synthase; their reactions bypass the two carbon-losing decarboxylation steps. At steady operation, the cycle can export a four-carbon product while regenerating the acceptor molecule.

The carbon bookkeeping is simple and powerful. Two acetyl-CoA molecules bring four new carbons into the cycle. Oxaloacetate serves as a regenerated four-carbon acceptor, and a four-carbon succinate equivalent can be made available to downstream metabolism without requiring carbon loss at the two bypassed decarboxylations. The figure depicts the net conceptual cycle; in plants the actual reactions and intermediate exchange may be distributed across the glyoxysome and cytosol. Not every glyoxylate-cycle enzyme is exclusively located inside the peroxisome. Succinate can enter mitochondrial metabolism and carbon can subsequently reach gluconeogenesis through appropriate intermediates. The production of sugar is therefore a cooperation between organelles and cytosolic reactions, not a direct synthesis of glucose by one glyoxysomal enzyme.

Consider two C16 fatty-acid chains completely degraded to acetyl units in an idealized oilseed model. Each chain yields eight acetyl units, giving sixteen units total. If all sixteen acetyl units enter the idealized glyoxylate cycle in pairs, eight four-carbon products can be produced before any downstream carbon losses. The count conserves 32 carbons: eight times four equals 32. It is not a prediction of eight glucose molecules or an actual sugar yield. Gluconeogenesis from four-carbon intermediates involves further reactions, including possible decarboxylation; carbon is also diverted to respiration and growth. A precise glucose-yield question would have to specify these assumptions.

The dependence on the cycle changes as the plant develops. In a seedling living on oil reserves, glyoxysomal β-oxidation and carbon-conserving metabolism are particularly valuable. When photosynthetic tissues mature, newly fixed carbon becomes available and the demand for glyoxylate-cycle activity changes. Peroxisomes can acquire a different enzyme composition, with some seedling peroxisomal proteins removed and leaf-type enzymes imported. The precise pattern of organelle remodeling or replacement depends on species and tissue. The important conclusion is that glyoxysomes are functionally specialized plant peroxisomes, not a second kind of semiautonomous organelle. Their common import system helps the cell adjust matrix content to developmental need.

10. Leaf peroxisomes: recovering carbon during photorespiration

A mature green leaf has abundant chloroplasts. Rubisco normally adds carbon dioxide to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), supporting the Calvin cycle. However, Rubisco can also act as an oxygenase. One oxygenation of RuBP, a five-carbon molecule, yields one three-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate and one two-carbon 2-phosphoglycolate. The latter cannot simply proceed through the normal Calvin cycle in the same manner as 3-phosphoglycerate. Its recovery is necessary both to reclaim carbon and to prevent interference with chloroplast metabolism. Photorespiration is the pathway that handles this problem.

The first conversion occurs in the chloroplast: 2-phosphoglycolate is dephosphorylated to glycolate. Glycolate moves to the peroxisome, where glycolate oxidase converts it to glyoxylate. The reaction transfers electrons to oxygen and produces hydrogen peroxide: glycolate + O₂ → glyoxylate + H₂O₂, using a simplified acid-form equation. This is precisely the kind of reaction for which peroxisomal localization is advantageous. Catalase removes much of the peroxide formed, allowing a carbon-recovery reaction to operate without uncontrolled accumulation of its oxidative by-product.

Glyoxylate is converted into amino-acid intermediates by aminotransferase reactions, notably generating glycine. Glycine moves to the mitochondrion. There, the coordinated reactions of glycine decarboxylase and serine hydroxymethyltransferase convert two glycine molecules into one serine, releasing carbon dioxide and ammonia and producing reducing equivalents. The reaction joins carbon from two two-carbon substrates into a three-carbon product; the fourth carbon is released as CO₂. Ammonia must be reassimilated, which creates an additional metabolic demand. It is not the peroxisome that performs the main glycine decarboxylation step.

Serine returns to the peroxisome. Transamination gives hydroxypyruvate, which is reduced to glycerate by hydroxypyruvate reductase. The required reducing equivalents are supplied through metabolism and shuttle reactions; the peroxisome does not need a respiratory chain to carry out this reduction. Glycerate returns to the chloroplast, where glycerate kinase uses ATP to form 3-phosphoglycerate, allowing recovered carbon to re-enter the Calvin cycle. This sequence illustrates why the leaf peroxisome is positioned functionally between chloroplast and mitochondrion even though the three remain anatomically distinct.

The carbon economy can be understood without learning all the intermediates at once. Two oxygenation events produce two molecules of 2-phosphoglycolate, containing four carbons in total. Recovery ultimately yields one three-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate and releases one CO₂. Thus, three of the four carbons in this particular recovery branch are retained, or 75%. The two three-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate molecules formed directly by the initial Rubisco oxygenation reactions are separate and should not be included in the denominator for this branch. If the question instead asks about total carbon across both original RuBP molecules, the accounting must include those products too. Clearly identifying the starting pool avoids misleading percentages.

Photorespiration increases when Rubisco oxygenation is favoured, often at elevated temperature or when stomatal closure restricts internal CO₂ availability relative to O₂. It consumes metabolic resources and releases previously fixed CO₂, reducing net carbon assimilation compared with ideal carboxylation. However, simply abolishing the recovery pathway without preventing oxygenation is harmful because 2-phosphoglycolate and related metabolites still form. Photorespiration also interacts with the redox and nitrogen metabolism of the leaf. The pathway should therefore be understood as a necessary response to oxygenation rather than as an optional wasteful reaction that can be removed without consequence.

Imagine a plant with normal Rubisco oxygenation but strongly reduced glycolate oxidase activity. Glycolate may accumulate and carbon recovery through the usual pathway can be restricted, even though peroxide generated by that particular enzyme may decrease. A plant with normal glycolate oxidase but reduced catalase instead continues to generate peroxide and may show oxidative injury under high photorespiratory flux. The two defects can both impair growth yet have different immediate biochemical signatures. This is a central type of CSIR-NET reasoning: identify the blocked step, predict substrates and products, then infer physiological outcomes.

The glyoxylate of photorespiration and the glyoxylate of the seed glyoxylate cycle share a chemical identity, but that does not make the pathways interchangeable. In the seed, the distinctive function is carbon conservation during use of acetyl-CoA. In the leaf, the starting problem is recovery of phosphoglycolate derived from Rubisco oxygenation. The enzyme complement, substrate source, destination of carbon and developmental context distinguish the two specialized roles. Other plant peroxisomal functions include selected hormone-biosynthetic steps, such as β-oxidation reactions in jasmonate formation, but the core organelle principle is the same: the imported enzyme set determines metabolic specialization.

11. Protein targeting: how the matrix obtains its enzymes

The diversity of peroxisomal function creates a molecular sorting problem. Every matrix enzyme discussed above is made using genetic information in the nucleus and translated by cytosolic ribosomes. A newly synthesized catalase molecule and a newly synthesized cytosolic enzyme start in the same aqueous compartment, yet their destinations differ. A membrane cannot distinguish their intended functions by inspection. The information that enables selection lies in targeting signals and in the proteins that recognize those signals.

Most soluble peroxisomal matrix proteins are recognized by one of two types of peroxisomal targeting signal. PTS1 is a short signal at the extreme carboxyl terminus, with the classical example Ser-Lys-Leu (SKL). Several variants function in different proteins, and neighboring residues can influence recognition. The signal is usually retained in the imported protein. Its position matters: if additional amino acids are appended after a typical PTS1, it may no longer be recognized effectively, even though the original three-residue sequence still occurs within the protein. The receptor for PTS1 cargo is PEX5, a soluble protein that cycles between the cytosol and peroxisome.

PTS2 is a different signal near the amino terminus, generally represented by a conserved pattern within a short peptide rather than a universal single sequence. PEX7 recognizes PTS2 cargo, with additional receptor-associated factors depending on the organism. In mammals and plants the PTS2-containing extension of some cargo proteins is cleaved after import. PTS1 and PTS2 therefore differ in position, receptor recognition and, in some cases, processing. Both accomplish the same general task: selecting a soluble matrix protein from the cytosol for delivery to the organelle.

The transport sequence starts when a receptor binds its cargo. The receptor-cargo complex then docks at a peroxisomal membrane complex involving proteins such as PEX13 and PEX14. A transient import apparatus allows cargo to be transferred into the matrix. The receptor must subsequently be released and returned to the cytosol, where it can bind another cargo molecule. The receptor recycling step involves ubiquitination and ATP-dependent action of the PEX1 and PEX6 ATPases. The essential point is that energy is needed to maintain the import cycle; it is not necessary to imagine a mitochondrial-like proton gradient or a lysosomal acidic lumen powering this process. Detailed structural models of the transient import pore are beyond the required level, and the precise mechanism is not identical to a permanently open channel.

A striking difference from many mitochondrial import pathways is that peroxisomes can import folded proteins and even certain assembled protein complexes. A mitochondrial precursor entering the matrix through common TOM/TIM pathways generally has to be kept in an import-competent, relatively unfolded state. Peroxisomal import does not universally require unfolding of the cargo. This ability has experimental consequences: a folded fluorescent reporter can enter the peroxisomal matrix if it bears an accessible targeting signal. Some proteins lacking a conventional signal can also enter associated with a partner that bears a suitable signal, a phenomenon called piggyback import. That exception should not be mistaken for evidence that targeting signals are unnecessary for the import system overall.

A typical test of targeting compares the same fluorescent protein with and without the C-terminal SKL signal. The unmodified reporter is largely diffuse in the cytosol and may enter the nucleus according to its size. The reporter ending in SKL is expected to appear as discrete fluorescent puncta that co-localize with an established peroxisomal marker. If the reporter has SKL followed by an additional bulky tag, targeting may be disrupted because the signal is no longer at the extreme C terminus. The experiment supports the sufficiency of the signal for that reporter in cells possessing working import machinery. It does not prove that every endogenous peroxisomal protein uses that signal.

Loss of PEX5 function can cause mislocalization of many PTS1 proteins, even if peroxisomal membrane structures are still present. The membrane protein marker may continue to appear as puncta while matrix cargo remains diffuse. This situation illustrates the difference between an organelle's physical presence and its biochemical completeness. A PEX7-specific defect may preferentially affect PTS2 import, depending on the particular mutation and system. By testing both types of cargo, a student can distinguish a recognition defect from a broader defect in membrane docking or receptor recycling.

The pathway is also distinct from the secretory route. Lysosomal soluble hydrolases typically enter the ER during synthesis, pass through Golgi compartments and are sorted toward endosomes, often through mannose-6-phosphate recognition in mammalian cells. A typical PTS1-bearing peroxisomal matrix enzyme is made in the cytosol and imported post-translationally without passing through that lumenal ER–Golgi pathway. The ER can nevertheless participate in peroxisomal membrane biogenesis. The route used by a soluble matrix protein should not be confused with the route used by a membrane constituent.

12. Biogenesis, growth, division and turnover

A cell cannot maintain its peroxisomes by importing only soluble enzymes. It also needs new lipids and membrane proteins, particularly when an organelle grows or divides. The assembly machinery is collectively described using peroxins, many of which are encoded by genes with names beginning PEX. These proteins have different responsibilities: some recognize matrix cargo, some form the docking or recycling machinery, and others help deliver membrane proteins or promote organelle division. A PEX protein is not necessarily a peroxide-metabolizing enzyme.

For many peroxisomal membrane proteins, PEX19 acts as a cytosolic chaperone and targeting factor. PEX3 and PEX16 participate in membrane protein targeting or assembly in various systems. The details vary among organisms and protein classes. Unlike a soluble protein ending in SKL, a membrane protein has hydrophobic segments that must be properly inserted into a lipid bilayer. It would be misleading to assume that all membrane proteins use the PEX5 matrix-import pathway. The two sorting problems are physically different.

Peroxisomes can increase in number through growth and division of existing organelles. As new membrane components and enzymes arrive, an existing compartment elongates or changes shape and can be divided into smaller compartments. PEX11-family proteins contribute to membrane remodeling and proliferation, and dynamin-related proteins participate in division; some division machinery is shared with mitochondria. Growth and division are especially useful when increased substrate demand requires more organelle capacity. Division does not require replication of peroxisomal DNA, because no such genome exists.

Peroxisomes can also form by de novo biogenesis involving the ER and, in some mammalian systems, contributions from mitochondria-derived membrane precursors. A conservative textbook model is that preperoxisomal membrane structures assemble key components, acquire import competence and subsequently import matrix proteins. The exact balance of de novo formation and growth of pre-existing organelles depends on organism and cellular conditions. Thus, two absolute statements are incorrect: peroxisomes do not invariably arise solely by division, and they do not invariably arise only by budding directly from the ER. The well-supported conceptual distinction is between membrane assembly and selective matrix loading.

One way to understand this distinction is to consider a cell with a defect in matrix protein import. It may contain membrane-bound structures sometimes termed peroxisomal ghosts, which possess selected membrane markers but lack much of the normal matrix enzyme content. If the missing import component is restored, some membrane structures may become capable of accumulating matrix enzymes. In contrast, a cell with a defect that prevents assembly of key membrane components may first need to reconstruct membrane structures. Different mutant phenotypes therefore reveal different stages of biogenesis.

Peroxisome abundance also depends on removal. Cells can dispose of selected peroxisomes by a form of selective autophagy called pexophagy. Delivery of organellar material to the lysosome or vacuole allows degradation and reuse of its constituents. This links back to the earlier lysosome and vacuole chapters: an organelle that performs oxidative metabolism may itself become cargo for a degradative compartment. It does not digest itself by lysosomal acid hydrolases as its normal function. The detailed regulation of autophagy belongs to the cell-cycle and cell-death chapter; here it explains why organelle number can change even without a change in division rate.

In a simplified counting model, if N is the number of peroxisomes, F the number newly formed or added by division per unit time, and D the number removed per unit time, then dN/dt = F − D. A population with 60 organelles that gains 12 and loses 4 in a specified interval finishes with 68. The calculation does not prove that the total enzyme mass increased by 13.3%, because division can partition existing matrix content and newly formed organelles can differ in size. Counting organelles and measuring enzyme activity provide complementary observations. This is why a peroxisome proliferation phenotype should be interpreted using both morphology and functional assays.

13. Distinguishing peroxisomes, lysosomes and mitochondria

By now it should be possible to identify an organelle from the logic of its activity rather than simply its shape. The lysosome is a single-membrane acidic compartment containing hydrolases, with many substrates delivered through endocytosis or autophagy. The peroxisome is also single-membraned but typically contains oxidases and catalase rather than a system specialized for acid hydrolysis. Its matrix is not defined by the strongly acidic environment typical of lysosomes; peroxisomal pH can vary with organism and conditions. The presence of a single membrane establishes a structural similarity, not an equivalence of functions.

Mitochondria and peroxisomes share fatty-acid metabolism in mammals, and both depend on oxygen in important reactions. A mitochondrial inner membrane forms cristae, houses the respiratory chain and supports ATP synthesis by a proton-motive force. A peroxisome has one limiting bilayer and no comparable energy-transducing respiratory membrane. Mitochondria possess their own DNA and ribosomes, although most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear encoded; peroxisomes possess neither. An experiment showing that an isolated particle consumes oxygen and contains fatty-acid oxidizing enzymes is consequently insufficient to distinguish them. Detection of peroxide-producing acyl-CoA oxidase, catalase, respiratory-chain markers and the presence or absence of a double membrane can resolve the ambiguity.

The central plant vacuole provides a useful further comparison. It occupies a large fraction of many mature plant cells and is enclosed by the tonoplast. Its roles include solute storage, pH homeostasis, turgor and hydrolysis. A glyoxysome may sit beside a lipid droplet in an oilseed but is neither a vacuole nor an oil-storage compartment. The lipid droplet provides a stored substrate, the glyoxysome transforms fatty-acid-derived carbon, and other compartments turn the exported metabolites into usable carbohydrates. Confusing a stored material with the organelle that metabolizes it is a common source of incorrect exam answers.

An intact cell must coordinate these functions. If a damaged peroxisome is removed by autophagy, lysosomal or vacuolar degradation helps recycle its proteins and lipids. If peroxisomal β-oxidation shortens a very-long-chain fatty acid, mitochondria may continue energy metabolism. If glycolate is produced in a chloroplast, leaf peroxisomes and mitochondria cooperate in its recovery. The boundaries between organelles allow biochemical specialization while exchanges between them permit a complete cell-level outcome.

The comparison table is a way to organize these causal distinctions. It lists representative features rather than universal marker rules. For instance, catalase may also have a cytosolic pool, and a peroxisomal protein can be mislocalized in a mutant. A single unusual observation should prompt a check of experimental conditions. When several independent features agree, organelle identity is much more confidently established.

14. Integrating evidence: distinguishing production, removal and targeting defects

An exam may describe an unfamiliar mutant rather than ask directly for a definition. The best starting point is to identify what has actually been measured. Is the experiment tracking the concentration of a substrate, the rate at which it is converted, the location of an enzyme or the number of organelles? These observations answer different questions. A high substrate concentration can arise from increased delivery or reduced consumption. A high enzyme abundance does not guarantee high enzyme activity. A normal organelle count does not guarantee correct matrix content.

In the synthetic data below, all measurements are expressed as a percentage of a matched control except the last column, which describes localization. Assume equal protein loading, comparable cell number and identical assay conditions. Mutant A shows normal oxidase activity, low catalase activity and elevated peroxide. A peroxide-removal defect is a reasonable first inference. Mutant B shows low oxidase activity and much lower peroxide production, even though catalase is normal. The oxidase step or access to its substrate is a candidate. Mutant C shows a strong reduction in both measured matrix enzyme activities and diffuse PTS1 fluorescence while a membrane marker remains punctate. An import defect is a strong explanation. An enzyme assay in cell fractions, however, may report low organellar activity while total cellular enzyme activity is retained; one must check what the denominator and fraction actually represent.

The same reasoning applies when an inhibitor is added. If an enzyme is inhibited in an intact organelle, its immediate substrate tends to accumulate and its immediate product tends to decline, provided other fluxes are unchanged. Over time, feedback, altered enzyme expression and alternative pathways may change this simple expectation. Inhibition of catalase affects peroxide clearance rather than the chemical capacity of acyl-CoA oxidase directly. Inhibition of acyl-CoA oxidase affects the first step of β-oxidation and its peroxide-production rate. Distinguishing the immediate consequence from the later cellular adaptation is important.

A complementary experiment uses protease protection. Suppose a tagged soluble protein is recovered with a peroxisomal fraction. An externally added protease digests a protein exposed on the cytosolic surface but generally cannot reach an intact membrane-enclosed matrix protein. If the tagged protein survives protease alone but is degraded after detergent is added to disrupt the membrane, this supports an internal location. Controls should show that the protease is active and the membrane remained intact in the absence of detergent. A protein resistant to the protease even after detergent treatment would not provide the same evidence. Likewise, a protein trapped inside another contaminating organelle would also be protected, so fraction identity still needs confirmation.

A final quantitative example illustrates the difference between enrichment and recovery. A homogenate contains 100 mg protein and 2000 enzyme units of catalase activity, giving 20 U mg⁻¹. A purified fraction contains 5 mg protein and 600 U total activity, giving 120 U mg⁻¹. Enrichment equals 120/20 = 6-fold; recovery equals 600/2000 × 100 = 30%. A sixfold enrichment does not mean 600% recovery. If the fraction also contains mitochondrial marker activity, one cannot call it pure on the strength of catalase enrichment alone. Comparing multiple markers and microscopy allows a more complete conclusion.

The study of peroxisomes therefore combines three complementary kinds of thinking: reaction stoichiometry explains peroxide formation and removal, carbon conservation explains the metabolic specialization of oilseeds and leaves, and protein targeting explains how the correct enzyme set is maintained. In the next chapter on mitochondria, the focus changes to an oxidative organelle that conserves energy across a specialized inner membrane. The transition is a useful contrast: oxygen-dependent metabolism need not imply oxidative phosphorylation, and cellular compartmentalization can support quite different forms of biochemical work.

15. Part B — Original conceptual and assertion–reason questions

Attempt each question before opening its explanation. These are original practice questions, not reproduced examination papers.

1. Which feature most directly identifies a typical peroxisome?

A. Acid phosphatase and acidic digestion; B. Acyl-CoA oxidase with catalase; C. Thylakoid ATP synthase; D. Nuclear pores.

Answer and reasoning: B. Coupled peroxide production and removal is characteristic. A describes lysosomal chemistry, C photosynthetic membranes and D the nuclear envelope.

2. Which statement about peroxisomal structure is correct?

A. It contains its own DNA; B. It has cristae; C. It has one lipid bilayer and no ribosomes; D. Every peroxisome has a urate oxidase crystal.

Answer and reasoning: C. It imports nuclear-encoded proteins. A and B confuse it with mitochondria; D incorrectly treats an optional inclusion as universal.

3. Which oxygen species is not a free radical?

A. Hydroxyl radical; B. Superoxide; C. H₂O₂; D. All three have an unpaired electron.

Answer and reasoning: C. Hydrogen peroxide lacks an unpaired electron but can participate in oxidative damage. A and B are radicals, so D is false.

4. During catalatic turnover, one oxygen molecule is released per:

A. One peroxide; B. Two peroxide molecules; C. Four peroxide molecules; D. One NADH.

Answer and reasoning: B. The balanced reaction is 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. A and C have the wrong stoichiometry; NADH is not a substrate of this net reaction.

5. Which enzyme distinguishes the first ordinary peroxisomal β-oxidation step from mitochondrial β-oxidation?

A. Acyl-CoA oxidase; B. Thiolase; C. Enoyl-CoA hydratase; D. ATP synthase.

Answer and reasoning: A. Its reduced flavin transfers electrons to oxygen, generating peroxide. B and C occur in the overall β-oxidation chemistry of both organelles; D is not a β-oxidation enzyme.

6. Which pair is distinctive for the glyoxylate-cycle bypass?

A. Rubisco and catalase; B. Isocitrate lyase and malate synthase; C. Citrate synthase and succinate dehydrogenase; D. PEX5 and PEX7.

Answer and reasoning: B. These reactions avoid the carbon-losing decarboxylations. A belongs to different roles; C is not distinctive for the bypass; D recognizes targeting cargo.

7. During photorespiration, glycine decarboxylation occurs principally in:

A. Chloroplast stroma; B. Mitochondrion; C. Peroxisome; D. Vacuole.

Answer and reasoning: B. Peroxisomes oxidize glycolate and participate in amino-acid conversions; the mitochondrial step releases CO₂ and ammonia. A produces the initial oxygenation product; D is not the core route.

8. Which pairing is correct?

A. PTS1–PEX7; B. PTS2–PEX5 only; C. PTS1–PEX5; D. Mannose-6-phosphate–PEX5.

Answer and reasoning: C. PEX5 recognizes PTS1, PEX7 recognizes PTS2 with associated factors. Mannose-6-phosphate is a lysosomal sorting signal, not the peroxisomal PTS1.

9. Assertion: Peroxisomes can import certain folded proteins. Reason: They contain their own ribosomes.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and reasoning: C. Folded cargo import is a property of the targeting apparatus, not internal translation. Peroxisomes have no ribosomes.

10. Assertion: The glyoxylate cycle can support net four-carbon product formation from acetyl units. Reason: Its bypass avoids the two ordinary decarboxylation reactions.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and reasoning: A. Conservation of carbon is the direct explanation. The regenerated oxaloacetate is not a net carbon source.

11. Which statement about peroxisomal proliferation is best?

A. Requires peroxisomal genome duplication; B. Can occur by growth and division and also by de novo assembly; C. Requires lysosomal hydrolase delivery via M6P for every matrix enzyme; D. Always increases ATP production inside peroxisomes.

Answer and reasoning: B. These are supported biogenesis routes. A invents a genome, C confuses two targeting systems, D invents respiratory ATP synthesis.

12. Which statement is correct for ordinary plant fatty-acid β-oxidation?

A. Mainly mitochondrial; B. Peroxisomal, with downstream cooperation from other compartments; C. Occurs on thylakoids; D. Requires mitochondrial DNA in each glyoxysome.

Answer and reasoning: B. Plant β-oxidation is peroxisomal, although subsequent metabolism involves other organelles. The other options assign the process to incorrect structures.

16. Part C — Original numerical and analytical questions

Use the stated assumptions. All experimental values are illustrative, not results from published studies.

1. A preparation fully decomposes 18 µmol peroxide catalatically. What is the maximum oxygen yield?

Worked answer: 18/2 = 9 µmol O₂. Water formed is 18 µmol. A prediction of 18 µmol oxygen ignores the coefficient of two; gas measurement can be lower due to dissolution or other reactions.

2. An oxidase consumes 24 µmol O₂ in a minute, producing equimolar peroxide. Of that peroxide, 16 µmol undergoes catalatic turnover and 8 µmol peroxidatic turnover. Compute net O₂ uptake.

Worked answer: Catalatic turnover returns 16/2 = 8 µmol O₂. Peroxidatic turnover returns none. Net uptake = 24 − 8 = 16 µmol min⁻¹. This assumes no other oxygen fluxes.

3. Peroxide production is 15 nmol min⁻¹ and linear removal has k = 5 min⁻¹. What happens if production doubles while k falls to 2.5 min⁻¹?

Worked answer: Initially H* = 15/5 = 3 nmol. Afterwards H* = 30/2.5 = 12 nmol, a fourfold rise. Neither change alone accounts for the full effect. Assumes fixed linear removal and steady production.

4. A C26 saturated chain is shortened to C14. Find cycles, acetyl units and catalatic oxygen regeneration.

Worked answer: (26 − 14)/2 = 6 cycles; six acetyl units and six peroxide equivalents are formed in the idealized oxidase scheme. Complete catalatic breakdown returns three O₂ equivalents. Carbon check: 14 + 6 × 2 = 26.

5. Under the full theoretical oxidation model, how many β-oxidation cycles and acetyl units arise from a C18 saturated even chain?

Worked answer: Nine acetyl units and eight cycles. The last cycle cleaves a four-carbon substrate into two acetyl units. This is carbon accounting, not a claim of complete peroxisomal oxidation in a mammalian cell.

6. A fraction has 4 mg protein and 480 catalase units. Its homogenate had 80 mg and 1600 units. Calculate enrichment and recovery.

Worked answer: Specific activities: 480/4 = 120 and 1600/80 = 20 U mg⁻¹. Enrichment = 6-fold; recovery = 480/1600 × 100 = 30%. Purity still requires other markers.

7. Two equally sized cells have the same peroxide production, but cell X has a lower peroxide steady state and clears an added pulse faster. Under the linear model, which has greater k?

Worked answer: X. H* = P/k and t½ = ln(2)/k. Both observations agree with greater effective removal. This cannot distinguish catalase activity from other loss processes without further experiments.

8. Six Rubisco oxygenation events generate six 2-phosphoglycolate molecules. How many carbon atoms are recovered via the standard photorespiratory branch?

Worked answer: Six two-carbon molecules contain 12 carbons. Pairwise recovery gives three three-carbon glycerate/3-PGA molecules, retaining nine carbons; three CO₂ molecules release the remaining three. The direct 3-PGA products of Rubisco oxygenation are excluded.

9. A seedling lacks isocitrate lyase but retains β-oxidation. Predict its capacity to turn fatty-acid acetyl units into net carbohydrate carbon.

Worked answer: β-oxidation can still release acetyl-CoA, but the carbon-conserving glyoxylate bypass is impaired, reducing the normal net conversion of acetyl units toward carbohydrate. Respiration and other reserve substrates may remain available; loss of the enzyme does not imply absence of all ATP production.

10. A reporter ending in SKL is punctate. The same reporter with SKL followed by a six-residue tag becomes diffuse. What conclusion is strongest?

Worked answer: The position or accessibility of the PTS1 signal matters. This result does not show the tag destroyed peroxisomes or that SKL is never functional; test co-localization with a membrane marker and expression of both constructs.

11. In a mutant, PTS1 and PTS2 reporters are both diffuse, while a peroxisomal membrane marker remains punctate. Is a catalase-only defect a good explanation?

Worked answer: No. Catalase loss alone would not generally block recognition and import of both reporters. A shared import, docking or receptor-recycling defect is a better hypothesis, but the data do not identify a specific PEX gene.

12. A tagged enzyme resists external protease but is digested after detergent addition. What can and cannot be inferred?

Worked answer: The tag is protected by a detergent-sensitive barrier, consistent with a matrix location. The assay alone cannot identify which membrane compartment encloses the protein or rule out all contamination. Include fraction markers, protease activity and membrane integrity controls.

13. A leaf mutant accumulates glycolate but makes less peroxide through the glycolate reaction. Another makes normal glycolate oxidase product but accumulates peroxide. Distinguish the likely defects.

Worked answer: The first is consistent with a block in glycolate oxidation; substrate increases and peroxide production decreases. The second is consistent with impaired peroxide removal, such as catalase loss. Growth defects alone cannot distinguish them.

14. A spherical peroxisome splits into two equal-volume spherical daughters without change in combined volume. What is the ratio of total daughter surface area to original area?

Worked answer: Each daughter's radius is r/2^(1/3), so combined area is 2 × 4πr²/2^(2/3) = 2^(1/3) times the original, approximately 1.26. Additional membrane area is required. Shape and lipid transfer complicate real division.

15. A particle consumes oxygen, contains catalase and is enriched in a respiratory-chain enzyme. Is the fraction definitely peroxisomal?

Worked answer: No. A mixed mitochondrial and peroxisomal fraction is plausible. Improve separation, measure multiple markers, examine membrane structure and distinguish peroxide production from ATP-linked respiration. Oxygen consumption alone is not sufficient.

16. A mutant accumulates very-long-chain fatty acids but retains normal mitochondrial respiration and correctly localized catalase. What hypothesis fits?

Worked answer: A selective peroxisomal substrate-transport or fatty-acid processing defect fits better than complete organelle loss or generalized respiratory failure. Test acyl-substrate entry and enzyme activity; normal catalase localization does not prove every peroxisomal pathway is intact.
