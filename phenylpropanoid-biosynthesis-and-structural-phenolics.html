1. From aromatic amino acids to structural phenolics

A growing xylem vessel presents a remarkable physiological problem. Its wall must withstand the inward force produced when water is pulled towards leaves, yet it must be manufactured by a living cell using soluble metabolites. Other tissues face different problems: they must protect against ultraviolet radiation, inhibit pathogens, or mobilize poorly soluble iron. A family of aromatic compounds helps meet these needs. Phenylpropanoid metabolism links aromatic amino acid synthesis to the construction of these protective and structural materials.

The preceding chapter established how phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate enter the shikimate pathway and eventually supply phenylalanine. Phenylpropanoid metabolism begins when phenylalanine is diverted away from protein synthesis. Its characteristic ancestral skeleton contains a six-carbon aromatic ring attached to a three-carbon side chain (C6–C3). A phenolic compound, in contrast, is defined structurally by a hydroxyl group attached directly to an aromatic ring. The two definitions overlap but are not identical: cinnamic acid is an early phenylpropanoid without a phenolic hydroxyl group; gallic acid is a phenol that can arise from an earlier shikimate intermediate.

A single precursor can support a network of products. Some remain soluble and can be stored as glycosides or esters. Some are incorporated into cell walls. Others become components of inducible defense. To understand their functions, distinguish the precursor skeleton, the chemical transformation, the cellular location and the final physiological consequence. This chapter concentrates on hydroxycinnamates, lignin, lignans, coumarins and stilbenes; the detailed flavonoid and tannin branches belong to the next chapter.

The term specialized metabolism is preferable to the older term secondary metabolism when describing biological importance. A compound can be dispensable for survival under an artificial laboratory condition yet essential for competitive growth in soil, mechanical stability or resistance in nature. Lignin is the clearest example of a specialized pathway supporting fundamental vascular function.

Learning objectives

Predict the effects of blocking PAL, C4H and 4CL; distinguish monolignols from lignin units; explain oxidative polymerization and branch-specific chemistry; interpret isotope labeling and mutant phenotypes; compare lignans, coumarins and stilbenes.

2. The general pathway: PAL, C4H and 4CL

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) catalyses the nonoxidative elimination of ammonia from L-phenylalanine. The product is trans-cinnamate, retaining all nine carbons of the amino acid but losing its nitrogen. The double bond forms between the side-chain carbons formerly adjacent to the amino and aromatic groups. This is a lyase reaction, not a transamination: there is no obligatory amino-group acceptor such as 2-oxoglutarate, and no direct requirement for NADPH. PAL uses an internally generated electrophilic MIO group. The released ammonia/ammonium may be reassimilated through nitrogen metabolism.

Cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H, usually CYP73) introduces an oxygen atom at the para position of the aromatic ring, yielding p-coumarate. This cytochrome P450 monooxygenase is generally associated with the endoplasmic reticulum. Electrons supplied through a P450 reductase ultimately come from NADPH; one oxygen atom of O₂ enters the substrate and the other is reduced to water. Hydroxylation creates a phenolic group and a useful chemical handle for further substitutions. It does not add carbon atoms.

4-coumarate:CoA ligase (4CL) activates the carboxyl group to produce a thioester, p-coumaroyl-CoA. In the first half reaction ATP reacts with the acid to produce an acyl-AMP intermediate and pyrophosphate. CoA then attacks the activated acyl group, displacing AMP. Hydrolysis of pyrophosphate helps pull the overall reaction forward. The cell consumes one ATP molecule, but the energetic cost corresponds to two high-energy phosphate equivalents when PPi hydrolysis is included. CoA is not simply a transport tag: the thioester makes the acyl group chemically available to transferases, reductases and polyketide synthases.

These three enzymes create a common branch-point substrate rather than an obligatory linear pathway to every phenolic. PAL, C4H and 4CL often occur in multiple gene or enzyme forms, and their substrate preferences, expression and tissue location may partition carbon among branches. In some grasses bifunctional phenylalanine/tyrosine ammonia-lyases can produce p-coumarate directly from tyrosine, bypassing C4H for that fraction of flux. This exception matters when predicting mutant behavior across different plant species.

3. Hydroxycinnamic acids and cell-wall conjugates

Hydroxycinnamates share an aromatic ring and an unsaturated carboxylic side chain. Their substituent patterns are a compact way to understand the diversity generated downstream of p-coumarate. Caffeic acid has hydroxyl groups at positions 3 and 4; ferulic acid has a methoxy group at 3 and a hydroxyl at 4; sinapic acid has methoxy groups at 3 and 5 and a hydroxyl at 4. The methyl carbons of methoxy groups are supplied through S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), rather than by extending the original three-carbon side chain.

Textbook diagrams often connect the free acids in a straight sequence of hydroxylation and methylation. In living tissues the enzymes may act more efficiently on CoA esters, shikimate esters, aldehydes or alcohols. For example, HCT transfers the p-coumaroyl group from CoA onto shikimate or quinate; C3′H hydroxylates the ester, and a route involving reverse HCT or caffeoyl shikimate esterase (CSE) and 4CL can feed caffeoyl-CoA. Caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT) converts caffeoyl-CoA into feruloyl-CoA. Exact route usage varies among species and tissues; a network is more accurate than a single universal chain of free acids.

Hydroxycinnamates may also be esterified to sugars, quinic acid or wall polysaccharides. Chlorogenic acid is commonly 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid, an ester rather than a lignin unit. Ferulate esterified to arabinoxylans in grass walls can undergo oxidative coupling, cross-linking polysaccharides and sometimes connecting carbohydrate and lignin networks. Such cross-links change wall accessibility even if the amount of lignin itself does not change. Sinapate esters in Arabidopsis can accumulate in exposed tissues and contribute to UV screening. Therefore, phenolic pools in a solvent extract need not represent the phenolics bound within an insoluble wall.

A compound bearing both a carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl group has several possible ionization states. At typical cytosolic pH the carboxyl group is largely deprotonated, whereas the phenolic hydroxyl is often largely protonated. Esterification removes the free carboxyl group as an ionizable acid. Glycosylation and conjugation alter solubility, stability, transport and biological activity. These changes help plants keep reactive precursors under control.

4. Monolignol synthesis: building H, G and S precursors

Lignin synthesis requires a change in side-chain oxidation state. A carboxylic acid or its activated CoA thioester cannot be treated as equivalent to a cinnamyl alcohol. Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR) reduces a suitable hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA to a corresponding aldehyde, with release of CoA. Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) reduces the aldehyde to the corresponding primary alcohol. Both steps commonly use NADPH in the biosynthetic direction. The three canonical products are p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol and sinapyl alcohol.

The un-methoxylated p-coumaryl alcohol gives rise to p-hydroxyphenyl or H units. Coniferyl alcohol, with one methoxy substituent, gives guaiacyl or G units. Sinapyl alcohol, with two methoxy substituents, gives syringyl or S units. These labels describe aromatic units within lignin after coupling, not soluble precursors floating unchanged in the wall.

G precursor formation involves 3-hydroxylation and methylation as described in the preceding section. For S precursors, ferulate 5-hydroxylase (F5H, CYP84) often acts effectively on coniferaldehyde or coniferyl alcohol; caffeic acid/5-hydroxyconiferaldehyde O-methyltransferase (COMT) introduces a methyl group. The name F5H is historical and must not be interpreted as proof that only free ferulic acid is used. CAD then supplies the alcohol when the substrate is an aldehyde. A simplified arrow from G-type intermediates to S-type intermediates is useful so long as the enzyme chemistry and branch flexibility are remembered.

The relative proportions of H, G and S differ across plant groups and cell types. Many gymnosperm woods are G-rich; hardwood angiosperms often contain both G and S; grasses contain variable H, G and S with distinctive hydroxycinnamate conjugates. These are trends rather than taxonomic absolutes. Some noncanonical monomers can also become part of lignin. A mutation may therefore alter composition and linkages more dramatically than total lignin content. For example, CAD deficiency can lead to greater incorporation of aldehyde-derived structures instead of a complete absence of lignin.

5. Lignification: oxidative assembly in the cell wall

A lignified secondary wall is a composite material. Cellulose microfibrils supply tensile reinforcement, matrix polysaccharides organize their surroundings, and lignin fills and reinforces portions of the matrix. Lignin is a chemically heterogeneous aromatic polymer, not a polysaccharide. Its precursors are synthesized mainly in living cellular compartments associated with the cytosol and ER, then delivered to the apoplast. Transport and extracellular localization are regulated but not fully explained by a universal transporter mechanism. Neighboring living cells can contribute precursors to differentiating or dead tracheary elements.

In the wall, laccases use molecular oxygen as terminal oxidant; class III peroxidases use hydrogen peroxide. These enzymes oxidize phenolic substrates to radicals. The radical electron is delocalized across the aromatic and conjugated side-chain system. Radical coupling generates new C–O or C–C bonds, and additional chemical reactions stabilize the resulting structures. The common β-O-4 aryl ether linkage is accompanied by β-5, β-β, 5-5 and other linkages. Lignification can involve coupling of a monomer to an existing growing polymer and coupling among radicals of other sizes.

This is unlike synthesis of DNA or proteins: there is no nucleic acid template defining the exact monomer sequence of lignin. Nevertheless, lignification is not physiologically random. Gene expression determines which monolignols are supplied; laccase and peroxidase localization, wall microenvironment and radical chemistry determine where and how efficiently deposition occurs. G units retain an available aromatic C5 position, whereas S units have methoxy groups at both C3 and C5. S-rich lignins therefore tend to show fewer linkages that require free C5 positions and often a greater proportion of β-O-4 bonds. The detailed association depends on wall and species context.

During vascular differentiation, wall deposition and patterned lignification help form xylem conduits that resist collapse under negative pressure. Lignification of fibers supports upright stems and contributes to mechanical resistance. In the root endodermis, the lignified Casparian strip restricts free apoplastic movement and forces selective entry across membranes; later suberin lamellae provide a distinct additional barrier. Do not confuse these two materials: suberin includes substantial aliphatic polyester components, while lignin is an aromatic polymer.

The same chemistry can serve induced defense when deposition reinforces infection or wound sites. A plant that deposits too little lignin may suffer weak stems or vascular irregularity, although phenotypes depend on the specific mutation and environmental conditions. A plant producing excessive or ectopic lignin may also experience growth constraints. Biomass improvement must therefore optimize the location and chemistry of lignin, not indiscriminately eliminate it.

Mechanistic distinction

CCR and CAD manufacture reduced monolignols using reducing power; wall oxidases generate radicals using oxidants. These events occur at different stages and locations, so there is no contradiction between reduction during monolignol formation and oxidation during lignification.

6. Lignans versus lignin: controlled coupling of two units

Lignans are comparatively small molecules, typically made by oxidative coupling of two phenylpropanoid-derived C6–C3 units. The classical lignan framework has a bond between the side-chain β positions (8–8′) of the two units. Related compounds with alternative linkage positions are often termed neolignans. Unlike lignin, many lignans are discrete compounds with defined stereochemistry. A plant can modify the initially coupled skeleton through reduction, oxidation, methylation and ring rearrangement to generate many distinct products.

Pinoresinol formation illustrates how stereochemistry can be controlled. An oxidizing enzyme forms radicals from coniferyl alcohol. Dirigent proteins can guide the orientation of these radicals to favor a particular stereochemical coupling outcome. A dirigent protein is not itself the oxidase in this model; it guides the coupling of radicals generated by an oxidizing system. Pinoresinol/lariciresinol reductase (PLR) can reduce pinoresinol to lariciresinol and subsequently secoisolariciresinol in certain pathways. Other enzymes lead to compounds such as matairesinol.

Examples include sesamin in sesame, secoisolariciresinol derivatives in flax and podophyllotoxin in certain plants. Lignans may contribute to defensive or ecological interactions, but their functions differ substantially among compounds. The name lignan is similar to lignin because both involve phenylpropanoid chemistry and oxidative coupling, not because lignans are simply short fragments of every lignin polymer.

A carbon-labeling experiment starting with uniformly labeled phenylalanine could label both phenylpropanoid units of a lignan. A preparation producing mostly a single enantiomer despite radical formation suggests control of coupling orientation or subsequent selective reactions. An isolated oxidase producing a racemic product and a corresponding assay with a dirigent protein yielding an enriched enantiomer would support the stereochemical guidance model. The result should not be taken to show that all lignan pathways employ exactly the same dirigent protein.

7. Coumarins: hydroxylation, isomerization and lactonization

Coumarins have a benzopyran-2-one core, a fused aromatic ring and lactone ring. A lactone is a cyclic ester. Coumarin formation requires an appropriate ortho-hydroxylated cinnamate-derived substrate. The side-chain double bond must adopt geometry that brings the phenolic oxygen close enough to the carbonyl for intramolecular cyclization. The aromatic ring and the three side-chain carbons of the original phenylpropanoid are retained in the simple coumarin core.

Umbelliferone is a hydroxylated coumarin, while scopoletin carries hydroxyl and methoxy substituents. In Arabidopsis, feruloyl-CoA 6′-hydroxylase 1 (F6′H1), a 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase, participates in scopoletin biosynthesis through ortho-hydroxylation of feruloyl-CoA. Isomerization and lactonization form the fused ring, with enzyme-assisted steps documented in particular systems. This hydroxylase differs mechanistically from the P450 C4H: it requires Fe²⁺, oxygen and 2-oxoglutarate, yielding succinate and CO₂ from the cosubstrate. Further modifications generate coumarins such as fraxetin and sideretin.

Coumarins may be stored as glucosides. Scopolin, for instance, is the glucoside of scopoletin. Hydrolysis can release an aglycone under appropriate conditions, changing its mobility and activity. Some coumarins have antifungal or antibacterial activity; fluorescence under ultraviolet light can be useful for observing certain coumarins but is not sufficient proof of compound identity. Chemical separation and spectral or mass measurements are required for confirmation.

A striking physiological role occurs in non-grass plants under iron limitation. In alkaline soils, ferric iron is often poorly soluble. Roots of Arabidopsis secrete particular coumarins, including catechol-bearing fraxetin, which can mobilize ferric iron through complexation and, under suitable conditions, reduction. This improves the availability of iron to root uptake mechanisms. Scopoletin lacks the same adjacent free hydroxyl arrangement and is not equivalent to fraxetin in iron mobilization chemistry. Mutants impaired in appropriate coumarin production or export may show chlorosis on poorly available iron even when the total soil iron content is high.

The iron phenotype is a useful reminder that phenylpropanoid metabolism supports mineral nutrition as well as chemical defense. Low iron availability is not always low total iron concentration. An experiment comparing wild type and a coumarin-deficient mutant at acidic versus alkaline pH, with a soluble iron rescue, can help separate a defect in iron mobilization from a generalized failure of chlorophyll synthesis.

8. Stilbenes: mixed shikimate and acetate origins

Stilbenes have a characteristic C6–C2–C6 framework. Resveratrol, 3,5,4′-trihydroxystilbene, is a familiar example found in grapevine and other plants. Its biosynthesis begins with the activated phenylpropanoid p-coumaroyl-CoA but requires three malonyl-CoA molecules. Stilbene synthase (STS), a type III polyketide synthase, performs successive decarboxylative condensations and a distinct cyclization involving an additional decarboxylation.

The starter contributes nine carbons, and three malonyl-CoA molecules contribute nine carbons before decarboxylation. Each malonyl extension loses one CO₂, leaving a 15-carbon intermediate. The stilbene cyclization loses one further carbon as CO₂, leaving the 14-carbon stilbene skeleton. The CoA groups are released as the acyl groups are transferred and condensed. In this way the carbon skeleton is constructed from two metabolic origins: a phenylpropanoid starter and malonate extensions.

Chalcone synthase (CHS), the entry enzyme of flavonoid biosynthesis, uses the same common starter and three malonyl-CoA molecules but directs the intermediate through a different cyclization, yielding a C15 chalcone. STS and CHS are related enzymes, yet product structures differ because of how the enzyme directs the chemistry of the common intermediate. A decrease in STS activity may leave more substrate available for competing reactions, but an increase in CHS product is not guaranteed; regulation, compartmentation and substrate availability must also be considered.

Resveratrol and related stilbenoids can function as phytoalexins in species that produce them, increasing in response to particular infections or stresses. A phytoalexin is an antimicrobial compound produced or accumulated in response to challenge, whereas a phytoanticipin is present constitutively or generated from a pre-existing precursor. The classification depends on the biological context, not simply the chemical family. Plants lacking STS do not necessarily lack chemical defense because alternative pathways may supply effective compounds. Stilbenoids can also undergo glycosylation, methylation or oxidative oligomerization; the simple C14 skeleton is the starting point rather than the endpoint of diversity.

9. Regulation, tissue specialization and metabolic trade-offs

Phenylpropanoid output is controlled at several levels. Transcription of PAL and branch enzymes changes with development, tissue identity and stress. Secondary wall differentiation is coordinated by transcriptional programs that include NAC and MYB factors; individual MYBs can favor lignin or other phenylpropanoid outputs. The regulatory network is not the same in every species or organ. A change in a transcription factor may alter many pathways at once, unlike a narrowly substrate-specific enzyme mutation.

Light, ultraviolet exposure, wounding and pathogen-associated signals can change pathway expression and metabolite accumulation. Light-exposed epidermal cells may favor soluble UV-screening phenolics, while developing fibers and vessels prioritize monolignol supply. The same enzyme family can participate in both programs through distinct isoforms. Hormone and defense signaling can also change the balance among growth, wall reinforcement and soluble metabolites.

Metabolic flux is not identical to concentration. A rapidly consumed intermediate can have a small pool despite carrying a large flux; a blocked pathway can cause an upstream compound to accumulate even while its downstream products decline. Soluble and wall-bound pools can move in opposite directions. A transcript increase does not prove enzyme activity increased, and enzyme activity in an extract does not alone prove flux in an intact cell. Substrate supply, redox state, enzyme localization, transport and turnover all matter.

The pathway also competes with protein synthesis for phenylalanine, with lipid and other polyketide reactions for malonyl-CoA, and with numerous methylation reactions for SAM. Increased lignification consumes carbon and reducing equivalents, but gains mechanical and hydraulic functionality. In plant biotechnology, changing lignin quantity or composition may improve enzymatic release of sugars from biomass; however, changes that weaken conducting or supporting tissues can reduce total plant yield. A useful engineering goal is often altered deposition or linkage composition with preserved vascular integrity, rather than minimum lignin at any cost.

10. Evidence, experiments and quantitative reasoning

Classical tracer experiments established precursor–product relationships by feeding isotopically labeled molecules and detecting label in downstream metabolites. Modern stable-isotope experiments use ¹³C-labeled phenylalanine with chromatography and mass spectrometry. Because PAL removes nitrogen rather than carbon, uniformly ¹³C₉-labeled phenylalanine can yield an M+9 cinnamate or p-coumarate isotopologue. Interpretation assumes the direct route and negligible label scrambling over the observation period. A fully labeled stilbene need not result from labeled phenylalanine alone because its second aromatic ring is assembled from malonate.

Genetic perturbations test whether an enzyme is required for a pathway in a defined organism. A strong PAL lesion may reduce several phenylpropanoid branches, while loss of F5H tends to shift lignin away from S units. CAD perturbation can change the oxidation-state distribution of incorporated residues. A 4CL isoform mutant may produce a tissue-specific or branch-specific effect. Complementation with a functional gene supports causality, and comparison of independent mutant alleles helps rule out unrelated background mutations.

Histochemical methods provide spatial information but have limitations. Phloroglucinol-HCl (Wiesner) staining detects particular cinnamaldehyde-related structures in lignified walls rather than total lignin uniformly. Mäule staining helps distinguish syringyl-rich lignin in appropriate tissues. A strong stain can reflect chemical composition rather than greater polymer mass. Acetyl bromide or gravimetric methods estimate total lignin using method-specific assumptions. Thioacidolysis or related degradative methods preferentially report units from susceptible linkages; nuclear magnetic resonance provides additional structural information. No single method simultaneously determines exact amount, spatial localization and all linkages.

Consider the following hypothetical comparison of age-matched stems sampled using the same dry-wall mass normalization. Wild type contains 200 mg lignin per gram of dry wall, with H:G:S proportions 5:55:40; mutant A contains 180 mg/g with proportions 5:90:5. Total lignin fell by (200−180)/200 × 100 = 10%. The S/G ratio fell from 40/55 ≈ 0.73 to 5/90 ≈ 0.056, a much larger compositional shift. The pattern is consistent with impaired S-branch formation (for example F5H-related activity), but does not identify the gene without additional evidence. The ratios are illustrative molar unit percentages, so multiplying by mass directly would only be an approximation requiring assumptions about average unit masses.

A second example concerns 4CL energetics. Activation of 12 µmol p-coumarate requires 12 µmol ATP, produces 12 µmol AMP and 12 µmol PPi, and consumes 12 µmol CoA if all substrate is converted and no recycling is counted. Subsequent complete PPi hydrolysis produces 24 µmol inorganic phosphate. The cost is equivalent to 24 µmol high-energy phosphate bonds for regenerating ATP from AMP. Distinguish molecules of ATP from energetic equivalents; they are not the same unit.

To study pathway competition, one could measure CHS and STS activity and quantify chalcone-derived and stilbene-derived metabolites in matched control and STS-overexpressing tissues. Include an empty-vector control, monitor phenylalanine and malonyl-CoA pools, and use a time course or tracer if flux is the question. A rise in stilbene concentration alone cannot establish how much carbon was diverted from flavonoids. Growth and dry-mass normalization are also necessary because altered development can change metabolite concentration by dilution.

A common error is to classify every phenol as a phenylpropanoid. The phenol designation concerns functional groups; the phenylpropanoid designation concerns a biosynthetic relationship and characteristic skeleton. Another error is to call lignin a polymer of phenylalanine. Phenylalanine first loses its amino group, and a series of reactions converts the resulting carbon skeleton into monolignol precursors.

Do not equate PAL with a transaminase, C4H with a dioxygenase of the 2-oxoglutarate class, or 4CL with a kinase. PAL eliminates ammonia, C4H inserts one oxygen atom into the substrate, and 4CL uses adenylation chemistry to produce a thioester. Likewise, laccases and peroxidases are oxidases responsible for radical generation, not reductases involved in alcohol production.

Neither G nor S is a universal marker of all lignin: actual lignins vary. An S/G shift is not equivalent to a change in total polymer concentration. Dirigent proteins can guide coupling in certain lignan pathways but do not impose a protein-like sequence on lignin. CHS and STS use the same common substrates but do not make the same carbon skeleton. Finally, not every plant produces resveratrol, and not every coumarin is equally effective at mobilizing iron.

12. CSIR-NET / SET practice with explained answers

Attempt each question before opening its explanation. Assertion–reason items require evaluating both statements and their logical relationship; numerical examples assume ideal stoichiometry unless specified.

1. MCQ

Which enzyme removes nitrogen from phenylalanine at pathway entry? A. C4H B. PAL C. CCR D. CHS

Answer and explanation

B. PAL eliminates ammonia without consuming an amino-group acceptor.

2. MCQ

Which product of 4CL formation is the activated acyl donor? A. Cinnamate B. p-Coumaroyl-CoA C. Sinapyl alcohol D. Pinoresinol

Answer and explanation

B. The CoA thioester enables subsequent acyl transfer and condensation.

3. MCQ

C4H belongs primarily to which enzyme class? A. Cytochrome P450 monooxygenase B. Lyase C. SAM-dependent methyltransferase D. Peroxidase

Answer and explanation

A. Oxygen and reducing equivalents are used to hydroxylate cinnamate.

4. MCQ

Which monolignol supplies G units? A. p-Coumaryl alcohol B. Sinapyl alcohol C. Coniferyl alcohol D. Caffeoyl shikimate

Answer and explanation

C. Coniferyl alcohol has one aromatic methoxy substituent.

5. MCQ

A lignin unit with two aromatic methoxy substituents is classed as A. H B. G C. S D. C

Answer and explanation

C. S stands for syringyl.

6. MCQ

Which pair correctly describes wall lignification? A. CAD and ATP B. Laccase and O₂ C. PAL and PPi D. 4CL and H₂O₂

Answer and explanation

B. Laccase uses molecular oxygen; peroxidases use H₂O₂.

7. MCQ

The classical lignan linkage is usually A. 8–8′ B. α-1,4 glycosidic C. peptide D. phosphodiester

Answer and explanation

A. It connects two phenylpropanoid side chains.

8. MCQ

Which chemical transformation closes a coumarin lactone ring? A. Intermolecular peptide formation B. Intramolecular esterification C. Transamination D. Phosphorylation

Answer and explanation

B. An appropriately positioned hydroxyl group participates in cyclization.

9. MCQ

Resveratrol biosynthesis commonly uses A. three IPP units B. p-coumaroyl-CoA and three malonyl-CoA C. two tryptophans D. only glucose

Answer and explanation

B. STS extends and cyclizes the phenylpropanoid starter.

10. Assertion–reason

Assertion: S-rich lignin often has fewer C5-dependent linkages. Reason: S units possess methoxy groups at both C3 and C5.

Answer and explanation

Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion because C5 substitution limits coupling at that position.

11. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Lignin is synthesized according to an mRNA-encoded monomer sequence. Reason: Monolignols are oxidatively coupled in the wall.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true. Enzymes regulate monomer production and radical generation but no sequence template specifies lignin.

12. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Blocking PAL can affect lignin and flavonoids. Reason: Both branches commonly receive carbon through phenylpropanoid entry reactions.

Answer and explanation

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. Alternative routes and isoforms may reduce the severity.

13. Assertion–reason

Assertion: A dirigent protein is necessarily the oxidase producing monolignol radicals. Reason: Dirigent proteins can control the orientation of radical coupling.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true. Radical generation and coupling guidance are distinct.

14. Numerical

How many ATP molecules and high-energy phosphate equivalents are consumed to activate 8 µmol p-coumarate? Express both in µmol.

Answer and explanation

8 µmol ATP and 16 µmol high-energy phosphate equivalents, assuming complete PPi hydrolysis.

15. Numerical

A hypothetical lignin contains 60 mol% G and 30 mol% S. After a mutation G is 85 mol% and S is 10 mol%. Compute S/G before and after.

Answer and explanation

0.50 before and 10/85 ≈ 0.118 after. Composition has changed; total mass cannot be inferred.

16. Numerical

How many carbons are in the resveratrol skeleton when a C9 starter reacts with three C3 malonyl units and four CO₂ are lost?

Answer and explanation

9 + 9 − 4 = 14 carbons.

17. Analytical

A mutant accumulates cinnamate, with low p-coumarate and low lignin. Which enzyme is a candidate? What test would strengthen the conclusion?

Answer and explanation

C4H. Test hydroxylase activity and restoration by a functional C4H allele; accumulation alone is not diagnostic.

18. Analytical

Why can CAD deficiency change staining without eliminating lignin?

Answer and explanation

Altered aldehyde-to-alcohol conversion can favor incorporation of alternative aldehyde-derived structures. Histochemical staining depends on structure as well as amount.

19. Analytical

A root mutant shows chlorosis only in alkaline, low-available-iron soil. Soluble iron restores growth. Suggest a hypothesis and a measurement.

Answer and explanation

A defect in iron mobilization, possibly coumarin synthesis or secretion; compare exuded fraxetin and other coumarins with wild type, alongside iron uptake.

20. Analytical

Both CHS and STS use the same substrates. Why does STS overexpression not guarantee an equal decrease in flavonoid mass?

Answer and explanation

Carbon pools, regulation, tissue localization, turnover and mass differences prevent a simple one-to-one concentration prediction. Trace flux to quantify diversion.

21. Conceptual

Explain the distinction between a phenolic and a phenylpropanoid using cinnamate and gallic acid.

Answer and explanation

Cinnamate is an early phenylpropanoid without a phenolic OH. Gallic acid is phenolic and may derive from early shikimate chemistry rather than PAL.

22. Conceptual

Why is the phrase 'lignin is a polymer of coniferyl alcohol' incomplete?

Answer and explanation

Lignin often incorporates multiple precursors, including H and S types and noncanonical monomers. Its composition and linkages vary.

23. Conceptual

What is the distinction between lignin and the Casparian strip-associated later suberin lamellae?

Answer and explanation

The Casparian strip is lignified; suberin lamellae are chemically distinct and rich in aliphatic polyester components.

24. Conceptual

What information does a ¹³C₉ phenylalanine pulse provide, and what does it not establish by itself?

Answer and explanation

Label in downstream C9 molecules supports direct carbon transfer. It does not by itself measure total pathway flux or distinguish all alternative routes.

13. Rapid revision and next concept

Start with the carbon skeleton: shikimate supplies phenylalanine, PAL removes ammonia, C4H adds a para hydroxyl and 4CL makes the activated thioester. Hydroxylation and methylation diversify the ring, while CCR and CAD reduce side-chain oxidation states to form monolignols. H, G and S units reflect zero, one and two methoxy groups respectively. Laccase- and peroxidase-generated radicals couple in the cell wall. Distinguish this heterogeneous polymer from the discrete lignan skeleton.

Coumarins form a fused lactone from an ortho-hydroxylated precursor; some mobilize iron through specialized root exudation chemistry. Stilbene synthase uses the same common substrates as chalcone synthase, but produces a C14 skeleton instead of a C15 chalcone. The next node, 6.G.06, follows the chalcone branch into flavonoid diversity, pigments and tannins. Revisit 6.G.04 if the carbon origins of aromatic intermediates are unclear.

One-minute recall

PAL → C4H → 4CL; CCR/CAD → H/G/S monolignols; wall oxidation → lignin; 8–8′ coupling → classical lignans; ortho-OH + lactone → coumarin; STS + 3 malonyl-CoA → C14 stilbene; CHS → C15 chalcone.
