1. The distribution problem: why plants need phloem

A mature leaf can absorb light, acquire carbon dioxide and use the products of photosynthetic electron transport to synthesize organic compounds. Yet a root tip buried in soil, a developing seed enclosed within a fruit and the cells of a young shoot require organic carbon even when they fix little or no carbon dioxide themselves. They need substrates for respiration, building blocks for new cell walls and membranes, and precursors for nitrogen-containing compounds. Photosynthesis and growth therefore often occur in different locations. A vascular plant cannot prosper merely by producing enough carbohydrate: it must also deliver that carbohydrate to tissues that require it.

The distance creates a physical problem. Imagine a soluble molecule moving through stationary water by random thermal motion. Its average displacement does not increase linearly with time. For one-dimensional diffusion, the characteristic time scales with the square of distance, t ≈ L²/(2D), where D is the diffusion coefficient. A tenfold increase in the journey therefore increases the characteristic diffusion time a hundredfold. Diffusion is invaluable across a membrane or a short cytoplasmic distance, but a mechanism relying only on diffusion through an entire stem would deliver assimilates too slowly for normal growth. Long-distance movement requires a different physical organization.

The two vascular systems solve complementary problems. Xylem provides the main pathway for water and dissolved minerals acquired by roots. Its conducting cells are usually dead at maturity and their strengthened walls resist collapse under tension. Phloem distributes organic solutes using living conducting cells joined into low-resistance pathways. The distinction is not simply that one tissue carries water and the other carries sugar. Both transport water; the difference lies in their characteristic cargo, conducting structures, pressure regimes and relationships with surrounding living tissues.

Photoassimilates are compounds produced from photosynthetically fixed carbon. Sucrose is the major long-distance transport carbohydrate in many plants. A molecule of sucrose contains two monosaccharide units and is a useful soluble form of carbon for distribution. Some species also move appreciable quantities of raffinose-family oligosaccharides or sugar alcohols. Starch, in contrast, is stored as a largely insoluble polymer and is not normally transported as intact starch granules in sieve tubes. Before a starch reserve can supply another organ, metabolic reactions must make carbon available in transportable soluble forms.

Translocation in this chapter means the movement of organic solutes through the phloem over relatively long distances. Loading transfers assimilates into the conducting system, axial transport moves sap along that system, and unloading delivers assimilates to receiving tissue. These are linked but distinguishable stages. A defect at any stage can reduce delivery despite the other two remaining functional. To understand why a fruit receives less sugar, for example, we must determine whether the leaf makes less, whether entry into phloem is impaired, whether the conducting route has become resistive, or whether the fruit fails to take up and use the incoming carbon.

A phloem tube does not have to recognize a root or fruit by its name and then push individual sucrose molecules toward it. Instead, metabolism and selective transport establish differences between regions; water responds to those differences, and the resulting hydraulic gradient drives movement of the solution. Understanding how a biochemical event at a source generates a physical driving force several centimetres or metres away is the central purpose of this chapter.

2. Source and sink are physiological roles, not permanent identities

A source is a region that makes assimilates available for net export to another region. A sink is a region that imports assimilates for use or storage. These definitions refer to net transfer over an appropriate time interval; they are not synonyms for leaf and root. A mature illuminated leaf often qualifies as a source because its carbon fixation exceeds the requirements of its own respiration, maintenance and growth. A newly expanding leaf may photosynthesize to some extent yet remain a sink because its own carbon production is insufficient to support its rate of expansion.

To see the logic, consider a hypothetical leaf that fixes 20 units of carbon per hour, consumes 5 in respiration and retains 7 for structural growth or net storage. Eight units remain for export if other carbon exchanges are negligible. Now consider an expanding leaf that fixes 5 units, consumes 3 in respiration and requires 9 for new biomass. Its local production leaves a deficit of 7 units, which must be met by importing carbon or drawing down an internal reserve. The young leaf is not automatically a source just because chlorophyll is present. What matters is its carbon balance.

During leaf development, the transition from importer to exporter can occur progressively as different parts of the leaf mature. An organ-level average may hide this spatial heterogeneity. When a young leaf expands, demand for cell wall material and respiration is high; as its photosynthetic apparatus develops and the growth rate changes, carbon fixation may exceed local requirements. This developmental transition changes the leaf's connection to the surrounding network. A tracer supplied to an older leaf may reach the younger leaf early in development and show much less net import after the latter becomes an exporting source.

Storage tissues illustrate reversibility particularly clearly. A potato tuber accumulating carbohydrate during tuber formation receives carbon from source leaves. During subsequent sprouting, starch reserves are mobilized and soluble carbon can be exported to the new shoot; the old storage tissue is now a source relative to that growing shoot. Similarly, a germinating seed may supply the embryonic axis with carbon from stored reserves before the seedling becomes photosynthetically independent. Importantly, a developing fruit generally functions as a sink; it should not be presumed to become an exporter simply because its growth slows or it ripens. Some green fruits fix carbon, but net export must be established by a carbon balance or transport experiment.

The dark period does not eliminate every source. A mature leaf may export carbon at night by degrading starch accumulated during the preceding light period. Conversely, illumination alone is not enough to establish high export if the leaf is damaged, has limited carbon dioxide supply, or cannot transfer sucrose into the phloem. The relevant quantity is net availability of exportable assimilate, not the instantaneous rate of photosynthesis in isolation.

A plant can contain several sources and many sinks at once. The vascular network determines which routes are available. A leaf close to a fruit is not guaranteed to deliver all of its exported carbon to that fruit because vein and stem connections, competing sinks and transport resistance affect allocation. Sink strength refers to a tissue's capacity to attract and use assimilates, and is influenced by its size and metabolic activity. Detailed mechanisms of sink competition are considered in the chapter on unloading; for the present, remember that the receiving tissue helps maintain the driving force rather than acting merely as a passive container.

3. Phloem anatomy: building a living conducting pathway

The vascular bundles of leaves join the transport pathways of the stem and ultimately communicate with roots and other organs. In many stems, primary phloem occupies the outer side of the xylem in a vascular bundle. In a typical woody dicot stem, secondary phloem forms outside the vascular cambium, while secondary xylem is produced inward. These are common anatomical patterns, not definitions that apply to every bundle arrangement. The ability to distinguish the living phloem from outer bark and adjacent wood is especially important when interpreting girdling experiments.

Phloem is a complex tissue, not a single type of cell. Its conducting elements are associated with metabolically active support cells, parenchyma and, in many plants, strengthening fibres. Angiosperm sieve-tube elements are elongated cells joined longitudinally. The walls at their junctions contain regions with enlarged pores called sieve plates. Sap can pass through these pores from one element to the next, allowing a series of cells to function as a continuous conducting route. A sieve tube is therefore a multicellular conduit, not simply a single cell stretching from leaf to root.

The development of a functional sieve-tube element involves selective simplification of its cellular contents. A mature element normally lacks a nucleus and many structures typical of a fully equipped living plant cell. Its large central vacuole and tonoplast are also lost during differentiation. Nevertheless, the plasma membrane remains intact, and a thin peripheral layer of cytoplasm contains a reduced complement of organelles and proteins. The cell maintains a living interface with its surroundings. Enucleation is not equivalent to cell death, although the conducting cell no longer possesses the independent biosynthetic capabilities of a nucleated cell.

Why reduce the contents of a living cell? A conventional densely packed cytoplasm and large vacuolar compartments would complicate the creation of an unobstructed axial path. The reduction of cellular contents and enlargement of intercellular pores make hydraulic movement more efficient while retaining the selective membrane properties needed to maintain an osmotic difference relative to surrounding tissues. This combination differentiates phloem from xylem, in which the main mature conducting elements are dead and lack a functional plasma membrane.

Companion cells are nucleated living cells intimately associated with angiosperm sieve-tube elements. The two commonly arise from the division of a shared precursor and remain connected by specialized plasmodesmata. Companion cells contain abundant metabolic machinery, and their close symplastic connection enables metabolic support and exchange of selected substances with sieve elements. They participate in maintaining the functional state of conducting cells and in the transfer of assimilates and other substances. Their precise roles vary along collection, transport and release phloem and between plant species.

The arrangement also resolves an apparent paradox. If a sieve element lacks its nucleus, how can a living conducting system sustain selective membrane transport and respond to injury? Essential proteins and metabolic support can be supplied through associated cells, and the remaining membrane and cytoplasmic machinery can carry out functions within the conducting element. The loss of a nucleus during differentiation does not abolish every biochemical reaction. However, the companion cell does not mechanically propel each sucrose molecule through successive sieve pores. Maintenance of the conducting system and generation of a pressure difference are distinct from the passive movement of sap along that pressure difference.

A sieve plate contains multiple pores, and the cross-sectional area available for liquid movement depends on the number and dimensions of open pores. The pores offer resistance; the sieve plate is not absent from a mature tube. Wider or more numerous open pores generally reduce this local resistance. Callose, a β-1,3-glucan-rich wall-associated material, can be deposited around sieve pores and alter their effective opening. Certain phloem proteins contribute to rapid sealing in particular angiosperms following disturbance. This sealing can limit the loss of valuable sap but also interrupts transport, especially when measuring phloem after cutting.

Gymnosperms possess conducting sieve cells rather than the typical angiosperm sieve-tube elements with strongly differentiated sieve plates. Their sieve areas are distributed across overlapping walls, and albuminous cells, also called Strasburger cells, provide metabolic association without generally sharing the same developmental origin as angiosperm companion cells. Both systems conduct organic solutes through living cells. A question asking whether every vascular plant has a sieve-tube element–companion cell pair must therefore be answered with the taxonomic distinction in mind.

The functional organization is best pictured at three scales. At the cellular scale, a sieve element is supported by a nearby living cell. At the tissue scale, several conducting elements connect in series and adjacent routes are arranged in parallel. At the organ scale, vein networks link exporting tissues with importers. A change in pore opening alters resistance locally; a change in the number of functional parallel tubes changes tissue-level conductance; a change in source or sink metabolism modifies the pressure field. These scales must not be confused when interpreting an experiment.

4. What moves in phloem sap?

Phloem sap is an aqueous solution with organic and inorganic constituents. Sucrose often dominates the transported carbon fraction, but other soluble carbohydrates occur depending on the species. Amino acids carry organic nitrogen and carbon to sinks, where nitrogen is needed for protein and nucleotide synthesis. Inorganic ions, particularly potassium, contribute to the solute environment and to cellular electrical and osmotic balance. The sap may contain organic acids, hormones and additional metabolites. Its composition reflects both the material available to the source and selectivity at the loading, retrieval and unloading interfaces.

Sucrose is a non-reducing disaccharide because its two anomeric carbons participate in the glycosidic linkage. This chemical property is one reason it is a relatively suitable transport carbohydrate, but it is not an exclusive explanation of its widespread transport. Synthesis in source cells, membrane transporter specificity, metabolism at sinks and evolution of vascular pathways all matter. Certain species employ additional transport carbohydrates. An exam question claiming that glucose, amino acids or potassium can never occur in phloem sap is stronger than the evidence warrants.

A high concentration of dissolved substances does more than carry nutrients. Solutes lower the chemical potential of water and thereby contribute to the osmotic component of water potential. A mixture containing 0.3 mol L⁻¹ sucrose and 0.1 osmol L⁻¹ of other effective solute particles has an ideal total osmolar concentration of 0.4 osmol L⁻¹, rather than 0.3. In an ideal solution, osmotic effects depend on the number of dissolved particles. In concentrated sap, interactions between solutes and differences in membrane permeability complicate exact predictions. Nevertheless, osmotic concentration remains a useful first approximation for understanding pressure generation.

The phloem also participates in long-distance communication. Selected proteins and RNAs can enter the sieve element–companion cell system and be delivered to other organs. An example from flowering biology is the FT protein, a mobile component of the floral induction signal in appropriate plants. Such transport connects perception in leaves with a response at the shoot apex. Some small RNAs and messenger RNAs also show selective mobility. These cases do not imply that every protein or RNA is freely admitted into sieve tubes, or that intact soil macromolecules normally travel without selection into every plant organ.

It is essential to distinguish access to the stream from movement within it. Selective trafficking at plasmodesmata and other interfaces determines whether a particular macromolecule can enter or leave the conducting pathway. Once present in the moving solution, a mobile substance may be carried along with the sap. Specific interactions, retention, degradation or regulated transfer can alter its observed distribution. Finding an RNA in a distant organ does not, by itself, show that every RNA uses the same route or that it retains a regulatory function after arriving.

Sap composition measurements require careful interpretation. Cutting a stem can damage many cells at once, allowing cell contents other than phloem sap to contaminate the sample. Some plants seal sieve pores rapidly, while others exude fluid from cut phloem more readily. Sampling through an aphid stylet can give more direct access to a functioning sieve element, although the sample still represents a particular tube, time and physiological condition. Different sap-sampling approaches need not yield identical concentrations. The central conclusion is that phloem carries a selective, dynamic aqueous cargo, not pure sucrose and not an indiscriminate suspension of all cellular material.

5. Discovery: separating the pathway from its driving force

Early observations of bark removal helped establish that the distribution of photosynthetic products requires a pathway outside the wood. In classical ringing or girdling experiments, removal of a ring of bark containing functional phloem interrupts the downward movement of assimilates while much of the xylem initially remains intact. In a typical stem with mature source leaves above the ring, carbohydrates accumulate in tissues on the source side. A swelling may develop above the ring. Roots below it receive less newly synthesized carbon and eventually suffer from lack of substrates.

The interpretation depends on anatomy and physiological state. The fact that leaves remain hydrated for a time after girdling is consistent with xylem continuing to supply water. It does not mean that xylem transport remains unchanged indefinitely. Root starvation, altered hormone movement and wound responses can later affect water relations and photosynthesis. Similarly, a swelling is not composed solely of sugar; growth and structural responses contribute to its appearance. The appropriate inference from a short-term, anatomically controlled experiment is that an intact phloem pathway is important for organic carbon transfer across the ring.

A girdle does not directly measure pressure or distinguish all proposed physical mechanisms for movement within phloem. It identifies a necessary anatomical route for the observed transfer. A second type of experiment is needed to determine where newly assimilated carbon travels. Radioactive carbon dioxide supplied to an illuminated source leaf is incorporated into organic molecules during photosynthesis. The appearance of radiolabel in stem vascular regions or distant sink tissues then provides a way to follow recently fixed carbon. Autoradiography can reveal spatial distributions, while chemical separation helps determine the compounds that contain the label.

Pulse–chase logic is useful here. A short exposure to labelled carbon dioxide creates a cohort of labelled assimilates. During a subsequent period with ordinary carbon dioxide, the labelled material is followed through the plant. If the label first appears near the source and then farther along a particular vascular pathway, the sequence supports translocation. A signal in a sink may include carbon that has already been metabolized into other compounds. It is therefore incorrect to equate total radioactivity in an organ directly with the quantity of intact sucrose there.

Another line of evidence comes from phloem-feeding aphids. An aphid inserts a fine stylet into a suitable sieve element to feed on its sugar-rich sap. If the insect body is removed while the stylet remains in communication with a functioning element, sap can continue to emerge from the open stylet. This observation is consistent with pressure inside the sieve tube being greater than atmospheric pressure. It is particularly informative when contrasted with a transpiring xylem conduit, whose water is commonly under tension rather than positive pressure relative to the atmosphere.

The 1930 pressure-flow model of Ernst Münch brought these anatomical and physiological observations together into a mechanism. Two osmotically active regions connected by a liquid pathway can establish different hydrostatic pressures if solute concentrations are maintained differently. A pressure difference then causes solution to flow through the connection. The model does not require a contractile organ comparable to a heart or a succession of molecular motors carrying each solute from one cell to the next.

Direct measurements of pressure and resistance are required to test whether the pressure difference can account for observed transport rates, not merely whether a positive pressure exists. Knoblauch and colleagues (2016) investigated morning glory plants and assessed sieve-tube structure, pressure and long-distance movement. Their results supported the feasibility of osmotically generated pressure-driven transport in that system. This is stronger evidence than observing sap exudation alone, because the driving force and the physical demands of movement were examined together. It does not mean that every plant has identical pressures or tube dimensions. A general physical principle can operate through different anatomical adaptations.

6. Water potential and the generation of positive pressure

Pressure flow begins with a physical property of water: its tendency to move in response to differences in chemical potential. Water potential Ψw expresses the chemical potential of water relative to a reference state in units of pressure. At the cellular scale, and ignoring gravitational and matric contributions for simplicity, Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. Here Ψs is the solute potential and Ψp is the pressure potential. Adding dissolved solutes generally makes Ψs more negative. Positive hydrostatic pressure contributes positively to Ψp; a liquid under tension can have negative Ψp.

The direction of water movement across a selectively permeable membrane depends on the difference in total water potential, not on solute concentration alone. If a source sieve element contains abundant sucrose, its solute potential may be substantially negative. But its hydrostatic pressure can also be positive. Water enters from an adjacent region if the latter has a higher total water potential than the sieve element at that moment. If the element already has sufficiently high pressure, the water-potential difference may be small or may even point outward despite the high solute concentration.

For an ideal dilute solution, the van't Hoff approximation expresses solute potential as Ψs ≈ −RTCosm, where R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature and Cosm is the osmolar concentration. Using R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T = 298 K and 0.40 osmol L⁻¹ gives Ψs ≈ −0.991 MPa. The product of pressure times volume per mole per kelvin, temperature and moles of particles per volume has units of pressure. The minus sign denotes a reduction of water potential relative to pure water. Real phloem sap is not always sufficiently dilute to behave ideally; exam calculations using this equation usually make the approximation explicit.

Suppose an idealized source phloem region has Ψs = −1.20 MPa and Ψp = +0.60 MPa, giving Ψw = −0.60 MPa. If nearby xylem water has Ψw = −0.30 MPa, water tends to enter the phloem. In a walled cell, the added water cannot expand the volume indefinitely. Expansion of the protoplast and the resistance of the cell wall increase the internal hydrostatic pressure. With fixed solute potential in a simplified model, Ψp = +0.90 MPa would give Ψw = −0.30 MPa, matching the external water potential and eliminating the net water-potential difference at that interface.

This numerical reasoning explains why phloem pressure is not simply equal to the magnitude of its solute potential. If the adjacent water reservoir were pure water at atmospheric pressure, the balance would give Ψp = −Ψs. But xylem water in a transpiring plant often has negative water potential. The equilibrium pressure for the example with xylem Ψw = −0.30 MPa is only +0.90 MPa, not +1.20 MPa. If xylem water potential falls further, the possible pressure supported by a fixed osmotic concentration decreases.

Living plants are open systems. During active translocation, water and solute entering a source region are continually carried away by axial flow; therefore exact local osmotic equilibrium is not necessarily reached. A small water-potential difference can sustain radial entry while a much larger longitudinal pressure difference drives bulk movement along the conducting path. The simplified equilibrium calculation is a useful way to estimate the relationship between osmotic concentration and pressure, not a claim that the entire functioning phloem network is at equilibrium.

This distinction between radial and longitudinal driving forces is central. Across a selective membrane, water responds to total water-potential differences and the permeability of the membrane. Along an open sieve-tube lumen, dissolved sucrose and water travel together as a solution, driven predominantly by hydrostatic pressure differences with a gravitational correction when height matters. A sieve plate is a porous connection, not an ideal semipermeable barrier that excludes sucrose while admitting water. Applying an osmotic equation across each sieve plate as though it were a membrane would misrepresent the transport pathway.

7. The Münch pressure-flow hypothesis: a connected mechanism

Consider a simple apparatus made from two osmometers, each separated from a surrounding water reservoir by a membrane permeable to water but not to the dissolved solute. Their interiors are linked by an open liquid-filled tube. Put a concentrated sugar solution into the first osmometer and a more dilute solution into the second. Water enters the more concentrated side, increasing hydrostatic pressure. If the second side has lower pressure, solution flows through the connecting tube. With no renewal or removal of solute, the concentration and pressure differences eventually diminish. Sustained flow requires maintained differences.

A source region of a plant supplies the corresponding biochemical input. Carbon fixation and metabolism make sucrose available in mature source tissues. Assimilates enter the collection phloem through pathways whose molecular details vary among species. Accumulation of osmotically effective solutes lowers the solute potential of the conducting system. Water can enter from neighbouring xylem or other water-supplying tissues, and the resulting hydration raises pressure against the cell walls. The source is thus a site at which chemical potential differences can be converted into a hydrostatic driving force.

The receiving region provides the complementary process. At a sink, assimilates leave the release phloem for metabolism, storage or growth. The removal of solutes makes the local solute potential less negative than it otherwise would be. Water can leave the conducting pathway if the local water-potential relations allow it. This removal, together with continuing flow and the hydraulic properties of the tissue, helps sustain a lower hydrostatic pressure than at the source. The sink pressure need not be zero or negative: it is commonly positive, just lower than that of the supplying source route.

The resulting longitudinal pressure gradient causes bulk movement of sap. The pressure gradient performs mechanical work on the liquid, overcoming friction within conduits and at their sieve plates. Water and dissolved solutes are carried together, although solute diffusion, selective membrane transport and exchange with surrounding tissues can occur simultaneously. There is no requirement for each sieve element to take a sucrose molecule from its upstream neighbour and actively deposit it in the next cell.

At the sink, some of the water may return to the nearby xylem and rejoin the transpiration stream. In other circumstances, water is used in tissue expansion, remains in the receiving organ, or follows other available pathways. The closed circulation often drawn in a textbook is an explanatory idealization: it connects the source's water uptake to the sink's water release without implying that each individual water molecule completes the same closed loop. The plant continues to absorb water from soil and lose it to the atmosphere while phloem transport takes place.

Energy enters the model in several forms. Photosynthesis provides chemical energy and fixed carbon; metabolism and membrane transport can establish or maintain the solute differences; respiratory metabolism supports cellular maintenance and active loading or retrieval when required. The mechanical energy of the moving sap derives from the pressure difference. It is appropriate to call axial mass flow passive with respect to direct ATP hydrolysis, but it is misleading to describe the entire source-to-sink transport system as independent of metabolism. Different species can use energetically different loading strategies while sharing the same broad hydraulic principle for axial flow.

A sink that stops consuming or storing incoming sucrose can accumulate soluble carbohydrate, reducing the capacity for continued unloading. This may alter local water relations and diminish the pressure difference that previously drove delivery. In a connected plant, flow may be redistributed to other sinks. Similarly, source loading can slow if carbohydrate supply falls or membrane transport is impaired. The pressure-flow model is therefore a dynamic coupling of carbon metabolism, water relations and hydraulic resistance, not a one-way conveyor running at a fixed speed.

A strong test of the hypothesis must connect cause and effect. A source pressure exceeding the sink pressure is necessary for the usual horizontal pressure-driven direction, but the observed magnitude of flow also depends on resistance. A positive pressure measured at one point cannot reveal the magnitude or even the sign of the pressure difference along another route. The explanatory model becomes predictive when source and sink pressures, conducting geometry and transport rate can be compared.

8. Quantitative hydraulics: pressure, resistance and carbon flux

For a simple horizontal conducting segment, the volume flow rate Q can be related to the pressure difference ΔP by Q = ΔP/Rh, where Rh is hydraulic resistance. When Q is expressed in m³ s⁻¹ and ΔP in pascals, Rh has units Pa s m⁻³. This linear relation is analogous to current flowing through a resistance in an electrical circuit, provided resistance is approximately constant under the conditions considered. The analogy clarifies why pressure alone is insufficient to predict transport: a high pressure difference can drive little flow through a highly resistive pathway.

For a Newtonian liquid moving steadily and laminarly through a straight rigid cylinder of radius r and length L, the Hagen–Poiseuille relation is Q = πr⁴ΔP/(8ηL), where η is dynamic viscosity in Pa s. To understand the fourth power, the viscous force opposing movement creates a radial velocity profile: for the ideal cylinder, u(s) = ΔP(r²−s²)/(4ηL), with radial coordinate s measured from the axis. Integrating velocity over circular annuli, whose area is 2πs ds, gives Q = ∫₀ʳ u(s) 2πs ds = πr⁴ΔP/(8ηL). Thus mean speed Q/(πr²) scales with r², while volume flow scales with r⁴ at fixed pressure difference.

The biological conclusion is not that every sieve tube exactly obeys a single-cylinder equation. Real conducting pathways contain sieve plates, varying diameters, peripheral cytoplasmic structures and connections with other cells. The effective hydraulic resistance of a tube includes both lumen resistance and resistance at the sieve plates. A narrow sieve pore can have a large influence, particularly when the available open area decreases. The fourth-power relation is a useful model for exploring how geometry and viscosity influence flow, not a substitute for measured resistance in a complex tissue.

If the radius of an ideal tube decreases from 10 µm to 5 µm while pressure difference, viscosity and length remain fixed, Qnew/Qold = (5/10)⁴ = 1/16. The mean speed decreases by a factor of four, because the cross-sectional area decreases by a factor of four. Students often incorrectly assign the factor of sixteen to mean velocity. The distinction matters whenever an exam presents a transport speed rather than a volume flow rate.

Resistances in series add. Two equal segments with resistance Rh require twice the pressure difference to sustain the same volume flow as one segment. In parallel, two independent identical tubes under the same pressure difference each carry ΔP/Rh, so total flow is twice the single-tube value and equivalent resistance is Rh/2. If one of ten otherwise identical parallel tubes becomes blocked, total conductance falls by one-tenth under fixed boundary pressures; it does not necessarily eliminate flow through all the others. A tissue-level observation must therefore be interpreted with knowledge of the number of functional pathways.

Gravity contributes to the hydraulic driving force for movement between elevations. For a solution of approximately uniform density ρ, the effective upstream-to-downstream driving pressure is ΔP + ρg(zup−zdown), where z is height and g is gravitational acceleration. For upward flow over 10 m with density about 1000 kg m⁻³, the gravitational cost is about 98,100 Pa, or 0.098 MPa. An upstream pressure excess of 0.30 MPa would leave about 0.202 MPa to overcome friction. In downward transport, gravity assists movement. Thus positive pressures alone do not specify flow direction, and height can matter even in a sugar-transporting tissue.

Carbon delivery is distinct from water delivery. If sucrose concentration C is reasonably uniform in the moving solution and other exchange is neglected, the advective molar flux F = CQ. When C is in mol m⁻³ and Q is in m³ s⁻¹, F is in mol s⁻¹. If C = 400 mol m⁻³ and Q = 2 × 10⁻¹² m³ s⁻¹, F = 8 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol s⁻¹, or 0.8 nmol s⁻¹. Multiplying by the molar mass of sucrose, approximately 342 g mol⁻¹, gives 0.274 µg s⁻¹ of sucrose. A higher solute concentration does not guarantee a greater delivery rate if the volumetric flow is sufficiently reduced.

Concentrated sugar solutions are more viscous than pure water, so increasing source sucrose can have opposing effects. Greater osmotic concentration can help generate pressure, while greater viscosity increases hydraulic resistance. A model predicting that doubling concentration always doubles carbon delivery neglects this coupling. In a real plant, membrane exchange, source supply, sink use, geometry and water availability may all change together. The most informative exam problems specify which factors are fixed and then ask for a prediction under those assumptions.

9. Directionality, exchange and the distributed phloem network

The usual description of xylem transport as upward and phloem transport as bidirectional is a statement about common whole-plant patterns, not an absolute rule about every individual conduit. A mature leaf below a young shoot can export upward. The same leaf can also supply roots below it through another path. Different sieve tubes, including tubes within the same vascular region, can therefore simultaneously carry sap in different directions. The net direction within one uninterrupted lumen at one cross-section, however, follows the local hydraulic gradient. A single homogeneous solution does not normally sustain simultaneous opposing bulk flows through the same cross-section.

At a branch point, the incoming liquid may divide between available outgoing pathways. Each branch has its own resistance and boundary conditions. If a fruit and a root are connected to a common source region and both maintain lower effective hydraulic potential, each may receive a fraction of the flow. A change in fruit metabolism can modify the local pressure and thereby alter the distribution. A change in vascular resistance can also change the fraction even without a change in sink metabolism. The pressure field is determined by the network, not by a preassigned single arrow for the whole plant.

Along a long transport pathway, phloem is not necessarily a completely sealed pipe. Sugars can leave the transport stream and be retrieved by neighbouring living cells; some are used by maintenance and growth tissues along the route. Retrieval mechanisms can maintain solute concentration and influence local pressure. Water can also exchange with surrounding tissues where water potentials and permeabilities permit it. These exchange processes explain why a simple model with loading exclusively at one end and unloading exclusively at the other is a useful first approximation rather than a literal description of every stem.

The pressure of a sieve tube can change rapidly after injury. The immediate loss of sap may reduce local pressure and disturb the normal flow field. Occlusion responses can restrict leakage but also increase local resistance. Sampling after injury may therefore underestimate transport or show an altered concentration profile. A failure to detect flow from a freshly cut tissue does not prove that the intact phloem was unpressurized or nonfunctional.

In drought, xylem water potential becomes more negative as water availability falls and transpiration continues. At unchanged phloem osmotic concentration, this reduces the pressure that can be supported by osmotic water uptake. The plant may adjust its solute concentration, stomatal conductance or growth demand; transport can thus be influenced by both hydraulic and metabolic responses. High sugar concentration under stress is not necessarily evidence of high translocation rate. Sugar may accumulate because export or sink consumption is impaired. A direct measurement of delivery or pressure is needed to distinguish these possibilities.

The xylem and phloem are thus hydraulically coupled but not identical. Water supply to a source may depend partly on the xylem's capacity to conduct under tension. The phloem distributes dissolved organic matter in a system of positive pressures. Return of water from phloem to xylem in some receiving regions links the two systems. The ability of plants to sustain both streams simultaneously depends on living tissues maintaining solute distributions and on conducting tissues retaining sufficient permeability and mechanical integrity.

10. Reading experimental evidence and distinguishing alternatives

An effective experiment starts by stating what would distinguish competing explanations. Suppose a researcher wants to determine whether a reduction in root carbon delivery originates at the source or in the transport pathway. One approach is to measure carbon fixation by the source leaf, labelled carbon leaving it, and the subsequent appearance of the label in the stem and root. If fixation and export remain normal but the label advances more slowly through a defined stem segment, a transport limitation becomes plausible. If fixation is normal but very little label exits the source, loading or source retention may be limiting. Neither pattern alone establishes a unique molecular mechanism.

Consider an idealized pulse experiment in which the arrival of the same tracer feature is recorded at two points 0.24 m apart. It arrives at the first detector after 8 min and at the second after 20 min. The apparent speed between the detectors is 0.24 m/(12 × 60 s) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹, or 1.2 m h⁻¹. Using the entire 20 min interval would incorrectly include time associated with events before the first detector. The value is an apparent transport speed for the measured tracer feature, not automatically the velocity of every molecule or the exact mean sap speed.

The shape of a tracer signal contains additional information. A narrow pulse may spread as it travels due to differences in velocities, exchanges with adjacent cells and the distribution of pathways. Its amplitude may decline because label leaves the conducting stream, becomes diluted or is metabolized. In a radioactive experiment, physical decay and detector geometry also matter. A lower detector signal is not equivalent to a slower movement rate; the arrival time and the amount of recovered label must be analyzed separately.

The aphid-stylet method provides a different measurement: access to phloem sap at a specific point. The continued exudation of sap indicates pressure relative to the outside environment when the communication with a functioning sieve element is maintained. A pressure-measuring device connected to such a pathway can provide a quantitative pressure estimate. If source pressure is 1.10 MPa and sink pressure is 0.70 MPa in a horizontal pathway, a pressure difference of 0.40 MPa is available. To decide whether this is sufficient for a measured flow rate, resistance must also be known or estimated. A pressure value without a resistance estimate is not a complete hydraulic test.

Comparing intact and girdled plants can establish pathway continuity. A suitable control experiences similar handling without interruption of the functional phloem. If radiolabel accumulates above the girdle and its arrival below is diminished while initial xylem continuity is preserved, the result supports the role of phloem in that transfer. If both xylem and phloem are severed, the experimental interpretation is much less specific because dehydration and general tissue damage can inhibit carbon fixation as well as movement. The anatomical precision of the perturbation determines the strength of the inference.

A metabolic inhibitor provides another possible perturbation. Imagine a source in which ATP-dependent proton pumping contributes to sugar accumulation. Inhibiting the pump can reduce the energy available for secondary active loading; sucrose accumulation and source pressure can decline. The resulting fall in translocation is consistent with the pressure-flow hypothesis, not evidence against it. By contrast, an isolated reduction of ATP production in a transport segment does not instantly remove a pre-existing pressure gradient. Over time, loss of cellular maintenance, retrieval or membrane integrity can alter hydraulic conditions. The timing and site of treatment therefore matter.

An especially useful examination strategy is to distinguish a mechanism from its consequences. Girdling addresses the anatomical pathway, tracer movement addresses carbon distribution, stylet exudation addresses positive pressure, and measurements of pressure difference and resistance address the capacity for bulk flow. Several independent kinds of evidence support a mechanism more strongly than repeating one experiment under nearly identical conditions. A model is strongest when it accounts for the direction, speed, energy relationships and sensitivity to biological perturbations together.

11. Worked problems: connect signs, units and predictions

The following examples use deliberately simplified values so that the physical relationships are visible. In each case identify whether the question concerns water transfer across a membrane, sap movement along a tube, or delivery of a particular solute. These processes are coupled but their mathematical descriptions are not interchangeable.

Example 1 — Radial water movement versus axial flow

Source phloem: Ψs = −1.4 MPa, Ψp = +1.0 MPa. Source xylem: Ψw = −0.2 MPa. Sink phloem: Ψs = −0.7 MPa, Ψp = +0.5 MPa. Sink xylem: Ψw = −0.4 MPa. Assume equal heights and ignore matric terms.

Source phloem Ψw = −0.4 MPa, so water tends to enter from xylem (−0.2 MPa). Sink phloem Ψw = −0.2 MPa, so water tends to leave toward xylem (−0.4 MPa). Axial hydrostatic pressure difference is 1.0 − 0.5 = +0.5 MPa from source to sink. Notice that the source phloem has a lower total water potential than the sink despite driving bulk flow toward the sink. An open conduit carries the whole solution, not just water across a semipermeable membrane.

Example 2 — Viscosity and radius change together

A tube's effective radius becomes 80% of its initial radius, while viscosity becomes 1.25 times its original value. In the ideal cylinder model with constant length and pressure difference, Q₂/Q₁ = 0.8⁴/1.25 = 0.32768. Flow is about 32.8% of its initial value. A 20% radius reduction is therefore much more consequential than a 20% volume-flow reduction in this model.

Example 3 — Carbon flux is not concentration

Initially C = 0.30 mol L⁻¹ and Q = 4 nL min⁻¹. Later C = 0.45 mol L⁻¹ and Q = 2 nL min⁻¹. Because 1 mol L⁻¹ × 1 nL = 1 nmol, the initial flux is 1.20 nmol min⁻¹ and the later flux is 0.90 nmol min⁻¹. Concentration increased by 50%, but molar delivery decreased by 25%.

Example 4 — A sink branch is restricted

Two branches initially have equal resistance and equal pressure differences. If one branch doubles its resistance while the other remains unchanged and the pressure differences are maintained, the restricted branch carries half its previous flow. Its new share is (1/2)/(1 + 1/2) = 1/3, rather than 1/2. In an actual network the pressure at the junction can adjust, so this answer applies to the specified fixed-pressure case.

12. Xylem and phloem: compare mechanisms without false opposites

Both vascular systems can be understood using physical gradients, but the dominant pressure-generation mechanism differs. Transpiration creates tension in the xylem water column as water evaporates from leaf surfaces. Water moves toward regions of lower hydraulic potential and is supplied through the roots. Phloem loading and unloading maintain osmotic differences that can generate positive hydrostatic pressures. The positive or negative sign of pressure is not, by itself, a statement about direction; flow responds to differences in hydraulic potential.

Xylem transport is not limited to pure water and minerals. Some organic compounds and signals occur in xylem sap. Phloem likewise carries ions as well as organic material. The biological distinction rests on the major functions and characteristic tissue organization. Xylem conducting elements are adapted for hydraulic conduction under tension; phloem conducting elements maintain a selective living boundary that enables osmotic pressure generation and regulated exchange with other cells.

A common examination error is to equate positive pressure with an active pumping mechanism. Positive pressure can arise by osmosis in a walled cell without a water pump. Negative pressure in xylem is not equivalent to a negatively charged sap. Osmotic potential is also not the same as hydraulic pressure: a sucrose-rich sieve tube may have a strongly negative solute potential and a strongly positive pressure potential at the same time.

13. Original NET and SET practice

Choose an answer before opening its explanation. These are original questions, not attributed past papers.

1. Which observation best distinguishes a mature sieve-tube element from a mature vessel element?

  1. Both lack a functional plasma membrane
  2. Sieve elements retain a living membrane and metabolic association with companion cells
  3. Sieve elements are always wider than vessels
  4. Vessel elements contain a functional nucleus
Answer and explanation

B. A sieve element is enucleate but living, unlike a typical mature vessel element. A and D reverse the cellular distinctions; C is not a defining feature.

2. An expanding leaf fixes some carbon but imports more carbon than it exports. It is a:

  1. source only
  2. xylem source
  3. net sink
  4. permanent source after chlorophyll appears
Answer and explanation

C. The role depends on net assimilate transfer, not chlorophyll. A and D confuse photosynthesis with export; B is not the relevant classification.

3. In a horizontal tube, source P = 0.9 MPa and sink P = 0.6 MPa. The immediate axial driver is:

  1. 0.3 MPa pressure difference
  2. 1.5 MPa pressure difference
  3. 0.6 MPa solute potential
  4. ATP hydrolysis at each sieve plate
Answer and explanation

A. Subtract downstream pressure from upstream pressure. B adds pressures; C confuses hydrostatic and solute potentials; D incorrectly assigns a motor to axial flow.

4. For ideal osmotic concentration 0.2 osmol L⁻¹ at 300 K, Ψs is approximately:

  1. +0.50 MPa
  2. −4.99 MPa
  3. −0.05 MPa
  4. −0.50 MPa
Answer and explanation

D. −0.008314 × 300 × 0.2 ≈ −0.499 MPa. A reverses the sign; B and C misplace the decimal.

5. Girdling that leaves xylem initially functional primarily tests:

  1. the necessity of a continuous phloem pathway
  2. the exact pressure gradient
  3. the number of ATP molecules hydrolysed per sucrose in a sieve plate
  4. the identity of every mobile RNA
Answer and explanation

A. It interrupts the organic-transport route. B requires pressure measurements; C assumes a nonexistent axial ATP stoichiometry; D requires molecular identification.

6. Two tubes of equal length carry the same Newtonian fluid under the same pressure difference. Their radii are 2r and r. Ideal Q ratio is:

  1. 2:1
  2. 4:1
  3. 16:1
  4. 8:1
Answer and explanation

C. Q scales with r⁴. The factor four describes the area ratio and also the mean speed ratio in this case, not volume flow. A and D apply inappropriate powers.

7. A cut aphid stylet exudes sap from a functioning sieve element. The strongest immediate inference is:

  1. the sieve element has negative pressure
  2. sap pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure
  3. every sugar molecule moved by active transport
  4. the sink pressure is exactly zero
Answer and explanation

B. Outward exudation supports positive local pressure. A is inconsistent; C and D are not measured by this observation.

8. Which scenario can explain simultaneous upward and downward transport in a plant?

  1. Equal opposite bulk currents in the same tube cross-section
  2. Universal downward transport plus diffusion only
  3. A heart-like pump at the root
  4. Separate sieve tubes connect different sources and sinks
Answer and explanation

D. Different routes have different pressure gradients. A is not ordinary single-stream bulk flow; B and C are incorrect mechanisms.

9. A storage tuber mobilizes reserves for a sprouting shoot. It now acts as:

  1. a source relative to the shoot
  2. a sink in all stages
  3. a nonvascular xylem conduit
  4. a source only if it is photosynthetic
Answer and explanation

A. Stored carbon can be exported without contemporary photosynthesis. B ignores developmental change; C misidentifies the tissue; D excludes reserve mobilization.

10. If a source loading inhibitor reduces source solute concentration and pressure, the decline in transport:

  1. disproves the pressure-flow model
  2. proves that each pore pumps sucrose
  3. is consistent with loss of osmotic pressure generation
  4. must be caused by gravity
Answer and explanation

C. The pressure model relies on maintained solute and pressure differences. A and B confuse energy input at loading with axial movement; D is unrelated to the treatment.

11. A source has Ψs −1.2 MPa and Ψp +0.8 MPa; adjacent xylem Ψw −0.2 MPa. Water tends to:

  1. leave phloem because pressure is positive
  2. enter phloem
  3. remain stationary because both values are negative
  4. move only upward
Answer and explanation

B. Phloem Ψw = −0.4 MPa, lower than xylem. A ignores the solute term; C ignores the difference; D confuses radial and vertical transport.

12. A tracer arrives at detectors 0.30 m apart at 5 min and 15 min. Apparent speed is:

  1. 0.02 m min⁻¹
  2. 0.06 m min⁻¹
  3. 0.3 m min⁻¹
  4. 0.03 m min⁻¹
Answer and explanation

D. Use the interval between detections: 0.30/10 = 0.03 m min⁻¹. A divides by 15, B by 5 and C ignores elapsed time.

13. In the ideal cylinder model, doubling viscosity at fixed radius, length and pressure difference:

  1. halves Q
  2. doubles Q
  3. quadruples Q
  4. leaves Q unchanged
Answer and explanation

A. Viscosity is in the denominator. B and C reverse this relationship; D ignores viscous resistance.

14. A high sucrose concentration in drought-stressed phloem necessarily indicates:

  1. faster carbon delivery
  2. larger tube radius
  3. neither faster delivery nor a known flow rate without additional information
  4. zero resistance
Answer and explanation

C. Delivery is concentration times flow. A neglects Q; B and D cannot be inferred from concentration.

15. Which statement about selected mobile proteins is defensible?

  1. All proteins enter phloem freely
  2. Certain proteins can be selectively transferred and delivered to distant tissues
  3. Proteins never occur in phloem sap
  4. Mobile proteins prove that sucrose travels only by diffusion
Answer and explanation

B. Selective mobility is compatible with bulk flow and regulated trafficking. A and C are unjustified absolutes; D does not follow from protein movement.

16. For upward transport over 10 m, ρ ≈ 1000 kg m⁻³ and ΔP = 0.25 MPa. The pressure available for friction is approximately:

  1. 0.348 MPa
  2. 0.25 MPa
  3. −0.25 MPa
  4. 0.152 MPa
Answer and explanation

D. Subtract ρgΔz ≈ 0.098 MPa. A adds gravity instead of subtracting it; B neglects elevation; C reverses the driving pressure.

17. Assertion–reason

Assertion: The source sieve tube may have positive hydrostatic pressure despite a negative solute potential. Reason: Osmotic water entry into a walled cell can increase hydrostatic pressure.

Answer and explanation

Both are true and the reason explains the assertion. Solute and pressure potential are distinct contributions to water potential.

18. Analytical design

A mutant fixes normal carbon but accumulates labelled sucrose in mature leaves and delivers little to roots. Suggest two measurements distinguishing defective loading from a resistive transport segment.

Answer and explanation

Measure labelled carbon entering the source phloem and compare tracer movement between stem positions after it enters. Low entry supports a loading defect; normal entry followed by unusually delayed movement along a segment supports increased transport resistance. Source pressure and tube anatomy offer further independent tests. Neither a whole-leaf sugar assay nor final root radioactivity alone identifies the limiting stage.

14. Consolidation and next chapter

The phloem is a living vascular network whose conductive architecture permits bulk movement of a solution. Source and sink identity depends on carbon balance and can change with development. Source loading and sink unloading alter osmotic conditions; water exchange with neighbouring tissues establishes positive but unequal pressures. The axial pressure difference, corrected for gravity when necessary, drives flow through a resistance. Carbon delivery depends on the concentration of transported compounds and the volume flow rate.

Recall without looking

Explain why enucleation is not death. Predict the outcome of girdling a stem with source leaves above the ring. Distinguish radial water potential from axial pressure. Explain why a sugar-rich sink can have positive pressure and why two sieve tubes may carry opposite flow directions. Derive the radius dependence of ideal volume flow and state its limitations.
