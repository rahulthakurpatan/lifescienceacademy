1. The distribution problem in a plant

A photosynthetic leaf makes more organic carbon than it needs for its own immediate maintenance during much of the growing season. A root tip, by contrast, may have vigorous respiration and cell division without receiving enough light to fix carbon. Between these organs there may be tens of centimetres of stem, or in a tree many metres of trunk and branches. The essential physiological problem is not simply making sugars, but delivering them to places where they can be used. Plants solve this problem through a specialised transport tissue, the phloem. Translocation is the movement of assimilates through that tissue from organs that export them to organs that import them. Assimilates are products of metabolism that have been incorporated into organic compounds; photoassimilates refer particularly to products derived from photosynthetic carbon fixation.

A useful starting distinction is between producing organic carbon and moving organic carbon. Carbon dioxide enters a leaf and is fixed into organic intermediates by photosynthesis. Some carbon is retained as temporary starch or used to build and maintain leaf structures. Another fraction is converted into soluble sugars for export. In a great many vascular plants sucrose is the principal sugar exported over long distances, although some species also transport substantial amounts of raffinose family oligosaccharides or sugar alcohols. The exact composition is not universal. Sugar transport supports root respiration, production of new leaves, seed filling, fruit development and seasonal storage. Nitrogenous compounds and selected signals can travel in the same vascular network, so the phloem is simultaneously a resource distribution pathway and a route of interorgan communication.

Why is a dedicated conduit useful? Diffusion is effective over short cellular distances but its characteristic time rises approximately with the square of distance. The relation t ≈ L²/(2D) describes one dimensional diffusion over a characteristic length L for diffusion coefficient D, under idealised conditions. Doubling the distance therefore roughly quadruples the time. If D is taken as 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m² s⁻¹ for an illustrative dissolved sugar in water, diffusion over one millimetre has a characteristic time of about 1,000 seconds, whereas diffusion over one metre would take approximately 10⁹ seconds, or about 32 years. Real tissue introduces additional barriers, and the estimate is not a measured transport time in plants. It shows why diffusion alone cannot reliably supply rapidly growing organs across a long stem. Bulk movement of liquid, in which dissolved molecules are carried along by the moving solvent, solves a fundamentally different physical problem.

The phloem is not an animal circulatory system in miniature. There is no central heart that contracts to propel sap around a closed loop, and a vascular strand does not have a universal direction of travel determined by its orientation relative to the ground. Instead, resource production, selective membrane transport and consumption establish local differences in water status and hydrostatic pressure. The differences drive fluid through a connected pathway. To understand this system, it is helpful to examine the identity of the source and sink before considering the anatomy and the physical model.

2. Sources, sinks and developmental change

A source is a tissue that exports a net quantity of assimilate to the transport system during a specified interval. A sink is a tissue that imports a net quantity. Both definitions concern a balance, not a fixed type of organ. A mature green leaf often exports sucrose because its photosynthetic production exceeds its local consumption and storage. A young leaf, even though it is green and can photosynthesise, may import carbon because cell division, wall synthesis and expansion demand more carbon than its developing photosynthetic machinery provides. As the leaf matures, the balance can change. The transition can occur progressively across the leaf rather than simultaneously in every cell.

Storage tissues demonstrate particularly clearly why the terms cannot be assigned permanently. A potato tuber accumulating starch is a sink for carbon from leaves. During sprouting, mobilisation of stored carbohydrate can make it a source for the developing shoots. Likewise, a germinating seed can release reserve carbon from storage tissues to its growing embryo before a photosynthetically competent shoot becomes the principal external source. A fruit filling with sugars is normally a sink even if parts of the fruit are photosynthetic. Detecting chlorophyll or observing some fixation of carbon dioxide does not establish net export. The relevant comparison is between total local supply and local expenditure plus accumulation.

A simple carbon balance clarifies this distinction. For an organ, the change in its carbon pool per unit time equals carbon fixation plus imported carbon minus exported carbon minus respiratory and other external carbon losses. If a mature leaf fixes ten arbitrary units of carbon per hour, loses three through respiration, and accumulates two in its tissues or storage pool, then five units remain as net export under the assumed absence of other losses. If a developing organ fixes two units, loses three through respiration and increases its tissue carbon by four, it requires a net import of five units. These examples are invented teaching balances, not measured values. A crucial point is that a growing source can still export and an active sink can still photosynthesise: the direction of net exchange follows from the entire balance.

One source can contribute carbon to several sinks. In a branching plant the pattern depends partly on vascular connections and partly on the transport and metabolic capacities of competing organs. An expanding apex and a root tip may be supplied at the same time by distinct branches of the phloem network. Their demand can vary with time. Removing a developing fruit can alter allocation to other growing tissues without implying that the remaining sinks have moved anatomically. The quantitative distribution of assimilates is developed further in the chapter on unloading and partitioning; here the key idea is that changing source and sink identities changes the hydraulic boundary conditions of transport.

Source status is also relative to time of day. In a leaf that accumulated starch during daylight, mobilisation of starch at night can sustain sucrose export even though contemporaneous photosynthetic fixation has ceased. Thus a darkened leaf is not instantaneously guaranteed to stop exporting. Distinguish a change in photosynthetic rate from a change in net carbon exchange, and a transient change in exchange from the eventual adjustment of the whole transport system.

3. The sieve element–companion cell partnership

The phloem is a complex living tissue, not a collection of identical sugar pipes. In angiosperms, its axial conducting cells are sieve tube elements arranged end to end. Their adjoining end walls are modified into sieve plates containing pores that permit passage of sap between adjacent elements. A series of sieve tube elements connected through these plates forms a sieve tube. Phloem also contains companion cells, parenchyma and, depending on the tissue and species, fibres and other specialised cells. Conducting sieve elements should be distinguished from the supporting cells of the tissue as a whole.

During differentiation a typical angiosperm sieve tube element loses its nucleus and much of the cellular machinery that would occupy its internal volume. This specialised reduction leaves a relatively unobstructed path for sap while retaining a functional plasma membrane and a limited complement of living cellular components. The element is therefore alive even though it has no functional nucleus at maturity. Enucleation in a sieve element is not equivalent to the cell death that produces a mature vessel element. Confusing the two obscures the organisation of plant vascular tissue: one conduit has a living membrane that maintains selective interactions with neighbouring cells, whereas a typical mature xylem vessel element is a nonliving conducting cell.

Companion cells are nucleated, metabolically active cells closely associated with the sieve elements. In angiosperms, a companion cell and its associated sieve element arise through division of a common precursor. Specialised plasmodesmata connect them and permit selected substances to move between their cytoplasms. The companion cell supports maintenance of the conducting cell and participates in the transport of assimilates into or out of the conducting complex, depending on location and species. Since a mature sieve element cannot perform ordinary nuclear transcription, its long term function depends strongly on this partnership. The connected cells are physiologically integrated, but their roles and cellular structures are not identical.

Sieve plates are both enabling structures and sources of resistance. A pore permits movement between neighbouring cells, yet sap must converge toward each opening and diverge after passing it. Several successive plates therefore contribute to the total hydraulic resistance of a tube. Pores vary in size and arrangement. Damage may trigger occlusion involving callose deposition and, in some angiosperms, phloem proteins. These responses can limit sap loss after injury, but a preparation that causes pore plugging may give a misleading impression of the state of an intact conducting tube. An anatomical image of a pore is not by itself a direct measurement of flow.

Gymnosperms offer an important comparison. Their conducting sieve cells generally have sieve areas rather than the highly specialised end wall sieve plates typical of angiosperm sieve tubes. Sieve cells are physiologically associated with albuminous cells, also known as Strasburger cells, rather than true companion cells arising from the same immediate precursor. The structural distinction does not imply that gymnosperms cannot use pressure driven assimilate transport. It instead shows that different anatomical organisations can serve the same broad physiological function. A question that equates all phloem with angiosperm sieve tube elements erases this distinction.

The position of phloem relative to xylem varies across organs and vascular arrangements. In the typical collateral vascular bundle of an angiosperm stem, phloem lies external to xylem, a fact that is important in interpreting a ringing experiment. Root vascular anatomy and specialised bundles should not be forced into this single pattern. What matters for transport is that the conducting systems lie within tissues across which water and solutes can be exchanged. Source phloem in leaf veins collects sugars, axial transport phloem distributes them, and sink phloem releases them to importing tissues. These are functional zones of a connected network, not three entirely unrelated conducting cell types.

4. Phloem sap: resources and information

Phloem sap is an aqueous solution containing transported organic carbon, inorganic ions and other dissolved components. Sucrose is common because it is soluble, provides carbon and energy to importing organs, and is nonreducing under ordinary conditions. Sucrose contains glucose and fructose joined through their anomeric carbons; its transport does not require that each sucrose molecule be hydrolysed into monosaccharides before entering the long distance stream. Species differ in the sugars that dominate the sap, so an examiner may describe a plant transporting sorbitol or raffinose family sugars without contradicting the general concept of phloem translocation.

Amino acids in phloem sap carry organic nitrogen and support protein synthesis in developing organs. Potassium and other ions contribute to osmotic properties and electrical balance, and hormones or hormone related compounds may be transported in particular circumstances. A sap sample is therefore not equivalent to a pure sucrose solution. In a simple osmotic calculation, all osmotically effective particles contribute to solute potential. Using sucrose concentration alone to estimate the entire osmotic potential is an approximation that can omit significant contributions from other solutes.

Some proteins and RNAs enter the phloem through regulated pathways. For example, the flowering signal involving FLOWERING LOCUS T protein in Arabidopsis illustrates how information originating in leaves can affect a distant shoot apex. The existence of a mobile protein is not evidence that every protein is mobile, that all phloem proteins act as signals, or that intact proteins from the soil normally move freely through the plant. Delivery depends on access to the conducting system, stability during transport, exit into relevant tissues and activity in the recipient. Finding a molecule in a sap sample establishes presence only if the sample is authentic; demonstrating a physiological signaling function requires further evidence.

The physical movement of sap and the selective transport of individual compounds must be separated. Once a soluble constituent is inside an unobstructed conducting path, it can be carried with the moving fluid. Whether the constituent crosses from a companion cell into that path is a different question. Whether it subsequently leaves the path and produces a response is different again. This distinction explains how the phloem can have a common mechanism of long distance movement while the distribution of particular signals or metabolites remains regulated.

5. The Münch pressure-flow hypothesis

The pressure-flow hypothesis, associated with Ernst Münch and developed in the early twentieth century, explains how local osmotic effects can establish a long distance hydrostatic pressure difference. Consider a simplified system consisting of two compartments connected by a tube that conducts solution. The compartments exchange water with their surroundings across membranes that allow water to pass while restricting the dissolved sugar. At the source end, addition of sugar lowers the water chemical potential inside the compartment. Water enters if its potential is higher outside. Because the compartment has finite volume and a mechanically resisting boundary, water entry raises hydrostatic pressure. At the sink end, removal of sugar tends to raise solute potential and reduce the osmotic tendency to retain water. Water may leave and hydrostatic pressure can be lower than at the source.

This is the fundamental conversion in the model: a difference in solute concentration across a water permeable boundary is transformed into a difference in hydrostatic pressure. The axial pressure gradient then moves solution through the connecting tube. In a plant, loading assimilates into collection phloem tends to create the high pressure boundary condition, while release and utilisation in sink tissues tend to sustain a lower pressure boundary condition. Xylem and intervening tissues supply or receive water according to local water potential differences. The diagram of a pair of osmometers in a water bath captures this relationship but it is not a literal anatomical drawing of a plant. The surrounding water in a real plant has its own spatial gradients, the tube exchanges material along its path, and different tissues do not always behave as ideal semipermeable compartments.

Water potential provides the language needed to predict the direction of lateral water exchange. Ignoring gravity and matric terms over a small local distance, Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. Here Ψs represents the usually negative contribution from solutes and Ψp is hydrostatic pressure relative to the chosen reference. If the source phloem initially has Ψs = −1.2 MPa and Ψp = +0.3 MPa, its total Ψw is −0.9 MPa. If nearby xylem has Ψw = −0.5 MPa, water tends to enter the phloem. As pressure increases, the potential difference diminishes. If concentrations were held constant and water came to local equilibrium, Ψp would reach +0.7 MPa because −1.2 + 0.7 = −0.5. This is an equilibrium estimate, not a claim that real transport phloem everywhere is exactly equilibrated with nearby xylem.

At a sink, unloading can reduce the solute concentration of the conducting complex. If the sink phloem has Ψs = −0.7 MPa and Ψp = +0.4 MPa, its water potential is −0.3 MPa. Adjacent xylem at −0.5 MPa has lower water potential, so water tends to leave that phloem region. Under continuing loading and unloading, water entry, water exit and axial flow can coexist in a dynamic steady state. A source pressure of +0.7 MPa and a sink pressure of +0.4 MPa provide a pressure drop of 0.3 MPa. Both pressures are positive; the sink is not required to be under tension or at atmospheric pressure.

The axial flow should not be described simply as osmosis from source to sink. Osmosis refers to water movement across a selectively permeable boundary, and it helps establish the pressure difference. Along a conducting sieve tube the sap moves as a liquid mixture through openings. The driving force of the axial bulk flow is the hydrostatic pressure gradient, with gravity included where appropriate. The dissolved sugar moves with the solution even though individual molecules also diffuse relative to it. This distinction is particularly valuable in an examination problem that deliberately compares a water potential gradient across membranes with a hydrostatic gradient along a conduit.

The energy question also needs two answers. The axial movement of sap down a pressure gradient is passive in the hydraulic sense. It is not driven by a molecular motor that carries individual sucrose molecules along every sieve element. Generating and maintaining the gradient, however, may require metabolic energy: photosynthesis supplies carbon and energy indirectly, active loading in many plants depends on proton gradients generated by ATP consuming pumps, and sink metabolism sustains removal. Other plants load using different mechanisms, including passive symplastic transfer. Calling the whole process either entirely active or entirely passive conceals this separation between formation of a driving force and dissipation of that force by flow.

6. Pressure, resistance and quantitative reasoning

For a simple horizontal tube, the volumetric flow rate Q can be represented by Q = ΔP/Rh, where ΔP is the pressure difference between its ends and Rh is hydraulic resistance. This expression assumes a specified path, a steady flow regime and an effective resistance appropriate to the fluid and geometry. In SI units Q is measured in cubic metres per second, ΔP in pascals, and Rh in pascal seconds per cubic metre. Hydraulic conductance is the reciprocal of resistance. A pressure difference does not determine the rate by itself: two tubes with different resistances can have different flows despite identical end pressures.

For fully developed laminar flow through a rigid, straight cylindrical tube of radius r and length L containing a Newtonian liquid of viscosity η, the ideal Poiseuille relation is Q = πr⁴ΔP/(8ηL). The corresponding resistance is 8ηL/(πr⁴). A real sieve tube has sieve plates and a complex internal geometry, so this formula is a model for the open lumen rather than an exact description of the entire vascular pathway. Its most useful insight is sensitivity to radius: if all other quantities are fixed, doubling the radius increases the ideal volumetric conductance sixteenfold. Doubling length or viscosity instead doubles the ideal resistance. The same sixteenfold statement should not be applied blindly to a sieve plate, whose pores and entrance geometry contribute their own resistance.

If a horizontal transport path has an effective resistance of 2 × 10¹⁸ Pa s m⁻³ and a pressure drop of 0.2 MPa, the pressure drop must first be converted to 2 × 10⁵ Pa. Dividing by the resistance gives a volumetric flow rate of 10⁻¹³ m³ s⁻¹. Since one cubic metre contains 10¹⁵ picolitres, this is 100 pL s⁻¹. If the sap contains 0.4 mol L⁻¹ sucrose, or 400 mol m⁻³, the convective sucrose flow is Q times concentration, equal to 4 × 10⁻¹¹ mol s⁻¹. The calculation assumes a fixed concentration and neglects the diffusive contribution. It predicts a rate of carbon delivery for a stated pathway; it is not an estimate of any particular species.

A second quantity is velocity. For a tube with cross sectional area A, the mean sap velocity is Q/A. A high volumetric flow through a wide path need not imply high velocity. If a source exports the same sugar concentration through two tubes but one tube delivers twice the volumetric flow, it delivers twice the sugar per unit time in the simple convective model. If instead the source pressure rises while viscosity also rises because the sap becomes more concentrated, the two effects compete. Increased concentration cannot be assumed to increase flux in exact proportion because the pressure, resistance and concentration can all change.

Gravity can be incorporated by considering the hydraulic head or an equivalent pressure contribution. With elevation z measured upward and fluid density ρ, flow tends toward lower values of P + ρgz in an otherwise uniform fluid path. For waterlike density, the gravitational pressure equivalent over ten metres is about 0.098 MPa. Upward pressure driven flow therefore requires enough pressure difference to overcome both gravity and resistance; downward flow can be aided by gravity. Neither conclusion establishes a universal anatomical direction for phloem. A source below a developing shoot can drive upward flow if the hydraulic driving force is sufficient.

A steady transport path can be represented by serial resistances of lumen segments and sieve plates. Serial hydraulic resistances add when the same volume flow passes through all components. Parallel branches provide alternative pathways and share the total flow according to their conductances and boundary pressures. Suppose two sinks have equal pressure but their connecting paths have resistances R and 2R. With a common source pressure and no other differences, their volumetric flow rates are in the ratio two to one. If sink pressure or concentration changes, the ratio need not be preserved. This is the minimum network reasoning required before interpreting resource allocation in a whole plant.

7. Directionality and the xylem–phloem relationship

A mature leaf can supply an apex above it and a root system below it. This does not require two opposing streams to pass simultaneously through the same cross section of one simple sieve tube. Different connected conducting paths can have different boundary pressures and therefore different net directions of flow. At a branch point, sap can be distributed into alternative routes. In a single unobstructed path at a given instant, a pressure gradient sets the net bulk direction; individual solutes may diffuse relative to the flowing liquid, but random molecular motion is not a second oppositely directed bulk stream.

Xylem and phloem both support long distance transport through fluid conduits, yet their typical driving conditions differ. During transpiration, xylem is commonly under negative hydrostatic pressure, and the evaporation of water from leaf surfaces contributes to the tension pulling the water column. Phloem typically operates under positive turgor generated in relation to solute loading. Xylem carries predominantly water and mineral nutrients, whereas phloem carries organic assimilates and selected signals along with water and ions. These contrasts are not absolute descriptions of every fluid constituent or pressure under every physiological condition: root pressure can make xylem pressure positive, and phloem flow can change or cease as local conditions change.

The two tissues exchange water through surrounding tissues. At a source, the entry of water into phloem helps develop the pressure needed for export. At a sink, sugar release may permit water to move toward xylem or other local tissues. It is misleading to assume that each water molecule leaving sink phloem returns immediately to the same source through the xylem. In a transpiring plant, xylem is part of a broader hydraulic system supplying many organs, and water can be retained, lost or redistributed. The simplified water recycling loop explains the pressure mechanism without asserting a closed circulation.

Changes in xylem water potential can affect the pressure that a given phloem solute concentration can sustain. If nearby xylem becomes more negative during drought, the same source sugar concentration may support a lower equilibrium turgor than under well watered conditions. At the same time drought can modify photosynthesis, sink growth, viscosity and transport capacity. A reduction in assimilate delivery during drought is therefore consistent with pressure-flow reasoning but is not proof that one particular variable caused the reduction. Interpreting an experiment requires measurements that separate hydraulic changes from changes in carbon production or demand.

8. Experiments: from pathway identification to causal testing

A ringing or girdling experiment removes a ring of bark from an appropriate woody stem while attempting to preserve the functioning xylem. In a typical arrangement, the removed tissue interrupts the external phloem. Sugars and other assimilates can accumulate above the interruption when leaves above the girdle continue producing carbon and downward export is blocked. Below the girdle, roots may receive less newly fixed carbon and eventually experience starvation. Water supply to leaves may persist initially through the intact xylem. The observation supports the conclusion that a principal pathway for downward organic translocation lies in the removed tissues rather than exclusively within xylem.

A girdle is not a perfectly specific molecular intervention. The wound can affect hormone transport, cambial tissues, water relations and metabolism; over longer periods secondary injury may alter xylem function. An appropriate comparison uses otherwise similar ungirdled or sham treated stems and measurements at defined times. Carbon labeling can help distinguish accumulation of newly exported assimilates from pre-existing reserves. Girdling alone identifies a transport route and demonstrates the consequence of interruption; it does not measure the source to sink hydrostatic pressure difference and cannot independently establish the quantitative pressure-flow relation.

Aphids obtain sap using a stylet that can penetrate a sieve element. When a stylet remains in a conducting element after separation from the insect, sap may exude through it under pressure. The resulting sample can be analysed for sugars and other constituents. Positive exudation is consistent with the existence of positive hydrostatic pressure in the sampled sieve element. Pressure measurement using an appropriate instrument provides stronger quantitative evidence than the simple observation of exudation. The identity of the penetrated tissue, the effect of wounding and changes in the sampled pathway must be considered. An exudate composition or flow rate should not be treated as an undisturbed whole plant measurement without those qualifications.

Radiocarbon tracing provides a different kind of information. Exposing a source leaf to carbon dioxide containing carbon-14 can label photosynthetic products. Detection of radioactivity in other organs at later times reveals movement of recently assimilated carbon. Chemical separation is needed if the question concerns which labeled compounds carry the carbon. Autoradiography can reveal spatial distributions, while repeated sampling can estimate the timing of arrival. A pulse followed by unlabeled carbon dioxide distinguishes the movement and subsequent metabolism of a defined labeling period. Radioactivity in a sink is not automatically a measure of sucrose concentration because labeled carbon may have been converted into other compounds. A label front also need not move at precisely the mean sap velocity when exchange, mixing and metabolism occur.

A strong mechanistic test combines measurements that constrain the physical model: source and sink pressure, transport path resistance, length and flow rate. Knoblauch and colleagues investigated these factors in morning glory (Ipomoea nil) in a 2016 eLife study. Their work connected transport distance with adjustments in conducting tissue properties and pressure, providing evidence that a pressure driven mechanism can account for long distance transport in that system. The general lesson is not that every plant has the same pressure difference or sieve pore dimensions. It is that an apparent mismatch between transport rate and a model's predicted pressure requirement can sometimes be explained by testing its anatomical and hydraulic assumptions rather than rejecting the driving mechanism outright.

Consider an illustrative test in which two comparable stems have the same end pressures, but in one the conducting pathway is partially obstructed. A lower flux in the obstructed stem would be consistent with higher hydraulic resistance. If the flux remained unchanged, one should test whether local loading or unloading changed the pressure profile or whether other conducting paths compensated. A single observation of maintained transport does not establish the absence of resistance. Conversely, observing a positive source pressure alone is insufficient to prove that the source to sink pressure drop is large enough to explain the measured flow. Evidence for the pathway, evidence for pressure, evidence for transport direction and evidence for the quantitative driving relation answer related but distinct questions.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A source is always a leaf. Source is defined by net export; mobilising storage organs can become sources.
  2. A green young leaf must export. Its demand can exceed carbon fixation, making it a net importer.
  3. Sieve elements are dead. Mature angiosperm sieve elements lack a nucleus but retain a living plasma membrane.
  4. Companion cells are the main axial conduit. Sieve tubes conduct the axial sap stream; companion cells support their function.
  5. All vascular plants have companion cells. Gymnosperms possess sieve cells associated with albuminous cells.
  6. Phloem carries only sucrose. Sap also includes water, ions, amino acids and selected signaling molecules.
  7. Osmosis drives the entire axial stream. Osmosis establishes pressure through lateral exchange; axial bulk flow is pressure driven.
  8. The sink must have negative pressure. Both endpoints may have positive pressure, with lower pressure at the sink.
  9. ATP powers every sucrose molecule along the tube. Metabolism can generate the driving gradient; axial bulk movement is passive.
  10. Every sieve tube transports both ways simultaneously. Distinct pathways can have opposite net directions.
  11. Girdling directly measures turgor. It interrupts a pathway and does not directly measure pressure.
  12. Aphid exudation proves the full quantitative model. It supports positive pressure; testing adequacy requires pressure drop, flow and resistance.
  13. A tracer measures the pressure gradient. It demonstrates carbon movement and distribution, subject to metabolism and mixing.
  14. A doubling of lumen radius doubles conductance. Ideal Poiseuille conductance scales with radius to the fourth power.
  15. All phloem water returns to the same leaf. Water can enter other tissues or the transpiration stream; the plant is not a closed loop.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

Which cell forms the main axial conducting path in angiosperm phloem?

  1. Companion cell
  2. Sieve tube element
  3. Tracheid
  4. Guard cell
Answer and explanation

B. Sieve elements connect through sieve plates. Companion cells maintain the conducting complex, tracheids conduct xylem sap, and guard cells regulate stomata.

2. Part B

Which property identifies a source organ?

  1. Absence of photosynthesis
  2. Positive xylem pressure
  3. Presence of chloroplasts alone
  4. Net export of assimilates
Answer and explanation

D. Source and sink describe net carbon exchange, rather than a single cellular feature.

3. Part B

What moves sap axially in the Münch model?

  1. Hydrostatic pressure difference
  2. Direct ATP hydrolysis by sucrose
  3. Evaporation inside each sieve tube
  4. Random diffusion alone
Answer and explanation

A. Osmotic water entry creates a pressure difference; the mixture flows down the hydraulic gradient.

4. Part B

Gymnosperm conducting sieve cells are associated with which cells?

  1. Guard cells
  2. Vessel elements
  3. Albuminous cells
  4. Root hairs
Answer and explanation

C. Albuminous cells provide physiological support; true companion cells characterise angiosperms.

5. Part C

Two horizontal tubes have identical effective resistance. Their pressure drops are 0.4 and 0.1 MPa. What is their flow ratio?

  1. 1:4
  2. 2:1
  3. 1:1
  4. 4:1
Answer and explanation

D. For equal resistance Q is proportional to pressure drop. The ratio is 0.4/0.1 = 4.

6. Part C

Source phloem has solute potential −1.0 MPa and pressure +0.3 MPa. Adjacent xylem water potential is −0.4 MPa. Predict the initial water exchange.

  1. Phloem to xylem
  2. Xylem to phloem
  3. No exchange
  4. Direction depends on sucrose molecular mass alone
Answer and explanation

B. Phloem water potential is −0.7 MPa, below xylem potential. Water moves toward the phloem if the pathway is permeable.

7. Part C

A plant continues exporting recently fixed carbon through an obstructed pathway at the original measured rate. Which follow-up most directly tests hydraulic compensation?

  1. Count chloroplasts in roots
  2. Measure stomatal number only
  3. Measure local pressures and alternative conducting paths
  4. Determine genome size
Answer and explanation

C. Maintained flow can reflect a changed pressure profile or alternate routes. Pressure and pathway measurements test these possibilities.

8. Part C

A storage organ accumulates carbon during summer and mobilises it during spring sprouting. Which inference is justified?

  1. It may change from a net sink to a source
  2. Its xylem must permanently reverse
  3. Its companion cells become vessel elements
  4. Its sieve plates disappear
Answer and explanation

A. Net import during storage and net export during mobilisation define a developmental source–sink transition.

Revision and mastery

Distinguish net source–sink exchange, living sieve elements, local osmotic exchange, axial hydrostatic flow and the separate evidence supporting each. Review the assumptions behind Q = ΔP/Rh and the ideal lumen fourth-power relation.
