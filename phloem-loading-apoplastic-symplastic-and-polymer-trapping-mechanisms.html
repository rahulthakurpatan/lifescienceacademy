1. The carbon distribution problem

A mature illuminated leaf can produce more organic carbon than it needs for its own immediate growth. A root tip, an enclosed young leaf and a developing seed, by contrast, may require carbon before they are able to manufacture enough through photosynthesis. The separation between production and consumption is a consequence of the specialization of plant organs. To sustain the whole organism, the photosynthetic leaf must transfer a portion of its carbon to the conducting tissue. The movement of photoassimilates into the collecting phloem of a source is called phloem loading. It is distinct from the longitudinal movement of sap and from the subsequent delivery of solutes to a sink.

Carbon dioxide enters the Calvin–Benson cycle in the chloroplast, where carbon is incorporated into organic molecules. A fraction of triose phosphate can leave the chloroplast in exchange for inorganic phosphate through a transporter of the inner envelope. In the cytosol, the pathways of carbohydrate metabolism produce sucrose. Sucrose-phosphate synthase transfers a glucosyl group from UDP-glucose to fructose-6-phosphate, generating sucrose-6-phosphate; sucrose-phosphate phosphatase hydrolyses the phosphate ester. This sequence produces free sucrose that can be used by the cell, stored temporarily or exported. Not all fixed carbon immediately becomes exportable sucrose. Some is retained in chloroplast starch, some enters biosynthetic pathways and some is consumed through respiration. At night, starch mobilization can supply carbohydrate for sucrose production after photosynthetic carbon fixation has stopped.

Sucrose is a nonreducing disaccharide composed of glucose and fructose whose anomeric carbons participate in the glycosidic linkage. Compared with a free reducing hexose, it is less reactive in reactions involving an exposed carbonyl group. It is water soluble and can carry substantial carbon in a single dissolved particle. These characteristics are compatible with transport, but they do not by themselves explain why every species uses a particular transport sugar. Some plants export significant quantities of raffinose-family oligosaccharides and others transport sugar alcohols in addition to sucrose. The biochemical identity of the transported carbohydrate must therefore be measured rather than assumed from the presence of photosynthesis.

A source is defined by net export, not by the anatomical name of an organ. An immature leaf can import assimilate while a mature leaf of the same plant exports it. A storage organ may become a source when reserves are mobilized. Within a source leaf, the collecting phloem of the minor veins receives sugars across relatively short radial distances from surrounding photosynthetic tissue. The conducting phloem carries the loaded sap over long distances. Loading is thus a local transfer process that establishes conditions for a different physical process, pressure-driven mass flow.

A source leaf must solve two related problems. First, carbohydrate must cross a series of interfaces between mesophyll cells and the sieve element–companion cell complex. Second, loading must sustain a sufficient osmotic and hydrostatic driving force for export while the source itself remains metabolically viable. A pathway that accumulates carbohydrate in the phloem but excessively depletes the surrounding cells would not be sustainable. Conversely, very high carbon fixation is not enough if the transfer pathway into the conducting system has become limiting. These distinctions help explain why a transport-defective plant may accumulate carbohydrate in leaves while growing poorly elsewhere.

2. How the evidence for loading mechanisms developed

Before a mechanistic explanation of phloem loading was possible, physiologists had to distinguish the movement of assimilates from the movement of water and mineral nutrients. The conducting tissues differ in their structure, contents and typical driving forces. Girdling experiments, in which a ring of bark containing phloem is removed while the inner xylem remains substantially intact, can reveal the dependence of assimilate transport on the phloem. Carbohydrate may accumulate above the girdled region while tissues below receive less from the leaves. This classical observation supports the role of phloem in assimilate distribution. It does not distinguish whether sucrose entered that phloem through plasmodesmata or across a membrane, because girdling interrupts the longitudinal pathway after local loading has occurred.

The pressure-flow hypothesis, associated with Ernst Münch, offered a physical framework for explaining long-distance transport. In such a framework, maintenance of solute concentrations at sources and sinks can generate hydrostatic pressure differences. The important question for loading physiology then becomes how the source establishes its sugar concentration. A pressure-flow model does not itself require that sucrose be pumped across a membrane in every plant. Either an energetically coupled transporter or a symplastic pathway may supply solutes that participate in establishing a pressure gradient. Thus evidence supporting mass flow cannot be used to select one of the three loading mechanisms.

As the sugar composition of leaves and phloem became better understood, researchers recognized the potential importance of concentration differences at the loading interface. In plants whose sieve tube sugar concentrations are high relative to nearby photosynthetic tissues, simple diffusion of the same sugar down a concentration gradient appears insufficient to explain accumulation. An energy-coupled step provides a possible solution. The demonstration that uptake of sucrose by plant membranes can depend on the proton electrochemical gradient established a biochemical mechanism by which a neutral solute may accumulate against its concentration gradient. However, the existence of a sucrose uptake system does not demonstrate that all species depend on it for the primary loading step; transporter function may include retrieval of sugar that leaks from the conducting pathway.

The development of cell-specific localization techniques helped connect transport proteins to anatomically defined interfaces. Localization of a sucrose symporter in a minor vein companion cell is more informative for loading than an observation of transporter expression somewhere in the entire leaf. Nonetheless, expression at that site is a prediction of a possible function rather than an independent proof of necessity. A protein could participate in retrieval or operate only under particular developmental conditions. Genetic experiments subsequently strengthened the connection between specific transporters and physiological assimilate export. The SUC2 study in Arabidopsis provides an instructive example because altering a known transporter produced defects in source-to-sink distribution rather than merely altering uptake in an isolated preparation.

Identification of the receiving transporter left the upstream question of how sucrose became available in the extracellular space. An intact lipid bilayer is a strong obstacle to free passage of sucrose; the receiving symporter cannot recover substrate unless it is delivered outside the exporting cell. The identification of sucrose-transporting SWEET proteins, and the phloem loading defects in Arabidopsis with disruption of SWEET11 and SWEET12, supplied a molecular explanation for an important part of the export step. The discovery of a facilitated export system was conceptually significant because it showed how an energetically uphill accumulation pathway can contain a passive step. The proton motive force is used on the receiving side; the facilitated efflux step supplies substrate to it.

The recognition of distinct companion cell types and extensive plasmodesmatal connectivity led to a complementary line of investigation. In some species, the anatomy of minor veins appeared especially well suited for direct cytoplasmic movement from photosynthetic cells to the phloem. If these plants also transport substantial quantities of raffinose-family sugars, the conversion of incoming sucrose into larger molecules offers an explanation for solute accumulation without a compulsory extracellular sucrose uptake step. Genetic suppression of galactinol synthase in Verbascum, reported in 2007, tested whether the biochemical conversion actually mattered for export. The outcome supports the functional significance of RFO synthesis in that system.

Comparative studies of plants with different minor vein anatomy challenged the expectation that all species must actively concentrate sugars at the loading interface. If mesophyll cytosol can already sustain sufficiently high sucrose activity, direct symplastic movement may be adequate. In 2009, the comparative investigation by Rennie and Turgeon placed particular emphasis on evidence for passive loading in many plants, including woody species. This enlarged the set of mechanisms that a general model must explain. It is not a chronological sequence in which passive loading is a replacement for the other mechanisms; the three strategies describe existing physiological diversity.

The scientific history is useful for examination reasoning because each kind of evidence addresses a different question. Girdling can establish that a particular conducting tissue is important for export. A transport assay can establish that a protein moves a substrate under particular conditions. Localization can identify the likely tissue interface. A mutant can test the functional contribution of the protein or pathway. Comparative anatomy and measurements of sugar pools can identify alternative routes in different plants. Students should not substitute evidence from one category for evidence required in another. In a problem where a newly isolated transporter is found to carry sucrose in cultured cells, it would be premature to classify its native species as an apoplastic loader without additional physiological observations.

3. How source leaves maintain the supply of exportable sucrose

The carbon delivered to a minor vein depends on how a leaf divides freshly fixed carbon among pathways that compete for common intermediates. In illuminated mesophyll cells, the chloroplast converts a portion of the triose phosphates into starch while the cytosol receives another portion through the triose phosphate/phosphate translocator. Export from the chloroplast is linked to return of inorganic phosphate, because the exchange helps maintain the phosphate required for chloroplast metabolism. Cytosolic sucrose synthesis releases phosphate in its final dephosphorylation step. Thus the synthesis of transport sugar is not independent of chloroplast carbon metabolism. If carbohydrate export and utilization are restricted, the availability of metabolic intermediates and the regulation of photosynthetic reactions can change.

An individual mature leaf may store starch during part of the day while simultaneously exporting sucrose. This arrangement buffers differences between the instantaneous rates of carbon fixation and carbon demand. In darkness, a leaf does not receive the same influx of newly fixed carbon, yet stored starch can be broken down to supply metabolizable carbohydrate. The chloroplast releases breakdown products that enter cytosolic metabolism, and sucrose synthesis and export may continue. An observed reduction in export after darkening therefore does not imply that phloem loading stops immediately; the response depends on the rate of reserve mobilization and on the metabolic state of the leaf before darkness.

A useful mass balance considers a source tissue containing a pool of accessible sucrose, expressed in micromoles. The rate of change of this pool is the rate of sucrose formation minus the sum of its export, local consumption and transfer to other storage pools, plus any input from reserves. At steady state, the pool size is constant even though its constituent molecules are continually exchanged. The rate of sucrose production can be large while the pool remains small if export and utilization are equally large. Conversely, a large pool may occur when export slows and supply temporarily exceeds removal. This is an important general distinction between a pool, measured in units of amount or concentration, and a flux, measured in amount per unit time.

Consider two illustrative leaves with equal tissue volume and a sucrose pool of 40 micromoles at the start of an observation. Leaf A produces 15 micromoles of sucrose per hour, consumes 5 locally and exports 10; the pool remains at 40. Leaf B produces the same amount and consumes 5 but exports only 3, leading to a net increase of 7 micromoles per hour in the absence of other storage responses. After two hours the model predicts a pool of 54 micromoles in leaf B, even though its export performance is poorer. A student who ranks loading performance according to measured sucrose content alone would incorrectly conclude that leaf B has the superior export system. If the additional carbon is diverted into starch, the measured sucrose difference may be reduced, but the deficit in export may persist.

The same reasoning applies to genetic comparisons. A mutant that accumulates sugar and starch in mature leaves while showing reduced delivery of newly fixed carbon to roots is compatible with a block in loading. However, a mutation that damages the conducting phloem or weakens sink uptake could produce a similar pattern. Distinguishing these mechanisms requires measurements of local vein accumulation, export along the conducting pathway and utilization in the sink. It is also important to compare developmentally equivalent leaves. A partially expanded leaf that is still a net importer cannot be expected to show the same behavior as a fully expanded source, even if both possess photosynthetically active chloroplasts.

Transport capacity changes as a leaf develops. During its transition from sink to source, metabolic activity, vascular differentiation and transporter deployment change in relation to the capacity to export. Different regions of a growing leaf need not cross the transition at exactly the same time. A measurement that averages the entire leaf may obscure regional differences in loading. This matters when studying promoter activity: a sugar transporter reporter expressed strongly in a mature zone and weakly in an immature zone can reveal a spatial association with source function, but the reporter itself does not measure the direction of sugar movement. The combined evidence of local carbon fixation, tracer export and transporter expression is more convincing.

The molecular structure of sucrose also has implications for its movement. It is electrically neutral at physiological pH, so the electrical potential across a membrane does not supply a direct electrophoretic force on sucrose. Its hydration and size limit simple passage through the lipid bilayer. Thus an inside-negative membrane cannot accumulate sucrose merely by attracting it electrically. Coupling to proton movement solves this problem by linking the movement of a neutral molecule to the favorable electrochemical gradient of a positively charged ion. In contrast, a cytoplasmic pathway through plasmodesmata bypasses the need for a sucrose membrane transporter at the relevant interface. The same substrate can therefore move by distinct mechanisms according to cellular architecture.

Other carbohydrate forms illustrate the distinction between production and transport identity. A photosynthetic cell produces many phosphorylated metabolites and may accumulate starch, but phosphate esters and large insoluble starch particles are not the principal bulk photoassimilates carried through the sieve tube. The export form must be compatible with long-distance movement in an aqueous sap and with recovery by receiving organs. Sucrose, raffinose-family oligosaccharides and certain sugar alcohols meet these requirements in different species. The presence of a carbohydrate within a leaf is not evidence that it is a major transport form; analysis of sap composition is needed to determine the molecules actually leaving the source.

4. Cellular geography of a minor vein

The first requirement for interpreting a loading mechanism is to locate the relevant cell boundaries. Mesophyll cells synthesize carbohydrate and are separated from one another by walls and plasma membranes. Bundle sheath cells surround vascular tissues in many leaves. Phloem parenchyma lies within the vascular region and can act as an intermediary in radial transfer. Sieve elements form the longitudinal conduits of the angiosperm phloem and are joined end to end by sieve plates. A mature conducting sieve element lacks a functional nucleus, and its maintenance depends substantially on a closely associated nucleated companion cell. Specialized plasmodesmatal connections allow communication between the two.

The sieve element and its companion cell are best regarded as a functional pair, often called the SE–CC complex. This expression does not imply that their membranes have identical transporter distributions. A transport protein may be expressed predominantly in the companion cell, the sieve element or another cell in a vein. To understand its role, identify the membrane on which it resides and the direction of the physiological gradient. A protein located in phloem parenchyma that releases sucrose outside its plasma membrane performs a different task from a protein in the companion cell that absorbs sucrose from that extracellular space.

The apoplast includes interconnected cell walls and extracellular spaces outside plasma membranes. The symplast consists of living cytoplasmic domains connected through plasmodesmata. A solute following a symplastic route can move from cytoplasm to cytoplasm without repeatedly crossing lipid bilayers. When the route contains an apoplastic interval, the solute must cross a membrane to enter that interval and another membrane to re-enter the symplast. These crossings are significant because sucrose, a polar molecule, does not freely permeate an intact hydrophobic lipid bilayer at the rates required for physiological loading.

Companion cell anatomy varies. Ordinary companion cells are associated with many apoplastic loading systems; some transfer cells develop wall ingrowths that increase the surface area available for membrane transport. Intermediary cells, characteristic of many polymer-trapping species, have dense cytoplasm and abundant specialized plasmodesmata at the interface with bundle sheath cells. A large membrane area can accommodate more transport proteins, and a high plasmodesmatal connectivity can support substantial intercellular flux. These are plausible functional relationships, not a rule that microscopic appearance alone identifies every plant's loading strategy.

It is particularly important to distinguish a connection linking a companion cell to its own sieve element from a connection linking the complex to neighboring mesophyll-associated cells. All functional complexes need internal communication, including those that load through the apoplast. The observation of plasmodesmata between a sieve element and its companion cell therefore does not demonstrate that the source-to-complex entry is symplastic. It is the connectivity and transport properties of the external interface that discriminate between the competing routes.

5. Architecture and physical constraints at the loading interface

Plant cells are mechanically attached to one another through their cell walls. Yet their internal contents are not necessarily isolated: plasmodesmata provide continuous or regulated connections across walls. Each plasmodesma is lined by plasma membrane and commonly contains a central strand related to the endoplasmic reticulum, termed the desmotubule. The cytoplasmic sleeve is a major route for intercellular passage of small soluble molecules. Because the effective dimensions of this sleeve are much smaller than the thickness of an ordinary cell, a small change in the geometry or regulation of the connection can alter transfer substantially. This is different from diffusion through the broad aqueous interior of a cell.

The number of plasmodesmata per unit wall area provides an anatomical estimate of connectivity. However, a count does not establish the conductance of an interface. Individual channels may vary in shape, openness and length, and solute permeability can be affected by their state. Callose, a β-1,3-glucan that can accumulate around plasmodesmatal neck regions, is associated with regulation of intercellular permeability. An increase in callose can restrict movement through particular connections. A treatment affecting callose can also influence other structures, including sieve plates, so a reduction in export after such treatment must not be attributed automatically to the loading interface alone.

The two interfaces surrounding an intermediary cell create a useful conceptual example. On one side, the intermediary cell receives sucrose from the bundle sheath; on the other, it must transfer its products to the sieve element. If both interfaces were equally restrictive to raffinose and stachyose, the synthesis of these sugars would lead to accumulation within the intermediary cell without effective delivery to the transport stream. If both interfaces freely passed these larger sugars, back-leakage toward the bundle sheath could weaken the trap. A functioning polymer-trapping pathway requires the spatial pattern of effective permeability to be compatible with the direction of net assimilate transfer. The size of a molecule alone is not enough to explain directional distribution without considering which interface it encounters.

In apoplastic loaders, transfer-cell wall ingrowths offer a different solution to the local throughput problem. Wall ingrowths increase the plasma membrane surface area available within a limited tissue volume. If the transporter density per unit membrane area remains constant, more total membrane area can increase the number of transport sites. The effect on actual flux still depends on substrate availability and the ability to maintain ion gradients. A large membrane surface exposed to a sugar-poor apoplast will not guarantee substantial loading. Structural adaptation is meaningful in the context of the molecular machinery and local gradients.

The geometry of the minor vein network also determines how far carbohydrate travels before reaching a loading interface. A photosynthetic cell located near a minor vein has fewer intervening cellular boundaries than a cell farther away. In a simple one-dimensional series of diffusive interfaces, each interface contributes resistance, and the same steady-state flux must pass through each one if there is no production or consumption between them. A restrictive interface can dominate the combined resistance. Multiple parallel connections, in contrast, can increase total conductance. Real source leaves are branched three-dimensional networks with local metabolism, so the simple series model is most useful for identifying why a single narrow bottleneck can be influential.

An analogy with electrical circuits clarifies the distinction between a serial and parallel arrangement, provided its limits are recognized. If two membrane steps occur sequentially, strengthening the second does little to overcome a complete block in the first because both are needed to complete the route. If two loading paths are available in parallel, loss of one may leave some transport through the other. A double perturbation of parallel pathways can therefore produce a stronger phenotype than either alone. The analogy does not imply that biological transporters are linear resistors: their activity can saturate, they are regulated, and the substrates and gradients can change when one route is impaired.

Electron microscopy can reveal fine anatomical features that ordinary light microscopy cannot resolve. For loading studies, it is important that counts are compared at homologous interfaces and adjusted for the size of the wall being examined. Comparing plasmodesmata in a sieve element–companion cell wall with those in a bundle sheath–companion cell wall without recognizing their different functions can mislead. A small number of highly permeable channels may also behave differently from a larger number of narrow channels. An anatomical hypothesis should therefore be tested against physiological evidence from tracer transfer and carbohydrate export.

For a simplified exercise, imagine two species with identical concentration differences at a given interface. Species A has twice the effective channel number of species B, and each channel has the same permeability in both species. The predicted diffusive conductance of the interface in A is twice that in B. If the channels in A have only half the permeability of those in B, the predicted conductances instead become equal. These conditional predictions help interpret a question that presents both anatomical and physiological observations. They also show why a student cannot deduce a quantitative transport rate merely by counting visible connections.

6. Apoplastic loading: two membrane crossings

In the well-studied apoplastic pathway of Arabidopsis thaliana, sucrose can reach the phloem parenchyma through symplastic connections from surrounding tissues. It then exits those cells into the extracellular compartment and is recovered into the sieve element–companion cell complex. This arrangement explains why the name apoplastic loading does not mean that the complete journey from photosynthetic mesophyll to sieve tube occurs outside cells. It refers to a decisive extracellular step on the final approach to the conducting complex.

SWEET11 and SWEET12 are sucrose transport facilitators that contribute to efflux from Arabidopsis phloem parenchyma. A facilitator changes the permeability of a membrane to a solute. It provides a route for movement down an appropriate chemical potential difference rather than directly coupling movement to ATP hydrolysis. For a neutral sugar in the simplified case of dilute solutions, net flux through a facilitator is toward lower concentration. If the gradient reverses, the net flux may reverse too. The observed physiological role of SWEET11 and SWEET12 is thus not equivalent to a chemically irreversible export pump.

Why does sucrose not simply equilibrate between the phloem parenchyma and the apoplast until net export ceases? The second transport step continuously removes sucrose from the extracellular compartment. The plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase uses energy released by ATP hydrolysis to move protons from the cytoplasm to the apoplast, generating an outward proton concentration gradient and an inside-negative electrical potential. Protons therefore have a favorable electrochemical gradient for re-entry into the cytoplasm. A sucrose–proton symporter such as Arabidopsis SUC2 couples proton entry to sucrose uptake. This allows the transporter to accumulate sugar even when its cytosolic concentration exceeds that of the surrounding extracellular solution.

The distinction between primary and secondary active transport follows directly from the energy coupling. The ATPase is a primary active transporter because ATP hydrolysis directly powers proton export. SUC2 is a secondary active transporter: it exploits the gradient created by the ATPase. SWEET-mediated efflux is facilitated diffusion. If a question describes the complete sequence as active phloem loading, it is referring to the capacity of the pathway to accumulate sugar by an energy-dependent mechanism. It does not mean that each membrane crossing is itself active.

The two steps are functionally coupled even though the transporters are not necessarily physically associated. Increasing symporter activity can lower the extracellular sucrose concentration and favor further facilitated efflux, provided there is adequate sucrose in the parenchyma. Reducing symporter activity may initially increase extracellular sucrose and weaken the gradient for further efflux. At a later time, altered sugar metabolism and signaling may produce additional changes. Predictions about immediate transporter function should therefore be separated from longer-term phenotypes involving compensatory responses.

High sucrose concentration within the conducting complex is compatible with this arrangement because the external interface has relatively restricted symplastic permeability. If the loading cells were freely connected to an extensive lower-concentration cytoplasm, an accumulated sugar pool could leak back down its gradient. Limited back-diffusion helps maintain accumulation. This does not require total absence of plasmodesmata at every relevant interface. Effective permeability, transporter capacities and the local geometry of the route together determine the flux.

7. Chemiosmotic energetics and quantitative predictions

The energy cost of moving a charged solute across a membrane has both a chemical and an electrical contribution. For transfer of one mole of protons from the apoplast to the cytoplasm, a simplified electrochemical free-energy change is ΔG(H⁺) = RT ln(a_in/a_out) + F(ψ_in − ψ_out). R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, a is the effective activity of protons, F the Faraday constant and ψ the electrical potential. The charge number for the proton is positive one. A negative result indicates that inward movement is thermodynamically favorable. Because pH is the negative base-ten logarithm of proton activity, the chemical term may also be written as −2.303RT(pH_in − pH_out).

Consider an illustrative cell whose apoplastic pH is 5.5, cytosolic pH is 7.0 and inside-negative membrane potential is −120 mV at 298 K. The proton activity ratio inside to outside is 10^(−1.5), or about 0.0316. The chemical contribution is approximately −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical contribution is 96,485 C mol⁻¹ multiplied by −0.120 V, or −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹. The total free-energy change is about −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹ for inward proton movement. The negative signs of the two terms reflect independent favorable gradients, not two separate ATP molecules consumed for each incoming proton.

For neutral sucrose, the idealized free-energy change of inward transfer is RT ln(C_in/C_out). Suppose one proton is coupled to one sucrose molecule, and the transporter operates reversibly without losses. The net free-energy change of the coupled process is the sum of the proton term and the sucrose term. At equilibrium, this sum is zero. The ideal limiting concentration ratio is therefore exp(20,140/(8.314 × 298)), approximately 3.4 × 10³. This is a thermodynamic upper bound under the chosen assumptions, not a prediction that the companion cell actually accumulates sucrose to thousands of times its extracellular concentration. Osmotic constraints, leakage, nonideal solute behavior, imperfect coupling and the metabolic use of sucrose reduce or otherwise constrain attainable concentrations.

Now imagine that the apoplastic pH rises from 5.5 to 6.5 but membrane potential and cytosolic pH remain unchanged. The chemical contribution becomes about −2.85 kJ mol⁻¹, a loss of approximately 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ of driving force. In the reversible one-to-one model, the limiting accumulation ratio decreases by a factor of ten, because the external proton activity has decreased tenfold. A change in membrane potential also changes the accumulation limit. Depolarization from −120 to −60 mV makes inward proton movement less favorable by about 5.79 kJ mol⁻¹. Both effects demonstrate why the electrical and pH components of the gradient must be included.

Thermodynamics tells us whether the coupled transfer is possible and defines an equilibrium limit; it does not specify how rapidly a transporter operates. A transporter with a very favorable free-energy change can still be slow if there are few transporters, if the substrate concentration is low or if the turnover rate is small. A saturable rate relationship may be represented schematically by v = Vmax C/(Km + C), with C the external substrate concentration. For an illustrative transporter with Vmax of 12 arbitrary flux units and Km of 2 mM, an external sucrose concentration of 2 mM gives a rate of 6 units; 6 mM gives 9 units. This equation assumes other relevant variables are fixed. A proton-coupled carrier in a living membrane is not fully described by external sucrose concentration alone.

An exam experiment may compare uptake at several pH values while maintaining the same external sucrose concentration. If uptake decreases when the outside becomes less acidic, the result is consistent with proton coupling, but it could also reflect pH-dependent changes in transporter conformation or membrane integrity. Measuring membrane potential, proton flux, substrate uptake and cell viability together provides a stronger mechanistic test. An inhibitor that dissipates both proton gradients can reveal their importance, but its effects on the many other proton-dependent processes in the cell must be considered.

8. From membrane pump to sucrose accumulation: interpreting perturbations

The plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase is central to many plant transport processes because it maintains an electrochemical gradient of protons. The pump uses ATP hydrolysis to drive outward proton movement against the prevailing gradient. In a living cell, this process is opposed by pathways that permit inward movement of protons or other positive charges. The electrical potential and pH difference represent the balance of pump activity, ion conductances, cotransport and buffering. Pump activity can change without a proportional change in either measured pH or membrane potential if the opposing fluxes change at the same time.

A sucrose–proton cotransporter binds substrates and changes conformation in a coordinated manner. In the physiological uptake direction, the transporter uses the tendency of protons to re-enter the cell to favor the movement of sucrose into the same compartment. For a neutral sugar and one positively charged proton moved together, the coupled transfer is electrogenic: one net positive charge is delivered inward in the ideal one-to-one cycle. The transfer of a sugar molecule by itself would not move charge. Electrogenic cotransport can therefore be examined by measuring changes in membrane current in a suitable expression system when sucrose is supplied under defined ionic conditions.

Consider a membrane preparation or an experimentally controlled cell that expresses a sucrose symporter. If sucrose addition produces an inward current in the presence of an inward proton gradient, this is consistent with electrogenic substrate-coupled transport. A corresponding signal should be reduced or absent in a suitable negative control lacking the transporter. Testing other sugars helps establish substrate specificity. Changing the external pH while controlling membrane potential can help separate the chemical contribution from the electrical contribution. None of these measurements alone establishes the protein's location in the intact leaf. Localization and transport assays address different parts of the functional hypothesis.

SWEET transporters operate differently. For facilitated transport of electrically neutral sucrose, net movement need not generate a directly coupled proton current. Uptake may be observed in an experimental system when external sucrose exceeds internal sucrose, even though the transporter contributes to efflux in its normal tissue context. This is not contradictory. Transport direction follows the gradient in the physiological compartment in which the protein operates. An experiment using a heterologous cell that contains a low internal sugar concentration can reveal transport into the cell without showing the normal direction of movement across phloem parenchyma in a source leaf.

The physiological function of a transporter is often revealed by the effect of changing its activity in a defined cell type. Imagine a plant with a fully functional SWEET-mediated export step but a severe reduction in companion cell sucrose–proton uptake. Initially, sucrose released into the apoplast may be removed more slowly. The extracellular concentration may rise, decreasing the chemical driving force for SWEET-mediated efflux. With prolonged impairment, source leaf sugar pools, cellular metabolism and development may change. It is not necessary for the SWEET protein itself to be defective for the overall serial pathway to slow.

Conversely, consider a plant with an efficient SUC transporter that receives little extracellular sucrose because the upstream export step is impaired. The symporter cannot increase uptake substantially in the absence of substrate. A direct test of the receiving transporter can supply sucrose to the relevant extracellular region under carefully controlled conditions. Recovery of uptake in such a test would be compatible with an intact receiving step. Failure to recover could indicate additional impairment, insufficient access to the transporter or secondary changes in cell viability. Such a rescue experiment separates substrate limitation from intrinsic transport capacity more clearly than simply measuring expression of both genes.

Protonophores dissipate proton electrochemical gradients by increasing effective proton permeability across membranes. If a source leaf relies on proton-coupled sucrose uptake, reducing the available gradient is expected to compromise the energetically uphill step. But protonophores can affect mitochondrial and chloroplast function as well as plasma membrane gradients, depending on the treatment. Reduced assimilate export after exposure may therefore partly reflect reduced ATP production or carbon fixation. A short-term uptake experiment with controlled sugar availability and monitored membrane potential is more informative than a long-term growth comparison alone. Interpretation should distinguish a predicted direct effect from broader secondary physiological effects.

When ATP is limited, H⁺-ATPase activity may decrease, weakening the proton motive force. Yet ATP shortage is not a selective perturbation of the loading pump: it may also disrupt sucrose synthesis, protein maintenance, ion homeostasis and the movement of carbohydrates through other tissues. An examiner can test this distinction by presenting three interventions: direct disruption of SUC2, dissipation of the membrane proton gradient and inhibition of general ATP production. All can reduce apoplastic loading, but their specificity differs. The most direct evidence for the contribution of the symporter comes from interventions that change its activity while keeping other components as similar as possible.

A transporter can become saturated with substrate even when the thermodynamic gradient remains favorable. In a simplified uptake curve, increasing external sucrose initially produces a strong increase in rate, whereas at high concentrations the curve approaches a plateau. If the total number of functional transporters is doubled and each transporter operates identically, the maximal rate may approximately double. Changing substrate affinity instead alters the concentration at which half the maximum is reached. A question that presents altered rate curves therefore asks whether the perturbation is consistent with changes in transporter abundance, affinity or an unmeasured variable such as the proton gradient. It is important to specify the assumptions under which the simple interpretation holds.

The concentration gradient of sucrose and the proton motive force must be treated separately. At an external sucrose concentration of 1 mM and internal concentration of 100 mM, the uphill sugar term is RT ln(100), approximately 11.41 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. A proton gradient yielding −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹ in the same direction would make the combined one-to-one transfer favorable by about −8.73 kJ mol⁻¹. If the proton gradient were weakened to −8 kJ mol⁻¹, the combined change would instead be positive, approximately +3.41 kJ mol⁻¹. The same sugar gradient can therefore be supported under one membrane condition but not under another. The calculation says nothing about whether the transporter is expressed; favorable energetics are necessary for spontaneous net coupled transfer, not sufficient to establish a measurable flux.

9. Passive symplastic loading

In passive symplastic loading, sucrose enters the collecting phloem through plasmodesmata, without a required apoplastic uptake step or conversion into larger transport sugars. A source mesophyll can maintain a high cytosolic concentration of sucrose through photosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism. Continuous removal of sugar through the conducting phloem and consumption at sinks can help maintain a net inward gradient toward the sieve element–companion cell complex. The word passive refers to the local transfer mechanism: no dedicated metabolic or membrane energy-coupling event is required to move sucrose uphill at the loading interface.

Passive loading still depends on living metabolism. Carbon fixation is required to replenish the sucrose exported by a photosynthetic source, and cells need ATP for homeostasis. Sink metabolism helps maintain net transport. The absence of a proton-coupled loading step is not equivalent to absence of ATP use anywhere in the leaf or plant. The most useful classification asks which process drives sucrose across the decisive local interface.

In a simplified diffusion model, net molar transfer per unit time is J = PA(C_donor − C_receiver), where P denotes effective permeability, A the relevant interfacial area and the concentrations are expressed in consistent units. If P has units of metres per second, A square metres and concentration moles per cubic metre, J is moles per second. The equation predicts no net diffusive transfer at equal concentrations, although individual molecules may still exchange in both directions. Increasing effective permeability increases the magnitude of flux at a fixed concentration difference. Real plasmodesmata have complex geometry, and pressure-driven solvent motion may contribute, so the expression should be treated as a simplified concentration-driven component.

Suppose the donor cytosol contains 80 mM sucrose and the receiving complex contains 50 mM. Their difference is 30 mM. If the receiving concentration increases to 70 mM, without any other change, the difference falls to 10 mM and the simplified diffusive flux falls to one third of its initial value. If the receiving concentration exceeds the donor concentration, the predicted diffusive component reverses. A high total sugar concentration measured in a whole-leaf extract cannot be substituted automatically for the donor cytosolic concentration because the leaf contains several compartments and cell types.

Vacuoles often occupy a large fraction of the mesophyll cell volume. Sugar stored within the vacuole is not at the plasmodesmatal entry unless it first crosses the tonoplast into the cytosol. The concentration relevant to diffusion is therefore local cytosolic activity, not a bulk measurement divided by total leaf volume. A leaf with moderate bulk sugar content may still sustain a high cytosolic concentration. This compartment distinction is one reason why studies of loading mechanisms combine anatomy, compartment-sensitive estimates and transport observations.

10. Polymer trapping: metabolic conversion and selective connectivity

Polymer trapping solves the accumulation problem by changing the chemical identity of incoming sucrose. Sucrose enters an intermediary companion cell through specialized plasmodesmata from neighboring bundle sheath cells. Inside the intermediary cell, enzymes convert part of the imported sucrose into larger raffinose-family oligosaccharides. These products have restricted movement back toward the bundle sheath through the relevant plasmodesmatal interfaces, yet they can enter the adjacent sieve elements. The model therefore couples a diffusional entry step to an energy-dependent metabolic transformation and asymmetric effective permeability.

Galactinol synthase catalyzes the production of galactinol from UDP-galactose and myo-inositol, releasing UDP. Galactinol then acts as a galactosyl donor. Raffinose synthase transfers a galactosyl group from galactinol to sucrose, forming raffinose and releasing myo-inositol. Stachyose synthase adds a further galactosyl group from galactinol to raffinose, producing stachyose and releasing myo-inositol. The pathway consumes activated sugar donors; although raffinose synthesis does not directly use a plasma membrane proton gradient, the generation and turnover of the substrates depend on metabolism. Polymer trapping is accordingly classified as active symplastic loading.

Raffinose contains three monosaccharide residues and stachyose four. Their larger molecular sizes can reduce their permeability through the bundle sheath–intermediary cell interface relative to sucrose. The term polymer is conventional in the name polymer trapping; the principal products are soluble oligosaccharides rather than long insoluble polymers like cellulose. They remain available for osmotically driven water relations and for export. A trap based on conversion of carbohydrate into insoluble starch would not operate in the same way because starch granules cannot enter the conducting sap as freely dissolved sugars.

The concentration of sucrose alone in an intermediary cell can remain low enough to favor further sucrose entry while the total concentration of transport carbohydrates becomes high. In a simplified example, assume donor cytosolic sucrose is 70 mM while the intermediary cell contains 40 mM sucrose and 90 mM combined raffinose and stachyose. The sucrose gradient favors movement into the intermediary cell, while the total concentration of dissolved transport sugars is greater there. This is not a violation of diffusion because the gradient of one chemical species does not determine the diffusion of a different species. The increased sugar pool can contribute to a more negative osmotic potential, favoring water entry when hydrostatic pressure permits.

The often-used description of the plasmodesmata as molecular sieves is helpful but incomplete. They are regulated cellular structures, not rigid filters with a universally fixed pore diameter. Molecular shape, hydration, effective channel dimensions, pressure and other structural factors can influence transport. Polymer trapping requires effective retention of the products relative to sucrose, not absolute proof that every larger molecule is permanently unable to move back. Leakage may occur, and species can use more than one loading route. The core prediction is that metabolic conversion plus selective intercellular transfer contributes to net sugar accumulation and export.

The biochemical carbon accounting must also be kept distinct from osmotic accounting. One mole of raffinose contains 18 moles of carbon atoms, compared with 12 in one mole of sucrose. One mole of stachyose contains 24 moles of carbon atoms. Each dissolved molecule nonetheless counts approximately as one osmotically active particle in an ideal dilute solution. Adding galactose increases carbon carried per molecule but does not create extra solute particles simply by combining two solutes. The complete metabolic and transport system determines the resulting osmotic concentration. The polymer trap explanation should not be reduced to the false idea that larger molecules inherently exert greater osmotic pressure at equal molarity.

11. Raffinose-family metabolism and the logic of a biochemical trap

The activated donor UDP-galactose supplies the galactosyl group that is eventually added to sucrose during raffinose-family oligosaccharide synthesis. Galactinol synthase transfers galactose from UDP-galactose to myo-inositol, forming a small soluble galactoside known as galactinol. Raffinose synthase then transfers the galactosyl group to sucrose. The released myo-inositol can be reused in galactinol formation. Stachyose synthase extends raffinose by another galactosyl transfer from galactinol. This is a transfer pathway rather than simple attachment of free galactose to sucrose without an activated intermediate.

The steps can be combined to derive a useful net relationship. For one raffinose molecule formed, galactinol synthase consumes UDP-galactose and myo-inositol, while raffinose synthase consumes the galactinol and sucrose and releases myo-inositol. Cancelling galactinol and myo-inositol from the two equations gives the simplified overall conversion of sucrose plus UDP-galactose into raffinose plus UDP. For stachyose production from sucrose, two galactosyl transfers are needed. The inositol carrier can cycle, but the activated galactose donor must be replenished. This accounting explains why the process depends on metabolism despite the absence of a direct ATP-consuming transmembrane sucrose uptake event.

A useful comparison is with the metabolic trapping of glucose inside a cell following phosphorylation, although the mechanisms are not identical. In both cases, conversion reduces the pool of the incoming chemical species and produces a different compound with distinct permeability properties. In the polymer trap, the relevant barrier is a plasmodesmatal interface and the products remain transportable through another connection. In glucose phosphorylation, the relevant membrane and transporter properties differ. The analogy is limited to the general principle that reaction and selective permeability can be coupled to maintain net entry.

A mass balance for sucrose in an intermediary cell includes sucrose arriving from neighboring cells, sucrose consumed by raffinose synthesis, sucrose exported directly into the sieve element and other metabolic consumption. For the raffinose pool, production through raffinose synthase is balanced by conversion to stachyose, transfer into the sieve element, possible back-leakage and other consumption. Even if total soluble carbohydrate is increasing, the sucrose pool may remain stable or decrease. In a steady-state model, the sum of each pool's inputs equals the sum of its outputs. This does not require that raffinose concentration be greater than sucrose concentration in every species or at all times.

Suppose an intermediary cell receives 12 arbitrary units of sucrose molecules per unit time. Seven are converted to raffinose, three are exported directly as sucrose, and two are consumed locally. If five of the seven raffinose molecules leave toward the sieve element while two are converted into stachyose and subsequently exported, the net direct transport stream contains three sucrose, five raffinose and two stachyose molecular units per unit time. This simplified balance omits local recycling, leakage and the origin of the additional galactosyl groups. Carbon equivalents exported must include the carbon entering as activated galactose donors, not merely count the number of sucrose molecules that originally crossed the boundary. The example highlights why a flux reported as moles of molecules is not equivalent to a flux reported as moles of carbon.

A defect in raffinose synthase and a defect in galactinol synthase have different immediate predictions. If galactinol synthase is impaired, the supply of galactinol is expected to fall when there is no adequate alternative source. Both raffinose and stachyose production may decline. If raffinose synthase is specifically impaired while galactinol synthesis remains active, galactinol may accumulate and downstream products may decline, depending on competing uses. In a defect confined to stachyose synthesis, raffinose can still be produced and exported, so the change in transport composition may be more pronounced than the change in total export. These predictions depend on enzyme redundancy, regulation and the specific organization of the species under study.

Raffinose-family oligosaccharides can occur outside the phloem and serve functions other than long-distance transport. Consequently, detecting raffinose in a leaf extract does not establish that polymer trapping is the predominant loading mechanism. The evidence is stronger when the enzymes are associated with intermediary cells, the products are found in the transport stream and disruption of the pathway reduces loading. Similarly, detecting sucrose in the sap of a species that exports raffinose does not refute polymer trapping. Not every sucrose molecule entering an intermediary cell must be converted before reaching the sieve element. The composition of exported carbohydrates reflects the balance of pathways operating in the tissue.

The word trap can invite the mistaken picture of a gate that closes behind each sucrose molecule. In reality, the model is about relative permeability and sustained metabolic conversion. If a small amount of raffinose escapes into the donor compartment, the trap can still function provided the rate of production and forward transport outweighs back-leakage. If back-permeability becomes high enough, however, the retention advantage is weakened. This suggests a useful experimental distinction: reduced RFO accumulation after an enzyme perturbation can reflect reduced synthesis, whereas reduced accumulation after a change in plasmodesmatal permeability may reflect increased leakage. Measurements of production rates and the localization of products would help discriminate the alternatives.

12. Connecting loading with pressure-driven translocation

All three loading strategies must be interpreted in the context of water potential and bulk flow. In a simplified cell or sieve tube, water potential is the sum of solute potential and pressure potential when gravitational and matric terms are negligible. The ideal van't Hoff approximation for a nonionizing solute is Ψs = −CRT, using a consistent unit system. Solute accumulation reduces water potential at a given hydrostatic pressure, and water can enter from neighboring tissues including xylem if their water potential is higher. Entry of water into a constrained volume increases hydrostatic pressure until water potential differences are reduced or maintained by continuing flux.

For a hypothetical sap at 298 K containing 0.30 mol L⁻¹ sucrose and no other osmotically significant solutes, Ψs is approximately −0.743 MPa using R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. If local xylem water potential is −0.20 MPa and the sap comes into local water-potential equilibrium, the required sieve tube pressure potential is approximately +0.543 MPa, because −0.743 + 0.543 = −0.200 MPa. This is an illustrative equilibrium calculation, not a claim about actual sieve tube pressure in a specific plant. In a flowing system, water exchange, other solutes, resistance and changes in volume affect the pressure.

A pressure difference between the source and sink regions can drive bulk sap movement. The local transfer of sugar into the collecting phloem is a different transport event from longitudinal movement. Sucrose can move uphill in concentration across a companion cell membrane while the sap subsequently moves down a hydrostatic pressure gradient. The concentration of sucrose along the tube may change relatively little over some distances, and pressure-driven flow can transport multiple solutes together. It is incorrect to describe the entire source-to-sink route as diffusion or to imagine a series of ATP-driven pumps pushing each sucrose molecule through every sieve plate.

The net export rate of a source depends on both local loading and downstream hydraulic transport. If a sink becomes less active, or flow resistance increases, carbohydrate may accumulate in the source even when its loading transporters are functional. Conversely, enhanced loading may not improve export if the conducting system or unloading process becomes limiting. This is why whole-plant growth data alone rarely establish which molecular component of the transport system is defective. A sound investigation measures several steps and relates each observation to a specific mechanistic prediction.

13. Worked models of steady-state loading and limiting steps

A mechanistic description becomes useful when it can predict how a change in one component affects the behavior of the complete pathway. For apoplastic loading, consider a small extracellular region between the exporting parenchyma and receiving companion cell. Let E denote the amount of sucrose in that region. Its rate of change is the sum of input by facilitated export and any other input, minus uptake by the receiving cell and any loss through alternative routes. At a steady state with negligible alternative routes, the efflux and uptake rates must be equal. The extracellular pool does not have to be large for a substantial amount of sucrose to pass through it each minute. This is a characteristic of an intermediate pool in a serial pathway.

A simplified quantitative model illustrates the adjustment of that intermediate. Assume donor sucrose concentration is fixed at 80 arbitrary concentration units, and facilitated efflux is proportional to the difference between donor and extracellular concentrations. Write the efflux as k(80 − E), where E now denotes the extracellular concentration in the fixed-volume model. Assume uptake at low external concentrations can be approximated by mE, where m combines properties of the receiver and the constant gradient. Both constants have units that convert concentration to flux. If k and m are equal and there is no competing loss, equality of the two rates yields E = 40 concentration units. The predicted flux is 40k. The particular concentrations are illustrative and not a claim that a real symporter remains in a linear kinetic regime at such a value.

Now reduce receiving capacity m to one quarter of k while leaving the donor concentration and k constant. The equation becomes k(80 − E) = 0.25kE, so 80 = 1.25E and E = 64. The flux is 16k. The apoplastic concentration has increased, but the export flux has decreased. The initially impaired receiving step causes a new intermediate concentration that reduces the net gradient for the exporting step. This explains why a secondary change in SWEET-mediated flux may appear in a plant even if SWEET itself is biochemically normal. Measuring the concentration of an intermediate without its input and output rates cannot identify which step has changed.

Next consider the case where the exporting coefficient k is reduced to one quarter of m, with m held at its original value. The equality becomes 0.25m(80 − E) = mE. The predicted E is 16, and flux is 16m. In the symmetric numerical example, reducing the upstream or downstream coefficient to the same fractional extent yields the same final transport rate but opposite changes in the intermediate pool. This is a powerful diagnostic pattern in an ideal serial pathway. In real leaves, feedback, additional transport routes and compartmental exchange may obscure it. The model is useful precisely because it states the assumptions that must be checked before applying the diagnostic rule.

Passive symplastic loading can be represented with a simpler series of resistances. Suppose sucrose must traverse two successive intercellular interfaces, with effective conductances G1 and G2. If there is no synthesis or consumption in the intermediate cell, steady-state flux J is G1(Csource − Cmiddle) and also G2(Cmiddle − Cphloem). Dividing each concentration drop by its associated conductance yields J/G1 and J/G2. Adding the drops gives Csource − Cphloem = J(1/G1 + 1/G2). The overall conductance is therefore the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocal conductances. This mathematical result is an elementary steady-state model for diffusion across consecutive barriers, not a complete hydraulic model of plasmodesmata.

For an illustrative source-to-phloem concentration difference of 60 units, let the two conductances be 2 and 1 flux units per concentration unit. Total resistance is 0.5 + 1 = 1.5 concentration units per flux unit, so flux is 40. If the first conductance doubles to 4 while the second remains 1, total resistance falls to 1.25 and flux rises only to 48, not 80. If instead the smaller conductance doubles to 2, total resistance becomes 1 and flux rises to 60. The greatest improvement in a serial system often comes from relieving the restrictive step. The result explains why increasing channel abundance at an already highly permeable interface may have little influence on overall export.

A parallel pathway behaves differently. If an apoplastic route and a symplastic route both deliver sucrose to the same conducting region, the total flux is the sum of their contributions. Suppose an inhibitor selectively abolishes the apoplastic route, and the symplastic flux remains unchanged immediately after treatment. If the initial total is 100 flux units and the residual is 35, the apoplastic contribution before treatment was 65 units under these assumptions. This inference would fail if the inhibition were partial or if the symplastic route compensated. Measuring the immediate effect and then following the longer-term response can help separate pre-existing parallel transport from later adaptation.

A polymer-trapping model requires separate state variables for sucrose and RFOs. If incoming sucrose is converted at rate v and exported directly at rate e, then steady-state sucrose entry must balance v plus e plus other consumption. RFO production contributes to a different pool. The forward export of RFOs and their leakage toward the donor must balance their production in the absence of other reactions. Increasing the conversion rate can lower intracellular sucrose and favor increased inward diffusion, but its benefit depends on the ability to remove the RFO products. If the connection to the sieve element becomes limiting, product accumulation may alter metabolism and transport. A larger enzymatic capacity therefore does not guarantee a proportionately larger export flux.

The osmotic contribution of the transported molecules depends on their total molarity and on the other solutes present. Suppose a source phloem sample contains 0.12 M sucrose and 0.08 M raffinose, and another sample contains 0.20 M sucrose alone. Under the ideal dilute approximation at the same temperature, the contribution of these sugars to osmotic potential is equal because both samples contain 0.20 M dissolved sugar particles. However, their carbon contents differ. The mixture contains 0.12 × 12 + 0.08 × 18 = 2.88 mol of carbon atoms per litre, whereas the sucrose-only sample contains 2.40 mol of carbon atoms per litre. The mixture carries 20 percent more carbon at the same ideal sugar osmolarity. This is an example of a possible physical consequence of carrying larger soluble carbohydrates, not evidence that this consequence alone drove the evolution of polymer trapping.

Pressure-driven export can be expressed as volume flow Q = ΔP/Rh in a simplified linear hydraulic description, where ΔP is the pressure difference and Rh is hydraulic resistance. The carbon flux through a section with a known carbon-equivalent concentration is the product of Q and that concentration. If the sap becomes more concentrated but volume flow decreases, carbon delivery may increase, decrease or remain unchanged depending on the magnitudes. For example, an illustrative flow of 2 microlitres per minute carrying 1 micromole of carbon equivalents per microlitre delivers 2 micromoles per minute. If concentration doubles while flow falls to 0.8 microlitres per minute, delivery is 1.6 micromoles per minute. The higher concentration has not compensated for the lower flow. This connects the local accumulation mechanism with the need for adequate longitudinal transport.

The simple hydraulic relation also illustrates why a high source pressure is not by itself sufficient to infer high loading. A large pressure can develop upstream of an obstruction while downstream flow is small. A loss of sink demand may reduce the source-to-sink pressure difference despite maintained source pressure. Conversely, water deficits can change the external water potential from which the phloem obtains water. When comparing transport rates, it is therefore valuable to measure both the driving forces and the resistances. The parameters of these models are not independent in living plants, but separating them conceptually allows more precise hypotheses and avoids treating every change in export as a defect in the membrane-loading proteins.

14. Experimental evidence: distinguishing the three pathways

The physiological existence of a transporter is not established solely by the detection of its messenger RNA in a leaf. An informative study links the location of the protein, its capacity to transport the relevant solute and the consequences of changing its activity in the intact plant. In Arabidopsis, Gottwald and colleagues investigated SUC2 through insertion mutants. The developmental and assimilate-export defects of those mutants support a physiological role for the sucrose transporter in phloem loading. Source carbohydrate accumulation combined with impaired growth of receiving tissues is consistent with a bottleneck in distribution, but the interpretation is particularly strong when connected to the known transport activity and cell-specific expression of SUC2.

Chen and colleagues identified the contribution of SWEET11 and SWEET12 to sucrose efflux in Arabidopsis and examined plants defective in these transporters. Their work supports the model in which facilitated export into the apoplast supplies substrate to a proton-coupled uptake step. The two proteins have overlapping functions, which illustrates why a single-gene mutant with only a modest phenotype does not establish that the corresponding step is dispensable. A double mutant can reveal redundancy. Expression in the relevant cells and sugar transport assays are needed to connect genetic phenotypes to a direction of transfer.

Polymer trapping was tested in Verbascum phoeniceum by McCaskill and Turgeon, who suppressed the expression of galactinol synthase genes. Reduced production of raffinose-family sugars was associated with impaired carbohydrate export and growth. This intervention differs from inhibiting a sucrose symporter: it interferes with metabolic conversion in the intermediary cell. The experiment supplies evidence that the conversion has functional significance, rather than the large oligosaccharides being merely incidental components of sap. A complete causal interpretation nevertheless considers the possibility that changing carbohydrate metabolism affects other physiological processes. Measuring the expected biochemical intermediates and the actual export phenotype connects the molecular perturbation more closely to the loading model.

Rennie and Turgeon compared transport strategies across diverse species using combinations of anatomical, physiological and carbohydrate observations. Their analysis supports the existence of passive loading in a substantial range of plants, particularly woody species. This matters because a theory inferred from one model organism cannot be imposed on every leaf. Plasmodesmatal abundance, sugar concentrations in cellular compartments and tracer accumulation patterns can contribute to classification. Each method has limitations, and convergence of evidence is more useful than a single observation.

A radioactive carbon dioxide pulse can label photosynthetically fixed carbon in a source leaf. During the chase period, researchers may follow redistribution into veins and sinks. Reduced label export can indicate impaired assimilate movement, but the total amount of label fixed must also be compared between treatments. If a mutant has reduced photosynthesis, it may deliver less label even with intact loading. A useful normalization expresses exported label as a fraction of the initial fixed label under comparable conditions. Additional measurements of total sucrose and other labeled products can reveal changes in metabolism rather than simple transfer.

A membrane-impermeant inhibitor applied to extracellular spaces may inhibit accessible sucrose carriers while leaving some symplastic pathways relatively unaffected. Sensitivity to such a treatment can support a contribution from an apoplastic step. The inhibitor must reach its intended target, and tissue integrity, respiration and photosynthesis must be monitored. Lack of inhibition may mean that the plant uses a symplastic route, but it may also mean that the compound fails to reach the relevant cells or does not inhibit a particular transporter. A negative result is interpretable only in relation to a validated positive control.

Fluorescent tracers can test symplastic continuity. A tracer introduced into one cell may pass through plasmodesmata into neighboring cells depending on its molecular properties and the physiological condition of the channels. Tracer movement demonstrates permeability to that tracer under the conditions of the experiment. It does not directly measure sucrose flux. Conversely, absence of movement could result from a tracer being too large or having chemical interactions different from those of sucrose. Combining tracer measurements with sugar analysis, genetic perturbation and anatomical observations helps distinguish possible mechanisms.

15. Measuring carbohydrate transport and interpreting real observations

A study of source-leaf export often begins by deciding what quantity should be measured. Assimilate production, local loading flux, phloem sap composition, speed of translocation and delivery to a sink are related but distinct quantities. A tracer applied to the source can reveal the path taken by labeled molecules, but the rate at which radioactivity appears at a distant location depends on all intervening processes. A strong experiment chooses a measurement appropriate to the hypothesis and includes complementary measurements when a single result admits several interpretations.

A pulse of labeled carbon dioxide supplies tracer to photosynthetic tissue for a defined interval. Unlabeled carbon dioxide during the chase allows redistribution of labeled metabolites to be studied. A researcher can measure the label remaining within the fed leaf and the label reaching other organs. The total label fixed during the pulse should be quantified or carefully controlled. If treatment A fixes 1,000 arbitrary units and exports 400, while treatment B fixes only 500 and exports 250, absolute exported label is higher in A but the fraction exported is higher in B, 50 percent compared with 40 percent. This result does not prove that B has a higher absolute sugar loading capacity. It shows why the absolute flux, fractional redistribution and carbon fixation rate must not be confused.

Tracer disappearance from the source is not necessarily identical to accumulation in measured sinks. Labeled carbon can be respired, retained in unmeasured tissues or converted into metabolites that are not included in a selected assay. A mass balance that tracks recovered label provides a check on interpretation. If a researcher reports that only ten percent of the original label remained in the leaf but finds five percent in all measured sinks, a large fraction of the label is unaccounted for. The conclusion that 90 percent entered the phloem would require additional evidence. Depending on the design, measuring evolved labeled carbon dioxide, surveying other tissues or quantifying total recovered label may resolve the discrepancy.

Autoradiography of a leaf exposed to labeled substrate can reveal accumulation patterns in veins. A sharp vein signal may be consistent with concentrating transport into the vascular system, especially if compared with the behavior of surrounding tissue. However, the amount of label in a vein depends on the exposure time and the rate at which label exits the vein. A low vein signal could indicate low entry or very rapid onward movement; a strong signal could reflect high entry or restricted exit. The temporal sequence of labeling is therefore valuable. Early accumulation and later redistribution provide more information than a single endpoint.

Phloem sap sampling presents additional challenges because sieve tubes are living, pressurized conduits that can respond to injury. Exudate from a cut tissue can contain material from damaged neighboring cells or extracellular spaces, and the quantity collected may depend on wound responses. Aphids insert specialized stylets into phloem tissue; in some experimental approaches, severed stylets provide access to phloem sap with reduced contamination from surrounding cells. Even a relatively pure sap sample describes the composition of the conducting stream at a sampling point, not the exact route by which each sugar entered it. The concentration of sucrose or raffinose is informative when combined with anatomy and mechanistic perturbation.

When measuring transport sugar composition, chromatographic methods can separate carbohydrates by their properties and allow comparison with known standards. A peak assigned to raffinose should have an identity supported by an appropriate analytical method, not merely by an expected retention order. Calibration is needed if peak areas are to be converted into molar quantities. Comparing mass concentration rather than molar concentration can change apparent rankings of solutes with different molecular masses. The osmotic contribution is related to the number of dissolved particles, whereas carbon delivery depends on the number of carbon atoms carried by those particles.

For a hypothetical sap containing 100 mM sucrose, 60 mM raffinose and 40 mM stachyose, the total concentration of these neutral molecules is 200 mM. Carbon carried by sucrose is 1,200 mM carbon equivalents, by raffinose 1,080 mM and by stachyose 960 mM. Total carbon equivalents are 3,240 mM. Sucrose represents half the sugar molecules but only about 37 percent of the carbon carried by the three carbohydrates. This is why the sugar composition can be discussed in terms of both osmotic concentration and transported carbon, and the two percentages may differ substantially.

Genetic studies offer ways to connect the observations to specific proteins. A comparison of wild type and a mutant is especially informative when the mutation is well characterized and multiple independently derived lines produce compatible results. Complementation, in which the functional gene is reintroduced, can strengthen the causal interpretation. Tissue-specific complementation is particularly useful for a transport protein because expression in a physiologically inappropriate cell may fail to restore the correct pathway. A transporter can be chemically competent in an assay yet fail to rescue a plant if it is not deployed at the interface where transport is required.

An experiment that compares several species should account for differences in leaf development, temperature, light and physiological state. A high leaf sugar content may arise from differences in source metabolism rather than the loading strategy itself. Comparing plasmodesmatal anatomy across species is informative when the sampled leaves are mature sources and equivalent minor vein interfaces are studied. A conclusion about a predominant strategy is strongest when the predicted anatomy, transport sugar composition, local gradients and response to perturbation converge. Cases where those indicators conflict are opportunities to investigate mixed mechanisms rather than reasons to force the species into a simplistic category.

16. Comparative physiology and mixed loading strategies

Species differences provide a natural test of any proposed universal loading mechanism. Arabidopsis thaliana is a useful example of active apoplastic loading because molecular genetic evidence supports the roles of both a sucrose efflux facilitator and a proton-coupled uptake system. Its experimental advantages include the ability to connect mutant phenotypes, tissue-specific expression and transporter function. An Arabidopsis-centered description is therefore an excellent way to understand the two-step apoplastic pathway, but it is not a sufficient description of all angiosperms. The choice of examples in a question should be interpreted in relation to the particular physiological evidence given.

Verbascum phoeniceum is a contrasting example for polymer trapping. The experimentally tested role of galactinol synthase in this species makes it useful for reasoning about the coupling of metabolic conversion to symplastic entry. It would be inappropriate to explain its characteristic RFO-dependent export solely by the activity of a proton-coupled sucrose symporter. At the same time, the presence of ordinary companion cells or sucrose transport proteins in a polymer-trapping species does not contradict a contribution from intermediary cells. Additional routes may function in particular vein types or under particular conditions. A statement that polymer trapping is important for a species is not equivalent to claiming that every single transported molecule entered through that mechanism.

Cucumber offers a documented example in which both apoplastic and symplastic pathways have been investigated. The coexistence of transport mechanisms is physiologically plausible because the collecting phloem includes several cell types and interfaces. A plant does not have to choose one universal solution at every point in its leaf. If the relevant cells operate different routes, the relative contributions can depend on location, developmental stage or physiological condition. Experimental evidence for a sucrose transporter should therefore be assessed together with evidence for RFO synthesis and symplastic connectivity. The presence of multiple plausible components is a starting point for testing their relative importance.

For woody species in which passive loading is supported, a key consideration is the concentration of soluble carbohydrates available in photosynthetic cell cytosol. A large reserve of soluble sugars in the leaf can potentially sustain diffusion toward conducting tissue while helping maintain the osmotic conditions of the source cells. However, a high total leaf concentration could also reflect vacuolar storage, developmental differences or restricted utilization. Evidence of passive loading requires that sucrose have access to the relevant cytoplasmic pathway and that the driving forces be consistent with net transfer. High carbohydrate abundance is neither necessary nor sufficient as a stand-alone criterion for classifying a species.

Sugar alcohols complicate simplistic classifications based solely on sucrose. Some plants translocate polyols such as sorbitol or mannitol along with other photoassimilates. Different sugars and sugar alcohols may depend on different transport systems. A species that transports more than one solute could use a combination of routes for loading them. When interpreting osmotic pressure or carbon export, the contributions of all major solutes should be considered. The present chapter focuses on the sucrose and RFO pathways assigned to the curriculum, but this exception illustrates why the transport form must be verified experimentally.

An instructive hypothetical comparison uses four observations for each of three plants. Species A has low apparent plasmodesmatal connectivity at the outer interface of its conducting complex, strong expression of a proton-coupled sucrose transporter in receiving cells, a large decline in short-term uptake after collapse of the proton gradient and sucrose-rich sap. Species B has high connectivity at that interface, a favorable cytosolic sucrose gradient and little response to a validated extracellular carrier inhibition, while its sap is sucrose-rich. Species C has highly connected intermediary cells, abundant RFOs in sap and reduced export after suppression of RFO synthesis. The observations support apoplastic, passive symplastic and polymer-trapping contributions respectively. Each classification is strongest because several independent observations agree, not because any one feature is uniquely diagnostic.

A fourth hypothetical species has RFO-rich sap, intermediary cells and strong evidence that extracellular carrier inhibition reduces export by about 40 percent without compromising photosynthesis. It would be an error to force this result into either a pure apoplastic or a pure polymer-trapping model. One possibility is a parallel apoplastic contribution alongside polymer trapping. Another is a contribution from membrane retrieval after initial symplastic loading. A more precise experiment would separately perturb the RFO pathway and the membrane transporter, use tissue-specific information about the transporter and measure early accumulation in the collection phloem. If the combined perturbation is more severe than either individual perturbation under otherwise comparable conditions, the result supports a contribution from both components, although the relationship between them may still require investigation.

The value of such comparisons extends beyond memorizing species names. In examination questions, a plant may be presented without a familiar taxonomic identity. Students can still infer likely routes by matching experimental predictions to the data. They should look for the nature of the membrane crossing, the availability of a concentration gradient, the chemical form of the transported sugar and the consequence of disrupting a predicted energy-dependent step. When information is incomplete, a scientifically sound conclusion identifies which mechanism is supported rather than declaring one uniquely proven. This habit of conditional reasoning is particularly valuable for questions that ask what further experiment would distinguish two hypotheses.

17. Designing discriminating experiments

To identify a mechanism rather than a mere association, a researcher begins with competing explanations that make different predictions. Suppose a newly studied species has abundant sucrose in phloem sap, a sucrose transporter expressed in minor veins and some plasmodesmata connecting the collecting complex to surrounding cells. An apoplastic-loading model predicts that sucrose must cross an extracellular interval and that the relevant receiving transporter contributes substantially to entry. A passive symplastic model predicts that direct cytoplasmic transfer accounts for much of the loading, provided the sucrose gradient and effective connectivity are suitable. Both models can accommodate the observed transporter and plasmodesmata, because a transporter might retrieve leaked sugar and plasmodesmata might serve communication rather than bulk loading. The initial observations are therefore insufficient to discriminate between the models.

A targeted perturbation should be chosen to alter a predicted essential step while preserving other functions as much as possible. For the apoplastic model, one could compare the early export of newly fixed carbon in plants with reduced expression of the candidate receiving transporter and control plants. Matching leaf age and photosynthetic conditions reduces variation in substrate supply. A simultaneous measurement of local uptake or tracer enrichment in the minor veins helps identify the stage at which the deficit occurs. Restoring expression specifically in the cells thought to mediate uptake would provide evidence that the location of the transporter matters. A rescue expressed in a different tissue would be less decisive for the loading interface.

For the passive model, a perturbation of symplastic permeability may be informative if its effects are restricted to the candidate interface. A reduction in tracer movement across that interface followed by reduced source export would be compatible with a direct cytoplasmic route. However, a manipulation of plasmodesmata can also change movement of signaling molecules and alter development, and a broad change in callose deposition may influence longitudinal flow through sieve plates. Measurements soon after a localized perturbation are more informative than developmental abnormalities alone. A control that establishes the effect on the intended intercellular connection is needed before a negative or positive export result can be interpreted confidently.

For polymer trapping, the experiment can target one of the metabolic steps responsible for production of RFOs. A reduction in galactinol synthase expression should reduce the capacity of that pathway when the enzyme is limiting and other isoforms do not compensate. Predicted downstream outcomes include changes in the ratio of sucrose to RFOs in the transport stream and possible reductions in export. Measuring the sugar composition is important because it tests the biochemical connection between the perturbation and the physiological outcome. If the altered enzyme expression does not affect RFO concentrations or production, a negative export phenotype cannot be taken as strong evidence against polymer trapping: the treatment may have failed to disrupt its intended biochemical target.

The same perturbation can produce different outcomes depending on the operating conditions of the plant. An enzyme or transporter that is present in excess may tolerate partial reduction without an appreciable decrease in total flux. A severe reduction may reveal a phenotype only when source activity or sink demand is high. This is not evidence that the protein becomes a different transporter at high demand; it reflects the changing relation between capacity and required throughput. Testing a range of conditions can reveal whether a component sets the limit in one physiological state but not another. In genetic analysis, the absence of a phenotype is not identical to absence of function.

Consider an illustrative study with four treatment groups, each fixing 100 units of labeled carbon during the pulse. Wild type delivers 60 units to sinks after a defined chase. A mutant with reduced SWEET activity delivers 25, a mutant with reduced SUC activity delivers 22, and a double mutant delivers 20. These data are compatible with a serial route because disruption of either essential step causes a large decline and the combined disruption adds little further decline. They do not establish that the two proteins physically interact. A residual export pathway, incomplete gene disruption or differences in downstream metabolism can explain the remaining label reaching sinks. Measurements of extracellular sucrose and cell-specific transport activity would help test the serial-pathway interpretation.

In a second illustrative study, wild type delivers 60 units, a perturbation of RFO synthesis delivers 42, a perturbation of extracellular sucrose uptake delivers 40, and the combined perturbation delivers 20. The relatively large additional effect is compatible with contributions from two parallel pathways, provided that the manipulations are comparably effective and do not broadly reduce photosynthesis or phloem function. The experimental design should include biological replicates, report the variation between plants and avoid treating numerical differences as meaningful without assessing measurement uncertainty. The data are a model teaching example, not a published experimental outcome. Their value lies in comparing the predictions of pathway architectures.

Temporal patterns can strengthen inference about cause and effect. Suppose reduced sucrose transporter activity is followed within minutes by lower minor vein uptake, whereas starch accumulation and reduced photosynthetic capacity appear over several days. The temporal order supports an interpretation in which transport failure precedes the metabolic and developmental changes. If photosynthesis instead falls before export decreases and the transporter activity remains unchanged, substrate limitation becomes a more plausible initial explanation. Timing alone does not prove causality, but it helps exclude explanations that require the downstream effect to precede its proposed cause.

A final experimental challenge is the interpretation of whole-plant yield. Increased accumulation of a storage carbohydrate in a fruit or seed after manipulation of a source transporter could arise from improved loading, changes in sink metabolism, altered flowering time or differences in the number of receiving organs. A plant with fewer sinks might allocate more carbon to each remaining sink while producing less total yield. Consequently, measuring the mass of one organ is not a direct assay of phloem loading. Relevant data include total carbon fixation, number and strength of sinks, source-leaf export, phloem sugar composition and total accumulation across receiving tissues. The strongest conclusions connect a defined molecular change to its immediate physiological effect and then follow the consequences through the plant.

Students preparing for analytical examinations should practice identifying the measured variable, the proposed causal mechanism and the control needed to distinguish alternatives. An experimental observation can be consistent with several models, and the correct answer may be the most limited conclusion rather than the most elaborate explanation. Conversely, when genetic, anatomical and biochemical evidence agree, the combination can support a stronger mechanistic inference than each part in isolation. This is the reasoning framework that connects the molecular details of phloem loading with the wider problem of assimilate partitioning.

18. Regulation, diversity and integrated predictions

The three loading mechanisms are idealized strategies that help organize physiological observations. A single leaf may contain multiple companion cell types, and a species can operate parallel apoplastic and symplastic routes. An anatomical classification should therefore be treated as a prediction about the predominant pathway rather than proof of exclusivity. A transport inhibitor that reduces export by half might reveal one route among several, or it might simply inhibit a single route incompletely. Discriminating those possibilities requires information about target engagement, dose dependence and the composition of transported carbohydrates.

Environmental conditions affect the supply of substrate and the capacity for export. Reduced light decreases the immediate production of photoassimilate, but mobilized starch may partly sustain sucrose export during darkness. Water deficit changes leaf and xylem water potential, turgor, stomatal conductance and carbon fixation. If long-distance export declines during water deficit, the change may arise from reduced loading substrate, altered membrane energetics, increased sap viscosity, a smaller hydraulic driving force or altered sink demand. The observation alone does not identify a single cause. A source-to-sink analysis therefore asks whether the perturbation primarily affects production, loading, longitudinal movement or sink delivery.

An increase in source carbohydrate can itself alter gene expression and metabolism. Plants experiencing an export limitation may accumulate sucrose and starch and downregulate aspects of photosynthesis through feedback. In that case, lower carbon fixation observed at a late time can be a consequence rather than the initial cause of transport impairment. To establish temporal order, measure early changes after a defined perturbation and compare them with later physiological responses. Genetic rescue that restores export before other traits recover supplies stronger causal evidence than a static comparison of healthy and impaired plants.

In applied physiology, improving a loading transporter is sometimes proposed as a route to enhanced crop productivity. The underlying reasoning is sound only if transport through that step is a substantial limitation to the whole system. Increasing one protein may fail to improve yield when sink capacity, nitrogen availability or hydraulic transport is limiting. An intervention may also alter carbohydrate availability in the apoplast, which can affect other biological interactions. The appropriate examination-level conclusion is that carbon partitioning is a system property rather than a direct function of one gene's expression. 19. Misconceptions and examination traps

All loading is active membrane transport
Only the apoplastic pathway requires the characteristic proton-coupled uptake step. Passive symplastic loading can use a sucrose gradient, and polymer trapping is metabolically active but symplastic. Questions exploit the tendency to equate phloem accumulation with a universal membrane pump.
SWEET is a sucrose ATPase
SWEET is a facilitator, not a direct ATP-driven pump. In a two-step diagram, energy coupling occurs through the proton gradient at SUC/SUT uptake. Identify both transporters and the ATPase separately.
SUC2 uses ATP directly
SUC2 couples proton and sucrose transport. The primary ATP-consuming step is proton export by H⁺-ATPase. The fact that an ATP shortage can impair SUC2 function does not make SUC2 a primary active pump.
Apoplastic loading means the entire leaf pathway is extracellular
Upstream sucrose transfer can be symplastic. The defining extracellular interval is near the loading interface. Trace each cell boundary rather than classifying every part of the route by its final step.
Plasmodesmata between sieve element and companion cell prove symplastic loading
Their internal connection is required in apoplastic loaders too. The relevant outer entry interface links the complex with surrounding cells. Compare the correct interfaces in anatomical questions.
Passive loading does not require living metabolism
The local transfer does not require an uphill coupling reaction, but metabolism maintains substrate supply and physiological gradients. Do not confuse passive local movement with an ATP-free plant.
High bulk leaf sucrose automatically favors symplastic loading
A bulk concentration includes vacuolar and other inaccessible pools. Local cytosolic concentration and permeability determine the direct diffusive component. Questions may deliberately provide whole-leaf rather than cytosolic data.
Polymer trapping converts sucrose to starch
The characteristic products are soluble RFOs, such as raffinose and stachyose, that can enter sap. Insoluble starch would not supply freely dissolved transport sugar in the same way.
RFO synthesis uses no energy because sucrose enters by diffusion
Activated galactose donors require metabolism. Polymer trapping is an active strategy even though entry of sucrose can be diffusional. Distinguish pathway energetics from the physical mechanism of entry.
Raffinose has a greater ideal osmotic effect than sucrose at equal molarity
Each molecule contributes approximately one particle. Larger carbohydrates can carry more carbon per particle, not necessarily exert larger ideal osmotic pressure at equal molarity. Calculate molarity and carbon equivalents separately.
The trap must block all RFO movement everywhere
Selective relative return permeability is required, while forward transfer to sieve elements must remain possible. The same molecule can encounter different effective interfaces. Absolute blockage of every connection would hinder export.
A transporter expressed in a minor vein proves its loading role
Expression does not establish necessity or distinguish loading from retrieval. Seek transport function, localization and functional perturbation. A question may supply only a reporter image and ask what conclusion is warranted.
Inhibitor-insensitive export proves a passive route
The inhibitor may be ineffective, fail to reach its target or leave polymer trapping intact. Verify target engagement and test the RFO pathway before drawing the conclusion.
A high sugar pool necessarily means high loading flux
Pool size may increase when export slows. Use a mass balance and direct movement measurements. The classic trap compares static source carbohydrate with delivery to sinks.
High source turgor alone proves high longitudinal transport
The pressure difference and hydraulic resistance determine flow. Absolute source pressure may remain high despite an obstruction or weak sink. Distinguish loading from downstream conductivity.
All species must use one exclusive loading strategy
Mixed strategies are documented, and different interfaces may contribute in one plant. Interpret combined perturbations and composition data rather than forcing a binary classification.

20. Original CSIR-NET and SET practice

These questions are original examination-style exercises, not previous-year questions. Numerical and experimental datasets are illustrative unless a cited study is explicitly identified.

Part B — conceptual foundations

1. Which event defines phloem loading?

  1. Longitudinal movement through sieve plates
  2. Transfer of photoassimilates into collecting phloem
  3. Removal of sucrose from a root sink
  4. Transpiration through stomata
Answer: B

Loading establishes the assimilate supply to the collecting phloem. Longitudinal mass flow and unloading are subsequent processes; transpiration is primarily a water-loss process.

2. Which compartment belongs to the apoplast?

  1. Cytoplasmic sleeve of a plasmodesma
  2. Companion cell cytosol
  3. Interconnected cell-wall space
  4. Chloroplast stroma
Answer: C

The apoplast is outside plasma membranes. The cytoplasmic sleeve connects symplastic domains; the other two choices are intracellular compartments.

3. Which pairing correctly describes the major Arabidopsis sucrose loading proteins?

  1. SWEET11/12: facilitated export; SUC2: proton-coupled uptake
  2. SWEET11/12: ATP-driven uptake; SUC2: facilitated export
  3. SWEET11/12: proton pump; SUC2: starch synthase
  4. SWEET11/12: RFO synthesis; SUC2: water channel
Answer: A

SWEET11 and SWEET12 facilitate sucrose movement across the parenchyma membrane. SUC2 uses the inward proton electrochemical gradient. Neither is the primary ATP-driven pump.

4. What supplies the immediate driving force for SUC2-mediated accumulation?

  1. Hydrolysis of sucrose
  2. Direct hydrolysis of ATP by SUC2
  3. Xylem tension alone
  4. Inward proton electrochemical gradient
Answer: D

SUC2 is a secondary active transporter. The H⁺-ATPase establishes the gradient by primary active transport; neither sucrose hydrolysis nor xylem tension directly powers the symporter.

5. Which statement describes a SWEET facilitator?

  1. It always exports sugar irrespective of gradients
  2. It permits net sugar movement according to its chemical-potential gradient
  3. It transports sugar only through plasmodesmata
  4. It phosphorylates sucrose to drive uptake
Answer: B

A facilitator is reversible in principle; physiological direction depends on the gradient and location. It does not constitute an ATP-driven pump, a plasmodesmatal channel or a metabolic enzyme.

6. Which structure directly connects neighboring cytoplasmic domains?

  1. Sieve plate only
  2. Xylem pit membrane
  3. Plasmodesma
  4. Cuticle
Answer: C

Plasmodesmata cross cell walls and enable symplastic connectivity. Sieve plates connect successive sieve elements; pit membranes and cuticles do not provide the same cytoplasmic connection.

7. Which cell is especially associated with polymer trapping?

  1. Intermediary companion cell
  2. Guard cell
  3. Root hair
  4. Tracheid
Answer: A

Intermediary cells in many polymer-trapping plants combine specialized symplastic interfaces with RFO synthesis. Guard cells, root hairs and tracheids do not carry out this characteristic loading mechanism.

8. The first committed step of RFO synthesis produces which compound?

  1. Stachyose directly from glucose
  2. Cellulose
  3. Sucrose phosphate
  4. Galactinol
Answer: D

Galactinol synthase transfers galactose from UDP-galactose to myo-inositol. Galactinol subsequently supplies galactosyl groups for raffinose and stachyose formation.

9. Which substrate receives a galactosyl group to produce raffinose?

  1. Maltose
  2. Sucrose
  3. Stachyose
  4. Starch
Answer: B

Raffinose synthase adds a galactosyl residue from galactinol to sucrose. Stachyose is produced from raffinose by a subsequent transfer; maltose and starch are not the substrates of this reaction.

10. Which statement distinguishes passive symplastic loading from polymer trapping?

  1. Only passive loading involves living cells
  2. Polymer trapping lacks plasmodesmata
  3. Polymer trapping uses metabolic conversion to help maintain net entry and retention
  4. Passive loading requires direct ATP hydrolysis by a sucrose pump
Answer: C

Both routes can use plasmodesmata. Polymer trapping additionally couples entry to RFO synthesis and selective permeability, whereas passive loading does not require this conversion.

11. Why is SUC2-mediated transport called secondary active transport?

  1. It couples sucrose uptake to a gradient established by another energy-dependent process
  2. It operates only in secondary phloem
  3. It uses sunlight directly
  4. It moves sugar exclusively down its gradient
Answer: A

The proton motive force established by the H⁺-ATPase can pay the free-energy cost of uphill sucrose transport. The classification is energetic, not anatomical or dependent on direct light absorption.

12. Which change most directly increases the electrical favorability of inward proton transfer?

  1. A more positive cytoplasmic potential
  2. Equal pH on both sides, with all other variables fixed
  3. Depolarization
  4. A more negative cytoplasmic potential
Answer: D

For inward H⁺ movement, F(ψin − ψout) becomes more negative when the inside becomes more negative. Depolarization reduces this component. Changing pH concerns the chemical component.

13. Which observation by itself is insufficient to prove polymer trapping?

  1. RFO pathway suppression reduces export while RFO production falls
  2. Raffinose detected somewhere in a whole-leaf extract
  3. Localized RFO synthesis with selective interfaces and RFO-rich sap
  4. Evidence of inward sucrose transfer coupled to RFO accumulation
Answer: B

Raffinose can occur in non-phloem tissues and serve other metabolic functions. The other observations connect RFO synthesis more directly to transfer into the transport stream.

14. At equal molarity and temperature, ideal osmotic contributions of sucrose and raffinose are approximately what?

  1. Raffinose is three times larger
  2. Sucrose is twice as large
  3. Equal
  4. Both zero
Answer: C

Both are nonionizing dissolved molecules; ideal osmotic pressure depends on particle concentration, not carbon number. Nonideal behavior can matter at high concentrations.

15. Which experiment is directly associated with the functional significance of RFO synthesis in Verbascum?

  1. Suppression of galactinol synthase
  2. Removal of the coleoptile tip
  3. Measurement of guard-cell potassium only
  4. Disruption of chloroplast DNA replication
Answer: A

McCaskill and Turgeon examined galactinol synthase suppression and the effects on RFO-dependent export. The other interventions do not selectively test this loading pathway.

16. Which feature is compatible with an apoplastic loader?

  1. No plasmodesmata anywhere in its leaf
  2. A universal absence of companion cells
  3. Only passive transport across every membrane
  4. Limited external symplastic connectivity together with proton-coupled sucrose uptake
Answer: D

Restricted return permeability can help sustain sucrose accumulation. Apoplastic loaders still possess plasmodesmata, including those linking sieve elements with their companion cells.

17. In an ideal polymer-trapping model, which permeability pattern is required?

  1. RFOs must never enter the sieve element
  2. Sucrose can enter intermediary cells, RFO return is restricted, and RFOs can reach sieve elements
  3. All interfaces must exclude sucrose
  4. Starch particles must freely cross into the phloem
Answer: B

The pathway depends on net entry of sucrose, selective retention of larger products and forward delivery. Absolute blockage of forward RFO transfer would frustrate the transport mechanism.

18. Which class of protein primarily generates the plasma membrane proton gradient?

  1. Raffinose synthase
  2. SWEET facilitator
  3. H⁺-ATPase
  4. Aquaporin
Answer: C

The plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase directly couples ATP hydrolysis to proton export. Sugar facilitators, metabolic enzymes and water channels do not perform that primary pumping function.

19. What is the physiological meaning of a source?

  1. A tissue showing net assimilate export
  2. A tissue that always fixes nitrogen
  3. Any leaf regardless of developmental stage
  4. A tissue that always stores starch
Answer: A

Source identity depends on net carbon movement. A young leaf can be a sink and later become a source; stored carbohydrate may also be mobilized from a nonphotosynthetic organ.

20. Which observation is expected if loading slows while production and local use initially remain unchanged?

  1. Immediate elimination of every leaf carbohydrate
  2. All fixed carbon must enter roots
  3. No change in any carbon pool is possible
  4. Accessible source carbohydrate tends to accumulate
Answer: D

A mass balance predicts that the pool increases when production exceeds consumption and export. Later feedback or storage can modify the measured sucrose pool.

Part C — analytical reasoning

21. At 298 K, inward proton movement has ΔG = −18 kJ/mol and inward sucrose movement has ΔG = +12 kJ/mol. For ideal 1:1 coupling, which conclusion follows?

  1. Net ΔG = +30; transfer is unfavorable
  2. Net ΔG = −6; transfer is favorable
  3. Net ΔG = +6; transfer is unfavorable
  4. Net ΔG = −30; transfer rate is necessarily maximal
Answer: B

Add the two signed free-energy terms: −18 + 12 = −6 kJ/mol. The negative sum permits net coupled uptake but says nothing about the turnover rate or transporter abundance.

22. External pH increases by one unit while cytosolic pH, potential and temperature remain fixed. What happens to the ideal 1:1 sucrose accumulation limit?

  1. It increases tenfold
  2. It is unchanged
  3. It falls tenfold
  4. It falls only twofold
Answer: C

Proton activity outside falls tenfold, reducing the favorable inward chemical term by RT ln 10. The equilibrium sucrose concentration ratio therefore decreases by a factor of ten.

23. A membrane is depolarized from −120 to −60 mV at fixed pH. What happens to the inward proton free-energy term at 298 K?

  1. It becomes about 5.79 kJ/mol less favorable
  2. It becomes about 5.79 kJ/mol more favorable
  3. It is unchanged because protons are neutral
  4. It becomes exactly zero
Answer: A

The electrical term changes by F × 0.060 V = +5.79 kJ/mol. The proton is charged, so depolarization weakens inward energetic favorability. The chemical term need not vanish.

24. For v = 12C/(2+C), rates at 2 and 6 mM are respectively what?

  1. 2 and 6
  2. 12 and 36
  3. 6 and 12
  4. 6 and 9
Answer: D

Substitution gives 24/4 = 6 and 72/8 = 9. The approach to Vmax is saturable; doubling or tripling substrate does not imply proportional rate at these concentrations.

25. An illustrative passive-loading interface has constant permeability. Donor sucrose is 80 mM; receiver sucrose increases from 50 to 70 mM. What is the new diffusive flux relative to the original?

  1. Three times larger
  2. One third
  3. Unchanged
  4. Reversed
Answer: B

The original concentration difference is 30 mM and the new difference is 10 mM. The flux is one third while remaining directed toward the receiver.

26. A receiver contains 40 mM sucrose and 90 mM RFOs; the donor contains 70 mM sucrose and negligible RFOs. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Sucrose diffusion inward is impossible because total receiver sugar is larger
  2. RFOs must cross back at the same rate as sucrose
  3. Sucrose can diffuse inward despite higher total receiver sugar
  4. The receiver must contain no dissolved molecules
Answer: C

Diffusion of sucrose depends on its own chemical-potential difference, not total sugar molarity. RFO movement depends on their own gradients and permeability.

27. With 0.30 M ideal sucrose at 298 K and xylem water potential −0.20 MPa, what pressure gives local water-potential equilibrium?

  1. About +0.54 MPa
  2. About −0.94 MPa
  3. About +0.20 MPa
  4. About −0.54 MPa
Answer: A

Ψs = −0.30 × 0.008314 × 298 ≈ −0.743 MPa. P = −0.20 − (−0.743) = +0.543 MPa. A negative pressure would not satisfy this equilibrium.

28. Sample A contains 0.20 M sucrose; B contains 0.12 M sucrose plus 0.08 M raffinose. Under ideal assumptions which statement holds?

  1. B has lower total sugar molarity
  2. B has twice the osmotic pressure
  3. A carries more carbon
  4. Same sugar osmolarity; B carries 20% more carbon
Answer: D

Both have 0.20 M sugar particles. A contains 2.40 M carbon equivalents; B contains 1.44 + 1.44 = 2.88 M, or 20% more.

29. In a steady-state serial model, efflux = k(80−E), uptake = mE. If m = k/4, what is E?

  1. 16
  2. 64
  3. 40
  4. 80
Answer: B

Set 80−E = E/4; hence E = 80/1.25 = 64. Sixteen is the corresponding efflux difference, not the intermediate concentration.

30. In the same model, what is E if k = m/4 instead?

  1. 64
  2. 40
  3. 16
  4. 0
Answer: C

Set (80−E)/4 = E, giving 80 = 5E and E = 16. Impairing upstream input lowers the intermediate pool, in contrast to impairment of the receiving step.

31. Two serial interfaces have conductances 2 and 1 and a total concentration difference 60. What steady-state diffusive flux is predicted?

  1. 40
  2. 180
  3. 60
  4. 120
Answer: A

Add resistances: 1/2 + 1/1 = 1.5. Divide the total difference by resistance: 60/1.5 = 40. Adding conductances would be appropriate only for parallel routes under comparable gradients.

32. A leaf fixes 1000 label units and exports 400; another fixes 500 and exports 250. What can be concluded?

  1. The second has higher absolute export
  2. Both export equal fractions
  3. The first has the higher exported fraction
  4. The second has a higher exported fraction but lower absolute export
Answer: D

The fractions are 40% and 50%; absolute values are 400 and 250. Neither comparison alone establishes intrinsic loading capacity, since substrate production differs.

33. A phloem sample has 100 mM sucrose, 60 mM raffinose and 40 mM stachyose. Which total carbon-equivalent concentration is correct?

  1. 200 mM
  2. 3240 mM
  3. 2400 mM
  4. 3600 mM
Answer: B

Compute 100×12 + 60×18 + 40×24 = 3240 mM carbon atoms. Two hundred mM is the total molecular concentration, not carbon equivalents.

34. A source exports 2 μL/min at 1 μmol C/μL. A treatment yields 0.8 μL/min at 2 μmol C/μL. Carbon export becomes what?

  1. 4 μmol/min
  2. 2 μmol/min
  3. 1.6 μmol/min
  4. 0.8 μmol/min
Answer: C

Multiply volume flow by carbon concentration. The control delivers 2 and treatment delivers 1.6 μmol/min; increased concentration does not compensate for reduced volume flow.

35. SWEET function is intact but SUC uptake is abruptly impaired in an ideal two-step system. What initial change is most consistent?

  1. Apoplastic sucrose removal slows, potentially raising its concentration
  2. The donor must instantly lose every sucrose molecule
  3. Raffinose synthase must directly increase
  4. The plasma membrane becomes freely permeable to sucrose
Answer: A

The downstream uptake step consumes the extracellular pool. Reduced removal can increase that pool and weaken the SWEET gradient. None of the other changes follows directly.

36. A transporter mediates sucrose uptake in a heterologous cell but is proposed to mediate efflux in phloem parenchyma. What resolves the apparent contradiction?

  1. Every transporter is an irreversible pump
  2. The study proves the phloem hypothesis false
  3. Sucrose changes electrical charge when transported
  4. A facilitator can reverse net direction with the sugar gradient
Answer: D

The heterologous system may present a different chemical potential difference. Transport capability is not a demonstration of physiological direction in the intact plant.

37. A species shows minor-vein SUC expression, abundant outer-interface plasmodesmata and sucrose-rich sap. Which next test best distinguishes predominant loading pathways?

  1. Count total chloroplasts alone
  2. Perturb the candidate receiving transporter and measure local uptake/export with photosynthesis controls
  3. Measure leaf length only
  4. Assume SUC expression proves apoplastic loading
Answer: B

The candidate transporter may load or retrieve sugar. Cell-specific perturbation plus local transfer and controlled substrate supply tests the functional role; anatomy and expression alone do not establish it.

38. An inhibitor lowers export but also halves photosynthetic carbon fixation. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. The inhibitor uniquely blocks SUC2
  2. The plant necessarily uses polymer trapping
  3. A selective loading effect is not established without controlling substrate supply and other effects
  4. Photosynthesis is irrelevant to export
Answer: C

Both carbon production and movement may change. Comparing local uptake with matched substrate availability and verifying target engagement would help distinguish loading from upstream limitations.

39. An RFO-synthesis mutant has unchanged RFO production. Its export is also unchanged. What does this establish?

  1. The experiment has not demonstrated effective perturbation of the proposed RFO pathway
  2. Polymer trapping is definitively absent
  3. RFO synthesis has no role in any species
  4. All sucrose crosses the apoplast
Answer: A

If the intended biochemical output is unchanged, redundancy or insufficient perturbation is plausible. A negative export phenotype cannot refute the pathway without target engagement.

40. Suppression of galactinol synthase reduces RFO synthesis and export in a plant with intermediary cells. Which model is most directly supported?

  1. Xylem cohesion alone explains sugar loading
  2. All plants use apoplastic transport
  3. An ATP-driven sucrose pump is the only possible route
  4. RFO synthesis contributes to active symplastic loading
Answer: D

The change links the metabolic conversion to export in the relevant system. It does not establish exclusivity or imply that every plant uses this mechanism.

41. Control, first perturbation, second perturbation and combined perturbation yield export values 60, 25, 22 and 20. Which model is compatible, but not proven?

  1. Two completely independent routes with no shared steps and equal capacities
  2. A serial route with both steps essential and limited residual export
  3. Absence of any loading defect
  4. Direct physical binding between the two proteins is proven
Answer: B

Both single perturbations have severe effects and the combination adds little, consistent with serial dependence. Residual pathways and incomplete perturbation complicate inference; physical interaction is not established.

42. In a second dataset the four values are 60, 42, 40 and 20. What is a reasonable interpretation if perturbations are specific and effective?

  1. The results prove the two proteins are identical
  2. Neither pathway contributes
  3. Parallel contributions are compatible with the greater combined effect
  4. Only starch formation changes
Answer: C

Each perturbation leaves substantial export and the combined effect is stronger. This supports contributions from both components, although shared regulation or indirect effects must still be tested.

43. A source initially produces 15 μmol/h, consumes 5 and exports 3. Starting at 40 μmol, what pool is predicted after two hours if other fluxes are zero?

  1. 54 μmol
  2. 46 μmol
  3. 20 μmol
  4. 76 μmol
Answer: A

Net accumulation is 15−5−3 = 7 μmol/h. Over two hours it is 14 μmol; the final pool is 54. Pool size and export flux are distinct.

44. A transporter has Vmax 12 and Km 2 mM. Its abundance doubles with unchanged properties and gradients. What does the simplest model predict?

  1. Km doubles, Vmax unchanged
  2. Both become zero
  3. Km halves and Vmax unchanged
  4. Vmax doubles to 24, Km unchanged
Answer: D

In the ideal model, Vmax scales with the number of functional transporters while Km reflects the apparent substrate dependence of each. Real cells may have additional limiting factors.

45. A polymer-trapping interface becomes highly permeable to raffinose and stachyose in both directions, with RFO synthesis unchanged. What is a plausible direct consequence?

  1. Enhanced retention regardless of gradients
  2. Increased back-leakage can weaken the trap
  3. Sucrose becomes an ion
  4. The pathway must become entirely ATP independent
Answer: B

Selective retention depends on relatively low return permeability of the RFOs. Increasing it can reduce accumulation even if their production continues.

Integrated and SET-style practice

46. Assertion: Polymer trapping is active symplastic loading. Reason: The local entry of sucrose is coupled to metabolism-dependent conversion into RFOs and selective retention.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer: A

Both statements are correct. Although sucrose entry can be diffusional, the metabolic conversion helps establish accumulation and retention, distinguishing the overall strategy from passive symplastic loading.

47. Assertion: All sucrose entering a sieve element must cross a plasma membrane from the apoplast. Reason: Plasmodesmata can connect the cytoplasm of neighboring cells.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer: D

The assertion incorrectly generalizes apoplastic loading. Symplastic routes use plasmodesmata; the reason correctly identifies their cytoplasmic connectivity.

48. Which combination is most appropriate for identifying a predominant polymer-trapping route?

  1. Leaf shape and root length
  2. Intermediary-cell anatomy, RFO-rich sap and functional disruption of RFO synthesis
  3. Girdling alone
  4. Detection of a generic ATPase in any tissue
Answer: B

The combined anatomical, chemical and perturbation evidence tests the predicted mechanism. Girdling establishes phloem dependence, not loading type; generic anatomy or ATPase expression is insufficient.

49. A model source has high pressure but longitudinal flow is very low. Which explanation remains possible?

  1. Loading is necessarily absent
  2. High pressure automatically means high flow
  3. High hydraulic resistance or a small source-to-sink pressure difference
  4. Sucrose has become negatively charged
Answer: C

Flow depends on pressure difference and resistance, not absolute source pressure alone. A high upstream pressure can occur near an obstruction.

50. Which observation best helps separate a primary loading defect from secondary feedback on photosynthesis?

  1. Early decline in local uptake before later carbohydrate accumulation and photosynthetic decline, with relevant controls
  2. A single late measurement of leaf colour
  3. Counting only the number of sinks
  4. Assuming the first measured change is always the original cause
Answer: A

A time course with appropriate controls helps establish temporal order and mechanistic specificity. It cannot prove causation alone but is more informative than a static late phenotype.

21. Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

Loading is the local acquisition of assimilates by source collecting phloem. Apoplastic loading uses SWEET-mediated export followed by SUC/SUT-mediated uptake supported by the H⁺-ATPase. Passive symplastic loading can use an accessible sucrose gradient through plasmodesmata. Polymer trapping combines symplastic entry with metabolic conversion to RFOs and selective interfaces. The three mechanisms can coexist. All supply soluble assimilates that contribute to osmotic water entry and pressure-driven export, but their local transfer energetics differ.

Essential equations and terminology

  • Inward proton free energy: ΔG = RT ln(ain/aout) + F(ψin − ψout).
  • Neutral sucrose transfer: ΔG = RT ln(Cin/Cout); coupled uptake requires a favorable sum under the assumed stoichiometry.
  • Simple diffusive transfer: J = PA(Cdonor − Creceiver).
  • Ideal solute potential: Ψs = −CRT. Water potential includes pressure potential.
  • Illustrative hydraulic flow: Q = ΔP/Rh; carbon delivery equals volume flow times carbon-equivalent concentration.

Know the distinctions among apoplast, symplast, SE–CC complex, intermediary cell, facilitated diffusion, secondary active transport, galactinol and RFO. Distinguish concentrations from fluxes, pressure from pressure difference, and carbon equivalents from osmotic particles.
