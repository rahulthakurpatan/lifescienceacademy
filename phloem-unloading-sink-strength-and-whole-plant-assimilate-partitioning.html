1. From carbon fixation to carbon use

A germinating seedling may extend a root several centimetres before its first leaf has expanded. The root tip consists of living cells that divide, grow and differentiate, but it receives little light and cannot meet its carbon requirements by photosynthesis. Its carbon is initially supplied by reserves mobilized from the seed. Later, mature illuminated leaves become the main suppliers. A vascular plant therefore faces two distinct nutritional problems: manufacturing organic carbon and delivering it to distant tissues that need to build structures, maintain living cells or accumulate reserves. Photoassimilates are organic compounds derived from photosynthetic assimilation of inorganic carbon. Sucrose is the principal long-distance transported carbohydrate in many flowering plants, although some species transport substantial quantities of raffinose-family oligosaccharides or sugar alcohols. A carbohydrate molecule that arrives near a growing cell has not yet necessarily become available to that cell. Unloading and subsequent delivery determine whether transport actually supports growth.

A source is a tissue that exports more of the relevant assimilate than it imports over the interval of interest. A sink is a net importer. These terms describe physiological roles rather than fixed anatomical categories. A mature leaf under adequate illumination is normally a source, whereas a young leaf may receive sugars from older leaves despite possessing chloroplasts. The young leaf may have some photosynthetic activity, but its carbon demand for expansion and its respiratory cost exceed its own assimilation. As it matures, its photosynthetic supply increases and its relative growth demand changes. Portions of a leaf may pass from import to export at different times. A storage organ such as a tuber can be a powerful sink during formation and later become a source during sprouting when its reserves are mobilized. It is the sign of the net carbon balance that matters, not the name of the organ.

Long-distance movement takes place in phloem sieve tubes. Mature angiosperm sieve elements have lost their nucleus and much of the ordinary cytoplasmic apparatus; companion cells sustain their function. Sieve plates connect successive elements to make a continuous pathway for fluid flow. Photosynthate reaches the source sieve element-companion cell complex by loading, moves along the tube under a hydrostatic pressure gradient and finally exits in a recipient region. In this chapter unloading refers to the exit from the conducting sieve element system and, when explicitly stated in a broad sense, the series of subsequent transfers that deliver the assimilate to sites of use. The site of unloading and the site of metabolism need not coincide. A root tip can receive solute from a spatially restricted zone of differentiating phloem even though actively growing cells are distributed farther away.

Three separate quantities must be kept in view. The concentration of sucrose in phloem sap measures the amount per unit fluid volume. The flow rate measures how much sap passes a cross section per unit time. The solute transport rate depends on both. A tissue with a high sugar concentration is not necessarily importing rapidly. It could have received sugar in the past and now retain it because metabolism or transport out of that compartment has slowed. Similarly a sink with a low steady-state sucrose concentration can import rapidly if sucrose is consumed or stored as quickly as it arrives. These distinctions are essential when interpreting metabolite measurements, mutants and isotope experiments.

2. Pressure flow and the physical significance of unloading

The pressure-flow hypothesis associated with Ernst Münch gives a physical explanation for transport between source and sink. At a source, increasing solute concentration in the sieve element lowers its osmotic potential. Water moves across permeable membranes according to water potential differences, usually entering the conducting system from nearby tissues including xylem. Entry of water into a confined volume generates positive hydrostatic pressure. At a sink, removal of solute and movement of water out of the sieve tube can contribute to a lower pressure. A pressure difference along a connected sieve tube drives bulk fluid flow. The individual sucrose molecules are passengers in this moving solution. No requirement exists for an ATP-consuming carrier to transfer every molecule from one sieve element to the next along the whole length of the tube.

For a first approximation, the water potential of a cell can be expressed as Ψw = Ψs + Ψp, with osmotic potential Ψs and pressure potential Ψp. The equation neglects gravitational and matric contributions when they are small across the local membrane. Osmotic potential is negative relative to pure water at the reference pressure, while turgor pressure is usually positive in hydrated living cells. For an ideal dilute non-electrolyte, Ψs = −CRT, where C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, R = 0.008314 MPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A 0.40 mol L⁻¹ sucrose solution would therefore have an ideal osmotic potential of about −0.99 MPa. A 0.20 mol L⁻¹ solution gives about −0.50 MPa. The calculation is illustrative: actual phloem contains several solutes and concentrated solutions can deviate from ideality.

Suppose the local water potential at the source and sink were both −0.30 MPa and equilibration across membranes were complete. The pressure potentials inferred from the two example sucrose concentrations would be approximately +0.69 MPa at the source and +0.20 MPa at the sink. The source-to-sink pressure difference would be about 0.49 MPa. The simplifying assumption of equal local water potentials is important; actual xylem water potentials can vary between sites. A sink can have lower sucrose concentration yet not have the predicted lower pressure if other terms differ. The direction of bulk fluid flow must ultimately be evaluated from the pressure gradient and relevant hydraulic pathway rather than from sugar concentration alone.

Representing the pathway by an effective hydraulic resistance Rₕ, the volume flow Q is approximately ΔP/Rₕ. If Q is measured in microlitres per hour and sucrose concentration in micromoles per microlitre, their product gives micromoles transported per hour. If 0.5 µL h⁻¹ of sap contains 0.40 µmol µL⁻¹ sucrose, its advective sucrose transport is 0.20 µmol h⁻¹. A change in sap concentration can affect both the amount carried per unit volume and the pressure available to drive flow, so the two factors should not generally be treated as independent experimental variables. In a network of tubes, resistance includes effects of tube geometry, sieve plates and sap properties. The elementary relation is a useful approximation, not a complete description of a living transport network.

Unloading matters because it couples the downstream metabolism of a sink to the driving conditions of the transport system. Removing sugar from a sink-side sieve element can prevent local solute accumulation that would otherwise reduce the ability of the source to maintain a pressure difference. Water may be recycled through surrounding tissues and xylem, but the exact pathway depends on organ anatomy and local water relations. A growing fruit, for example, also retains water to increase volume. It would be incorrect to assume that every molecule of water entering a source sieve tube necessarily returns immediately to the xylem at a terminal sink. The pressure-flow framework predicts the net direction of volume movement without requiring this oversimplified closed circuit.

3. Cellular pathways: symplast and apoplast

Two major cellular routes connect sieve elements to recipient tissue. The symplast is the collective cytoplasmic continuum of cells connected by plasmodesmata, narrow membrane-lined channels crossing cell walls. The apoplast consists principally of the cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membrane. A solute that moves continuously through plasmodesmata stays within the symplastic domain. A solute that leaves a cell into the extracellular space crosses its plasma membrane and must cross another plasma membrane to reach the cytosol of an adjacent intact cell. A route can be mixed: a sugar may leave the sieve element through plasmodesmata, move through a few layers of connected cells and then encounter a cell boundary where membrane transport is required.

Symplastic unloading is particularly important in many actively growing vegetative sinks. When a continuous aqueous pathway is open between the sieve element and recipient cells, sucrose can move by diffusion, pressure-driven convection or a combination of the two. The diffusive component depends on a concentration gradient and the conductance of the connection. The convective component depends on fluid movement and carries dissolved solute with it. In a simple one-dimensional representation, solute flux density J = uC − D(dC/dx), where u is fluid velocity, C concentration and D the effective diffusion coefficient. In the absence of bulk flow, the second term describes movement down a concentration gradient. With positive fluid velocity, the first term describes transport in the direction of fluid movement. In actual plasmodesmata, geometry, steric restriction and interaction with channel contents affect the effective coefficients. The formula clarifies why evidence of movement through a cytoplasmic connection does not, by itself, establish that diffusion is its only mechanism.

Plasmodesmata differ in their permeability according to cell identity, developmental stage and regulation. Deposition of callose, a β-1,3-glucan, around a plasmodesmatal neck can restrict movement. Reduction of that barrier may increase the permeability of an existing connection. The number of connections is not the only determinant of transport capacity. Their shape, effective aperture and physiological state can be equally important. Small metabolites can move through channels that exclude larger proteins or RNA-containing complexes. Conversely some specialized connections support movement of selected macromolecules. A tracer that readily reaches an adjacent cell provides evidence about the behavior of that tracer under the experimental conditions, not a universal size limit for all transported material.

In apoplastic transfer, release of sucrose from a donor cell into its cell wall can be mediated by a facilitative transporter, including members of the SWEET family in well-established examples. Facilitation means that the transporter permits net movement down an appropriate chemical potential gradient; its name does not establish an intrinsic outward direction regardless of concentrations. Recipient cells can acquire sucrose using SUT or SUC family proton-coupled transporters. Proton motive force is maintained in part by plasma membrane H⁺-ATPases that consume ATP to export protons. Because the outside solution is usually more acidic and the inside electrically negative, inward proton movement can provide free energy for sucrose uptake against its own concentration gradient. This is secondary active transport: ATP hydrolysis occurs at the proton pump rather than at the sucrose symporter. Alternatively cell-wall invertase can hydrolyse extracellular sucrose into glucose and fructose, followed by uptake of hexoses via appropriate transporters.

Thus the question whether unloading requires energy must be divided into its physical steps. Movement through open plasmodesmata does not require one ATP per molecule crossing, but the maintenance of metabolism, turgor and channel properties has energetic costs. SWEET-mediated facilitation is not directly ATP-driven; coupled uptake can depend on the energy supplied by the proton gradient. A sink also expends energy converting assimilates into cellular materials. Calling an entire tissue simply passive or active conceals these distinct processes. Experimental inhibition of metabolism may reduce import even if the first step of sieve element exit is passive, because consumption and compartmentation maintain the downstream gradients.

4. Root tips and experimental identification of a pathway

A root apex provides an especially useful biological example because the cells that must grow are not the same cells that receive long-distance delivery. Newly formed vascular cells differentiate behind the apical meristem. Functioning protophloem elements deliver carbohydrates near the elongating and dividing regions of the root. Solute must then move laterally from vascular tissue to the surrounding tissues and longitudinally toward areas that lack fully differentiated sieve elements. The anatomical endpoint of a conducting strand is therefore not the metabolic endpoint of the carbon it carries.

Ross-Elliott and colleagues examined phloem exit in Arabidopsis roots in a study published in eLife in 2017. Their work combined live imaging, three-dimensional electron microscopy and modelling. The results support a specialized connection between protophloem sieve elements and phloem-pole pericycle cells, including funnel-shaped plasmodesmata. Solute movement in this system has a convective component as well as a diffusive one. The pericycle acts as an intermediate compartment through which delivered molecules can reach other tissues. This is evidence for a specific unloading architecture in Arabidopsis roots; it is not a reason to assign funnel-shaped connections to every organ or species. Proteins and smaller solutes need not display identical transfer patterns.

A fluorescent tracer offers a way to visualize the spatial sequence of transport. A useful experiment introduces a suitable phloem-mobile marker to a source and observes fluorescence over time in the recipient region. If the signal first appears in the sieve element, then a defined adjacent cell and later additional tissues, the time sequence is compatible with a transfer route through those cells. Interpretation requires controls for optical bleed-through, tissue damage, extracellular leakage, metabolism of the tracer and possible tracer sequestration. A molecule that becomes trapped in vacuoles can accumulate brightly without indicating that those vacuoles form the main route of translocation. Endpoint fluorescence alone gives much less information than a time-resolved experiment combined with anatomical identification.

To distinguish impaired unloading from impaired transport toward the root, measure delivery into the root phloem and appearance outside it separately. Consider a treatment that increases callose specifically near plasmodesmata at the unloading interface. If a small fluorescent tracer continues to arrive within the conducting system but spreads less efficiently into surrounding cells, the observation supports an interface defect. If both the phloem signal and external signal decrease, a source or long-distance transport defect remains possible. A second measurement of root sugar or growth helps connect the movement of the marker with the physiological consequence, but cannot replace the spatial evidence. A strong test changes the suspected route in the relevant cell type and measures both upstream delivery and downstream response. Loss of root growth alone could also reflect a direct disturbance of cell division, water availability or hormone signaling.

5. Developing leaves and the source–sink transition

The developmental transition of a leaf demonstrates why the direction of assimilate movement cannot be predicted from the upward or downward orientation of a vascular strand. Young leaves rely on imported carbon for expanding cell walls, nucleic acid synthesis, protein turnover, respiratory energy and construction of photosynthetic machinery. Early in development, incoming sugars can move from veins toward growing cells through local transport paths appropriate to the tissue. As photosynthetic capacity and vascular organization mature, a greater portion of the leaf can supply its own requirements. The balance eventually shifts toward net export to other sinks.

Define the leaf's carbon balance over an interval in units of carbon per unit time. Net carbon available for export equals carbon fixation minus respiratory loss minus net carbon accumulation within the leaf, provided other inputs and losses are negligible. If fixation is 12 units, respiration is 3 and growth plus storage demand is 14 units, the difference is −5 units, indicating a required net import of five units under the assumptions. At a later stage fixation may rise to 25, respiration to 5 and accumulation may decrease to 8, leaving a net export of 12 units. These are hypothetical teaching values, not measurements from a particular species. The accounting deliberately treats carbon equivalents rather than confusing the mass of sucrose with the mass of carbon in sucrose.

The switch can be spatially heterogeneous. Portions of a developing leaf may be photosynthetically mature while other regions remain dependent on import. A source and a sink need not be separate organs in every case. Within a whole plant, conducting routes connect particular source leaves more efficiently to some recipient organs than others. The vascular architecture can therefore cause a labeled leaf to preferentially supply a subset of sinks. This pattern, often discussed in terms of sectoriality or orthostichy, is compatible with whole-plant pressure flow because network connectivity matters. A nearby sink may receive less labeled carbon than a more distant but better connected sink.

Removing an older source leaf can deprive its dependent young leaves of carbon, but the magnitude of the response depends on redundancy among source leaves and on developmental stage. A remaining mature leaf may compensate by increasing export if its photosynthetic and loading capacities permit. If an expanding leaf has already become partly self-supporting, the response to removal may be smaller. For an experimental comparison, use plants of similar size and developmental age, standardize light, and account for wounding effects. Distinguish the immediate redistribution of current assimilate from longer-term changes in leaf area, photosynthesis and organ growth.

6. Fruits, seeds and developmental barriers

A developing fruit may import assimilates both to support tissue expansion and to accumulate substances that contribute to the nutritional properties of the mature organ. Cell division is initially important in many fruits; expansion and storage become dominant at later stages. Different species store different mixtures of sucrose, glucose, fructose, starch and other compounds. Sugar accumulation influences osmotic potential and can contribute to water uptake, but an increase in fresh mass is not a direct measure of net carbon import. A fruit that increases its water content without increasing dry matter has gained mass without an equivalent gain in assimilate-derived material.

Some fruits use extensive symplastic transfer at certain stages and a greater apoplastic component at others. The change can reflect developmentally regulated connectivity or membrane transport capacity. It is safer to infer pathway identity from tracer behavior, transporter localization and physiological tests than to assume a universal route from fruit identity. Extracellular invertase can maintain a low sucrose concentration in the cell wall by splitting sucrose into glucose and fructose, which may subsequently be imported. Hydrolysis also increases the number of dissolved particles per sucrose molecule before subsequent transport and metabolism, potentially affecting local osmotic conditions. However this is not proof that invertase always determines fruit size; membrane uptake, water relations, enzyme capacity and developmental controls can impose other limitations.

Seeds illustrate an unavoidable topological issue. The developing embryo and endosperm receive nutrition from maternal tissues, but the maternal and filial tissues do not form one uninterrupted symplastic continuum across their interface. Consequently assimilates must cross an extracellular boundary and enter recipient cells through membrane-dependent transfer at some stage of their journey. There can still be symplastic routes within maternal or filial domains. In some grains, transfer cells with wall ingrowths increase plasma membrane surface available for exchange. Their morphology is consistent with a capacity for intensive transfer, although evidence of function must also establish transport protein activity and solute movement.

Chen and colleagues studied three Arabidopsis SWEET transporters required for normal seed filling in a 2015 Plant Cell paper. The combined loss of SWEET11, SWEET12 and SWEET15 impaired seed filling; starch accumulated in the seed coat rather than the embryo. The spatial distribution is especially informative. If only whole-seed starch had been measured, accumulation in the donor tissue could have obscured shortage in the filial tissue. Instead the results support a defect in the transfer of assimilates between compartments. The interpretation is strengthened by expression and localization evidence and by the partially redundant roles of related transporters. It does not mean that every SWEET family member performs the same function or that these transporters directly hydrolyse ATP.

A sink can retain abundant carbohydrate in one compartment while another compartment remains starved. This possibility is critical when reasoning about mutant phenotypes. If a seed coat accumulates carbohydrate while the embryo grows slowly, adding more sugar to the source leaf may fail to resolve the underlying boundary limitation. Restoration of the appropriate membrane transfer capacity may be more relevant, provided the embryo is developmentally competent to use that sugar. A source limitation, a transport-path limitation and a recipient metabolic limitation can produce superficially similar reductions in seed weight. Their locations and predictions differ.

7. Sink strength and the metabolism of imported carbon

Sink strength describes the capacity of a recipient organ to attract and utilize assimilates, commonly interpreted through sink size and sink activity. Sink size represents the amount of importing tissue, while sink activity describes uptake or utilization per unit sink size under specified conditions. If two roots have the same mass but one imports more sugar per unit mass and time, their sink activities differ. Conversely two tissues with equal specific activity can have different total import because one contains more active tissue. The product of size and specific activity has units of total assimilate uptake per unit time, provided the measures are consistent. The identity is descriptive and does not independently determine the causal limits of import.

Suppose root A contains 3 g of importing dry matter and has a measured activity of 4 µmol sucrose equivalents g⁻¹ h⁻¹. Its observed import is 12 µmol h⁻¹. Root B contains 5 g but has an activity of 2 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹, giving 10 µmol h⁻¹. Although B is larger, A currently imports more. These hypothetical values illustrate why larger organs are not automatically stronger sinks. If source supply becomes severely limited, neither root may realize its potential capacity. A measure of actual import in one supply condition should not automatically be interpreted as a fixed maximum capacity.

Imported sucrose can follow several fates. Invertase hydrolyses it into glucose and fructose. Sucrose synthase catalyses a reversible reaction between sucrose and UDP, yielding UDP-glucose and fructose in the cleavage direction. UDP-glucose can contribute to the formation of cell-wall polysaccharides or enter connected carbohydrate metabolism. The fate of the products depends on substrate concentrations, cellular compartment, enzyme activity and the needs of the tissue. Invertase isoforms occur in different compartments, including the cell wall, vacuole and cytosol. Their locations matter because hydrolysis on opposite sides of a membrane affects different concentration gradients. It is therefore insufficient to measure total invertase activity in an organ and infer precisely which cellular step changed.

Respiration uses imported carbohydrate to generate ATP and reducing equivalents needed by growing cells. Incorporation into starch, lipids, protein carbon skeletons and cell-wall polymers contributes to storage or structural growth. Vacuolar sequestration of soluble sugars can also reduce cytosolic free-sugar concentration. All these fates can help sustain import by preventing accumulation of freely exchangeable sugar, though the effect depends on transport conductance and feedback. Conversion of sucrose into a polymer does not destroy its carbon; the carbon becomes less osmotically active per stored monomer equivalent and less immediately available for diffusion back toward the vascular tissues.

Starch storage requires metabolic conversion and energy investment. A potato tuber can accumulate a large reserve during its growth phase, then later mobilize this reserve during sprouting. At the scale of the whole plant, carbon partitioning changes with developmental demand. A crop's harvestable yield depends not only on the amount of carbon fixed but also on how much reaches the harvested organ, how much is retained there and how much is lost through respiration. The relationship between sink capacity and yield is consequently conditional rather than linear.

8. Whole-plant partitioning and mobile signals

A plant supplies multiple sinks simultaneously, including expanding leaves, roots, flowers, developing seeds and storage organs. Partitioning describes the distribution of newly assimilated carbon among these competing destinations; allocation can also include the subsequent use of carbon within organs. Competition arises because source supply, vascular capacity and recipient uptake capacity are finite. A sink that becomes stronger may capture a larger fraction of available carbon, but the response of other sinks depends on whether total source export increases, remains fixed or declines. It is not generally valid to predict a fixed proportional reduction in every other sink.

Consider a hypothetical plant exporting 100 µmol carbon equivalents per hour to three sinks: roots receive 35, fruits receive 45 and expanding leaves receive 20. If fruit import increases to 60 and total export stays at 100, the other sinks together must receive 40 instead of 55. The mass balance does not determine how that reduction is divided between roots and leaves. Their connection to sources, hydraulic resistances and unloading capacities remain relevant. If source export increases to 115, the additional fruit demand might instead be met without reducing the total delivered to the other sinks. A carbon balance is valuable for identifying possible and impossible outcomes but must be combined with physiological information to predict a particular allocation pattern.

A source-limited plant cannot supply enough carbohydrate to satisfy the demands of its sinks. Low light, severe loss of functional leaf area or reduced carbon fixation can produce such a condition. A sink-limited plant has inadequate capacity to remove and utilize available assimilate despite potential photosynthetic production. Removal of developing fruit can reduce aggregate sink demand and lead to carbohydrate accumulation in leaves, sometimes followed by downregulation of photosynthesis. However the response varies with remaining sinks, storage capacity, environmental conditions and the time allowed for acclimation. An immediate change in phloem flux and a longer-term change in photosynthetic capacity are not the same outcome.

The phloem also carries signals, including certain hormones, proteins and RNAs. A classic example is the FLOWERING LOCUS T protein, which contributes to long-distance flowering induction in Arabidopsis. Work by Corbesier and colleagues in 2007 supports movement of FT protein from leaf vascular tissues toward the shoot apex. A signal is not required to be a quantitatively important part of carbon nutrition. A small amount of a regulatory protein can alter developmental decisions and thereby change future sink demand. Movement of a mobile signal involves selection, entry, translocation, exit and response; evidence that a molecule is present in phloem does not by itself prove its biological function at a distant site.

The plant therefore operates as an interacting system rather than as a source mechanically filling passive containers. Source photosynthesis responds to sugar status and the environment, transport responds to pressure and pathway resistance, and sink tissues respond to metabolism, development and signaling. A change at one location may affect distant organs through changes in flux or regulatory communication. The fundamental reasoning task is to specify which process is altered, identify the direction of the initial effect, and distinguish it from secondary compensatory responses.

9. Quantitative reasoning about the driving forces

The most useful extension of the pressure-flow hypothesis for understanding unloading is to ask what happens to pressure and sugar concentration when the capacity of the sink changes. Imagine a source connected to one sink through a sieve tube. At the beginning of an experiment, the source maintains a supply of sucrose, the sink removes it continuously and water can move between the conducting system and adjacent tissue. If a blockage is introduced at the unloading interface, sucrose may accumulate upstream of the blockage. As the osmotic potential becomes more negative, local water exchange can increase pressure in that region. The difference between source and sink pressure can decline. This is one way a defect localized in a recipient tissue can reduce long-distance translocation without destroying a sieve plate in the transport pathway.

The prediction is conditional. If the source simultaneously reduces loading, if the sink region loses water, or if another sink accepts more assimilate through a branch in the network, the observed pressure and concentration changes may differ. An experiment examining the first minutes after a specific blockage may show a different pattern from an experiment examining an acclimated plant after several days. The immediate response reflects altered transport; the later response may include changes in gene expression, tissue growth and the number of functional vascular elements. In interpreting a mutant, distinguish a direct consequence of removing a transport protein from indirect consequences of chronic developmental defects.

For a neutral solute such as sucrose, the chemical potential difference between two ideal solutions at the same pressure is approximated by RT ln(C₂/C₁). Moving sucrose from a dilute cell-wall solution into a more concentrated cytosol is unfavorable by this criterion. For example, if the extracellular concentration is 5 mM and the cytosolic concentration is 50 mM, the tenfold accumulation requires about RT ln 10 = 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. A uniporter that only facilitates diffusion cannot sustain this accumulation at equilibrium. A coupled transporter can do so if the free energy released by downhill movement of another species exceeds the unfavorable contribution. This calculation neglects activity corrections and the pressure contribution to the solute chemical potential, reasonable for a simple membrane-transport exercise but not for arbitrarily concentrated solutions.

For a proton moving from outside a cell to inside, the free energy change is RT ln([H⁺]in/[H⁺]out) + F(Vin − Vout), where F is the Faraday constant and voltage is expressed in volts. Suppose the outside pH is 5.5, the inside pH is 7.0 and the membrane potential is −0.12 V relative to the extracellular space. The proton concentration ratio is 10⁻¹·⁵, so the chemical term is approximately −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹. The electrical term is approximately −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹ using F = 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹. The total is about −20.1 kJ mol⁻¹. Under these illustrative conditions, downhill movement of one proton could in principle provide enough free energy for tenfold sucrose accumulation. Actual rates depend on the transporter and its coupling stoichiometry, competing fluxes, membrane potential maintenance and regulation. Energy feasibility does not predict a measured transport rate by itself.

A pH shift can change transport even when extracellular sucrose concentration is constant. Raising external pH from 5.5 to 6.5 reduces the chemical contribution available for proton entry by RT ln 10, approximately 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K, if all other quantities remain fixed. Depolarizing the membrane from −120 to −60 mV makes the electrical contribution approximately 5.79 kJ mol⁻¹ less favorable. Both manipulations weaken the driving force for proton-coupled uptake. In an actual plant, the proton pump, buffering, ion channels and respiration respond to such changes; the assumption of fixed voltage or pH must be stated. A direct test of transport coupling may compare uptake at controlled pH in a suitable membrane system with an appropriate control lacking the transporter. A tissue-level pH treatment alone is less specific because it can also alter enzyme activity and cell viability.

This reasoning predicts a useful experimental distinction between an energy-coupled importer and a facilitative exporter. If a donor cell contains more sucrose than the apoplast, a SWEET-mediated outward flux may still be thermodynamically possible when the recipient proton gradient is weakened. But reduced recipient uptake permits extracellular sugar to accumulate, diminishing the donor-to-apoplast gradient. Over time, donor export may decline indirectly. Measuring only total import would not reveal whether the exporter itself was inhibited. Measuring concentrations on both sides of the relevant boundary together with transporter localization and short-term uptake provides a stronger interpretation.

Diffusion and convection in the symplast can also be compared quantitatively. In a simple channel model, the Péclet number Pe = uL/D compares the characteristic importance of advective movement with diffusion across a distance L. It is dimensionless because u has units of length per time and D has units of length squared per time. For an illustrative effective velocity of 2 µm s⁻¹, a path length of 1 µm and an effective diffusion coefficient of 4 µm² s⁻¹, Pe is 0.5. If velocity rises to 20 µm s⁻¹ without changing the other values, Pe becomes 5. The first condition suggests that diffusion is relatively important; the second favors advective transport on that scale. A precise prediction of net flux requires the actual concentration gradient and boundary conditions, since convection can coexist with diffusion in the opposite direction. The numbers are not measured values for a particular plasmodesma.

At the scale of the long-distance conduit, hydraulic resistance is also relevant. For an ideal cylindrical tube of length L and internal radius r carrying a Newtonian fluid with dynamic viscosity η in steady laminar flow, the Hagen–Poiseuille approximation gives Q = πr⁴ΔP/(8ηL). At fixed pressure drop and viscosity, doubling radius increases ideal volume flow sixteenfold. Doubling length halves it. Real sieve tubes have pores and constrictions at sieve plates, changing diameters and sometimes substantial contributions from the plates to total resistance. Therefore the ideal equation teaches sensitivity to dimensions, not a license to calculate exact plant translocation rates from a single radius measurement. In morning glory, Knoblauch and colleagues combined measurements of pressure, geometry and flow-related properties to test whether pressure-driven transport is physically plausible over long distances. The study is important because it addresses a quantitative prediction rather than simply observing that sugar is found in phloem.

The central distinction is between a driving force and a pathway conductance. A favorable pressure gradient does not ensure large volume flow if resistance is high; a large concentration difference does not ensure large solute flux across an impermeable boundary. For exam reasoning, calculate the force and identify the path separately, then predict the direction and approximate magnitude only to the extent justified by the assumptions.

10. Storage tissues: tubers, stems and changing demand

A storage sink has a nutritional role different from that of a meristem. A meristem invests incoming carbon in cell production and associated respiration; a storage tissue can retain substantial quantities of carbon for future use. Many potato tubers accumulate starch in amyloplasts, whereas sugarcane stems can store high concentrations of soluble sucrose. Oil-rich seeds retain a substantial portion of their assimilate carbon in lipids. These products differ chemically, in their osmotic effects, in the metabolic steps required for synthesis and in the way they can later be mobilized. Similar rates of sucrose arrival do not imply that two organs accumulate the same product or dry mass.

A stolon is a modified stem that can form a potato tuber. The growing stolon tip initially undergoes elongation and subsequently changes its developmental program when tuberization begins. A study by Viola and colleagues, published in 2001 in The Plant Cell, compared movement of a fluorescent symplastic marker and radiolabeled assimilates during this transition. The observations supported a developmental change from primarily apoplastic unloading in the elongating stolon to predominantly symplastic unloading in the early swelling tuber. The important conceptual result is that unloading strategy can change within the development of one organ. A student who memorizes that a stem always uses one particular pathway will not be able to explain this observation.

The two labels used in such a study answer related but distinct questions. Carbon derived from a radiolabeled source can reach a destination through routes available to the native compounds into which the isotope becomes incorporated. A fluorescent membrane-impermeant marker in the cytosol can reveal continuity through connections accessible to the marker. When the radiolabel spreads beyond phloem but a suitable fluorescent marker does not, a membrane-separated step becomes a plausible interpretation. When both spread into the same recipient tissues, a continuous symplastic path becomes plausible. However a tracer's physical properties must be considered, and the identity of labeled metabolites must not be assumed solely from the radioactive signal. Evidence from ultrastructure and from biochemical analysis can help discriminate between transport and tracer-specific behavior.

Inside the tuber, sucrose arriving from the phloem enters metabolic networks that produce hexose phosphates and ultimately starch. The conversion of many soluble molecules into a polymer changes the relationship between stored carbon and osmotic particle number. Carbon in starch granules is no longer present as an equivalent concentration of freely dissolved sucrose. Thus starch synthesis can maintain storage capacity without causing the extremely negative osmotic potential that would result if the same quantity of carbon were held entirely as small dissolved molecules in a fixed volume. It also creates a pathway for maintaining low concentrations of particular soluble intermediates, helping sustain the gradients that support import.

Storage does not make an organ independent of respiration. The cells remain alive, maintain membrane gradients, synthesize and repair proteins, transport intermediates and regulate their vacuoles and plastids. A condition that limits oxygen availability may inhibit the capacity to process incoming sugar or energize transport even when the source remains productive. In a prolonged disturbance, the tissue may accumulate some metabolites while synthesis of stored product slows. Such an accumulation should not be interpreted as improved performance of the storage sink. A direct measure of the rate of accumulation of starch or another storage product, preferably expressed in dry mass or carbon equivalents over time, provides information different from a snapshot of total soluble sugar.

The change from storage to mobilization is equally important. During sprouting, a tuber supplies emerging shoots and roots before the young shoot has become a strong photosynthetic source. Enzymatic mobilization makes stored carbon available for export or local respiration. A tissue that has a large carbon pool may fail to act as a source if it cannot mobilize that pool, export the soluble products or maintain viable conducting tissue. The distinction between pool size and flux therefore applies both when reserves are deposited and when they are remobilized.

Sugarcane illustrates another form of storage. High sucrose accumulation in the stem depends on a combination of unloading, transport between cellular compartments, metabolism and retention. Storage of soluble sucrose imposes osmotic consequences that must be accommodated through cellular water relations. The details of unloading and compartmentation vary with developmental state and tissue type; an idealized diagram of a vacuole receiving sugar should not be interpreted as evidence that a single transporter controls every stem compartment. Since sucrose concentration is affected by both the amount of sucrose and tissue water content, comparing the concentration in two stems without considering dry matter and water content may give a misleading estimate of stored carbon per organ.

A practical reasoning exercise concerns two storage organs with equal dry mass gain but different respiration rates. If both were supplied solely by imported carbohydrate and lost no other carbon, the organ with greater respiratory carbon loss must have received more carbon over the measurement interval to achieve the same gain. Thus a higher rate of import is not necessarily associated with higher storage efficiency. Conversely an organ with lower measured import might retain a larger fraction of its imported carbon. One must distinguish the efficiency of transport into the organ from the efficiency of conversion of delivered carbon into harvestable product.

This is why strengthening a particular storage enzyme does not guarantee a corresponding rise in whole-plant yield. When the enzyme is rate limiting, its enhanced activity may increase conversion and sustain demand. If carbon supply, unloading, plastid transport, oxygen availability or another enzyme limits the flux, increasing its activity alone may have little effect. If the strengthened organ draws more carbon from roots or expanding leaves, later source capacity may decline. A whole-plant assessment must therefore include both the intended sink and the remaining organs rather than assuming the targeted organ can be optimized independently.

11. Carbon pools, turnover and the meaning of measured sink activity

A metabolite pool is the amount of a substance within a defined compartment at a given time. A flux is a rate of movement or conversion, expressed as amount per unit time. The distinction is elementary but powerful because many apparent contradictions in plant physiology result from confusing these two quantities. A leaf with a large sucrose pool may have low or high export, depending on its production and removal rates. A sink with little free sucrose may have a vigorous supply if the incoming molecules are quickly metabolized. Concentration also depends on the compartment volume, so neither amount nor concentration by itself measures a flux.

Consider a sink cytosolic sucrose pool S, measured in micromoles. If sucrose import is I, sucrose synthesis within the compartment is Vsyn, metabolism is Vmet and export or sequestration is Vout, a simple accounting relation is dS/dt = I + Vsyn − Vmet − Vout. At a steady state, the left side is zero even though all four rates can be substantial. For an illustrative case, import 12, local synthesis 1, metabolism 8 and sequestration 5 µmol h⁻¹ balance. If metabolism increases from 8 to 10 while the other rates initially remain unchanged, S declines at 2 µmol h⁻¹. The decline may then change membrane transport or trigger adjustments, so the rates cannot be assumed constant indefinitely. The equation is not a complete kinetic model; it states conservation of material in the specified pool.

Steady state is not the same as equilibrium. In an equilibrium system, net processes have no thermodynamic driving force. In a metabolically active cell, continued production, removal and maintenance of ion gradients can sustain a nearly constant pool far from equilibrium. Unloading can be rapid when the sucrose pool remains nearly constant because the flux into it is matched by the flux out of it. This is analogous to the constant water level in a basin with inflow and outflow, but the analogy omits the selective membrane pathways and nonlinear biochemical regulation of a living cell. A useful measurement of turnover requires following labeled material or separately estimating production and consumption.

If a sucrose pool has amount 30 µmol and total removal at steady state is 10 µmol h⁻¹, the apparent residence time defined as pool divided by removal rate is 3 hours. This does not imply that every molecule remains exactly 3 hours. Molecules mix and leave probabilistically, and subcompartments may have different exchange rates. If two sinks maintain the same pool but one has twice the removal flux, it has half this apparent residence time. A short pulse of labeled carbon may be replaced or transferred to downstream products faster in the higher-turnover sink even though the chemical concentration remains similar.

Specific activity has a different meaning in isotope experiments than in the descriptive sink strength equation. In radiochemistry, specific activity often refers to radioactivity per amount of a substance, for example becquerels per micromole. In the sink strength framework, specific sink activity is an uptake or utilization rate per unit mass or other size measure. A question using both concepts must define its terms. If 200 Bq of radiolabeled sucrose is recovered in a tissue, the corresponding amount of labeled plus unlabeled sucrose cannot be calculated without information about the isotopic labeling of that pool and appropriate accounting of radioactive decay and metabolite identity. A ratio of radioactivity between organs is not automatically a ratio of total carbon uptake if the organs were supplied with different proportions of labeled carbon.

The carbon balance of an entire sink can be described using a different control volume. Net gain of carbon in tissue equals imported carbon plus any local carbon fixation, minus exported carbon, respiratory carbon dioxide and other carbon losses. This equation includes all relevant carbon-containing compounds, not only sucrose. Conversion of sucrose into starch is an internal transfer within the organ and does not constitute loss from this larger control volume. By contrast, conversion of organic carbon into carbon dioxide that escapes the organ is a loss. The same reaction may thus be a sink for one metabolite pool without being a loss of total organ carbon.

Suppose an importing organ gains 18 mg carbon per day in its structural and storage compounds. It fixes no carbon locally, exports 2 mg carbon per day to another tissue and respires 6 mg carbon per day; assume all other losses negligible. Its gross import must be 26 mg carbon per day. The fraction of gross import retained as net accumulation is 18/26, approximately 69.2 percent. Another organ gains the same 18 mg but respires 12 mg and exports nothing, requiring 30 mg import per day under its own assumptions. Comparing net accumulation alone would fail to distinguish their gross import. The arithmetic is illustrative and does not imply universal efficiencies of roots or seeds.

A source-side carbon budget similarly separates production from export. In daylight, photosynthetic carbon can enter sucrose synthesis, transient starch or local metabolic pathways. At night, previously accumulated reserves may provide substrate for continued sucrose production and export despite the absence of current photosynthetic fixation. Thus a nocturnal increase or decrease in delivery to a sink depends partly on the source leaf's preceding carbon budget. A short pulse of labeled carbon applied during the day may not uniformly label all reserve pools. Carbon reaching a sink at night could be derived from both recently labeled and earlier unlabeled reserves. Interpret the fraction of label in a sink in the context of the source labeling history.

When assessing sink activity experimentally, the choice of denominator can change the apparent ranking. Expressing import per gram fresh weight can penalize an organ that contains much water, whereas dry mass more directly reflects accumulated material. Expressing uptake per cell may be useful when comparing cell-level activity but misleading if cells differ greatly in size. Expressing import per unit surface area may be appropriate for a membrane interface and less useful for comparing whole organs. Report both total import and the normalized quantity when possible, and identify exactly which biological question the normalization addresses.

12. Environmental regulation of source and sink relationships

Light provides energy for carbon fixation and influences plant development. Decreasing light commonly reduces the amount of carbon available for export, but the timing and distribution of the response depend on the reserves and priorities of the plant. A leaf entering a period of darkness may maintain export for a time using starch accumulated previously. In continuous or repeated low light, reserves can decline and competition between sinks becomes more pronounced. New leaf production can slow, reducing future source capacity. A single measurement of phloem sugar concentration during darkness cannot by itself establish whether total daily allocation to roots has decreased.

Water status influences transport through its effects on both metabolism and the hydraulic system. A moderately water-deficient plant may reduce stomatal conductance, limiting carbon dioxide uptake and therefore carbon fixation. At the same time more negative xylem water potential changes the conditions for water entry into phloem. A plant can accumulate soluble compounds to help maintain turgor, but the concentrations required for osmotic adjustment may affect viscosity and metabolism. Severe dehydration can impair translocation even if sugars are present in source leaves. It would be incorrect to conclude that because sucrose concentration rises in a stressed leaf, more sucrose must be reaching a distant sink. Volume flow and recipient utilization must also be considered.

The sign of a water stress effect on the fraction allocated to roots should not be memorized as an unconditional rule. Under some conditions, shoot expansion declines more strongly than root growth and a greater fraction of available carbon can be retained below ground. Under severe stress, absolute growth and carbon delivery can decrease in both root and shoot. Relative allocation and absolute flux can therefore move in opposite directions. If total carbon available for growth declines from 100 to 50 units per day while the root share rises from 30 to 40 percent, root delivery decreases from 30 to 20 units even though its percentage rises. This is a simple but frequent type of analytical examination trap.

Temperature changes biochemical rates and fluid properties. A reduction in temperature may slow sucrose metabolism and membrane transport in a sink, lowering demand; it may also influence phloem sap viscosity and source photosynthesis. Different tissues and species differ in temperature sensitivity and acclimation capacity. A localized cooling experiment can help distinguish some of these effects. If a recipient organ is cooled while the source leaf remains at its original temperature, the effect on that organ's import may reflect changed recipient metabolism, local transport or local water relations. To establish that a particular enzyme is responsible, additional biochemical or genetic evidence is required. Rewarming and recovery provide useful controls for irreversible damage.

Mineral nutrition influences the demand for carbon skeletons and energy. When roots acquire and assimilate nitrogen, carbon metabolites are needed to support amino acid synthesis and other processes. An association with nitrogen-fixing microbes can create additional carbon demand from symbiotic tissues. Phosphate availability affects energy metabolism and biosynthesis and can influence the pattern of growth. The physiological response to nutrient deficiency involves developmental changes as well as immediate changes in uptake. If phosphate-deficient plants have a greater fraction of biomass in roots, this does not prove that a phosphate transporter directly alters phloem unloading; the change may reflect regulation of root growth or inhibition of shoot growth.

The environmental response can be studied by independently manipulating sources and sinks. A split-root system allows treatment of one portion of the root while another portion experiences a different condition. If a source leaf is pulse-labeled and the treatment changes the distribution of label between root halves, the result provides evidence of differential allocation under the treatment. The system still shares a common shoot, so hormonal signals or changes in total supply may affect both halves. Pairing the labeling result with measurements of root mass, metabolic demand and water status is necessary to distinguish differences in import activity from differences in tissue quantity.

A common misconception is that redistribution toward one stressed organ must be an intentional or optimal allocation. Physiological systems operate through local feedbacks, developmental programs and physical constraints; a change in allocation can be adaptive in a particular context or can arise because another sink has become impaired. A higher percentage of carbon delivered to a surviving organ may reflect the loss of other recipients, not active preferential delivery. To evaluate a proposed adaptive response, compare both absolute and relative fluxes and ask whether the change improves a relevant outcome such as survival or later recovery. The observed partitioning pattern alone does not demonstrate the evolutionary or physiological benefit.

When interpreting a whole-plant response, separate three timescales: immediate changes in water status and transport; intermediate metabolic adjustments; and slower alterations in growth, tissue architecture and source area. Measurements made after different treatment durations can be consistent even if the observed changes have opposite signs. For instance, an initial decline in sink metabolism might cause sugar accumulation upstream, whereas after acclimation the source may produce less sugar and the accumulated pool may decline. The underlying transport defect may persist even when the concentration returns toward its initial value.

13. Experiments that separate loading, transport, unloading and metabolism

One of the earliest ways of testing the role of phloem in assimilate movement was to remove a ring of outer stem tissue while leaving much of the inner xylem intact. In a typical girdling experiment on an appropriate woody stem, the treatment interrupts the phloem connection across the ring. Assimilates can accumulate on the source side of the interruption and recipient tissues beyond the ring may receive less newly fixed carbon. The observation supports a role for phloem in carbon distribution and shows that xylem transport alone does not normally replace it. The experiment does not identify the detailed mechanism of unloading into any particular sink, nor does it by itself prove the pressure-flow hypothesis. The wound also alters local signaling, growth and sometimes water relations.

Interpreting the location of accumulation requires identifying the source and sink. If a mature leaf lies above the girdle and roots below it receive assimilates from that leaf, accumulation can occur above the interruption. But phloem can normally move in the opposite anatomical direction along other conducting strands, from lower mature leaves toward an upper developing shoot. In this configuration the source side of a blockage is determined by actual connectivity and flow, not a universal rule that all sugar accumulates above every blockage. In a complex plant, alternative pathways may allow some delivery around a local treatment.

An aphid inserts its feeding stylet into a vascular plant and can access the pressurized phloem sap. Under suitable conditions, severed stylets have been used to obtain sap samples. Exudation and measured sap properties provide information about the conducting solution and pressure environment. However sampling may select particular conducting elements, and puncture or injury can alter flow. A sample collected from a cut surface can be contaminated by neighboring cells or diluted by other fluids. Therefore phloem sap composition should be interpreted in light of the collection method. The fact that sucrose can be recovered from a stylet does not establish which sink will receive it.

Radiolabeled carbon can be used to follow the products of photosynthetic assimilation. In a pulse-chase experiment, a source leaf receives a limited pulse of labeled carbon dioxide. It fixes some of the carbon into organic compounds. Subsequent exposure to unlabeled carbon dioxide constitutes the chase. Tissue samples collected at different times reveal where the label appears and how it is distributed among soluble sugars, storage products and other fractions. An early arrival time provides information about movement of labeled material, but the measured delay can include fixation, metabolic conversion, loading, axial transport, unloading and detection. The length of the conducting path divided by the arrival delay is consequently not necessarily the true velocity of sap.

Fractionating labeled metabolites is important. After the pulse, radioactivity recovered from a root may be present in sucrose, glucose, amino acids, organic acids or structural and storage materials. An increase in labeled starch in a sink demonstrates that carbon derived from the source has entered that pool, not that starch itself was transported through the sieve tube. If labeled carbon dioxide is released by the sink, respiratory metabolism of labeled substrate has occurred. Recovery of only a portion of the administered label is expected because some remains in source tissues, some is redistributed, and some may leave the plant. Quantitative interpretation requires a defined denominator and appropriate collection of relevant fractions.

For a worked hypothetical example, consider two matched plants given equal short pulses. In control plants after a defined chase, 50 units of radioactivity are recovered in the source, 25 in roots, 15 in developing leaves and 10 in respiration or measured other pools. In treated plants the corresponding values are 65, 10, 15 and 10. The most direct interpretation is reduced appearance of label in roots with greater source retention. The unchanged leaf fraction suggests that root delivery is more affected than leaf delivery under this treatment, but the data do not identify the precise step. A root-specific unloading defect, a branch-specific axial transport defect or reduced root demand could all produce related patterns. Additional measurements of labeled carbon in the root conducting tissue and adjacent cells help discriminate them.

Fluorescent probes can complement isotope experiments because they provide spatial information at cellular resolution. A marker that cannot freely cross intact plasma membranes after activation may trace cytoplasmic continuity. If it appears in sink parenchyma after arriving in a sieve tube, a symplastic connection becomes a candidate route. If a native assimilate arrives beyond the phloem while the marker remains in the conducting cells, either an apoplastic step or selective restriction of the marker may explain the discrepancy. The two probes must be considered jointly, and a negative fluorescent signal should be distinguished from a signal below the detection threshold.

Genetic perturbations can be designed to affect candidate transporters in particular cell types. A mutant lacking a sucrose transporter may display stunted roots or underfilled seeds. Such a phenotype is suggestive but cannot establish whether the protein normally functions in loading or unloading. The gene may be expressed in multiple tissues, and the mutant may have secondary developmental defects. Complementation with expression restricted to a relevant cell population can give stronger evidence about the location of function. If restoring transporter activity at the recipient interface improves filling while restoration only in mature source leaves does not, the result supports a recipient-side requirement, provided expression and activity are adequately established.

A combination of biochemical, anatomical and physiological measurements is often more informative than any individual assay. Transporter localization indicates where a protein may act; a transport assay establishes its capacity for substrate movement; tracer movement indicates in vivo distribution; and mutant or perturbation phenotypes test its necessity under specified conditions. A correlation between transporter abundance and sink growth cannot alone establish a causal role, since growth itself may induce transporter expression. The strongest examination answer identifies which result establishes each part of the proposed mechanism and states what remains uncertain.

14. How sink enzymes and transporters work together

Unloading does not end when sucrose crosses the plasma membrane of a recipient cell. The continued use of imported material depends on the sequence of reactions and compartment transfers that follows entry. A membrane pathway can deliver substrate only if the neighboring metabolic system can accommodate it. Likewise an enzyme may have a high catalytic capacity in a laboratory extract but make little contribution to carbon movement in an intact organ if its substrate is not delivered to the appropriate compartment. The question is not whether transport or metabolism is more important in all plants. It is which step limits a particular flux under a particular set of conditions.

Consider the three common locations of invertase activity. Cell-wall invertase acts in the apoplast and can alter the chemical composition of the extracellular solution encountered by membrane transporters. Vacuolar invertase acts on sucrose after it has entered the vacuole, affecting vacuolar glucose, fructose and sucrose pools. Cytosolic invertase acts within the cytosol, where sucrose also participates in sucrose synthase reactions and other metabolic pathways. All three hydrolyze sucrose into glucose and fructose, but their substrates reside on different sides of membranes. An increase in the activity of vacuolar invertase cannot be assumed to lower cell-wall sucrose concentration directly. The latter effect would depend on intermediate transport and metabolic interactions.

Hydrolysis in the apoplast illustrates how an enzyme can support a transport gradient. If sucrose is delivered from a donor cell into the extracellular space and invertase converts it into hexoses, the extracellular sucrose pool may remain relatively low. A facilitative sucrose exporter can then continue outward movement as long as the chemical potential in the donor remains higher. For sustained operation, the products of hydrolysis must also be removed or otherwise accommodated. If glucose and fructose build up outside the cells, water relations change, and the uptake and metabolism of those products can become limiting. Cell-wall invertase does not act as a substitute for recipient hexose transporters.

In a contrasting apoplastic route, sucrose itself can be absorbed by a proton-coupled transporter without prior extracellular hydrolysis. The existence of a large hexose pool in a recipient tissue does not necessarily identify which route was used. Imported sucrose can subsequently be hydrolyzed inside the recipient cell. Conversely hexoses generated outside may be taken up and metabolically converted back into sucrose or other carbohydrates. To distinguish these possibilities, measure extracellular sugars, use cell-specific enzyme localization and follow the earliest changes in labeled metabolites. A total sugar assay performed hours after transport is not sufficient to locate the original cleavage reaction.

Sucrose synthase differs chemically from invertase. In the cleavage direction its cosubstrate is UDP, and its products are UDP-glucose and fructose. The activated sugar nucleotide can be used for biosynthetic reactions. Invertase uses water and releases free glucose rather than UDP-glucose. The enzyme names can be misleading to a beginner: sucrose synthase is reversible and can operate in cleavage under many sink conditions, whereas sucrose-phosphate synthase belongs to a distinct pathway of sucrose biosynthesis. A student's prediction about carbon routing should be based on substrates, products and compartments instead of the apparent implication of the enzyme name.

An idealized enzyme-limited model provides a useful example. Let a sink receive substrate at an extracellular interface, and suppose the enzyme that consumes it has rate v = Vmax S/(Km + S), where S denotes substrate concentration, Km is the concentration giving half the maximum rate and Vmax is the rate at saturation. These parameters describe the simplified Michaelis–Menten relation under its assumptions. A sink enzyme with Vmax = 20 µmol h⁻¹ and Km = 5 mM would operate at 10 µmol h⁻¹ at 5 mM and approximately 16 µmol h⁻¹ at 20 mM. Increasing concentration fourfold from 5 to 20 mM increases the rate only 1.6-fold. The rate approaches the maximum as the enzyme becomes saturated. If the source can supply at most 8 µmol h⁻¹ to this compartment, however, the steady-state throughput cannot exceed 8 solely because the enzyme is capable of a higher rate.

To connect this model with a living plant, recognize that the measured Vmax may depend on total enzyme quantity, the proportion of enzyme in an active form and the conditions of the assay. Invertase activity can be regulated by protein inhibitors and by the chemical conditions within its compartment. Alterations in enzyme abundance need not translate proportionally into changes in net carbon flux. The behavior of a pathway containing multiple steps can be particularly insensitive to an increase in the capacity of a step that already exceeds the throughput of the rest of the system. This explains why a correlation between enzyme activity and sink strength in one developmental stage does not guarantee that raising that enzyme's activity in another stage will strengthen the sink.

Evidence from the maize Miniature1 locus illustrates the physiological significance of a cell-specific sucrose-cleaving enzyme. The study identifying Miniature1 with a cell-wall invertase expressed in the endosperm found that sufficiently low enzyme activity impaired normal kernel development and affected associated maternal tissues. This links an enzyme at a recipient interface with the development of tissues supplied through that interface. The observation does not establish a universal one-to-one proportional relationship between invertase activity and kernel mass. It supports an important role for the enzyme under the conditions of that genetic system, including a range in which a severe reduction limits development.

The functional interpretation of such a mutant can be strengthened by separating enzyme activity from the developmental stage of the tissue. For example, compare kernels at equivalent days after pollination and document cell number, tissue size and the anatomical localization of the enzyme. If the mutant contains fewer recipient cells, total enzyme activity might decline partly because less tissue is present. Measuring enzyme activity per appropriate tissue unit and examining early developmental stages can help determine whether the activity change precedes the growth defect. A genetic rescue that restores enzyme function in the relevant recipient region provides another line of evidence. No single endpoint measurement proves every step of the causal chain.

At the tonoplast, transport across the vacuolar membrane influences whether imported carbon is immediately accessible to cytosolic metabolism. Sequestering a soluble sugar in a vacuole may support long-term accumulation while limiting the cytosolic pool; remobilizing it has the opposite potential. The vacuolar lumen can maintain a composition different from that of the cytosol because the membrane contains selective transport proteins and electrochemical gradients. Treating the whole cell as one perfectly mixed compartment obscures this regulation. In a sweet fruit, the concentration in expressed juice may predominantly reflect vacuolar contents and need not equal the sucrose concentration at the plasma membrane interface where uptake takes place.

Finally, the stoichiometry of carbohydrate transformations must be distinguished from the stoichiometry of transport. One mole of sucrose contains twelve moles of carbon atoms. Complete hydrolysis yields one mole each of glucose and fructose, each containing six moles of carbon atoms. The number of dissolved sugar molecules doubles if both products remain dissolved, while the number of carbon atoms is conserved. In a simple ideal solution of fixed volume, this increases the osmotic contribution of the sugar pool. But if one of the hexoses is rapidly taken up or metabolized, the extracellular osmotic effect may not approach the idealized doubling. A quantitative solution should state what is held constant and what biological processes have been omitted.

15. Mobile signals, sugar sensing and developmental feedback

A sieve tube carries more than nutritional carbohydrate. Amino acids and other low-molecular-weight compounds are important transported substances, and particular proteins and RNAs can also move through the phloem. These molecules may influence growth and development in distant tissues. Transport of a signaling molecule should be understood as a separate functional category from transfer of bulk nutritional carbon, although the two functions share part of the vascular infrastructure. A signal may alter the demand for assimilate without supplying a meaningful fraction of the carbon needed to construct the responding organ.

One example is florigen, the mobile floral induction signal. Under inductive photoperiodic conditions in Arabidopsis, leaf vascular tissues express FLOWERING LOCUS T, usually abbreviated FT. The protein participates in communication with the shoot apex. At the apex it acts with transcriptional regulatory partners to promote the transition toward flowering. A leaf that perceives an appropriate day-length regime can therefore influence developmental events in an organ spatially separate from it. The detailed timing mechanisms belong to sensory photobiology and developmental biology; the relevant feature here is transport between a leaf and the apex and the subsequent alteration of the plant's distribution of growth demands.

Experiments on FT helped distinguish between several possibilities. A gene could be expressed in the leaf and produce a protein that moves to the apex. Alternatively its transcript could move and be translated in the apex. A further possibility is that expression in the leaf triggers a different mobile signal. Corbesier and colleagues provided evidence supporting protein movement in the induction process. A well-designed movement experiment must distinguish material actually transported from material synthesized locally after another signal arrives. Tissue-specific gene expression, suitable labeling of the product and tests of activity in recipient tissue can contribute to this distinction. The activity of a tagged protein must be evaluated because the tag can affect mobility or function.

A second example involves a small regulatory RNA. The microRNA miR399 participates in regulation of phosphate homeostasis in Arabidopsis. During phosphate deficiency, its abundance increases in appropriate tissues, and it can function as a mobile signal between shoot and root. PHO2, which encodes a protein involved in regulation of phosphate transport and homeostasis, is an important target. Experiments including grafting have supported long-distance movement and a functional response in the recipient. The importance for this chapter is that phloem transfer can communicate information about nutrient status from one part of the plant to another. The signal changes nutrient handling rather than simply adding phosphate to the recipient tissue.

A grafting experiment physically joins the shoot of one plant, the scion, to the root system of another, the rootstock. Once vascular connections become functional, a molecule originating in one genotype can potentially be detected in the other. In an informative design, a donor produces a distinguishable form of the candidate molecule, while the recipient does not express that form. Detection of the donor-specific molecular identity in the recipient helps establish movement. Demonstration of a recipient response related to the candidate strengthens the functional interpretation. Controls include grafts between nonproducing genotypes and tests for contamination of recipient samples by donor tissue. An altered recipient phenotype alone is insufficient because another mobile molecule could be responsible.

The term mobile should not be applied indiscriminately to all RNA or protein species detected in a phloem sample. Some material may originate from damaged cells during sample collection. Even genuinely transported molecules can be passengers without an established signaling function. Conversely a signal with major physiological effects may be present in small quantities. The evidence needed to establish a signal's role includes not just detection in the conducting sap but also a source, transport, delivery to the appropriate target and a specific functional effect. This is an important distinction between the transport of selected macromolecules described in the syllabus and unsupported claims that every cell has unrestricted access to every phloem macromolecule.

Sugar itself can convey information about carbon availability. A growing cell must coordinate energy use and biosynthesis with supply, because producing expensive structures when carbon is scarce can impair viability. Conversely when suitable carbon, nutrients and developmental signals are available, anabolic pathways can support expansion and division. Sucrose and its metabolites participate in regulatory networks that affect these decisions. A useful example is trehalose 6-phosphate, a metabolic intermediate whose abundance is related to sucrose status in many plant contexts. It helps connect carbon availability with growth and metabolic regulation. Its relationship to sucrose is regulatory, not a statement that it replaces sucrose as the major long-distance carbohydrate.

SnRK1 is a protein kinase involved in regulation of energy and carbon metabolism, particularly under conditions of low energy or carbon availability. Trehalose 6-phosphate can influence SnRK1 activity in appropriate tissues and contexts. The TOR kinase network, in turn, participates in growth regulation in response to favorable nutritional and environmental conditions. At NET and SET level, the essential concept is that sink metabolism responds to carbon status through regulatory mechanisms as well as by simple substrate availability. It is not necessary to assign every downstream phosphorylation event to this chapter. The fact that sucrose concentration and trehalose 6-phosphate often correlate does not make the relationship independent of developmental stage, compartment or other regulatory signals.

This connection clarifies why an external supply of sucrose can have effects that are not explained entirely by carbon nutrition. In a controlled growth experiment, increasing sugar might both provide a metabolic substrate and alter regulatory pathways. To distinguish these possibilities, compare the response with appropriate osmotic controls and examine metabolic and signaling readouts separately. An osmotically similar compound that cannot be metabolized in the same way may help test whether the effect is merely due to water potential, but it must itself be chosen carefully because plants can transport or sense several sugar-like molecules. A molecular perturbation of a signaling component can provide an additional test, provided general viability is not compromised.

Developmental hormones interact with these nutritional responses. Cytokinin can affect cell division and developmental activity; auxin participates in organ formation and branching; ABA can influence growth and water relations. The demands of a developing organ arise from its program of division, expansion, differentiation and reserve accumulation. Therefore a hormone that alters the number or activity of growing cells can change total sink demand even if it does not directly modify a sucrose transporter. In some cases hormone action also changes expression or activity of transport or metabolic proteins. The interpretation should specify whether a direct effect on the unloading interface has been demonstrated or whether the observed allocation shift is secondary to changes in growth.

The onset of flowering, fruit set and seed filling creates new demands on mature source leaves. However a mature source does not necessarily export a fixed amount of carbon regardless of those demands. Changes in sugar utilization downstream can alter leaf carbohydrate status and feed back on photosynthetic metabolism, reserve allocation and export. This makes the source–sink system a regulated network. A demand signal can increase the potential to use assimilate, but physical transport capacity, water supply and source productivity still impose limits. A signal that promotes a developmental transition cannot create the carbon or nitrogen required for that new growth.

16. Branching vascular networks and competition among sinks

The single-source, single-sink model is useful for learning the pressure-flow mechanism, but an actual plant has a branched vascular system. Many conducting strands run in parallel and connect particular source regions to particular sink regions. Anatomical connections are not equivalent merely because two organs lie at the same distance from a leaf. A leaf trace may connect preferentially to a particular sector of the stem and to organs supplied through that sector. Anastomoses, where connections exist between pathways, may allow redistribution. Consequently distance is one determinant of resistance, not the sole determinant of destination.

A simplified parallel-path model clarifies the distinction. Suppose two sink pathways share a common upstream phloem pressure of 0.80 MPa. Sink A is at 0.30 MPa and has an effective hydraulic resistance of 0.10 MPa h µL⁻¹. Sink B is at 0.40 MPa and has resistance of 0.20 MPa h µL⁻¹. The illustrative volume flows are QA = (0.80 − 0.30)/0.10 = 5 µL h⁻¹ and QB = (0.80 − 0.40)/0.20 = 2 µL h⁻¹. A receives more sap despite the fact that both paths originate at the same pressure. If concentrations were identical, the ratio of advective carbon delivery would equal the flow ratio, 5:2. If their concentrations differed, delivery ratios would have to include those differences.

In a real plant the upstream pressure would not necessarily remain fixed when one branch changes. It depends on source loading, water exchange and outflow to all accessible sinks. If sink A reduces its unloading capacity, pressure at the shared junction may change and some flow may be redirected. Alternatively the source may reduce its export. A prediction based on a fixed upstream pressure is valid only when the question supplies that assumption or when the experimental conditions justify it. A more realistic network calculation would simultaneously solve water and solute balances at its junctions. The simple parallel model is valuable for showing why sink demand must be considered with path resistance.

Assimilate competition can therefore arise without any direct inhibition between the two sink organs. Both depend on a shared upstream supply or shared conducting segment. When one recipient increases import, the other may receive less if the shared supply is limited. Removing one sink can relieve the constraint and permit greater delivery to the remaining sinks. But the effect may be small if the removed organ was connected to a different source sector, if the remaining sink has reached its metabolic limit or if the source responds by decreasing export. A whole-plant carbon budget supplies necessary constraints; vascular anatomy and physiology determine which possible allocation is realized.

A fruit-thinning experiment exemplifies these considerations. Removing some developing fruits often decreases competition among remaining fruits. If carbon availability was limiting, the remaining fruits may accumulate more dry matter per organ. Yet total fruit yield might decline even while the average fruit becomes larger. Consider a hypothetical plant bearing ten fruits of 40 g dry mass each, for a total of 400 g. If thinning leaves six fruits and each reaches 60 g, the total is 360 g. Average dry mass rises by 50 percent, but total fruit dry mass declines by 10 percent. A conclusion about commercial or biological benefit must distinguish per-organ size from total yield and consider fruit quality, resource use and the timescale of production.

The converse manipulation, increasing the number of sinks, can sometimes increase total harvested biomass even if individual organs are smaller. For a plant with spare source capacity, additional active sinks may sustain photosynthesis or redirect assimilate that otherwise accumulates in nonharvested tissues. At greater sink number, the capacity of the source or transport system may become limiting. The resulting relationship between organ number and total yield can be nonlinear. It is inappropriate to infer a universal optimum fruit number from a theoretical model without specifying source area, light, developmental stage, environmental conditions and the particular crop.

Partitioning also changes when source organs are manipulated. Partial shading can reduce fixation in selected leaves while other leaves continue to assimilate carbon. Changes in export from the shaded leaf can be partly compensated by unshaded leaves, but the capacity to substitute depends on vascular connection and time. A label applied to one unshaded leaf may appear in a different combination of recipient organs after shading of neighboring leaves. Such observations can help map functional connectivity. They do not necessarily mean that the plant constructed new vascular pathways during the short experimental interval; the relative pressure gradients and use of existing connections may have changed.

When two labels are applied to different source leaves, each can supply a different set of sinks, with possible overlap. For example, if source A predominantly labels upper leaves and source B predominantly labels roots, that pattern could reflect vascular sectoriality and the positions of active sinks. To determine whether the same individual sieve tube carries sap simultaneously in opposite directions, however, measurements at the organ level are inadequate. Bulk flow at a cross section of an individual tube has one net direction at a particular instant in the simple pressure-flow picture. Adjacent tubes can sustain different directions, and an individual tube's direction may change over time if the driving gradient reverses.

The question of source and sink competition also involves export along the transport pathway. A stem is not simply an inert pipe. Living cells surrounding the phloem can require carbohydrate for maintenance and growth, and assimilates may leave the conducting system and be retrieved by nearby transporters. Local exchange can influence the amount of material reaching more distant recipients. The classification of a stem as transport phloem does not imply that every sucrose molecule entering its conducting system reaches an endpoint unchanged. Some material may be consumed or temporarily stored during passage, and some may be returned to the transport stream. In an isotope experiment, this can produce delayed appearance in a distant sink or distribution to tissues along the route.

An effective examination strategy is to identify the control volume and the network topology before applying conservation. A single tube may be treated as one pathway with a pressure difference; a branch junction requires conservation of inflow and outflow; a whole organ requires inclusion of local production, consumption and storage. These are compatible descriptions at different scales. Confusion arises when a quantity such as sucrose concentration from one scale is used as if it were a measure of whole-organ import from another scale.

17. Predicting physiological outcomes from integrated evidence

The entire transport system can be understood through a diagnostic sequence. Begin by asking whether the source can produce the relevant assimilate. Then determine whether it can load the assimilate into conducting cells. Next examine whether the axial path delivers the material to the recipient region. At the sink, distinguish the first exit step from subsequent movement to the target cell. Finally examine uptake, metabolism, storage and the growth response. Each stage can limit delivery or alter a measured phenotype. This sequence is more useful than assigning every weakly growing organ the same diagnosis of low sink strength.

For example, a mutation that decreases sucrose loading in all mature leaves can reduce carbohydrate delivery to roots, young leaves and reproductive organs simultaneously. In the absence of compensatory routes, sugars may accumulate in source tissues while sinks become carbon limited. But similar accumulation could occur when many sink organs are removed or fail to metabolize imported carbon. To discriminate, measure the appearance of label in source phloem early after fixation. A loading defect is expected to reduce entry into the conducting system even when sink demand is maintained. A downstream defect may initially permit entry but reduce sustained export through altered pressure and feedback. The precise prediction depends on time and the severity of the perturbation.

A second situation concerns a mutant with reduced membrane uptake in the maternal-to-filial transfer pathway of a seed. Leaves retain normal photosynthetic capacity, and developing seeds receive labeled carbon in maternal tissue, but embryo accumulation is low. This pattern localizes the likely limitation downstream of axial delivery. If extracellular sucrose builds up and the relevant recipient uptake protein is absent, a membrane influx defect becomes plausible. If maternal cytosolic starch accumulates and the extracellular sugar supply is low, inadequate export from maternal tissue becomes plausible. If sucrose enters the embryo normally but labeled reserve products remain low, a metabolic defect within filial tissue becomes plausible. Each prediction requires compartment-resolved measurements, not simply a whole-seed assay.

One can turn these distinctions into a quantitative model. Consider a pathway with a potential source supply of 30 µmol h⁻¹, an axial transport capacity of 22, an unloading interface capacity of 14 and a metabolic utilization capacity of 25. In a simplified serial-capacity approximation with no accumulation at steady state, throughput cannot exceed the smallest capacity, 14 µmol h⁻¹. If an intervention raises metabolic utilization to 35 while unloading stays at 14, the limiting capacity is unchanged. If unloading capacity rises to 24, axial transport at 22 now becomes the minimum. This is not a full kinetic description; real rates are coupled through concentrations and feedback and several steps can share control. It nevertheless prevents the mistake of assuming that enhancement of any downstream enzyme must increase whole-path throughput.

Suppose instead a treatment alters the permeability of plasmodesmata in a root recipient zone. A short-term tracer experiment shows that a small dye arrives normally in the protophloem but moves into adjacent recipient cells at half the control rate. A larger protein probe fails to move detectably under either condition. The small dye result supports a treatment-dependent restriction at or near the unloading zone. The protein result alone does not establish whether the treatment changed protein transport, because the assay did not detect movement even in controls. To infer size selectivity, use probes with suitable properties and sufficient detection sensitivity, and compare their distribution after equivalent delivery into the conducting system.

A third scenario involves a nutrient or water treatment that changes the relative amount of carbon distributed to two recipient organs. The result can be interpreted only after knowing whether the numbers describe percentage of recovered label, absolute label amount, carbon flux, fresh mass or dry mass. Consider a labeled leaf that provides 40 units of recovered label to roots in one treatment and 30 in another. If total recovered label in all sinks is 100 and 60 units respectively, the root fractions are 40 and 50 percent. The root share rises, but the root label amount falls. A claim that roots imported more carbon would require evidence about total labeled input and actual carbon flux, which the percentage alone does not supply. Even when the labeled amount falls, the total unlabeled flux could behave differently if the specific labeling of delivered sucrose has changed.

Assessing plant productivity introduces a further distinction between photosynthetic efficiency, transport efficiency and allocation efficiency. A leaf can fix more carbon per unit light without delivering a greater proportion of that carbon to reproductive organs. A recipient may receive a large fraction of exported assimilate but convert relatively little of it to harvestable product because maintenance respiration is high. A plant with strong reproductive sinks may allocate relatively little to roots, potentially reducing later nutrient acquisition. These tradeoffs explain why a single physiological measurement is not a comprehensive description of crop performance. An investigation should measure the relevant combination of source production, delivery, retention and whole-plant growth.

The harvest index is commonly expressed as harvested economic yield divided by total relevant biomass, often using dry matter and an explicitly defined aboveground biomass denominator for grain crops. It describes an allocation outcome rather than an instantaneous phloem flux. For example, if a plant produces 120 g of aboveground dry matter including 48 g of grain, the harvest index is 0.40. A second plant with 100 g aboveground dry matter and the same 48 g grain has a higher harvest index of 0.48 despite identical grain yield. Improving harvest index and improving absolute yield are therefore distinct goals. A change in index does not prove that phloem unloading was the primary cause.

At the examination level, a mechanistic explanation must match the strength of the evidence. If a manipulation reduces sink growth but no transport measurement is provided, the justified conclusion is a growth impairment with several possible causes. If a cell-specific perturbation changes sugar movement across a defined interface while upstream delivery is unchanged, a localized transport role becomes more convincing. If physiological rescue restores both flux and growth, the chain of evidence becomes stronger still. A comprehensive answer should state the most likely mechanism, the observations supporting it and a test that would discriminate it from the main alternative explanation.

The transport and allocation processes considered here also connect the preceding plant physiology chapters with specialized metabolism and stress physiology. Photosynthesis provides carbon compounds, respiratory pathways supply energy, nitrogen assimilation integrates carbon skeletons with mineral nutrition, and hormonal and light signals influence the developmental identity and activity of organs. Water potential and membrane transport establish the physical framework for their distribution. Specialized metabolites and stress responses can create additional demands on the same carbon and energy resources. The student who can trace these connections is prepared to reason across the syllabus without confusing general transport mechanisms with the specific biochemical pathways that are taught elsewhere.

The vascular cambium and its differentiating derivatives represent another important recipient of assimilate in woody stems and roots. Cell division, expansion, synthesis of cellulose and other wall materials, and in many derivatives deposition of lignin require carbon and metabolic energy. The stem can therefore be a route for long-distance transport and an active sink for lateral growth at the same time. During a season of vigorous cambial activity, delivery to developing wood and bark may contribute a substantial portion of the whole-plant carbon budget. At other times, some living stem tissues store carbohydrates that can later be mobilized. If a pulse of labeled carbon appears in differentiating xylem cells, it does not follow that the labeled carbohydrate was carried there primarily in the ascending transpiration stream; transfer from nearby phloem into cambial and differentiating tissues is also a plausible route. Distinguish the identity of the recipient tissue from the conduit responsible for long-distance delivery.

Stable carbon isotopes provide a complementary tracing strategy. Carbon dioxide enriched in carbon-13 can be supplied to a source leaf and the subsequent enrichment of tissues or identified metabolites measured by appropriate mass spectrometric methods. Unlike carbon-14, carbon-13 is stable rather than radioactive. Naturally occurring carbon-13 creates a background abundance, so interpretation depends on enrichment relative to suitable unlabeled controls. If two tissues have the same isotope enrichment but different total carbon amounts, they do not necessarily contain the same quantity of newly labeled carbon. To estimate labeled-carbon distribution, account for both pool size and the degree of enrichment. If the question supplies atom-fraction excess, multiplying it by total carbon amount estimates excess carbon-13 in that pool. The value does not directly establish gross import unless source enrichment, turnover, losses and other inputs are known. The experimental logic remains the same across radioactive and stable labels: identify the molecule or carbon pool measured, track its origin and follow the relevant time interval.

The complete plant transport system contains two principal vascular conduits with contrasting properties. Soil water and dissolved mineral ions enter roots and cross selective living tissues before reaching xylem. Evaporation from leaves helps establish the tension that drives water through the xylem. In a photosynthetic source leaf, part of the fixed carbon is converted to transportable carbohydrates and loaded into the living phloem. Local exchange of water between xylem, phloem and surrounding cells influences the pressure that drives assimilate movement. In recipient regions, unloading transfers sugars and selected other compounds out of the sieve element system. Recipient metabolism and local water relations determine how the delivered materials support growth, respiration or reserve accumulation. The two vascular streams are coupled through water and tissue physiology, but they are not a single closed circulation equivalent to an animal blood vascular system.

Macromolecular mobility within the living plant must also be distinguished from absorption from soil. Roots normally acquire mineral nutrients in appropriate ionic or small-molecule forms through selective interfaces. The ability of particular RNA or protein molecules to move between connected plant cells or through the phloem does not establish unrestricted uptake of intact large soil biomolecules into the xylem and delivery to every tissue. The molecular identity, access to a particular membrane or plasmodesmatal route and evidence of selective passage determine which transfer can be claimed. This distinction resolves a common ambiguity in broad syllabus wording about transport of water, ions, solutes and macromolecules: their movements are linked in plant physiology, but not all classes of substance follow every route.

18. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. A leaf is always a source.

Correct concept: A developing leaf can import more carbon than it exports.

Why the mistake occurs: Students associate chloroplasts with net export.

Exam reasoning: Compare fixation, respiration and accumulation before assigning its role.

2. A root is always a sink.

Correct concept: A storage root can mobilize reserves and act as a source during renewed growth.

Why the mistake occurs: The usual role of growing roots is generalized across developmental stages.

Exam reasoning: Classify by net exchange at the stated developmental stage.

3. Phloem always moves sugar downward.

Correct concept: Flow follows pressure differences along connected tubes; different tubes may carry sap in different directions.

Why the mistake occurs: Textbook diagrams often show a leaf above a root.

Exam reasoning: Identify the source, sink and connected conducting pathway.

4. High sucrose concentration proves high import.

Correct concept: Concentration is a pool property influenced by delivery, consumption and volume.

Why the mistake occurs: A large measured pool seems to imply rapid arrival.

Exam reasoning: Measure transport rate and account for removal.

5. All unloading is diffusion.

Correct concept: Symplastic transport can involve convection as well as diffusion.

Why the mistake occurs: Plasmodesmata are introduced as channels for intercellular diffusion.

Exam reasoning: Consider both concentration and pressure-driven flux.

6. All unloading requires direct ATP hydrolysis.

Correct concept: Different steps can be passive, facilitative or secondarily active.

Why the mistake occurs: Metabolism-dependent import is equated with a direct ATP pump.

Exam reasoning: Specify the transporter or pathway and its immediate energy source.

7. SWEET always pumps sugar outward.

Correct concept: Facilitated net movement depends on the solute chemical potential gradient.

Why the mistake occurs: The name is encountered in outward-loading examples.

Exam reasoning: Determine the concentrations and the transporter location.

8. SUT directly hydrolyses ATP.

Correct concept: Proton-coupled uptake uses the proton electrochemical gradient maintained by separate pumps.

Why the mistake occurs: The term active transport obscures the primary/secondary distinction.

Exam reasoning: Trace ATP hydrolysis to H⁺-ATPase and proton entry to the symporter.

9. Invertase and sucrose synthase produce the same products.

Correct concept: Invertase produces free glucose and fructose; sucrose synthase uses UDP to produce UDP-glucose and fructose in the cleavage direction.

Why the mistake occurs: Both enzymes are described as sucrose-cleaving.

Exam reasoning: Write the reaction including cosubstrates before predicting metabolic fates.

10. All invertase activity has the same transport effect.

Correct concept: Cell-wall, cytosolic and vacuolar isoforms act on different pools.

Why the mistake occurs: Whole-tissue enzyme assays hide compartmentation.

Exam reasoning: Locate the enzyme relative to the membrane interface.

11. The maternal seed coat and embryo form one continuous symplast.

Correct concept: A maternal-to-filial transfer includes an apoplastic boundary.

Why the mistake occurs: A developing seed looks like a single integrated organ.

Exam reasoning: Identify which tissues belong to distinct generations and membrane domains.

12. A large sink necessarily has greater sink strength.

Correct concept: Total realized activity depends on size and specific activity, as well as source supply.

Why the mistake occurs: Mass is easier to observe than per-mass uptake.

Exam reasoning: Multiply compatible size and activity values.

13. Label found in starch proves starch was translocated.

Correct concept: Source-derived labeled carbon can be metabolized into starch at the sink.

Why the mistake occurs: Isotope identity is confused with compound identity.

Exam reasoning: Separate the labeled atom from the chemical species carrying it.

14. Higher percentage allocation means higher absolute delivery.

Correct concept: A greater fraction can accompany a lower total amount.

Why the mistake occurs: Percentages are mistaken for amounts.

Exam reasoning: Multiply each fraction by its own total.

15. All phloem proteins and RNAs are functional signals.

Correct concept: Transport, arrival and functional response must each be demonstrated.

Why the mistake occurs: Detection in sap is mistaken for evidence of biological function.

Exam reasoning: Require controls for contamination, tissue-specific origin and recipient action.

16. Larger fruits always mean higher total fruit yield.

Correct concept: Fruit number and mass per fruit jointly determine total yield.

Why the mistake occurs: Per-organ performance is treated as whole-plant productivity.

Exam reasoning: Calculate number × dry mass per organ.

17. A successful pressure-flow model predicts every sink's allocation from concentration alone.

Correct concept: Hydraulic resistance, water potential, connectivity and sink metabolism also matter.

Why the mistake occurs: Osmotic loading is simplified into a universal sugar-gradient rule.

Exam reasoning: Calculate hydrostatic pressure differences and identify the branch pathway.

18. Starch formation destroys the carbon imported into a sink.

Correct concept: It transfers carbon into an internal polymer pool; respiration can release carbon from the organ.

Why the mistake occurs: Disappearance of soluble sucrose is confused with carbon loss.

Exam reasoning: Choose a defined control volume and distinguish conversion from export or respiration.

19. Original examination practice

All questions are original teaching exercises. Numerical datasets and values in the problems are illustrative unless explicitly identified as published observations.

Part B — foundations and mechanisms

1. Which definition identifies a carbon sink over a specified interval?

  1. Any organ below ground
  2. An organ containing a high sucrose concentration
  3. A tissue that imports more assimilate than it exports
  4. A tissue lacking chloroplasts
Answer and explanation

C. A tissue that imports more assimilate than it exports. The defining variable is net import. Roots are often sinks, but a sprouting storage organ can be a source. A photosynthetic young leaf can be a net importer. Sugar concentration is a pool measurement, not the direction of net transfer.

2. What directly drives axial bulk flow in the Münch model?

  1. A hydrostatic pressure difference along the sieve tube
  2. A sucrose carrier at each sieve plate
  3. Negative xylem pressure acting as a direct suction on sucrose
  4. The direction of gravity
Answer and explanation

A. A hydrostatic pressure difference along the sieve tube. The hydrostatic pressure gradient drives solution flow through the conducting pathway. Osmotic gradients help generate pressure differences, but carriers are not needed at each sieve plate. Phloem flow can be upward or downward.

3. A solute passes directly through plasmodesmata between two living cells. This is:

  1. Primary active transport
  2. Symplastic transport
  3. Xylem loading
  4. Apoplastic transport
Answer and explanation

B. Symplastic transport. Plasmodesmata connect cytoplasmic domains. Apoplastic transport includes a membrane-separated extracellular step. Passage through a connection need not involve direct ATP hydrolysis.

4. Which statement best describes a SWEET sucrose transporter in a documented efflux pathway?

  1. It facilitates sucrose movement down its chemical potential gradient
  2. It always exports sucrose even against its gradient
  3. It directly hydrolyses ATP to pump sucrose
  4. It is a sieve plate pore
Answer and explanation

A. It facilitates sucrose movement down its chemical potential gradient. SWEET transporters can mediate facilitated sugar transfer; the gradient determines net direction. Proton-coupled sucrose uptake and ATP-dependent proton pumping are distinct mechanisms.

5. The immediate energy source for uptake by a proton-coupled sucrose symporter is:

  1. Hydrolysis of sucrose
  2. Hydrolysis of ATP by the sucrose symporter
  3. The proton electrochemical gradient
  4. A xylem pressure pulse
Answer and explanation

C. The proton electrochemical gradient. Downhill proton movement supplies free energy for coupled sucrose uptake. A separate H⁺-ATPase helps maintain this gradient. The symporter itself is not a primary ATPase.

6. Cell-wall invertase converts sucrose into:

  1. Glucose and fructose
  2. Two molecules of glucose
  3. Starch and fructose
  4. UDP-glucose and fructose
Answer and explanation

A. Glucose and fructose. Invertase hydrolyses sucrose using water to produce one glucose and one fructose. UDP-glucose is a product of the sucrose synthase cleavage reaction, not invertase.

7. Which cosubstrate is used in the cleavage direction of sucrose synthase?

  1. UDP
  2. O₂
  3. NADPH
  4. ATP
Answer and explanation

A. UDP. Sucrose synthase catalyses the reversible reaction sucrose + UDP ↔ UDP-glucose + fructose. ATP and oxygen participate elsewhere in metabolism, not as cosubstrates of this reaction.

8. Callose deposition near plasmodesmata generally tends to:

  1. Restrict symplastic connectivity
  2. Increase their aperture in every tissue
  3. Convert them into sieve plates
  4. Make sucrose synthesis independent of transport
Answer and explanation

A. Restrict symplastic connectivity. Callose at plasmodesmatal necks can reduce the effective opening and hinder intercellular movement. It does not itself convert a channel into a different vascular structure.

9. Which interface necessarily includes a membrane-separated transfer step in developing angiosperm seeds?

  1. Maternal-to-filial tissue interface
  2. Every pair of endosperm cells
  3. Each adjacent sieve element in a tube
  4. All mesophyll cells of the source leaf
Answer and explanation

A. Maternal-to-filial tissue interface. Maternal and filial tissues do not form one continuous symplast across their interface. Symplastic movement can still occur within each domain; adjacent sieve elements are connected by pores.

10. The phloem-pole pericycle is particularly important in the demonstrated unloading route of:

  1. All maternal seed coats
  2. All conifer needles
  3. The Arabidopsis root protophloem unloading zone
  4. Every ripe fleshy fruit
Answer and explanation

C. The Arabidopsis root protophloem unloading zone. In Arabidopsis root protophloem, Ross-Elliott and colleagues described a specialized pericycle intermediate. This anatomical result should not be generalized to every sink.

11. Which observation is sufficient to classify a leaf as a net source?

  1. Its fresh mass is increasing
  2. It contains chlorophyll
  3. It receives light at noon
  4. It exports more assimilate than it imports
Answer and explanation

D. It exports more assimilate than it imports. A mature illuminated leaf is typically a source, but the defining criterion is its carbon balance. Young photosynthetic leaves may still import carbon.

12. Which is the best explanation for the role of starch formation in storage sinks?

  1. It moves starch granules through phloem
  2. It requires no metabolic energy
  3. It directly opens all plasmodesmata
  4. It retains carbon in a polymer with a lower dissolved-particle contribution than equivalent soluble sugar
Answer and explanation

D. It retains carbon in a polymer with a lower dissolved-particle contribution than equivalent soluble sugar. Polymerization stores carbon in a relatively osmotically inactive form. Starch is generally synthesized in recipient tissue; its production does not imply phloem transport of granules.

13. Sink strength is commonly described in terms of:

  1. Sink size and activity per unit size
  2. Total plant height
  3. Xylem tension alone
  4. Leaf age multiplied by sap pH
Answer and explanation

A. Sink size and activity per unit size. The product of size and specific sink activity gives total activity under specified conditions. Source supply and conducting capacity still limit realized import.

14. Which molecule is a documented mobile floral signal in Arabidopsis?

  1. FT protein
  2. Cellulose
  3. Rubisco holoenzyme as a universal floral messenger
  4. Starch
Answer and explanation

A. FT protein. FT protein contributes to communication from leaf vascular tissue to the shoot apex. Cellulose and starch are structural or storage carbohydrates, not the documented florigen signal.

15. miR399 is associated with systemic regulation of:

  1. Direct starch granule transport
  2. Cyclic photophosphorylation
  3. Phosphate homeostasis
  4. Sieve plate lignification
Answer and explanation

C. Phosphate homeostasis. miR399 is a phosphate-responsive mobile small RNA linked to regulation of PHO2. Its role is signaling, not bulk phosphate or starch carriage.

16. A tuber can change from sink to source during:

  1. Formation of its first starch granule
  2. Sprouting and mobilization of stored carbon
  3. The absence of any reserve mobilization
  4. Permanent cessation of metabolism
Answer and explanation

B. Sprouting and mobilization of stored carbon. A storage organ imports during accumulation and can export mobilized assimilate when supplying a new shoot. The role depends on the net direction of carbon exchange.

17. Which quantity has units of amount per time?

  1. Dry matter fraction
  2. Sink sucrose pool size
  3. Sucrose concentration
  4. Solute flux through a defined cross section
Answer and explanation

D. Solute flux through a defined cross section. A transport rate is an amount per unit time. Concentration is amount per volume, pool size is amount, and a fraction is dimensionless.

18. Which is a valid inference from radiolabeled carbon appearing in sink starch after a source-leaf carbon dioxide pulse?

  1. Starch necessarily moved intact through phloem
  2. All source carbon was recovered
  3. The only route was symplastic
  4. Source-derived carbon reached the sink starch pool
Answer and explanation

D. Source-derived carbon reached the sink starch pool. Isotopic carbon can change chemical identity. Label in starch demonstrates incorporation into that pool, not that starch was the mobile compound or that a particular unloading pathway was used.

19. Trehalose 6-phosphate is relevant here primarily as:

  1. The structural polymer of sieve plates
  2. The universal dominant long-distance transported sugar
  3. A regulatory metabolite related to sucrose status
  4. A substitute for phloem water
Answer and explanation

C. A regulatory metabolite related to sucrose status. Trehalose 6-phosphate connects carbohydrate status to metabolic regulation in many contexts. It is not the principal long-distance transport sugar of most flowering plants.

20. When whole-plant carbon export is fixed, increased import by one sink must:

  1. Decrease the combined import of the other sinks if no other flux changes
  2. Cause all other sinks to become sources
  3. Leave the sum of remaining import unchanged
  4. Increase import by every other sink
Answer and explanation

A. Decrease the combined import of the other sinks if no other flux changes. Conservation constrains the total. It does not specify which competing sink loses the most or how vascular connectivity distributes the decrease.

Part C — analytical and experimental reasoning

21. A source sieve tube and a sink sieve tube both equilibrate with water potential −0.30 MPa. Their osmotic potentials are −0.95 and −0.45 MPa. What is the predicted pressure difference from source to sink?

  1. 1.40 MPa
  2. 0.50 MPa
  3. 0.20 MPa
  4. −0.50 MPa
Answer and explanation

B. 0.50 MPa. Using Ψp = Ψw − Ψs, source pressure is 0.65 MPa and sink pressure is 0.15 MPa. Difference = 0.50 MPa. Adding the osmotic magnitudes or reversing the subtraction gives the incorrect alternatives.

22. Source-to-sink pressure difference is 0.60 MPa and effective resistance is 0.20 MPa h µL⁻¹. Sucrose concentration is 0.30 µmol µL⁻¹. What is the advective sucrose transport rate?

  1. 0.18 µmol h⁻¹
  2. 3.0 µmol h⁻¹
  3. 0.90 µmol h⁻¹
  4. 2.0 µmol h⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. 0.90 µmol h⁻¹. Volume flow = 0.60/0.20 = 3 µL h⁻¹. Multiply by 0.30 µmol µL⁻¹ to obtain 0.90 µmol h⁻¹. The value 3 is volume flow, not sucrose flux.

23. In an ideal cylindrical tube, radius is doubled and length is doubled with pressure difference and viscosity unchanged. What happens to volume flow?

  1. It becomes sixteenfold
  2. It remains unchanged
  3. It becomes twofold
  4. It becomes eightfold
Answer and explanation

D. It becomes eightfold. Q is proportional to r⁴/L, so the radius change gives 16 and the length change divides by 2. The ratio is 8. Real sieve plates introduce additional resistance, so this is an idealized calculation.

24. A tracer reaches root protophloem normally after a treatment but its appearance in the adjacent pericycle declines. Which is the best initial conclusion?

  1. Photosynthesis has completely failed
  2. An unloading-interface restriction is plausible
  3. The tracer proves all proteins move freely
  4. The entire xylem is blocked
Answer and explanation

B. An unloading-interface restriction is plausible. Normal upstream arrival argues against a primary loading or axial-delivery failure for this marker. Reduced spread across the interface supports a localized defect, although marker-specific behavior and tissue viability must be tested.

25. A recipient cell accumulates sucrose tenfold relative to the apoplast at 298 K. Ignoring pressure and activity corrections, what is the uphill free-energy requirement?

  1. −5.71 kJ mol⁻¹
  2. Zero because sucrose is neutral
  3. Approximately +5.71 kJ mol⁻¹
  4. Approximately +57.1 kJ mol⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. Approximately +5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. For a neutral solute ΔG = RT ln(Cin/Cout) = 8.314 × 298 × ln 10 J mol⁻¹ ≈ +5705 J mol⁻¹. Neutrality eliminates a voltage term, not the concentration term.

26. External pH rises from 5.5 to 6.5 while intracellular pH and membrane voltage remain constant. How does the inward proton chemical free-energy term change at 298 K?

  1. It becomes about 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ more favorable
  2. It does not change
  3. It becomes about 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ less favorable
  4. It becomes zero in every case
Answer and explanation

C. It becomes about 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ less favorable. External proton concentration falls tenfold, so the ratio [H⁺]in/[H⁺]out rises tenfold. RT ln of the ratio increases by RT ln 10. Thus less chemical free energy is available to drive cotransport.

27. Two root systems have dry masses 3 and 5 g and specific import activities 4 and 2 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹. Which receives more?

  1. Both 12 µmol h⁻¹
  2. Root A, 12 versus 10 µmol h⁻¹
  3. The masses alone determine equal import
  4. Root B because it is larger
Answer and explanation

B. Root A, 12 versus 10 µmol h⁻¹. Multiply size by specific activity: A = 3×4 = 12; B = 5×2 = 10. The larger organ does not necessarily import more because its per-mass activity can be lower.

28. A steady cytosolic sucrose pool receives 12 µmol h⁻¹ from import and 1 from synthesis, loses 8 to metabolism and 5 to storage. Metabolism rises to 10 before other rates change. What is the initial pool change?

  1. −5 µmol h⁻¹
  2. −2 µmol h⁻¹
  3. +2 µmol h⁻¹
  4. Zero because it was at steady state
Answer and explanation

B. −2 µmol h⁻¹. The new balance is 12+1−10−5 = −2 µmol h⁻¹. The previous steady state no longer holds after perturbation. Later feedback may alter all rates.

29. A sink retains 18 mg carbon per day, exports 2 and loses 6 as respiratory CO₂ carbon, with no fixation or other losses. Its gross import is:

  1. 18 mg carbon day⁻¹
  2. 10 mg carbon day⁻¹
  3. 26 mg carbon day⁻¹
  4. 22 mg carbon day⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. 26 mg carbon day⁻¹. Import must cover net retention plus export plus respiration: 18+2+6 = 26. Ignoring losses or subtracting them from retention reverses the conservation equation.

30. Under control conditions total allocation is 100 units/day and roots receive 30%. Under stress total allocation is 50 and roots receive 40%. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Root flux is unchanged
  2. Root share increases but absolute root allocation falls by 10 units
  3. Root flux must double
  4. Root flux increases by 10 units
Answer and explanation

B. Root share increases but absolute root allocation falls by 10 units. Root allocation changes from 30 to 20 units/day. The percentage rises while the actual amount decreases. Relative allocation alone cannot diagnose increased delivery.

31. A seed mutant has normal label arrival in maternal vascular tissue, excess starch in seed coat and low embryo label. Which is the best mechanistic hypothesis?

  1. Xylem water necessarily flows backward
  2. Defective maternal-to-filial transfer
  3. The embryo is exporting starch to leaves
  4. All leaf fixation has ceased
Answer and explanation

B. Defective maternal-to-filial transfer. Upstream delivery and maternal retention together suggest a downstream barrier. The SWEET seed-filling example illustrates this pattern. Metabolic alternatives and precise transporter localization still require tests.

32. During tuber development, a radiolabel spreads into storage tissue in both stages, while a cytoplasmic fluorescent tracer spreads only in the swelling stage. Assuming equivalent tracer delivery and detection, the pattern is most compatible with:

  1. No vascular transport in the first stage
  2. A conversion of starch into a fluorescent protein
  3. Universal apoplastic unloading at all stages
  4. A developmental increase in symplastic connectivity
Answer and explanation

D. A developmental increase in symplastic connectivity. Radioactive carbon can traverse membrane pathways, whereas a suitable cytoplasmic marker tests accessible symplastic continuity. The later shared distribution supports a shift toward symplastic unloading, though marker selectivity should be controlled.

33. In a hypothetical serial pathway, capacities of source, axial transport, unloading and metabolism are 30, 22, 14 and 25 µmol h⁻¹. Unloading rises to 24, other capacities fixed. What is the new maximum throughput in the stated simplified model?

  1. 22 µmol h⁻¹
  2. 39 µmol h⁻¹
  3. 30 µmol h⁻¹
  4. 24 µmol h⁻¹
Answer and explanation

A. 22 µmol h⁻¹. The smallest capacity becomes axial transport at 22. In a living network flux control can be distributed; the simplified model assumes hard serial capacities with no accumulation.

34. Two pathways share pressure 0.80 MPa. Sink A pressure 0.30 and resistance 0.10; sink B pressure 0.40 and resistance 0.20, with consistent units. For equal sap sucrose concentration what is A:B delivery ratio?

  1. 4:5
  2. 5:2
  3. 2:5
  4. 1:1
Answer and explanation

B. 5:2. QA = 0.50/0.10 = 5 and QB = 0.40/0.20 = 2. Equal concentration gives identical proportionality between flow and advective delivery. Pressure difference alone does not account for resistance.

35. A selective inhibitor lowers recipient H⁺-ATPase activity but leaves donor SWEET transporters intact. What is the most plausible early consequence for a proton-coupled uptake route?

  1. SWEET immediately becomes an ATP-driven pump
  2. Weakened proton motive force can reduce recipient sucrose uptake
  3. Sucrose uptake must rise because the pump consumes less ATP
  4. All plasmodesmata must close
Answer and explanation

B. Weakened proton motive force can reduce recipient sucrose uptake. The proton pump sustains the gradient that powers coupled influx. Its inhibition can impair uptake even if donor efflux is initially functional. Subsequent apoplastic accumulation may reduce donor efflux indirectly.

36. Two organs contain 30 µmol sucrose at steady state. Removal rates are 10 and 20 µmol h⁻¹. What are their apparent pool residence times?

  1. Equal because pools match
  2. 0.33 and 0.67 h
  3. 10 and 20 h
  4. 3 and 1.5 h
Answer and explanation

D. 3 and 1.5 h. Pool divided by removal rate gives apparent residence time. Equal pool sizes do not mean equal turnover. These times describe an average approximation rather than the fate of individual molecules.

37. Control label recovery in source, roots, expanding leaves and other pools is 50, 25, 15, 10 units. Treatment gives 65, 10, 15, 10. Which additional result would best localize a root unloading defect rather than failure of axial delivery?

  1. A greater percentage of label in source tissue alone
  2. Normal root sieve-element label but low adjacent-cell label
  3. A source leaf containing chlorophyll
  4. Lower total root mass alone
Answer and explanation

B. Normal root sieve-element label but low adjacent-cell label. The spatial comparison localizes the difference downstream of arrival in the root conducting system. Root growth and total label recovery alone cannot separate loading, transport, unloading and metabolism.

38. An enzyme has Vmax = 20 µmol h⁻¹ and Km = 5 mM. Substrate rises from 5 to 20 mM. Which is the predicted rate change under the simple Michaelis–Menten model?

  1. 5 to 20 µmol h⁻¹
  2. 10 to 40 µmol h⁻¹
  3. 20 to 80 µmol h⁻¹
  4. 10 to 16 µmol h⁻¹
Answer and explanation

D. 10 to 16 µmol h⁻¹. At 5 mM, v = 20×5/(5+5) = 10. At 20 mM, v = 20×20/(5+20) = 16. The saturating relation prevents a fourfold increase in rate.

39. A donor-specific tagged mobile protein is detected in a wild-type graft recipient. Which additional result most directly strengthens the claim that it functions as a signal at the destination?

  1. The graft is longer than the control
  2. The donor has green leaves
  3. A specific recipient response dependent on the functional tagged protein
  4. The recipient contains unlabeled sugar
Answer and explanation

C. A specific recipient response dependent on the functional tagged protein. Movement and function are distinct. A specific functional response linked to the transported molecule, with appropriate controls and retained activity of the tag, supports the signaling claim.

40. A plant with ten fruits of 40 g dry mass is thinned to six fruits that each reach 60 g. Which conclusion is correct?

  1. Average fruit dry mass rises by 50%, while total fruit dry matter falls by 10%
  2. Total yield is unchanged
  3. Individual fruit size decreases
  4. Total fruit dry matter rises by 50%
Answer and explanation

A. Average fruit dry mass rises by 50%, while total fruit dry matter falls by 10%. Average mass changes from 40 to 60 g, a 50% increase. Total changes from 400 to 360 g, a 10% decline. Per-organ performance and total harvested yield are different quantities.

Additional SET-style, assertion–reason and quantitative practice

41. Assertion: Symplastic unloading does not necessarily consist only of diffusion. Reason: A pressure-driven fluid movement can carry solutes through accessible plasmodesmata.

  1. Assertion true, reason false
  2. Assertion false, reason true
  3. Both true and the reason explains the assertion
  4. Both true but unrelated
Answer and explanation

C. Both true and the reason explains the assertion. A cytoplasmic channel can support both a diffusive contribution and advective movement. Thus the presence of a symplastic pathway is not sufficient evidence that diffusion is the only mechanism.

42. Assertion: The extracellular hydrolysis of one mole of sucrose can increase dissolved sugar particle number. Reason: Invertase produces one mole each of glucose and fructose.

  1. Both true, but reason does not explain
  2. Both true and reason explains
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

B. Both true and reason explains. One dissolved sucrose molecule gives two dissolved monosaccharides if both remain in solution. The idealized particle number increases while total carbon is conserved; subsequent uptake alters the real osmotic effect.

43. Assertion: A high sucrose concentration in a sink always indicates high import. Reason: Pool concentration is influenced by both influx and removal.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Both false
  4. Both true and reason explains
Answer and explanation

A. Assertion false, reason true. The assertion confuses pool and flux. Slow utilization or reduced volume can raise concentration even while import falls; the reason is a correct explanation of why the assertion fails.

44. A recipient tissue lacks measurable local photosynthesis. It accumulates 25 mg carbon daily and respires 5 mg carbon with no export or other loss. Gross import is:

  1. 20 mg/day
  2. 30 mg/day
  3. 25 mg/day
  4. 5 mg/day
Answer and explanation

B. 30 mg/day. The imported carbon must supply both storage/growth and respiratory loss. 25+5 = 30 mg/day. The values concern carbon mass, not sucrose mass or total fresh mass.

45. In a hypothetical channel u = 20 µm/s, L = 1 µm and D = 4 µm²/s. What is Pe?

  1. 80 µm/s
  2. 20 µm
  3. 5, dimensionless
  4. 0.2, dimensionless
Answer and explanation

C. 5, dimensionless. Pe = uL/D = 20×1/4 = 5. Units cancel. This compares characteristic advective and diffusive effects but does not establish the net direction without boundary conditions.

46. Which experiment best distinguishes a change in dry carbon storage from water-driven enlargement of a fruit?

  1. Measuring both dry matter gain and fresh mass over time
  2. Measuring circumference alone
  3. Counting stomata in a source leaf only
  4. Measuring fruit color once
Answer and explanation

A. Measuring both dry matter gain and fresh mass over time. Fresh mass and size are influenced by water. Dry matter and, where necessary, carbon analysis more directly address stored material. Neither a single size measurement nor source anatomy resolves the distinction.

47. A tagged macromolecule is detected in phloem sap. What can be concluded from that observation alone?

  1. It is present in the sampled material; movement and function require additional controls
  2. It replaces sucrose as the dominant transported nutrient
  3. It enters all cells freely
  4. It must regulate every recipient
Answer and explanation

A. It is present in the sampled material; movement and function require additional controls. Detection does not establish source, route, unloading, biological action or absence of contamination. Grafting, tissue-specific labeling and functional assays can provide additional evidence.

48. A leaf fixes 25 carbon units, respires 5 and accumulates 8 in its own tissues per hour, with other fluxes negligible. Its net export is:

  1. 12 units/h
  2. 38 units/h
  3. −12 units/h
  4. 20 units/h
Answer and explanation

A. 12 units/h. Net export = fixation − respiration − accumulation = 25−5−8 = 12. The positive sign identifies net source behavior for this interval.

49. A crop has 48 g grain and 120 g total aboveground dry mass including grain. What is harvest index on this stated basis?

  1. 0.72
  2. 0.40
  3. 2.50
  4. 0.60
Answer and explanation

B. 0.40. Harvest index = harvested grain dry mass divided by stated total aboveground dry mass = 48/120 = 0.40. It is not an instantaneous import flux and denominator conventions must be stated.

50. Which pair of observations most directly supports a metabolic rather than an initial recipient membrane uptake limitation?

  1. Normal early entry of label into recipient cytosol, followed by reduced label in storage product
  2. No tracer in recipient conducting phloem
  3. Absence of recipient chlorophyll alone
  4. Low label in source and all sinks
Answer and explanation

A. Normal early entry of label into recipient cytosol, followed by reduced label in storage product. Normal entry indicates that the initial uptake step remains functional for the measured substrate. Reduced downstream incorporation suggests a subsequent metabolic or compartmentation limitation; specific assays identify the relevant step.

20. Revision and mastery

Photoassimilate allocation connects production, phloem loading, pressure-driven axial delivery, unloading and recipient metabolism. A source exports on balance; a sink imports. Roles change with development. Symplastic pathways use plasmodesmata, while apoplastic steps require membrane exit and entry. Unloading capacity depends on anatomy, transporters, metabolic use and storage. Actual allocation reflects the combined limitations of source supply, hydraulic connectivity, sink capacity and regulatory feedback.
