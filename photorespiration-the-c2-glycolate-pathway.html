1. The biological problem: recovering carbon after oxygenation

Imagine following a single molecule of carbon dioxide from the air to the sugars of a wheat leaf. It crosses the boundary layer around the leaf, enters through a stoma, dissolves in the water of the mesophyll and reaches a chloroplast. There, the enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, usually called Rubisco, catalyses the reaction that incorporates inorganic carbon into organic material. Yet the same chloroplast contains oxygen, both from the atmosphere and from the water-splitting reactions of photosynthesis. Rubisco is able to use either gas. The resulting competition is not a peripheral curiosity: it creates a substantial metabolic burden in an illuminated C3 leaf.

The normal acceptor of carbon dioxide is ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), a five-carbon sugar carrying phosphate groups at both ends. Carboxylation gives two molecules of three-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Both products can proceed through the Calvin–Benson cycle. Oxygenation instead gives one molecule of 3-PGA and one molecule of two-carbon 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG). A chloroplast has no equivalent of the Calvin-cycle reduction sequence that would simply turn 2-PG into a triose phosphate. If oxygenation continues without an adequate removal mechanism, carbon becomes trapped outside the productive cycle and normal carbon metabolism is disrupted.

Photorespiration is the interconnected set of reactions that makes this situation tolerable. In the canonical leaf pathway, most of the carbon in two molecules of 2-PG is returned to the chloroplast as one molecule of 3-PGA. One carbon is released as carbon dioxide in the process. The pathway also moves nitrogen between amino acids and temporarily releases ammonia. It uses energy and reducing power, yet enables a C3 plant to survive an atmosphere in which oxygenation is unavoidable. Calling it merely a waste of photosynthesis overlooks the distinction between the cause of the problem and the mechanism that mitigates it.

The term photorespiration arose from physiological observations of oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide release associated with light. It does not mean that mitochondria cease to respire at night, nor does it mean that sunlight directly powers every reaction of the salvage pathway. Rubisco oxygenation is normally linked to illumination because RuBP regeneration and Rubisco activation depend on photosynthetic metabolism. Mitochondrial oxidation of respiratory substrates can continue independently of this supply of 2-PG. A leaf in the light can perform photosynthesis, mitochondrial respiration and photorespiration at the same time.

Distinguish three questions throughout the chapter. First, why does Rubisco oxygenate RuBP? Second, how are the products recovered? Third, how do the costs and protective functions of the recovery pathway affect the whole leaf? The answer to the second question cannot be used to claim that the oxygenation reaction itself increases net carbon gain. Conversely, evidence that oxygenation reduces assimilation cannot justify blocking the salvage pathway in a normal atmosphere. The two processes are mechanistically linked but their consequences are different.

How investigators distinguished photorespiration from ordinary respiration

An experiment with a green leaf can be deceptively difficult to interpret. If the leaf is held in darkness, the carbon dioxide leaving its surface primarily reflects respiration and other metabolic reactions that release inorganic carbon. If the light is switched on, the direction of net exchange usually reverses. It would be tempting to infer that the carbon dioxide-producing reactions have stopped. The observation proves only that the rate of uptake exceeds the rate of release. The separate processes cannot be identified from their difference unless there is another independent observation or a useful controlled perturbation.

One such perturbation is a change in the oxygen concentration around the illuminated leaf. The oxygen requirement of aerobic respiration might lead a beginner to predict that any increased carbon dioxide release in oxygen-rich air simply reflects increased respiratory activity. This interpretation is incomplete because the light-dependent inhibitory effect of oxygen on carbon dioxide fixation occurs in circumstances where Rubisco uses a shared substrate intermediate. The biological problem was to locate a reaction at which oxygen could specifically divert photosynthetic carbon. The relationship between gas composition and net assimilation gave a clue, but could not establish the molecular reaction.

In 1971, George Bowes, William Ogren and Richard Hageman reported evidence that the enzyme then called ribulose diphosphate carboxylase could produce phosphoglycolate. Their work connected a biochemical reaction with the physiological process under investigation. A purified-enzyme or enzyme-preparation experiment is particularly informative when substrates are defined, because it excludes many of the alternative cellular routes that might produce glycolate. If phosphoglycolate appears when RuBP and oxygen are available to the carboxylase, the proposal that the initiating reaction requires an entirely separate carbon oxidation pathway becomes less plausible. The discovery was not equivalent to identifying every later enzyme in the salvage sequence; it identified the origin of the troublesome substrate.

Subsequent work joined this result with the localization of glycolate metabolism in peroxisomes and glycine metabolism in mitochondria. Mutant analysis added a particularly strong form of evidence: interruption of an enzyme in the proposed route can compromise photosynthesis under conditions favouring oxygenation and be partly relieved by conditions that suppress that reaction. Such a rescue is mechanistically different from supplying ATP to a respiratory mutant. It reduces the rate at which a problematic substrate is formed. The fact that multiple defects along the pathway can show this conditional phenotype supports the existence of a linked series rather than an isolated carbon dioxide-producing enzyme.

This progression illustrates three independent levels of biological evidence. Gas exchange demonstrates an oxygen-sensitive physiological response. Enzyme chemistry identifies a reaction capable of producing the proposed intermediate. Genetics and organelle assays establish the functional significance of downstream reactions. Agreement across these levels is more convincing than identifying a correlation between oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange alone. When an examination asks which finding proves a particular enzyme is required, the strongest answer is usually the observation closest to that enzyme's specific activity, supported by appropriate controls. When the question instead asks whether the reaction is important for plant growth, physiological and genetic evidence becomes essential.

2. Rubisco carboxylation and oxygenation

Rubisco must bind and activate RuBP before either gas reacts. In the active catalytic site, a carbamylated lysine participates in coordination of magnesium; the carbon dioxide used to carbamylate the enzyme is distinct from the substrate molecule of carbon dioxide that will become part of the product. RuBP is converted to a reactive enediolate intermediate. Reaction with carbon dioxide gives a six-carbon intermediate that is hydrated and cleaved to yield two molecules of 3-PGA. Reaction with molecular oxygen instead forms an oxygenated intermediate whose breakdown produces 3-PGA and 2-PG. The competition occurs at the level of the reactive intermediate, not between two separate Rubisco enzymes.

Follow the carbon atoms before considering energy. During carboxylation five carbons from RuBP plus one inorganic carbon yield six carbons distributed equally between two 3-PGA molecules. Oxygenation incorporates no inorganic carbon. The original five carbons are distributed between three-carbon 3-PGA and two-carbon 2-PG. Thus oxygenation is not an alternative mechanism of net carbon fixation. Nor does oxygenation itself immediately release carbon dioxide. The characteristic carbon loss occurs later, during mitochondrial metabolism of glycine.

For a given enzyme at a given temperature, a useful kinetic description compares its carboxylation and oxygenation specificity. Let Vc and Vo be the rates of carboxylation and oxygenation, and C and O be the carbon dioxide and oxygen concentrations at Rubisco. If S is the specificity factor expressed on the same concentration basis, Vo/Vc = O/(S × C). This form follows from the competing substrate rate expressions, in which the common denominator cancels. It does not imply that either rate is linear in gas concentration under all conditions. The ratio is the quantity predicted by this expression, provided the enzyme and relevant conditions are held constant.

For example, suppose local dissolved oxygen is 240 micromolar and local dissolved carbon dioxide is 12 micromolar, with an illustrative specificity factor of 80 on a concentration basis. The predicted ratio is 240/(80 × 12) = 0.25. Four carboxylations accompany one oxygenation on average. If carbon dioxide falls to 6 micromolar without changes in oxygen or specificity, the ratio doubles to 0.50. The calculation does not require the absolute reaction rate to remain the same. The carboxylation rate could decrease while the fraction of turnovers attributable to oxygenation increases.

Gas partial pressure and dissolved concentration must not be mixed casually. Carbon dioxide and oxygen differ in solubility, and temperature changes both solubility and enzyme specificity. A specificity factor defined on a molar concentration basis is not automatically interchangeable with one defined from partial pressures. Use consistent units when applying a supplied equation. The atmospheric composition outside a leaf is not the gas environment directly experienced by Rubisco, because diffusion through stomata and mesophyll and the consumption of carbon dioxide establish concentration gradients.

The bifunctional nature of Rubisco explains why a carbon-concentrating mechanism helps. C4 and CAM plants raise carbon dioxide availability around Rubisco during their respective carbon assimilation phases. They do not replace the oxygenase-capable Rubisco with phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase for the Calvin cycle. Instead, they reduce the likelihood that oxygen reacts with Rubisco by changing its local substrate environment.

Deriving a competition model rather than memorizing a ratio

The kinetic specificity factor describes the enzyme's relative catalytic preferences, not the fraction of molecules of oxygen in the atmosphere. To obtain the rate ratio, begin by considering the same population of activated Rubisco catalytic sites. Let the apparent Michaelis constants for the two gases be Kc and Ko, and let their maximal velocities under the conditions of measurement be Vc,max and Vo,max. Suppose RuBP is sufficiently available and the two gases compete for the enzyme intermediate. A simplified denominator for the two rates is D = 1 + C/Kc + O/Ko. The relative term describing carboxylation is Vc,max × (C/Kc)/D. The corresponding oxygenation term is Vo,max × (O/Ko)/D.

Because both terms use the same denominator, division eliminates D. The ratio of carboxylation to oxygenation becomes [(Vc,max/Kc)/(Vo,max/Ko)] × C/O. The term in square brackets is the specificity factor S. Therefore Vo/Vc = O/(S × C). This derivation shows why the kinetic comparison involves both catalytic capacity and substrate affinity. A large maximal rate for carboxylation does not by itself establish a large specificity factor if the enzyme also has a high Michaelis constant for carbon dioxide. The ratios of rate capacity to Michaelis constant are the quantities that must be compared.

The simplified expressions are useful under defined conditions, but Rubisco activation, supply of RuBP, metabolic inhibition and diffusion can also constrain rates. In a leaf where the pool of activated Rubisco has been halved by a treatment, both rates may be reduced even if their ratio is unchanged. Conversely, a higher carbon dioxide concentration can alter both the ratio and the total distribution of enzyme turnovers. When the rate ratio is derived from an isolated enzyme experiment, one must not directly substitute external gas concentrations without a defensible conversion to conditions at the catalytic site.

Distinguish the ratio of oxygenation to carboxylation from the fraction of all Rubisco turnovers that are oxygenations. If r = Vo/Vc, the fraction f is Vo/(Vo + Vc) = r/(1 + r). A ratio of 0.25 corresponds to 20 percent of the total, not 25 percent, because the denominator of the ratio was the number of carboxylation events. A ratio of 1 means equal numbers of carboxylation and oxygenation reactions and therefore a fraction of one half. A ratio greater than 1 is possible in a sufficiently oxygenating environment, but a fraction greater than 1 is impossible. This conversion is particularly useful when a problem supplies the percentage of Rubisco turnovers rather than rates.

For an illustrative enzyme preparation, suppose Vc,max = 12 and Vo,max = 4 in the same rate units, with Kc = 15 and Ko = 300 micromolar. The specificity factor is (12/15)/(4/300) = 60. At C = 10 and O = 180 micromolar, the rate ratio is 180/(60 × 10) = 0.30. The predicted oxygenation fraction is 0.30/1.30, approximately 0.231. If another preparation has identical gas affinities but the oxygenation maximal rate is halved, its specificity factor doubles to 120 and the new ratio at the same gas concentrations is 0.15. The oxygenation fraction is then approximately 0.130. These are hypothetical teaching parameters, not measured characteristics of a named species.

The ability to make these distinctions also explains why selection for Rubisco performance is not simply selection for the highest possible reaction speed. The relevant leaf phenotype combines enzyme abundance, catalytic turnover, discrimination between gases, supply of RuBP and environmental carbon dioxide availability. The same biochemical property may carry a different physiological significance in a C3 leaf and a C4 bundle sheath, where local carbon dioxide differs. The important NET-level principle is competition at a shared catalytic system, not a claim that one kinetic property universally predicts photosynthetic performance.

3. Chloroplast: 2-phosphoglycolate removal

The two carbon atoms of phosphoglycolate remain valuable even though this molecule cannot directly serve as a Calvin-cycle substrate. The first salvage reaction is hydrolysis catalysed by phosphoglycolate phosphatase (PGLP). Water is used to release inorganic phosphate, leaving glycolate. This transformation does not remove either carbon, consume ATP or reduce the carbon skeleton. It changes the chemical form of the molecule so that the remaining steps of the pathway can proceed.

A common mistake is to confuse glycolate with glycerate. Glycolate is a two-carbon hydroxy acid; glycerate is a three-carbon hydroxy acid. The names sound alike, but their roles and carbon counts differ. Glycolate leaves the chloroplast near the beginning of the pathway, whereas glycerate returns near the end. The phosphate removed from 2-PG must also not be confused with the phosphate later transferred from ATP to glycerate. A released inorganic phosphate and an ATP-dependent phosphorylation are separate reactions at opposite ends of the salvage sequence.

Removal of 2-PG matters for more than carbon retention. Its accumulation can interfere with enzymes of photosynthetic carbon metabolism, including triose phosphate isomerase. An organism that lacked an effective phosphatase could experience an inhibition of carbon assimilation disproportionately greater than the amount of carbon sequestered in 2-PG, because a metabolic inhibitor changes the rates of other reactions. Thus the concentration of the offending intermediate as well as the rate of its formation matters.

In a mesophyll cell the chloroplast is surrounded by an envelope with an outer and inner membrane. Glycolate must cross this boundary to reach the peroxisome; the soluble pathway enzymes alone cannot explain a sustained flux across compartments. Chloroplast glycolate/glycerate transport protein PLGG1 participates in the exchange of these metabolites, while BASS6 can contribute to glycolate export. Their existence illustrates an important general lesson: a mutation in a transporter may produce a metabolic defect resembling a mutation in a catalytic enzyme, even if the catalytic enzyme is intact. The exact partition of transport flux through alternative routes depends on species and physiological circumstances.

In a steady-state diagram, the same quantity of carbon entering the first step must be accounted for in subsequent products, export, accumulation or side reactions. Real cells are not always in steady state. Following a rapid increase in oxygenation, 2-PG and glycolate pools may initially increase before downstream reactions adjust. Measurement of concentration alone does not tell whether flux has increased or decreased; a large pool can reflect rapid formation, slow consumption or both.

Phosphorylated metabolites, compartment boundaries and regulatory feedback

Why is 2-PG not transported directly into a peroxisome? Organic phosphate groups are strongly polar and usually carry negative charge under physiological conditions. They help retain intermediates within a metabolic compartment unless appropriate transport proteins exist. The phosphorylated intermediates of the Calvin cycle are therefore not interchangeable with free organic acids at a membrane. The first salvage enzyme catalyses hydrolysis rather than reduction or decarboxylation. It generates a chemically distinct product, glycolate, which can be handled by transport systems and by the subsequent oxidase.

In the chemical shorthand of the pathway, phosphoglycolate hydrolysis can be written as 2-PG + water → glycolate + inorganic phosphate. The equation tracks carbon and phosphate, with protonation states omitted. Since hydrolysis breaks the phosphate ester bond, it does not need an additional ATP to remove the phosphate. At the end of the cycle, phosphoryl transfer from ATP regenerates a phosphate ester on a different molecule, glycerate. The energetic expense of this final step is one reason why the initial hydrolysis cannot simply be treated as temporary storage of a high-energy phosphate that is later reused without cost.

Two Calvin-cycle reactions illustrate why regulation by 2-PG has effects that extend beyond its carbon content. Triose phosphate isomerase interconverts glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Both forms are required in appropriately balanced amounts for carbon rearrangement and biosynthesis. Sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase helps direct the regeneration phase by hydrolysing a bisphosphate sugar. In Arabidopsis, work by Flügel and colleagues in 2017 demonstrated inhibition of these enzymes by 2-PG and related changes in carbon metabolism when PGLP activity was altered. An inhibitor acting on regeneration can lower the supply of RuBP for subsequent carbon fixation. The consequences are therefore not restricted to the number of molecules of 2-PG accumulated.

Suppose a leaf has an initial pool of 100 arbitrary units of 2-PG and a formation rate of 20 units per minute. If removal is initially also 20 units per minute, the pool is at steady state. When phosphatase capacity is suddenly reduced so that actual removal is 12 units per minute, the initial accumulation rate is eight units per minute. If these rates stayed constant for five minutes, the pool would rise by 40 units. In a real leaf, the resulting inhibition of other photosynthetic enzymes could change the formation rate, and increasing substrate concentration could increase residual phosphatase turnover. The linear extrapolation is useful only over the interval in which the assumed rates hold.

Transport adds another constraint. For each two glycolates exported from the chloroplast, one glycerate must be imported if the canonical carbon loop is closed. A hypothetical obligatory one-for-one glycolate–glycerate exchanger could therefore exchange one of the outgoing glycolate molecules for the returning glycerate, but another export route or a different transport mode would be needed to account for the other glycolate molecule. The 2013 study of PLGG1 by Pick and colleagues and the 2017 study of BASS6 by South and colleagues provide experimental context for this accounting problem in Arabidopsis. The combined transporter defects were associated with a more severe metabolic phenotype than either single defect, consistent with complementary transport functions. The stoichiometry of the metabolic pathway provides a reason to look for such complementary functions before assuming a single obligatory exchange reaction is sufficient.

A transport defect and an enzyme defect can be distinguished by the distribution of intermediates between compartments. For instance, a chloroplast glycolate export defect can cause glycolate to accumulate even if its synthesis by PGLP remains normal. A peroxisomal oxidase defect can cause an apparently similar whole-leaf accumulation. Fractionation or other localization evidence, together with assays of catalytic activity and transport, is necessary to discriminate them. Whole-leaf metabolite measurements alone do not identify the compartment where a substance accumulated. A solution to a mutant-based question should specify the minimum evidence needed to choose between these alternatives.

4. Peroxisome: glycolate oxidation and amino-group transfer

Leaf peroxisomes contain enzymes that convert glycolate into glyoxylate. Glycolate oxidase uses oxygen as the electron acceptor and produces hydrogen peroxide. The carbon skeleton still contains two carbon atoms. This reaction is an oxidation because the alcohol group of glycolate becomes the aldehyde group of glyoxylate. Unlike a mitochondrial respiratory dehydrogenase, the oxidase transfers reducing equivalents directly to oxygen rather than to an electron transport chain for proton pumping. Hydrogen peroxide is therefore a normal product of photorespiratory metabolism, not automatically evidence of cellular damage.

Hydrogen peroxide can participate in signalling but becomes harmful when its production outpaces removal. Catalase catalyses the disproportionation of two hydrogen peroxide molecules into two water molecules and one oxygen molecule. Consequently, two glycolate oxidation reactions consume two oxygen molecules and produce two hydrogen peroxide molecules; complete catalase disproportionation releases one oxygen molecule. On this limited peroxisomal accounting basis, the net oxygen uptake is one molecule for two glycolate molecules. Rubisco itself has already consumed oxygen during oxygenation, and mitochondrial respiration may consume additional oxygen. A measured total oxygen flux cannot therefore be identified solely with the oxygen uptake of glycolate oxidase.

Glyoxylate next accepts an amino group. Glutamate:glyoxylate aminotransferase converts glyoxylate and glutamate into glycine and 2-oxoglutarate. The amino group transfers to the two-carbon skeleton; the five-carbon glutamate skeleton remains as 2-oxoglutarate. In a second important reaction, serine:glyoxylate aminotransferase converts glyoxylate and serine into glycine and hydroxypyruvate. These reactions depend on an available amino donor. Without such donors, a glyoxylate pool may rise and carbon flow may shift toward alternative reactions.

One can now understand why a textbook arrow from glycolate to glycine hides a coupling between carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Glycolate carries no nitrogen, but glycine carries an amino group. The nitrogen must originate in a pre-existing organic nitrogen compound. When the pathway operates at steady state, glutamate and serine participate in cyclic transfer and must be regenerated. Aminotransferases do not themselves generate new nitrogen from atmospheric dinitrogen or reduce nitrate. They rearrange nitrogen already present in metabolically useful forms.

Consider an experiment in which glycolate oxidase activity falls while chloroplast glycolate production remains high. Glycolate should tend to accumulate upstream, and provision of glyoxylate to downstream reactions should fall. In contrast, a reduction of catalase activity primarily compromises peroxide removal. Glycolate oxidase could continue functioning, at least initially, and downstream glyoxylate production might remain substantial. Later oxidative damage could affect many reactions. Distinguishing direct and secondary consequences prevents incorrect predictions that every blocked step necessarily produces the same metabolite pattern.

Peroxisomes have a membrane and are positioned close to chloroplasts and mitochondria in many photosynthetic cells. Proximity facilitates efficient exchange but does not establish that membranes have fused or that enzymes have migrated between compartments. A compartment diagram is a map of predominant reaction sites rather than a literal image of permanent physical contact.

Why oxidation and transamination occur as distinct steps

Glycolate and glyoxylate differ in the oxidation state of one carbon atom. The carboxyl carbon is retained, whereas the carbon bearing the alcohol group of glycolate is converted into the aldehyde group of glyoxylate. Glycolate oxidase is a flavin mononucleotide-containing enzyme. During catalysis, reducing equivalents from the organic acid first reduce the enzyme-bound flavin; transfer to molecular oxygen regenerates oxidized flavin and yields peroxide. The enzyme does not deliver these electrons to a proton-pumping membrane complex. This is the essential biochemical reason that the oxidation of glycolate does not directly conserve energy as mitochondrial ATP.

The choice of the peroxisome as a major site of this reaction is consistent with the general metabolic role of this organelle. It contains reactions that form peroxide and mechanisms that control peroxide concentration. Compartmentation does not make peroxide harmless, but it allows peroxide production to occur in proximity to detoxifying enzymes. Catalase can process peroxide without a stoichiometric requirement for an external electron donor because the peroxide molecules themselves provide both oxidizing and reducing equivalents during disproportionation. Peroxidase-mediated routes, by contrast, may consume reducing substrates. For quantitative questions the assumed route of peroxide removal determines whether oxygen is returned and whether additional reductant is consumed.

A consequence is that oxygen consumption at the level of a peroxisome can be interpreted only together with peroxide decomposition. Suppose a preparation is supplied with glycolate and shows oxygen consumption. Adding an effective catalase preparation may reduce the measured net consumption because some oxygen is regenerated. It does not necessarily inhibit glycolate oxidase. To determine glycolate oxidation directly, measure glyoxylate formation or substrate disappearance in addition to oxygen exchange. If oxygen electrode readings were interpreted as direct enzyme turnover without correction, catalase would appear to reduce oxidase flux even when the same quantity of glycolate was being oxidized.

Aminotransferases prepare the oxidized carbon skeleton for the mitochondrial phase. Pyridoxal phosphate is the usual cofactor for these amino-group transfer reactions. During one portion of the catalytic cycle the cofactor accepts an amino group from the donor amino acid; during the next, it transfers that nitrogen to the acceptor keto acid. The donor's carbon skeleton becomes a corresponding keto acid, while the acceptor becomes an amino acid. Glutamate supplies nitrogen and becomes 2-oxoglutarate. Glyoxylate receives nitrogen and becomes glycine. In the serine-dependent reaction, serine becomes hydroxypyruvate. No net ammonium release is required in these direct transfers.

Consider the combined transamination requirement for two glyoxylate molecules. One can receive the amino group from glutamate, and the other from serine. The result is two glycine molecules, one 2-oxoglutarate molecule and one hydroxypyruvate molecule. The two glycine molecules are needed in mitochondria to regenerate one serine molecule. The 2-oxoglutarate can contribute to regeneration of glutamate through ammonium reassimilation. Hydroxypyruvate already possesses the three-carbon skeleton required for return to the chloroplast after reduction. This arrangement explains how the pathway avoids the need to consume two glutamate molecules irreversibly for every pair of glycolates.

These reactions illustrate why the simple arrows of a pathway diagram are sometimes misleading. The arrow from glyoxylate to glycine is not a spontaneous conversion in which nitrogen appears from nowhere. The arrow from serine to hydroxypyruvate is not oxidative deamination with free ammonia as the immediate product. The two arrows are sides of coupled nitrogen-transfer reactions. When tracing an isotope of nitrogen, an aminotransferase must be represented with both its substrates and both its products; otherwise the apparent nitrogen disappearance is merely an omission from the diagram.

5. Mitochondrion: converting two glycines into serine

Glycine produced by the peroxisomal aminotransferases travels to the mitochondrion. Here two molecules of glycine, each containing two carbons and one amino nitrogen, provide the substrates for a linked sequence involving glycine decarboxylase (GDC) and serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT). One glycine undergoes cleavage. Its carboxyl carbon is liberated as carbon dioxide; its nitrogen is released as ammonia; and its remaining carbon is transferred into the folate-dependent one-carbon pool. In the next reaction that one-carbon unit is added to another glycine molecule to form serine.

The combined equation, written using the conventional biochemical shorthand for ammonia and proton balance, is 2 glycine + NAD⁺ + H₂O → serine + CO₂ + NH₃ + NADH + H⁺. At physiological pH most freely dissolved ammonia is present as ammonium, so NH₃ in the equation must not be interpreted as the only chemical species present in the mitochondrial matrix. Four input carbons are distributed as three in serine and one in carbon dioxide. Two input nitrogen atoms are distributed as one in serine and one in ammonia. The reaction produces one reduced nicotinamide cofactor per pair of glycines.

GDC is a multienzyme system rather than a single enzyme that directly converts two glycines into serine. Its P-protein performs the pyridoxal-phosphate-dependent glycine decarboxylation, transferring the remaining aminomethyl group to a lipoyl group on the H-protein. The T-protein transfers the one-carbon unit to tetrahydrofolate (THF), releasing ammonia. The L-protein reoxidizes the reduced lipoyl group with the aid of FAD and NAD⁺, resulting in NADH formation. The resulting 5,10-methylene-THF supplies the one-carbon group for SHMT, which combines it with another glycine to form serine and regenerates THF.

The distinction between these two glycine molecules is conceptually useful. The donor loses its carboxyl carbon and amino nitrogen, whereas the acceptor retains its own two-carbon backbone and receives an additional carbon. If the donor's carboxyl carbon carried an isotope label, that label would appear in released carbon dioxide. If the donor's alpha carbon carried the label, it could become the side-chain carbon of serine through the folate pool. In a living cell the labels can subsequently be redistributed, so a short pulse and a suitable chase are required to interpret primary products rather than later metabolism.

GDC activity depends on mitochondrial redox conditions because NAD⁺ must remain available to accept electrons. NADH formed in the matrix can be oxidized through respiratory pathways or linked to redox transfer between compartments. The photorespiratory contribution to mitochondrial NADH therefore does not imply that the glycine reaction is identical to the citric acid cycle. Both can provide reducing equivalents to the respiratory chain, but they begin from distinct carbon substrates and have different reaction products and physiological roles.

A block at GDC or mitochondrial SHMT can lead to glycine accumulation and reduced provision of serine. If oxygenation is suppressed by elevated carbon dioxide, the demand for glycine processing from photorespiration is reduced. This explains the high-carbon-dioxide growth rescue observed in certain Arabidopsis photorespiratory mutants. It is important to separate the suppression of metabolic demand from restoration of catalytic activity: elevated carbon dioxide need not repair a defective gene to alleviate its consequences.

Cofactor recycling and the consequence of redox imbalance

The lipoyl group on the H-protein functions as a flexible carrier that can move reaction intermediates between catalytic proteins. The P-protein removes carbon dioxide from glycine, and the remaining group is transferred onto the lipoyl carrier. The T-protein uses tetrahydrofolate as a carbon acceptor, generating methylene-THF while nitrogen is released. After these steps the lipoyl group must be returned to an oxidized state before it can accept another reaction intermediate. Reoxidation is accomplished by the L-protein, which ultimately reduces NAD⁺. Thus the system cannot indefinitely produce serine if NAD⁺ regeneration is prevented.

It is instructive to think of the H-protein and THF as catalytic carrier pools rather than net carbon products. If the number of available carriers is limited and downstream use becomes slow, the carrier pool can accumulate in a modified state. An apparently separate enzyme step may then slow because its preferred carrier form becomes scarce. For example, impaired SHMT activity could slow the recycling of THF from methylene-THF and thereby constrain the T-protein step. The severity depends on other sources and uses of folate intermediates. The idea generalizes to metabolic cycles such as the TCA cycle, where the availability of a regenerated acceptor controls continuing reaction flux.

In a matrix with a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio, the oxidation of the reduced lipoyl group by the GDC L-protein becomes less favourable than in a matrix where NAD⁺ is readily available. Mitochondrial electron transport can help restore NAD⁺ by oxidizing NADH. Plant mitochondria possess additional flexibility through alternative dehydrogenases and AOX, discussed in the previous chapter. Depending on the available routes, electrons from the glycine reaction may be oxidized with different degrees of proton translocation and energy conservation. The glycine decarboxylation reaction itself does not synthesize ATP, even when its NADH contributes to a respiratory pathway that does.

A simple comparison of two treatments clarifies the distinction. In the first, a specific defect prevents glycine cleavage while the mitochondrial electron transport machinery remains functional with other substrates. In the second, a severe inhibition of the conventional respiratory chain limits NAD⁺ regeneration and may secondarily slow glycine processing. Both treatments could result in increased glycine and depressed photosynthesis. The first directly interrupts the photorespiratory enzyme; the second alters the redox environment in which that enzyme operates. Providing an independently oxidizable respiratory substrate, measuring electron transport with several substrates, and assaying GDC in a controlled cofactor environment would help separate the mechanisms.

The nitrogen balance also has a subcellular implication. The free ammonia/ammonium released during glycine metabolism must ultimately be made available to the chloroplast's assimilatory enzymes. Ammonia and ammonium interconvert according to pH; the neutral and ionic forms have different membrane permeabilities. It is inappropriate to assume that the movement of nitrogen is trivial merely because the combined equation contains NH₃. For an examination concerned with the overall pathway, the central result is mitochondrial release followed by chloroplastic reassimilation. A question about transport or pH requires the distinction between chemical species and an explicit statement of the assumed transport process.

6. Returning carbon: hydroxypyruvate, glycerate and 3-PGA

Serine leaves the mitochondrion and returns to the peroxisome. In the canonical cycle, serine:glyoxylate aminotransferase uses serine as amino donor and produces hydroxypyruvate, while another glyoxylate becomes glycine. The carbon skeleton of serine remains three carbons long. The amino group transferred to glyoxylate accounts for the nitrogen retained within the salvage cycle. This is why the two glycine-forming transaminations are best understood together rather than independently.

Hydroxypyruvate reductase (HPR1 in the major peroxisomal route) uses NADH to reduce hydroxypyruvate to glycerate. The carbonyl group becomes an alcohol group. No carbon dioxide is lost in this reduction. The requirement for a peroxisomal supply of reducing equivalents is important: mitochondria produce NADH in the glycine conversion, but a molecule of NADH in the matrix cannot be assumed to move freely into the peroxisome. Metabolic redox shuttles, including malate-linked reactions, help transfer reducing equivalents between compartments. The precise routes used depend on the cellular context.

Alternative hydroxypyruvate reductases outside the peroxisome contribute to metabolic flexibility. For example, cytosolic HPR2 can participate in hydroxypyruvate reduction when flux through the main peroxisomal route is limited. This explains why loss of a major enzyme need not cause exactly the same degree of inhibition as loss of every route capable of catalysing the reaction. For an examination question, however, use the canonical peroxisomal HPR reaction unless a specified mutant, organism or alternate pathway changes the assumptions.

Glycerate travels back to the chloroplast and is phosphorylated by glycerate kinase. ATP provides the phosphate group and becomes ADP. The resulting 3-PGA is chemically the same Calvin-cycle intermediate obtained directly from Rubisco carboxylation. The recovered carbon can now enter the normal photosynthetic carbon pathway, for example by reduction to triose phosphate or through subsequent reactions contributing to regeneration of RuBP. Recovery of 3-PGA is not equivalent to completed sugar production, because the reduction and regeneration reactions require further resources.

Compare the first and last reactions of the pathway. The first removes a phosphate from a problematic two-carbon intermediate without ATP investment. The last transfers a phosphate to a useful three-carbon intermediate using ATP. The cycle is therefore not a reversal of phosphoglycolate hydrolysis. It changes the size of the carbon skeleton by combining carbon from two molecules and releasing a carbon dioxide molecule, and it moves through oxidation, transamination, decarboxylation, reduction and phosphorylation. Knowing this sequence allows the pathway to be reconstructed by reasoning about reaction chemistry rather than memorizing abbreviations.

Redox shuttles: supplying the right reducing equivalent in the right organelle

In a cell that contains several membrane-bound compartments, a list of chemical equations is insufficient to describe the conditions for metabolism. The location of each substrate matters. NADH is a large, charged molecule that does not freely diffuse across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Even if glycine cleavage produces exactly as much NADH as hydroxypyruvate reduction requires, the physical separation of these reactions creates a problem of transferring reducing equivalents. The solution is not necessarily movement of NADH itself. Instead, reversible metabolic reactions can reduce a transportable compound in one compartment and oxidize that compound in another.

Malate and oxaloacetate provide a useful example of such a pair. Malate dehydrogenase catalyses the reversible reaction between these two four-carbon acids, coupled to reduction or oxidation of a nicotinamide cofactor. Where NADH is available, reduction of oxaloacetate to malate can regenerate NAD⁺. If the malate is subsequently transported to a compartment in which the reverse reaction takes place, its oxidation supplies NADH there. The carbon skeleton can in principle be returned through an appropriate exchange process. In this way the reducing equivalent is transferred without assuming that the two compartments share the same free NADH pool.

This conceptual description is not a claim that every NADH molecule produced during glycine cleavage follows a single fixed itinerary through a particular shuttle. Plant redox metabolism also interacts with the cytosol and chloroplast, and the rates and direction of malate-linked reactions depend on metabolite concentrations, transport properties and cofactor ratios. A plant can derive peroxisomal reductant from more than one source. The canonical whole-cell balance, one NADH produced and one used per pair of 2-PG molecules, describes matching stoichiometric requirements rather than proving a unique molecular route.

Peroxisomal malate dehydrogenase is relevant because malate oxidation can supply NADH within the peroxisome for hydroxypyruvate reduction. Coupling the two reactions removes the NADH formed in the first reaction and regenerates NAD⁺. This can support further malate oxidation. If a treatment selectively reduces peroxisomal malate dehydrogenase activity, an initial consequence may be a reduction in the local provision of NADH even while mitochondrial NADH production remains normal. A decline in glycerate formation under these conditions would not demonstrate that glycerate kinase is defective. The bottleneck could instead be hydroxypyruvate reduction limited by reductant supply.

A useful experimental design separates the integrity of HPR from the availability of its cofactor. Measure hydroxypyruvate reduction in an enzyme extract while supplying sufficient NADH. If the extract displays normal HPR activity, but intact tissue has impaired photorespiratory carbon return, then provision or transport of reductant becomes a candidate explanation. Additional comparisons with compartment-specific malate dehydrogenase activity and metabolite distributions would strengthen the argument. An extract eliminates many membrane constraints, which is an advantage for measuring enzyme capacity but a limitation when predicting intact-cell flux.

The chloroplast also faces a compartment-specific ATP requirement. Glycerate kinase consumes ATP on the stromal side, where photophosphorylation supplies energy during illumination. GS2 similarly uses chloroplastic ATP during ammonium recovery. ATP made in mitochondria is not automatically identical in location or accessibility to ATP used by chloroplast reactions. A whole-leaf ATP balance can indicate metabolic costs, but it cannot replace a model of how each compartment obtains the nucleotide it needs. In questions comparing respiratory ATP production with chloroplast ATP consumption, count the energy transformations and then identify the compartment in which they occur.

At the end of the salvage route, the returned glycerate carbon is useful only if glycerate kinase operates. In a mutant lacking this kinase, upstream enzymes may still remove 2-PG and process glycolate, but carbon accumulates in glycerate rather than entering the Calvin-cycle intermediate pool efficiently. The 2005 identification of Arabidopsis glycerate 3-kinase completed the assignment of the basic enzymatic steps of the core pathway. An important conceptual point is that detoxification of the original substrate and successful carbon recovery are distinct criteria. The ability to remove phosphoglycolate at one end does not guarantee replenishment of phosphoglycerate at the other.

7. Reassimilating ammonia: the GS–GOGAT connection

The carbon skeletons alone do not complete photorespiration. For every two molecules of glycine processed through the canonical mitochondrial reactions, one nitrogen atom is released as ammonia. Unless this nitrogen is recaptured, the leaf will lose usable amino nitrogen or experience a potentially disruptive buildup of ammonium. In photosynthetic tissues much of this reassimilation is performed by chloroplast glutamine synthetase (GS2) and ferredoxin-dependent glutamate synthase (Fd-GOGAT).

Glutamine synthetase combines glutamate and ammonium with an expenditure of ATP to form glutamine. Glutamine carries two nitrogen atoms, one in its amino group and one in its amide group. Glutamate synthase then transfers the amide nitrogen of glutamine to 2-oxoglutarate, producing two molecules of glutamate with the help of reducing equivalents. One glutamate replaces the one used by glutamine synthetase. The other is the net product available to donate nitrogen during glyoxylate transamination.

The overall cycle can be understood without treating nitrogen recovery as nitrogen fixation. Nitrogen originally present in glutamate passes to glycine during one aminotransferase reaction. It becomes ammonium during mitochondrial glycine cleavage. Chloroplast GS and GOGAT transfer it back into glutamate. The 2-oxoglutarate formed by the earlier transamination provides the carbon skeleton needed for this replenishment. The nitrogen is thus recycled, whereas ATP and reductant are expended in restoring its useful organic form.

A plant deficient in chloroplast glutamate synthase may have difficulty maintaining amino donors for glyoxylate transamination and preventing ammonium accumulation under conditions of high photorespiratory flux. A reduction of photorespiration can relieve this particular demand on nitrogen assimilation. Nevertheless, because GS and GOGAT also participate in the primary incorporation of inorganic nitrogen, the consequences of their disruption are not necessarily confined to photorespiration. Tissue type, isoenzymes and nitrogen nutrition must be considered.

The nitrogen pathway provides a natural connection to the subsequent study of plant nitrogen metabolism. That later topic examines how nitrate is reduced, how ammonium derived from the soil or biological fixation enters amino acids, and how carbon skeletons support nitrogen assimilation. Here the essential point is that photorespiration creates a large internal nitrogen recycling demand even when a leaf is not taking up any new nitrogen from its surroundings.

Following nitrogen and carbon skeletons through a complete turnover

For a closed turnover involving two glycolate molecules, it is useful to assign temporary labels to two nitrogen atoms. Call one nitrogen N₁, initially in glutamate, and the other N₂, initially in serine. The glutamate-dependent glyoxylate aminotransferase transfers N₁ to one glyoxylate molecule, leaving behind 2-oxoglutarate. The serine-dependent aminotransferase transfers N₂ to another glyoxylate molecule, leaving behind hydroxypyruvate. Both nitrogen atoms are now in glycine molecules. When a pair of glycines is processed in the mitochondrion, the nitrogen of the cleaved glycine is released and the nitrogen of the acceptor glycine remains in the newly formed serine.

If the glycine carrying N₁ happens to be the cleaved molecule, N₁ appears in the free ammonium pool while N₂ returns in serine. If the glycine carrying N₂ is cleaved, the label distribution is reversed. It would be incorrect to assert that the nitrogen of the glutamate-derived glycine must always be the one released. Glycine molecules can mix in the mitochondrial pool, and the enzyme recognises substrates by their chemical properties rather than by the original amino donor. The stoichiometric requirement is one nitrogen returned in serine and one nitrogen released per pair of glycines, not a fixed identity for each labelled atom.

The next nitrogen-recycling reactions reconnect the 2-oxoglutarate produced during transamination with the ammonium released in mitochondria. Glutamine synthetase first incorporates ammonium into the amide group of glutamine. This requires ATP because direct addition of ammonium to a carboxyl group in glutamate is energetically unfavourable under ordinary cellular conditions. ATP-driven activation makes formation of the amide bond possible. In the next step, GOGAT transfers the glutamine amide nitrogen to 2-oxoglutarate and reduces the resulting carbon–nitrogen intermediate to form glutamate. The original glutamine carbon skeleton also returns as glutamate.

Cancelling intermediates in the two equations shows that the net result is conversion of 2-oxoglutarate and ammonium into glutamate at the expense of ATP and two electrons, with proton and water terms depending on the biochemical convention used. Glutamine does not appear in the net equation because it is formed and subsequently consumed. One glutamate also cancels because it is required at the beginning and returned at the end. This cancellation does not mean that a cell could start with no glutamate or no glutamine synthetase. A net equation describes overall turnover, not every prerequisite required to initiate or sustain the reactions.

The combination of carbon and nitrogen recycling makes 2-oxoglutarate especially important. It serves both as a respiratory intermediate and as the carbon skeleton into which ammonium is assimilated. If the supply of suitable carbon skeletons becomes limited, the activity of nitrogen assimilation can be constrained even when inorganic nitrogen and the relevant enzymes are present. Under the idealized closed photorespiratory cycle, the 2-oxoglutarate released by the glutamate aminotransferase can contribute to the same recycling system. In a real cell it is part of a shared metabolic pool and may enter other reactions. Its concentration is controlled by several inputs and outputs.

A tracer experiment with labelled ammonium illustrates the connection. In an illuminated leaf supplied with a small pulse of nitrogen isotope label, label might appear rapidly in glutamine and subsequently in glutamate or other amino acids. To infer that the labelled ammonium entered through GS followed by GOGAT, the position of the label within glutamine and the time course of labelling are useful. A high glutamine labelling signal alone is not proof that all labelled nitrogen has been transferred to glutamate. A GOGAT defect could allow glutamine formation while slowing subsequent nitrogen transfer. The pattern of intermediates and the activities of both enzymes help distinguish assimilation from incomplete recycling.

Now consider a plant exposed to a short period of low carbon dioxide at normal oxygen. Rubisco oxygenation is favoured relative to carboxylation, increasing the relative demand on photorespiratory nitrogen recycling. If the increase is accompanied by a sufficiently large absolute flux, ammonium production can increase. A glutamine synthetase capacity sufficient for the initial condition may then be inadequate for the new demand. Conversely, if the carbon dioxide concentration becomes so low that RuBP regeneration and carbon turnover collapse, absolute ammonium production need not continue increasing. The importance of substrate supply and flux is identical to that in the carbon balance; a ratio alone does not determine how much nitrogen moves through the system per unit time.

One of the classical genetic observations relevant to this reasoning is the 1980 study by Somerville and Ogren of Arabidopsis mutants deficient in leaf glutamate synthase activity. Impaired photosynthesis under conditions promoting photorespiration provided evidence for the requirement of effective ammonia recovery in a green leaf. This finding complements mutants in the carbon-salvage enzymes because it shows that a defect in the nitrogen component can compromise carbon assimilation. It is not necessary to postulate an independent new photosynthetic carbon fixation reaction catalysed by glutamate synthase. The enzyme is necessary for maintaining the metabolic conditions in which the existing photosynthetic pathway can function.

The presence of several nitrogen-assimilating isoenzymes also matters when moving from a leaf to a whole plant. Chloroplast GS2 and ferredoxin-dependent GOGAT have prominent roles in green photosynthetic tissue; other GS and GOGAT forms serve other tissues and functions. A generalized statement that a defect in any GS isoform must eliminate all photorespiratory nitrogen recovery is too strong. The relevant prediction depends on localization, tissue expression, residual capacity and the prevailing metabolic conditions. An examination question specifying chloroplastic GS2 in a green C3 leaf is giving a more precise condition than one that merely refers to all glutamine synthetase activity.

8. Carbon, energy and oxygen accounting

A reliable way to solve photorespiration questions is to define an accounting boundary and a number of oxygenation events before counting molecules. For two oxygenations, ten carbon atoms enter as two RuBP molecules. Six atoms emerge directly in two molecules of 3-PGA, while the other four emerge in two molecules of 2-PG. The latter four enter the salvage route. Three are recovered in one additional 3-PGA and one is released as carbon dioxide. Thus the salvage route recovers 75 percent of the carbon entering as 2-PG, whereas 90 percent of the original RuBP carbon remains in the three combined 3-PGA molecules. These percentages refer to different denominators and neither describes the whole-leaf photosynthetic efficiency.

Consider twenty oxygenation events under the canonical pathway with no net export of intermediates. There are twenty molecules of 2-PG and twenty direct 3-PGA molecules. The salvage route returns ten additional 3-PGA molecules and releases ten carbon dioxide molecules. Glycine processing releases ten ammonium equivalents. The direct and recovered 3-PGA together contain ninety carbon atoms; the ten carbon dioxide molecules account for the remaining ten atoms of the twenty five-carbon RuBP molecules. The bookkeeping is complete without adding any newly fixed carbon.

Two oxygenations require one ATP at glycerate kinase and one ATP at glutamine synthetase if all released ammonium is reassimilated by GS–GOGAT. GOGAT also requires reducing power equivalent to two electrons per glutamate synthesis reaction. One NADH is produced by GDC and one is used at hydroxypyruvate reductase; these redox equivalents may balance on a whole-cell stoichiometric ledger but their compartment-specific availability still requires transfer mechanisms. The stated ATP count is a direct recovery-and-nitrogen-recycling count, not the complete energetic opportunity cost of oxygenation. Further expenditure is necessary to restore Calvin-cycle intermediates, and carbon dioxide lost from the salvage pathway may need to be fixed again.

Let Vc be the Rubisco carboxylation rate and Vo the oxygenation rate, both measured per unit leaf area per unit time. If the canonical salvage pathway releases one carbon dioxide molecule per two oxygenations, and Rd represents nonphotorespiratory respiration in the light, a simplified steady-state net assimilation relationship is A = Vc − 0.5Vo − Rd. All three rates must be expressed in compatible units. For Vc = 32, Vo = 8 and Rd = 1.5 micromoles per square metre per second, the predicted net carbon assimilation is 32 − 4 − 1.5 = 26.5 micromoles per square metre per second. This model assumes that the represented fluxes adequately account for the carbon sources and sinks under the measurement conditions.

When nonphotorespiratory respiration is excluded, the chloroplastic carbon dioxide compensation point in the simplified Rubisco model is denoted Γ*. At this point Vc = Vo/2, so Vo/Vc = 2. Substitution into the specificity relation gives Γ* = O/(2S). The actual gas-exchange compensation point of a leaf can differ because respiration and diffusion affect observed net exchange. Consequently, a carbon dioxide concentration at which the leaf absorbs no net carbon does not prove that Rubisco has stopped working. Carboxylation and carbon release may balance even while both processes are substantial.

Energy questions frequently include extra assumptions about the cost of producing new sugar phosphates or re-fixing carbon dioxide. Such assumptions must be followed explicitly. It is incorrect to add a standard mitochondrial ATP yield to the pathway merely because GDC produces NADH, then also count that NADH as fully available for HPR. The same reducing equivalents cannot simultaneously pay two unrelated bills unless an additional source supplies one of them. Energy balances should state whether they refer to gross reactions, net electron-equivalent balances or actual ATP generation in a particular compartment.

Counting productive carbon gain and the extra demand on light reactions

A common source of mistakes is failure to distinguish three different quantities: carbon fixation by Rubisco, carbon retained after photorespiratory loss and net gas exchange after other respiration. If a set of Rubisco reactions includes 100 carboxylations and 40 oxygenations, the gross fixation of external or internal inorganic carbon by carboxylation is 100 carbon atoms per corresponding set of events. The canonical salvage of the oxygenation products releases 20 carbon atoms as CO₂. Before subtracting nonphotorespiratory respiration, the net biochemical carbon gain is therefore 80. If nonphotorespiratory respiration releases a further five carbon equivalents, the measured net gain under the assumed closed balance is 75. Each quantity answers a different biological question.

The calculation can also be checked by reconstructing all the organic products of Rubisco. A total of 140 RuBP molecules, containing 700 carbon atoms, participate in the reactions. The 100 carboxylations introduce another 100 carbon atoms from CO₂. Immediately after Rubisco, the products are 240 direct 3-PGA molecules and 40 molecules of 2-PG. They contain 720 and 80 carbon atoms, respectively, accounting for the 800 initial carbon atoms. The salvage pathway processes the 40 molecules of 2-PG, producing 20 additional 3-PGA molecules and releasing 20 CO₂. There are then 260 molecules of 3-PGA containing 780 carbon atoms. If 700 carbon atoms are reserved to regenerate the RuBP acceptor pool, 80 remain as net carbon gain before other respiration. This independent carbon check confirms the shorter rate-based calculation.

An energy ledger must similarly begin by choosing a boundary. The simplest recovery-only accounting starts with two 2-PG molecules and ends with one additional 3-PGA and completed nitrogen recycling. It includes the ATP used by glycerate kinase and GS and the reducing equivalents used by GOGAT. It also identifies the NADH production and consumption associated with GDC and HPR. The accounting does not include the energy expended in the preceding light reactions to regenerate the RuBP that was oxygenated. It also stops before reduction of the returned 3-PGA to a triose phosphate. These omissions are deliberate features of a narrowly defined boundary, not evidence that those additional reactions are energy-free.

A broader textbook balance considers the ATP and reducing power needed to support a continuing Calvin–Benson cycle together with oxygenation. Each molecule of 3-PGA reduced to a triose phosphate requires one ATP and one NADPH. Regenerating RuBP requires ATP at phosphoribulokinase. In a simplified complete-turnover model, assume that all direct and returned 3-PGA molecules undergo reduction, RuBP regeneration is complete, ammonium is reassimilated and mitochondrial NADH formation offsets the HPR redox demand on an electron-equivalent basis. Then for every carboxylation there are two molecules of 3-PGA to reduce and one RuBP to regenerate, yielding three ATP and two NADPH required per carboxylation. This is the familiar idealized Calvin-cycle energy cost.

For every oxygenation, one direct 3-PGA is formed and half a 3-PGA is recovered on average in the canonical salvage route. The 1.5 molecules of 3-PGA require 1.5 ATP and 1.5 NADPH equivalents for reduction. Regenerating the RuBP consumed by that oxygenation adds one ATP. Glycerate kinase and GS together consume one ATP per oxygenation when averaged over paired events. GOGAT adds half of a two-electron reducing equivalent per oxygenation. The resulting illustrative complete-turnover ledger is 3.5 ATP and two NADPH equivalents per oxygenation, alongside the 3 ATP and two NADPH equivalents per carboxylation. Thus a convenient idealized model is total ATP demand = 3Vc + 3.5Vo, and total two-electron reductant demand = 2Vc + 2Vo, expressed using compatible flux units. A ferredoxin-based reductant demand is expressed here in NADPH equivalents for bookkeeping, not as a claim that GOGAT necessarily uses NADPH directly.

For Vc = 20 and Vo = 8, the predicted ATP demand under these assumptions is 60 + 28 = 88 units per area and time, and the two-electron reductant demand is 40 + 16 = 56 units. Before subtracting other respiration, net assimilation is 20 − 4 = 16 units. The illustrative ATP cost per unit of net retained carbon is 88/16 = 5.5, compared with three for a hypothetical system with no oxygenation or other respiration. The reductant cost per net carbon is 56/16 = 3.5, compared with two in the no-oxygenation model. This demonstrates why an oxygenation can require metabolic resources even though it fixes no new inorganic carbon.

Do not convert these numbers directly into a universal whole-plant photosynthetic efficiency. The model assumes canonical carbon recovery, complete reduction of PGA, specified nitrogen reassimilation, steady-state acceptor regeneration and no net removal of photorespiratory intermediates. Alternative routes of carbon flow, other reductant demands, cyclic electron flow, mitochondrial electron partitioning, transport costs and incomplete recovery alter the broader energy balance. Recent analysis of multiple routes through photorespiration explicitly emphasizes that the energy costs depend on the metabolic boundary and the fate of intermediates. This conditional framing is important in examinations because two apparently different ATP totals can both be correct if they count different endpoints.

The same logic distinguishes gross photorespiratory release from carbon actually escaping the leaf. A carbon dioxide molecule released in a mitochondrion may diffuse into a chloroplast and be fixed again. It then contributes to gross carboxylation and also to gross photorespiratory release. The simplified net assimilation relationship counts both processes and therefore cancels this internally recycled carbon appropriately when the balance is closed. Adding an extra correction for re-fixation to a ledger that already counts all carboxylation would double count its benefit. In contrast, a measurement restricted to externally supplied labelled carbon might require explicit modelling of internal recycling. The definition of each measured flux determines the correct calculation.

9. Environmental controls and comparative physiology

In a well-watered C3 leaf, stomata allow carbon dioxide to diffuse from the air into the intercellular spaces and subsequently to chloroplasts. When water becomes scarce, a reduction in stomatal aperture can limit water loss, but it also restricts carbon dioxide entry. The local ratio of oxygen to carbon dioxide at Rubisco may consequently increase. The effect is especially important when illumination maintains a demand for carbon dioxide and RuBP regeneration remains active. At very severe stress, however, absolute photosynthetic and photorespiratory fluxes may both decline. Relative oxygenation and absolute oxygenation must not be confused.

Higher leaf temperature generally favours the relative oxygenation reaction of Rubisco in C3 plants because carbon dioxide becomes less competitive with oxygen through changes in enzyme specificity and gas solubility. Temperature also affects stomatal conductance, enzyme activity, respiration and electron transport. A whole-leaf temperature response therefore integrates many factors. The common statement that photorespiration increases with temperature is most defensible when interpreted as a tendency toward a greater oxygenation fraction under otherwise comparable conditions rather than a universal prediction of an increasing absolute flux at every temperature.

Lowering oxygen around a C3 leaf while maintaining carbon dioxide availability decreases competition at Rubisco and often increases net carbon assimilation. Raising carbon dioxide can similarly suppress the oxygenation fraction. The two manipulations are related but not perfectly interchangeable in whole plants: oxygen is also a substrate for mitochondrial respiration and other oxidases, and elevated carbon dioxide influences stomata and metabolism over time. Very low oxygen can compromise energy metabolism, so a low-oxygen treatment should not be treated as a specific and unlimited inhibitor of photorespiration.

C4 plants reduce the oxygenation burden using a spatial carbon-concentrating mechanism. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase initially fixes bicarbonate into a four-carbon acid in mesophyll cells. Decarboxylation in or near bundle sheath cells supplies carbon dioxide to Rubisco. CAM plants use temporal separation, initially fixing inorganic carbon into organic acids at night and releasing carbon dioxide during the day. These mechanisms cost energy and depend on coordination between carbon transport, decarboxylation and Calvin-cycle capacity. They reduce, but do not universally eliminate, photorespiration.

The existence of C4 and CAM mechanisms is evidence that the balance between oxygenation cost and concentrating-mechanism cost matters under natural conditions. A high carbon dioxide concentration in cool conditions can reduce the advantage of paying for an additional concentrating mechanism, whereas warm conditions with restricted carbon dioxide availability often favour a reduction in the oxygenation burden. This ecological tradeoff is distinct from the requirement of all oxygenating Rubisco systems to deal with their residual 2-PG production.

10. Experimental evidence and data interpretation

Before the biochemical route was established, measurements of gas exchange showed that illuminated leaves could release carbon dioxide and consume oxygen even while their net exchange indicated photosynthesis. Varying the atmospheric composition allowed investigators to test whether the loss depended on oxygen availability. A stronger inference came from connecting this physiological response to specific chemical intermediates, organelle activities and enzyme defects. No single gas-exchange trace identifies every step of the pathway.

Somerville and Ogren studied Arabidopsis mutants lacking normal glycine decarboxylase activity. In the 1982 study, the mutant showed a requirement for elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide; its isolated mitochondria lacked normal glycine-dependent oxygen consumption and release of labelled carbon dioxide from labelled glycine. The combination of a conditional growth phenotype with an organelle-specific biochemical defect linked the mitochondrial reaction to leaf carbon metabolism. Elevated carbon dioxide relieved the demand placed on the missing photorespiratory activity. It did not constitute genetic repair.

A related study of mutants deficient in mitochondrial SHMT showed how failure of a downstream folate-dependent reaction can constrain glycine processing. The linked reaction sequence means that a defect in accepting methylene-THF into serine may limit continued GDC turnover as folate recycling and metabolite pools change. A simple rule that an enzyme defect affects only its immediate substrate is useful for initial predictions, but it does not capture feedback effects in a coupled pathway.

A useful teaching experiment compares wild type and a GDC-deficient line at ambient and elevated carbon dioxide, keeping light, temperature, water and developmental age as similar as possible. The predicted pattern is impaired photosynthesis and elevated glycine in the mutant under photorespiratory conditions, with a diminished difference between genotypes when oxygenation is suppressed. Measurement of glycine alone is insufficient to establish the enzyme defect. A direct enzyme assay, genotype confirmation and appropriate comparison of mitochondrial integrity provide stronger evidence.

Isotope labelling allows carbon fate to be tested. A short pulse of labelled carbon dioxide introduces label into photosynthetically produced compounds; subsequent changes in enrichment can be monitored in 3-PGA, glycolate, glycine and serine. This experiment can show transfer between metabolic pools, but the order of detectable enrichment depends on pool sizes, dilution and rates of exchange. A compound with a large unlabelled pool might show slower fractional labelling even though label reaches it rapidly. Isotope kinetics require both chemical identity and quantitative interpretation.

An illustrative gas exchange dataset might show net assimilation of 15 at ambient oxygen and 21 at reduced oxygen in a C3 plant at fixed external carbon dioxide and light. The increase is consistent with reduced photorespiratory carbon loss and changed competition at Rubisco. It is not justified to identify the difference of six directly with photorespiratory carbon dioxide production, because carboxylation may also change as oxygen competition declines. Independent estimates or a model with stated assumptions are required to infer fluxes.

Experiments on catalase deficient plants can establish the importance of peroxide management, while transporter mutants can show that exchange between organelles constrains the pathway. These results answer different questions. A catalase phenotype supports the need for management of reactive oxygen species; a glycolate/glycerate transporter phenotype supports a role for membrane transport; a GDC phenotype supports mitochondrial glycine metabolism. Their combination makes the multi-organelle model stronger than an observation of oxygen consumption alone.

11. Why an energetically costly pathway remains essential

Photorespiration can limit productivity in C3 plants, yet the pathway protects the carbon metabolism on which productivity depends. The apparent paradox disappears when the cost of Rubisco oxygenation is separated from the benefits of repairing its consequences. If oxygenation remains unchanged, shutting down salvage would increase accumulation of 2-PG and reduce the return of useful carbon. A more promising conceptual route to improving efficiency is to suppress oxygenation or provide a more efficient means of processing its products while retaining the necessary detoxification function.

In illuminated leaves, consumption of ATP and reducing equivalents in carbon metabolism can help balance the supply generated by the light reactions. When carbon dioxide becomes limiting, photorespiration may provide an additional metabolic sink and thereby contribute to maintenance of photosynthetic electron transport and redox balance. This possible protective role is condition-dependent. Photorespiration itself produces hydrogen peroxide through glycolate oxidase, so its effect on oxidative stress depends on peroxide detoxification, the availability of reductant, the strength of other electron sinks and the degree of environmental stress.

Glycine and serine link the salvage pathway to amino acid and one-carbon metabolism. Their pool sizes reflect both the cyclic recovery of carbon and the diversion of metabolites toward biosynthesis. The canonical accounting assumes that all appropriate intermediates return to the cycle. If serine is withdrawn for other reactions, that assumption no longer holds and the carbon, nitrogen and energy bookkeeping must be adjusted. A real cell can exchange carbon and nitrogen with multiple pathways while maintaining the core photorespiratory mechanism.

Respiration, photosynthesis and photorespiration therefore form an interacting system, not a chronological sequence of isolated reactions. Photosynthesis provides RuBP and energy; Rubisco oxygenation provides the unwanted substrate; peroxisomes and mitochondria reshape the carbon and nitrogen; chloroplasts receive the salvaged 3-PGA and recover ammonium. Mitochondrial electron transport helps maintain redox availability, but the carbon-recovery reactions remain distinct from oxidative phosphorylation. Following substrate identity, enzyme location and the fate of carbon, nitrogen and electrons is the most reliable way to solve integrated problems.

The principal examination skill is to reconstruct this pathway from constraints. Every two-carbon glycolate must retain its two carbons until glycine cleavage; a three-carbon product cannot form from a single two-carbon substrate without another carbon source; released nitrogen must be accounted for; phosphate transfer requires a donor; and the products must cross the correct membranes. A model consistent with these constraints can be tested against mutant data, gas exchange and labelling experiments. That is the difference between knowing the names of the pathway and understanding its operation.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Photorespiration fixes carbon dioxide

Oxygenation uses RuBP and O₂ without adding inorganic carbon. The term photosynthetic carbon metabolism can obscure this distinction; track the five input carbon atoms.

Oxygenation releases CO₂ immediately

Rubisco makes 3-PGA and 2-PG; the canonical CO₂ loss occurs during mitochondrial glycine cleavage. Distinguish initial reaction products from overall products.

Glycolate and glycerate are identical

Glycolate has two carbons and glycerate has three. Their similar names make them common distractors in compartment-sequence questions.

All photorespiration occurs in mitochondria

The canonical pathway spans chloroplast, peroxisome and mitochondrion. Mitochondrial CO₂ release is only one step.

2-PG enters the Calvin cycle directly

2-PG requires salvage; accumulation interferes with carbon metabolism. Identify the specific intermediate returned, 3-PGA.

Glycolate oxidase produces NADH

It transfers electrons to oxygen to form peroxide. NADH is produced in mitochondrial GDC activity; distinguish an oxidase from a dehydrogenase.

Catalase consumes oxygen

Catalase disproportionation produces oxygen and water. Count oxygen uptake and evolution separately in a gas balance.

One glycine gives one serine

Two glycine molecules provide the four carbons needed for one three-carbon serine and one carbon dioxide. Check both carbon and nitrogen.

SHMT releases the canonical CO₂

GDC releases carbon dioxide and ammonia; SHMT transfers the one-carbon group to a second glycine.

Ammonia is a net nitrogen gain

It originates in amino nitrogen already present and must be reassimilated. Internal recycling differs from primary fixation.

Glycerate kinase makes ATP

It consumes ATP to phosphorylate glycerate. Reverse-direction assumptions fail the chemical accounting.

The salvage pathway recovers 90% of 2-PG carbon

The correct salvage fraction is 75%; 90% refers to retention from two RuBP molecules including direct 3-PGA.

C4 plants lack Rubisco oxygenase

C4 carbon concentration suppresses competing oxygenation. Rubisco still possesses oxygenase capacity.

Higher temperature guarantees greater absolute flux

The relative oxygenation tendency generally rises, but total flux may decline if photosynthesis is severely constrained.

Blocking salvage increases productivity

If oxygenation continues, blocked recovery causes 2-PG accumulation and lost carbon. Reduce oxygenation or improve salvage instead.

A mutant rescued by CO₂ has regained its missing enzyme

High CO₂ can lower substrate burden without restoring the gene or enzyme. Confirm enzyme activity independently.

  4. Pi
Answer and reasoning

B. NAD⁺ — The GDC L-protein transfers reducing equivalents ultimately to NAD⁺, producing NADH.

B9. Which product carries the transferable one-carbon unit after glycine cleavage?

  1. RuBP
  2. 2-PG
  3. Methylene-THF
  4. Glycerate
Answer and reasoning

C. Methylene-THF — THF accepts the one-carbon group; SHMT transfers it to another glycine.

B10. What does catalase produce from two H₂O₂ molecules?

  1. Two O₂
  2. Two CO₂
  3. NADH and O₂
  4. Two H₂O and O₂
Answer and reasoning

D. Two H₂O and O₂ — Disproportionation yields two water molecules and one oxygen molecule.

B11. Which amino donor yields 2-oxoglutarate during transamination?

  1. Glutamate
  2. Glycine
  3. Alanine
  4. Glycerate
Answer and reasoning

A. Glutamate — Glutamate:glyoxylate aminotransferase transfers the nitrogen from glutamate to glyoxylate.

B12. What is the main peroxisomal HPR substrate?

  1. Glycerate
  2. Hydroxypyruvate
  3. 2-PG
  4. RuBP
Answer and reasoning

B. Hydroxypyruvate — HPR reduces the carbonyl group of hydroxypyruvate to form glycerate.

B13. Which gas competes with CO₂ for the Rubisco reaction?

  1. N₂
  2. H₂
  3. O₂
  4. CH₄
Answer and reasoning

C. O₂ — The activated RuBP intermediate can react with oxygen as an alternative substrate.

B14. The canonical photorespiratory CO₂ release is associated with:

  1. PGLP
  2. Glycerate kinase
  3. Catalase
  4. GDC
Answer and reasoning

D. GDC — Decarboxylation of one glycine releases a carboxyl carbon as carbon dioxide.

B15. Which is the major returned Calvin-cycle intermediate?

  1. 3-PGA
  2. 2-PG
  3. Glycine
  4. Oxaloacetate
Answer and reasoning

A. 3-PGA — Glycerate phosphorylation returns 3-PGA to the chloroplast carbon pathway.

B16. The carbon-concentrating mechanism in C4 plants primarily:

  1. Eliminates Rubisco
  2. Raises CO₂ near Rubisco
  3. Blocks catalase
  4. Prevents all mitochondrial respiration
Answer and reasoning

B. Raises CO₂ near Rubisco — Higher local carbon dioxide reduces the relative oxygenation reaction without removing Rubisco.

B17. Which reaction directly recovers ammonium using reductant after GS?

  1. PGLP
  2. GOX
  3. GOGAT
  4. Catalase
Answer and reasoning

C. GOGAT — GOGAT transfers glutamine nitrogen to 2-oxoglutarate using reducing equivalents.

B18. The P-protein of GDC performs the initial:

  1. Glycerate phosphorylation
  2. Peroxide disproportionation
  3. Glutamine hydrolysis
  4. Glycine decarboxylation
Answer and reasoning

D. Glycine decarboxylation — P-protein catalyses the PLP-dependent removal of the glycine carboxyl group.

B19. Photorespiration is distinguished from TCA respiration because it starts with:

  1. Rubisco oxygenation
  2. Acetyl-CoA oxidation
  3. Succinate oxidation
  4. ATP hydrolysis
Answer and reasoning

A. Rubisco oxygenation — The origin of photorespiratory carbon is the 2-PG product of Rubisco oxygenation.

B20. Which molecule returns from the peroxisome for chloroplast phosphorylation?

  1. Glyoxylate
  2. Glycerate
  3. Glycine
  4. Serine
Answer and reasoning

B. Glycerate — Glycerate is the three-carbon substrate of chloroplast glycerate kinase.

Part C: Analytical questions

C1. For 36 oxygenations, how many additional 3-PGA molecules return through canonical salvage?

  1. 9
  2. 18
  3. 36
  4. 72
Answer and reasoning

B. 18 — Two oxygenations provide two 2-PG molecules, yielding one recovered 3-PGA. 36/2 = 18.

C2. For 36 oxygenations, how many total 3-PGA molecules are obtained directly and by salvage?

  1. 18
  2. 36
  3. 54
  4. 72
Answer and reasoning

C. 54 — There are 36 direct and 18 recovered 3-PGA molecules. The sum is 54.

C3. With Vc=40, Vo=12 and Rd=2 in compatible flux units, what is A?

  1. 24
  2. 26
  3. 34
  4. 32
Answer and reasoning

D. 32 — A=40−12/2−2=32. Subtracting all oxygenation events rather than half would overcount CO₂ release.

C4. With O=200, C=10 and S=80 in consistent concentration units, Vo/Vc equals:

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.50
  3. 2.5
  4. 4
Answer and reasoning

A. 0.25 — 200/(10×80)=0.25. The ratio compares rates, not absolute flux.

C5. At fixed O and S, local CO₂ doubles. What happens to Vo/Vc?

  1. Doubles
  2. Halves
  3. Unchanged
  4. Becomes zero
Answer and reasoning

B. Halves — The ratio is inversely proportional to C, so it halves under the stated assumptions.

C6. A GDC mutant accumulates glycine in air but grows better at elevated CO₂. Best explanation?

  1. CO₂ repairs DNA
  2. CO₂ replaces NADH
  3. Rubisco oxygenation is suppressed
  4. Catalase is deleted
Answer and reasoning

C. Rubisco oxygenation is suppressed — High CO₂ reduces the influx of photorespiratory substrate and hence the need for GDC.

C7. A leaf loses HPR1 but has a functional cytosolic HPR2. What is the strongest inference?

  1. All salvage must stop
  2. Rubisco ceases functioning
  3. Glycolate oxidase becomes catalase
  4. Alternative reduction may partly compensate
Answer and reasoning

D. Alternative reduction may partly compensate — A parallel hydroxypyruvate reduction route may preserve some flux; its capacity requires measurement.

C8. The carboxyl carbon of donor glycine is labelled immediately before GDC cleavage. Primary labelled product?

  1. CO₂
  2. NH₃
  3. ATP
  4. NADH
Answer and reasoning

A. CO₂ — GDC releases the carboxyl carbon of the cleaved glycine as CO₂.

C9. If the alpha carbon of donor glycine is labelled, which carbon product can receive it through THF?

  1. Peroxide
  2. Serine side-chain carbon
  3. Oxygen
  4. Inorganic phosphate
Answer and reasoning

B. Serine side-chain carbon — The remaining carbon becomes methylene-THF and then the additional carbon of serine.

C10. A phosphoglycolate phosphatase defect is induced in the light. Earliest expected substrate change?

  1. Glycerate falls to zero instantly
  2. Serine becomes CO₂ directly
  3. 2-PG tends to accumulate
  4. ATP production necessarily doubles
Answer and reasoning

C. 2-PG tends to accumulate — Formation by Rubisco can continue while 2-PG hydrolysis slows; downstream responses follow.

C11. Two glycolate oxidations with complete catalase removal produce what net peroxisomal O₂ uptake?

  1. 0
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. 1
Answer and reasoning

D. 1 — GOX consumes two O₂; catalase returns one O₂ from two H₂O₂. Net consumption is one.

C12. For ten canonical salvage turnovers, ATP directly used by GLYK and GS totals:

  1. 20
  2. 10
  3. 40
  4. 0
Answer and reasoning

A. 20 — Each turnover processes two 2-PG and consumes one ATP in GLYK and one in GS.

C13. If net oxygenation fraction increases while absolute Vc decreases strongly, which statement follows?

  1. Vo always rises
  2. Vo may rise or fall
  3. Vo always falls
  4. Vo becomes zero
Answer and reasoning

B. Vo may rise or fall — Vo is the product of Vc and Vo/Vc. Opposite changes in the two factors leave the result uncertain.

C14. A reduced oxygen treatment increases net assimilation by six units. What can be concluded?

  1. GDC flux was exactly six
  2. Rubisco was inactive initially
  3. The response is consistent with reduced photorespiration but not a direct flux measure
  4. Mitochondrial respiration is absent
Answer and reasoning

C. The response is consistent with reduced photorespiration but not a direct flux measure — Both carboxylation and oxygenation may change; additional constraints are needed to estimate GDC carbon loss.

C15. A mutant has normal GDC but defective mitochondrial SHMT. Which pool tends to rise under high oxygenation?

  1. Water
  2. Pi
  3. O₂
  4. Glycine
Answer and reasoning

D. Glycine — Limited coupled conversion of glycine to serine can restrict turnover and cause glycine accumulation.

C16. In a steady-state chloroplast model excluding Rd, what is Vc/Vo at Γ*?

  1. 0.5
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 4
Answer and reasoning

A. 0.5 — Net assimilation is zero when Vc=0.5Vo; gross reactions can continue.

C17. A transporter defect traps glycolate inside chloroplasts. Which is most direct?

  1. Increased peroxisomal substrate delivery
  2. Reduced glycolate transfer to peroxisomes
  3. GDC becomes chloroplastic
  4. GOX produces ATP
Answer and reasoning

B. Reduced glycolate transfer to peroxisomes — The defect restricts intercompartmental delivery even if downstream enzymes retain catalytic activity.

C18. For two oxygenations, what fraction of initial RuBP carbon is retained in combined 3-PGA?

  1. 50%
  2. 75%
  3. 90%
  4. 100%
Answer and reasoning

C. 90% — Two direct 3-PGA plus one recovered 3-PGA contain nine of ten input carbons.

C19. A GS inhibitor reduces ammonium assimilation during high photorespiration. Which immediate risk increases?

  1. New nitrogen fixation
  2. Glycerate export always stops first
  3. Rubisco changes into PEPC
  4. Ammonium accumulation
Answer and reasoning

D. Ammonium accumulation — GDC releases ammonium equivalents while GS-mediated recapture is compromised.

C20. An ATP ledger counts GDC NADH both as fully respired and fully used by HPR. What is wrong?

  1. Double counting reducing equivalents
  2. Incorrect carbon count only
  3. ATP cannot be produced in mitochondria
  4. HPR produces NADH
Answer and reasoning

A. Double counting reducing equivalents — A single reducing equivalent cannot be spent in both processes without a separate replenishing source.

Revision and active recall
