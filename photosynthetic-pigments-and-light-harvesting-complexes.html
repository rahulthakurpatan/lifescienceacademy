1. The biological problem: harvesting light without wasting it

A green leaf is a living solar-energy converter, but it does not behave like a green-painted surface that simply becomes warm in sunshine. When a chloroplast absorbs a suitable photon, some of the energy can be directed toward a molecular reaction that separates electrical charge. That event starts the conversion of radiant energy into chemical free energy. Later reactions can use the resulting electron flow to build a proton gradient and reduce a soluble electron acceptor. Carbon metabolism uses those resources to incorporate inorganic carbon into organic compounds. The first problem is therefore not how to make sugar directly from light, but how to intercept light and convey its energy to a site where chemistry can begin.

Imagine that every chloroplast contained only the small number of pigments strictly necessary for the initial electron-transfer reactions. Even if each reaction center were exceptionally efficient at charge separation, many photons would pass between centers without being intercepted. Under dim light a center might wait a long time between successful absorption events. Increasing the number of reaction centers would increase light interception, but each new center would require its own carefully assembled set of proteins, cofactors and downstream connections. An alternative is to associate a relatively large collection of light-absorbing molecules with each center. When any member of this collection becomes excited, its excitation can be conveyed toward the same photochemical machinery. This arrangement is a photosynthetic antenna.

The term antenna describes a functional system rather than a single chemical species. It includes pigments held by proteins, their spatial organization, the interactions between neighboring pigments, and the connections between peripheral and core antennae. The reaction center itself is also a pigment–protein complex, but its defining activity is a light-driven electron transfer that establishes a charge-separated state. Antenna and center are parts of one larger photosystem. The distinction is based on the predominant function of a component, not on whether it can absorb light. A reaction-center pigment can absorb a photon directly, and antenna pigments possess electrons and electronic excited states just as reaction-center pigments do.

This division of labor solves more than an optical problem. The sunlight falling on a leaf contains many wavelengths, while a single pigment absorbs some wavelengths much more efficiently than others. Combining chemically different pigments broadens the portion of the spectrum that the photosynthetic apparatus can intercept. Holding those pigments in proteins provides routes along which excitation energy can travel. The arrangement also gives the organism a way to change how strongly light is harvested and how much absorbed energy is dissipated rather than used. The best antenna for a shaded leaf is not necessarily the best antenna for a leaf that spends midday in full sunlight.

Photosynthesis is commonly summarized by an overall chemical equation relating carbon dioxide, water, carbohydrate and oxygen. That equation is useful for carbon and oxygen accounting, but it hides the physical separation between light capture and carbon assimilation. A photon has no carbon to donate to a sugar molecule. Its contribution is energy. In oxygenic photosynthesis the electrons used in the light reactions ultimately originate from water; light supplies the energy needed to move them into useful redox states. The carbon atoms of carbohydrate originate from carbon dioxide. Keeping matter and energy distinct prevents confusion later when following the two photosystems and the Calvin cycle.

There is also a limit to useful interception. If a photon excites a pigment while the relevant reaction centers cannot accept another excitation productively, absorbing additional photons may make little immediate contribution to chemical energy storage. The excitation can be lost as heat or fluorescence, or under unfavorable circumstances lead to reactive excited species and oxidative damage. Plants regulate this competition. A high rate of absorption is beneficial only when it is coupled to an appropriate rate of productive photochemistry or protective energy dissipation. The detailed molecular regulation of excess-light protection will be developed in a later chapter; here it is sufficient to recognize that the light-harvesting network is both a collector and a regulated distributor of energy.

A second distinction concerns levels of observation. The color of a leaf describes the light emerging from it; pigment absorption describes a molecular or tissue optical property; oxygen evolution and carbon assimilation describe biological activities. A leaf that looks dark green does not necessarily possess the highest carbon-assimilation rate. A sample that absorbs strongly may contain inactive centers. A sample with weak absorption may convert its few absorbed photons very efficiently. An examination question can exploit any of these distinctions by providing data at one level and asking what may be inferred at another. The correct inference follows the sequence from incident light through absorption, excitation transfer, reaction-center trapping and subsequent chemistry, rather than assuming that every measured property represents the entire process.

The organization of this chapter follows that sequence. We first describe the physical quantities used to characterize light, then examine the molecules that absorb it and the evidence for their participation in photosynthesis. With that background, the architecture and transfer mechanism of the antenna become intelligible. The final transition is from migration of excitation to charge separation in photosystems II and I. The chemical fate of the separated electrons belongs to the following chapters.

2. Photons, wavelength and the energy of light

Light is electromagnetic radiation. Its electric and magnetic fields vary as the radiation propagates, and its interaction with matter can also be described in terms of discrete packets of energy called photons. A wavelength is the distance between successive corresponding points in a periodic wave. Frequency is the number of oscillations occurring per second. For light in vacuum, the speed of propagation c equals wavelength multiplied by frequency. A frequency of one hertz means one oscillation per second. In plant physiology, wavelengths are usually reported in nanometers because visible light has wavelengths of a few hundred billionths of a meter.

The energy of one photon is E = hν, where h is the Planck constant and ν is the frequency. Substitution of ν = c/λ gives E = hc/λ, provided λ denotes the corresponding vacuum wavelength. This is an inverse relationship: doubling the wavelength halves the energy of an individual photon. A blue photon therefore has more energy than a red photon. The distinction between energy per photon and the number of photons arriving is fundamental. Light intensity expressed in watts measures energy delivered per unit time; a photon flux measures the number or amount of photons delivered per unit time. A red and a blue beam of equal power do not contain equal numbers of photons. Conversely, equal photon fluxes of those beams do not carry equal radiant power.

A conventional range used in plant physiology is photosynthetically active radiation, or PAR, approximately 400–700 nm. This range is a convenient measurement convention and broadly encompasses the wavelengths driving the light reactions in typical green plants. It is not an absolute biological boundary. Longer wavelengths can contribute in appropriate spectral combinations, and shorter wavelengths may be absorbed by pigments and other compounds but need not have the same usefulness or safety. Different photosynthetic organisms have evolved pigment systems adapted to different light environments. The meaningful question is whether absorption at a particular wavelength can populate an excited state that communicates with a functioning reaction center.

The energy needed to promote a pigment molecule from its electronic ground state into an excited state is determined by its molecular electronic structure. Absorption is selective because molecules do not possess a continuous range of arbitrary electronic energy levels. In a real pigment the electronic transition is accompanied by a range of vibrational possibilities and influenced by its solvent or protein environment. Consequently, the absorption spectrum consists of bands with finite widths rather than infinitely narrow lines. Chlorophyll has strong visible absorption bands in the blue and red regions, whereas many carotenoids possess broad absorption bands in the blue to blue-green region. The difference in their spectra follows from different conjugated electronic structures.

Consider a pigment excited by a blue photon rather than a red one. The more energetic photon can populate a higher excited electronic or vibrational state. The pigment usually relaxes very rapidly toward its lowest excited singlet state, transferring excess energy to surrounding molecular motion. Excitation energy transferred toward a reaction center generally comes from this relaxed excited state. It would therefore be incorrect to suppose that absorption of one blue photon necessarily produces two useful reaction-center excitations merely because blue light is more energetic. Ordinary photosynthetic light harvesting does not routinely divide the energy of one visible photon into two independent photochemical turnovers. Excess energy can be dissipated before the excitation is used.

Photons are counted on a molar scale when comparing light with quantities of metabolites. One mole contains the Avogadro number of photons. Multiplying the energy of one photon by the Avogadro constant gives the energy per mole of photons. Using the exact SI constants h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 299792458 m s⁻¹ and Nₐ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, the product Nₐhc is approximately 0.1196266 J m mol⁻¹. Inserting a wavelength expressed in nanometers and converting joules to kilojoules gives the convenient relationship Eₘ ≈ 119626.6/λ(nm) kJ mol⁻¹. A mole of 450 nm photons carries about 266 kJ, while a mole of 680 nm photons carries about 176 kJ. These values refer to incident radiant energy, not the amount of ATP generated.

For whole leaves, photosynthetic photon flux density is frequently reported in micromoles of photons per square meter per second. This is useful because the initial photochemical event involves individual absorbed photons. Suppose a monochromatic 660 nm beam delivers 100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Its molar photon energy is about 181.25 kJ mol⁻¹, so the radiant power density is approximately 18.1 J m⁻² s⁻¹, or 18.1 W m⁻². If only 80% of its photons are absorbed, the absorbed photon flux density is 80 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The remaining 20 µmol may be reflected or transmitted. To infer the photochemical rate, one must still know what fraction of absorbed excitations produce the relevant outcome.

Energy conservation alone places bounds on photosynthesis, but does not determine its efficiency. A particular photochemical conversion must be energetically possible, and its products must have sufficient free energy to support subsequent reactions. At the same time, useful conversion competes kinetically with other routes of excitation decay. A process may be energetically allowed yet inefficient if excitation is lost before it reaches an active center. Conversely, efficient transfer of excitation does not guarantee fast carbon assimilation if downstream metabolism is constrained. Energy and rate are related but noninterchangeable aspects of the biological problem.

A spectrally mixed lamp requires special care in calculations. The radiant power contributed by each wavelength interval depends on the energy per photon in that interval. A single conversion factor based on 550 nm cannot give an exact photon flux for an arbitrary mixture of wavelengths. It is valid only as an approximation under an explicitly stated spectral assumption. In a simple examination problem the beam is often monochromatic, which makes the conversion direct. When the problem compares white light and red light without providing the spectral distribution or photon flux, a numerical comparison of their photochemical effects may not be justified.

The duration of illumination matters independently of instantaneous flux. At a constant photon flux density of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, an exposure of one minute supplies 6000 µmol photons per square meter, or 0.006 mol m⁻². A shorter brighter exposure can supply the same number of photons, but the biological outcome need not be identical. Reaction-center turnover, acclimation and dissipation may respond differently to a high instantaneous excitation rate. At very low intensity and over short intervals one may approximate proportionality between the number of absorbed photons and the number of productive events. That approximation must be reconsidered when centers become saturated or metabolism changes.

These physical quantities are best treated as tools for understanding the biological sequence, not as a separate exercise in memorizing formulas. Wavelength determines the energy of an individual photon, spectral absorption determines the probability that the photon interacts with the antenna, photon flux determines how often such interactions can occur, and photochemical yield determines how effectively absorbed excitation contributes to an outcome. Each quantity answers a different question.

3. Absorption, reflection, transmission and the color of leaves

When light strikes a leaf, not all of it enters the photosynthetic machinery. Some photons are reflected at the surface or after scattering inside the tissue. Others leave the opposite surface and contribute to transmission. Still others are absorbed by molecules in the leaf. In an idealized energy accounting at a given wavelength, the incident fraction equals the sum of reflected, transmitted and absorbed fractions. If the measured reflectance is R and transmittance is T, both expressed as fractions of incident light, the absorptance is 1 − R − T. The equation assumes the measurements account for the relevant outgoing light and use the same wavelength and geometry. Absorptance is dimensionless and cannot exceed unity.

A green leaf usually reflects and transmits a larger fraction of green light than it does of wavelengths near the strong red and blue absorption bands of its chlorophylls. The light reaching our eyes is therefore enriched in green wavelengths. This explains its appearance without requiring the claim that green photons are unusable. A leaf contains many chlorophyll molecules and internal scattering structures, so a photon that is not absorbed on its first passage may encounter pigments again. Green light can penetrate relatively deeply into leaf tissue and can drive photosynthesis where it is absorbed. The proportion of green light absorbed by an intact leaf is much higher than one might infer from the minimum of a dilute chlorophyll-solution spectrum alone.

The distinction between an isolated pigment and a whole leaf is important. A dilute pigment extract can be placed in a transparent cuvette of known optical path length. Within the conditions of Beer–Lambert behavior, absorbance A is the base-ten logarithm of incident intensity divided by transmitted intensity. For a single absorbing species A = εcl, where ε is its molar absorption coefficient at the specified wavelength, c is the molar concentration, and l is the optical path length in units compatible with ε. In this simple geometry, transmittance is 10⁻ᴬ. A sample of absorbance one transmits one tenth of the incident light. If reflection and other losses are negligible, it absorbs ninety percent. Absorbance is logarithmic, whereas absorptance is a linear fraction.

This difference creates a familiar quantitative trap. An absorbance of 0.5 does not mean that half the incident photons are absorbed. The transmittance is 10⁻⁰·⁵, approximately 0.316; the absorbed fraction under the stated assumptions is approximately 0.684. Doubling the concentration of a pigment from an initial absorbance of 0.5 to 1.0 doubles absorbance, but it increases the absorbed fraction from about 68.4% to 90%, not to 136.8%. The incident fraction sets a physical upper bound. Absorption by dense tissues therefore does not necessarily grow linearly with pigment concentration, even when molecular absorbances obey the Beer–Lambert relation in an appropriately dilute solution.

In a leaf, scattering, orientation of internal structures, pigment packaging and heterogeneity complicate the use of a simple solution formula. Palisade and spongy mesophyll contribute different optical paths. Epidermal properties and internal air spaces can influence the distribution of light within the tissue. In addition, pigments are embedded in membrane-associated protein complexes, not dissolved as freely moving isolated molecules in a uniform solvent. Their absorption bands may be shifted relative to those measured in an extract. An experiment that seeks to compare spectral absorptance of intact leaves should therefore measure both reflection and transmission, rather than applying a solution extinction coefficient directly to a leaf thickness.

An absorption spectrum displays the wavelength dependence of optical absorption. When individual pigments are measured under specified conditions, peaks indicate wavelengths absorbed relatively strongly. An absorption peak is not an emission peak, and it is not automatically the wavelength at which photosynthesis proceeds fastest. Absorption provides the initial excitation, while photochemistry depends on whether the excitation travels successfully to a center and whether that center can carry out productive charge separation. A pigment unrelated to the reaction centers could absorb strongly and still make no direct contribution to photosynthetic electron flow.

The comparison of leaves grown under different light conditions illustrates this point. Two leaves could exhibit similar absorptance over the visible range yet contain different numbers of active reaction centers per unit area. One might have a large antenna feeding comparatively few centers; the other might distribute its pigments among more centers. At weak light the large effective antenna could be advantageous per center, whereas at high light the larger number of centers might provide more potential turnover capacity. Without measuring center activity, the spectrum alone cannot establish which leaf has the greater photosynthetic rate. The same distinction appears in mutant analyses, where loss of a pigment changes the optical signal but may also destabilize the complexes to which that pigment belonged.

A healthy leaf absorbs light for several other purposes as well. Photoreceptors absorb particular wavelengths and initiate developmental or physiological signaling. Some epidermal pigments attenuate potentially damaging radiation. Pigments involved in signaling or screening need not transfer excitation to a photosystem. Thus, a change in whole-leaf absorption can result from altered pigment composition outside the thylakoid antenna, and an investigator should avoid assigning every optical change to chlorophyll or carotenoids. The study of light as a signal is treated separately in sensory photobiology.

A useful way to reason about spectral data is to identify the sample and the denominator before interpreting the graph. A graph of absorbance for an extract normalized to its highest peak conveys spectral shape but not absolute pigment content. A graph of whole-leaf absorptance gives the fraction of incident photons intercepted by the tissue, including nonphotosynthetic absorbers. A graph of oxygen production per absorbed photon addresses a biological yield. The three can be compared meaningfully only when their measurement conditions and normalization are understood. Their differences are as informative as their similarities.
