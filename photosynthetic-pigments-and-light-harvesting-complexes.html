1. The biological problem: harvesting light without wasting it

A green leaf is a living solar-energy converter, but it does not behave like a green-painted surface that simply becomes warm in sunshine. When a chloroplast absorbs a suitable photon, some of the energy can be directed toward a molecular reaction that separates electrical charge. That event starts the conversion of radiant energy into chemical free energy. Later reactions can use the resulting electron flow to build a proton gradient and reduce a soluble electron acceptor. Carbon metabolism uses those resources to incorporate inorganic carbon into organic compounds. The first problem is therefore not how to make sugar directly from light, but how to intercept light and convey its energy to a site where chemistry can begin.

Imagine that every chloroplast contained only the small number of pigments strictly necessary for the initial electron-transfer reactions. Even if each reaction center were exceptionally efficient at charge separation, many photons would pass between centers without being intercepted. Under dim light a center might wait a long time between successful absorption events. Increasing the number of reaction centers would increase light interception, but each new center would require its own carefully assembled set of proteins, cofactors and downstream connections. An alternative is to associate a relatively large collection of light-absorbing molecules with each center. When any member of this collection becomes excited, its excitation can be conveyed toward the same photochemical machinery. This arrangement is a photosynthetic antenna.

The term antenna describes a functional system rather than a single chemical species. It includes pigments held by proteins, their spatial organization, the interactions between neighboring pigments, and the connections between peripheral and core antennae. The reaction center itself is also a pigment–protein complex, but its defining activity is a light-driven electron transfer that establishes a charge-separated state. Antenna and center are parts of one larger photosystem. The distinction is based on the predominant function of a component, not on whether it can absorb light. A reaction-center pigment can absorb a photon directly, and antenna pigments possess electrons and electronic excited states just as reaction-center pigments do.

This division of labor solves more than an optical problem. The sunlight falling on a leaf contains many wavelengths, while a single pigment absorbs some wavelengths much more efficiently than others. Combining chemically different pigments broadens the portion of the spectrum that the photosynthetic apparatus can intercept. Holding those pigments in proteins provides routes along which excitation energy can travel. The arrangement also gives the organism a way to change how strongly light is harvested and how much absorbed energy is dissipated rather than used. The best antenna for a shaded leaf is not necessarily the best antenna for a leaf that spends midday in full sunlight.

Photosynthesis is commonly summarized by an overall chemical equation relating carbon dioxide, water, carbohydrate and oxygen. That equation is useful for carbon and oxygen accounting, but it hides the physical separation between light capture and carbon assimilation. A photon has no carbon to donate to a sugar molecule. Its contribution is energy. In oxygenic photosynthesis the electrons used in the light reactions ultimately originate from water; light supplies the energy needed to move them into useful redox states. The carbon atoms of carbohydrate originate from carbon dioxide. Keeping matter and energy distinct prevents confusion later when following the two photosystems and the Calvin cycle.

There is also a limit to useful interception. If a photon excites a pigment while the relevant reaction centers cannot accept another excitation productively, absorbing additional photons may make little immediate contribution to chemical energy storage. The excitation can be lost as heat or fluorescence, or under unfavorable circumstances lead to reactive excited species and oxidative damage. Plants regulate this competition. A high rate of absorption is beneficial only when it is coupled to an appropriate rate of productive photochemistry or protective energy dissipation. The detailed molecular regulation of excess-light protection will be developed in a later chapter; here it is sufficient to recognize that the light-harvesting network is both a collector and a regulated distributor of energy.

A second distinction concerns levels of observation. The color of a leaf describes the light emerging from it; pigment absorption describes a molecular or tissue optical property; oxygen evolution and carbon assimilation describe biological activities. A leaf that looks dark green does not necessarily possess the highest carbon-assimilation rate. A sample that absorbs strongly may contain inactive centers. A sample with weak absorption may convert its few absorbed photons very efficiently. An examination question can exploit any of these distinctions by providing data at one level and asking what may be inferred at another. The correct inference follows the sequence from incident light through absorption, excitation transfer, reaction-center trapping and subsequent chemistry, rather than assuming that every measured property represents the entire process.

The organization of this chapter follows that sequence. We first describe the physical quantities used to characterize light, then examine the molecules that absorb it and the evidence for their participation in photosynthesis. With that background, the architecture and transfer mechanism of the antenna become intelligible. The final transition is from migration of excitation to charge separation in photosystems II and I. The chemical fate of the separated electrons belongs to the following chapters.

2. Photons, wavelength and the energy of light

Light is electromagnetic radiation. Its electric and magnetic fields vary as the radiation propagates, and its interaction with matter can also be described in terms of discrete packets of energy called photons. A wavelength is the distance between successive corresponding points in a periodic wave. Frequency is the number of oscillations occurring per second. For light in vacuum, the speed of propagation c equals wavelength multiplied by frequency. A frequency of one hertz means one oscillation per second. In plant physiology, wavelengths are usually reported in nanometers because visible light has wavelengths of a few hundred billionths of a meter.

The energy of one photon is E = hν, where h is the Planck constant and ν is the frequency. Substitution of ν = c/λ gives E = hc/λ, provided λ denotes the corresponding vacuum wavelength. This is an inverse relationship: doubling the wavelength halves the energy of an individual photon. A blue photon therefore has more energy than a red photon. The distinction between energy per photon and the number of photons arriving is fundamental. Light intensity expressed in watts measures energy delivered per unit time; a photon flux measures the number or amount of photons delivered per unit time. A red and a blue beam of equal power do not contain equal numbers of photons. Conversely, equal photon fluxes of those beams do not carry equal radiant power.

A conventional range used in plant physiology is photosynthetically active radiation, or PAR, approximately 400–700 nm. This range is a convenient measurement convention and broadly encompasses the wavelengths driving the light reactions in typical green plants. It is not an absolute biological boundary. Longer wavelengths can contribute in appropriate spectral combinations, and shorter wavelengths may be absorbed by pigments and other compounds but need not have the same usefulness or safety. Different photosynthetic organisms have evolved pigment systems adapted to different light environments. The meaningful question is whether absorption at a particular wavelength can populate an excited state that communicates with a functioning reaction center.

The energy needed to promote a pigment molecule from its electronic ground state into an excited state is determined by its molecular electronic structure. Absorption is selective because molecules do not possess a continuous range of arbitrary electronic energy levels. In a real pigment the electronic transition is accompanied by a range of vibrational possibilities and influenced by its solvent or protein environment. Consequently, the absorption spectrum consists of bands with finite widths rather than infinitely narrow lines. Chlorophyll has strong visible absorption bands in the blue and red regions, whereas many carotenoids possess broad absorption bands in the blue to blue-green region. The difference in their spectra follows from different conjugated electronic structures.

Consider a pigment excited by a blue photon rather than a red one. The more energetic photon can populate a higher excited electronic or vibrational state. The pigment usually relaxes very rapidly toward its lowest excited singlet state, transferring excess energy to surrounding molecular motion. Excitation energy transferred toward a reaction center generally comes from this relaxed excited state. It would therefore be incorrect to suppose that absorption of one blue photon necessarily produces two useful reaction-center excitations merely because blue light is more energetic. Ordinary photosynthetic light harvesting does not routinely divide the energy of one visible photon into two independent photochemical turnovers. Excess energy can be dissipated before the excitation is used.

Photons are counted on a molar scale when comparing light with quantities of metabolites. One mole contains the Avogadro number of photons. Multiplying the energy of one photon by the Avogadro constant gives the energy per mole of photons. Using the exact SI constants h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 299792458 m s⁻¹ and Nₐ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, the product Nₐhc is approximately 0.1196266 J m mol⁻¹. Inserting a wavelength expressed in nanometers and converting joules to kilojoules gives the convenient relationship Eₘ ≈ 119626.6/λ(nm) kJ mol⁻¹. A mole of 450 nm photons carries about 266 kJ, while a mole of 680 nm photons carries about 176 kJ. These values refer to incident radiant energy, not the amount of ATP generated.

For whole leaves, photosynthetic photon flux density is frequently reported in micromoles of photons per square meter per second. This is useful because the initial photochemical event involves individual absorbed photons. Suppose a monochromatic 660 nm beam delivers 100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. Its molar photon energy is about 181.25 kJ mol⁻¹, so the radiant power density is approximately 18.1 J m⁻² s⁻¹, or 18.1 W m⁻². If only 80% of its photons are absorbed, the absorbed photon flux density is 80 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The remaining 20 µmol may be reflected or transmitted. To infer the photochemical rate, one must still know what fraction of absorbed excitations produce the relevant outcome.

Energy conservation alone places bounds on photosynthesis, but does not determine its efficiency. A particular photochemical conversion must be energetically possible, and its products must have sufficient free energy to support subsequent reactions. At the same time, useful conversion competes kinetically with other routes of excitation decay. A process may be energetically allowed yet inefficient if excitation is lost before it reaches an active center. Conversely, efficient transfer of excitation does not guarantee fast carbon assimilation if downstream metabolism is constrained. Energy and rate are related but noninterchangeable aspects of the biological problem.

A spectrally mixed lamp requires special care in calculations. The radiant power contributed by each wavelength interval depends on the energy per photon in that interval. A single conversion factor based on 550 nm cannot give an exact photon flux for an arbitrary mixture of wavelengths. It is valid only as an approximation under an explicitly stated spectral assumption. In a simple examination problem the beam is often monochromatic, which makes the conversion direct. When the problem compares white light and red light without providing the spectral distribution or photon flux, a numerical comparison of their photochemical effects may not be justified.

The duration of illumination matters independently of instantaneous flux. At a constant photon flux density of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, an exposure of one minute supplies 6000 µmol photons per square meter, or 0.006 mol m⁻². A shorter brighter exposure can supply the same number of photons, but the biological outcome need not be identical. Reaction-center turnover, acclimation and dissipation may respond differently to a high instantaneous excitation rate. At very low intensity and over short intervals one may approximate proportionality between the number of absorbed photons and the number of productive events. That approximation must be reconsidered when centers become saturated or metabolism changes.

These physical quantities are best treated as tools for understanding the biological sequence, not as a separate exercise in memorizing formulas. Wavelength determines the energy of an individual photon, spectral absorption determines the probability that the photon interacts with the antenna, photon flux determines how often such interactions can occur, and photochemical yield determines how effectively absorbed excitation contributes to an outcome. Each quantity answers a different question.

3. Absorption, reflection, transmission and the color of leaves

When light strikes a leaf, not all of it enters the photosynthetic machinery. Some photons are reflected at the surface or after scattering inside the tissue. Others leave the opposite surface and contribute to transmission. Still others are absorbed by molecules in the leaf. In an idealized energy accounting at a given wavelength, the incident fraction equals the sum of reflected, transmitted and absorbed fractions. If the measured reflectance is R and transmittance is T, both expressed as fractions of incident light, the absorptance is 1 − R − T. The equation assumes the measurements account for the relevant outgoing light and use the same wavelength and geometry. Absorptance is dimensionless and cannot exceed unity.

A green leaf usually reflects and transmits a larger fraction of green light than it does of wavelengths near the strong red and blue absorption bands of its chlorophylls. The light reaching our eyes is therefore enriched in green wavelengths. This explains its appearance without requiring the claim that green photons are unusable. A leaf contains many chlorophyll molecules and internal scattering structures, so a photon that is not absorbed on its first passage may encounter pigments again. Green light can penetrate relatively deeply into leaf tissue and can drive photosynthesis where it is absorbed. The proportion of green light absorbed by an intact leaf is much higher than one might infer from the minimum of a dilute chlorophyll-solution spectrum alone.

The distinction between an isolated pigment and a whole leaf is important. A dilute pigment extract can be placed in a transparent cuvette of known optical path length. Within the conditions of Beer–Lambert behavior, absorbance A is the base-ten logarithm of incident intensity divided by transmitted intensity. For a single absorbing species A = εcl, where ε is its molar absorption coefficient at the specified wavelength, c is the molar concentration, and l is the optical path length in units compatible with ε. In this simple geometry, transmittance is 10⁻ᴬ. A sample of absorbance one transmits one tenth of the incident light. If reflection and other losses are negligible, it absorbs ninety percent. Absorbance is logarithmic, whereas absorptance is a linear fraction.

This difference creates a familiar quantitative trap. An absorbance of 0.5 does not mean that half the incident photons are absorbed. The transmittance is 10⁻⁰·⁵, approximately 0.316; the absorbed fraction under the stated assumptions is approximately 0.684. Doubling the concentration of a pigment from an initial absorbance of 0.5 to 1.0 doubles absorbance, but it increases the absorbed fraction from about 68.4% to 90%, not to 136.8%. The incident fraction sets a physical upper bound. Absorption by dense tissues therefore does not necessarily grow linearly with pigment concentration, even when molecular absorbances obey the Beer–Lambert relation in an appropriately dilute solution.

In a leaf, scattering, orientation of internal structures, pigment packaging and heterogeneity complicate the use of a simple solution formula. Palisade and spongy mesophyll contribute different optical paths. Epidermal properties and internal air spaces can influence the distribution of light within the tissue. In addition, pigments are embedded in membrane-associated protein complexes, not dissolved as freely moving isolated molecules in a uniform solvent. Their absorption bands may be shifted relative to those measured in an extract. An experiment that seeks to compare spectral absorptance of intact leaves should therefore measure both reflection and transmission, rather than applying a solution extinction coefficient directly to a leaf thickness.

An absorption spectrum displays the wavelength dependence of optical absorption. When individual pigments are measured under specified conditions, peaks indicate wavelengths absorbed relatively strongly. An absorption peak is not an emission peak, and it is not automatically the wavelength at which photosynthesis proceeds fastest. Absorption provides the initial excitation, while photochemistry depends on whether the excitation travels successfully to a center and whether that center can carry out productive charge separation. A pigment unrelated to the reaction centers could absorb strongly and still make no direct contribution to photosynthetic electron flow.

The comparison of leaves grown under different light conditions illustrates this point. Two leaves could exhibit similar absorptance over the visible range yet contain different numbers of active reaction centers per unit area. One might have a large antenna feeding comparatively few centers; the other might distribute its pigments among more centers. At weak light the large effective antenna could be advantageous per center, whereas at high light the larger number of centers might provide more potential turnover capacity. Without measuring center activity, the spectrum alone cannot establish which leaf has the greater photosynthetic rate. The same distinction appears in mutant analyses, where loss of a pigment changes the optical signal but may also destabilize the complexes to which that pigment belonged.

A healthy leaf absorbs light for several other purposes as well. Photoreceptors absorb particular wavelengths and initiate developmental or physiological signaling. Some epidermal pigments attenuate potentially damaging radiation. Pigments involved in signaling or screening need not transfer excitation to a photosystem. Thus, a change in whole-leaf absorption can result from altered pigment composition outside the thylakoid antenna, and an investigator should avoid assigning every optical change to chlorophyll or carotenoids. The study of light as a signal is treated separately in sensory photobiology.

A useful way to reason about spectral data is to identify the sample and the denominator before interpreting the graph. A graph of absorbance for an extract normalized to its highest peak conveys spectral shape but not absolute pigment content. A graph of whole-leaf absorptance gives the fraction of incident photons intercepted by the tissue, including nonphotosynthetic absorbers. A graph of oxygen production per absorbed photon addresses a biological yield. The three can be compared meaningfully only when their measurement conditions and normalization are understood. Their differences are as informative as their similarities.

4. Chlorophyll: molecular structure, absorption and biological function

The major photosynthetic pigment of ordinary green plants is chlorophyll a. The molecule consists of a magnesium-containing chlorin macrocycle and a long hydrophobic phytol ester chain. A chlorin is related to a porphyrin but has a different degree of reduction in its ring system. The large ring system contains conjugated bonds, allowing its electrons to interact with visible light. Four ring nitrogen atoms coordinate a central Mg²⁺ ion. The extended arrangement of bonds is responsible for the accessible electronic transitions that give chlorophyll its characteristic visible absorption. The phytol chain has a different structural role: it contributes to the hydrophobic character of the pigment and helps accommodate it within pigment–protein complexes in the thylakoid membrane. It is not the principal chromophore.

The chromophore is the part of a molecule principally responsible for absorption at particular wavelengths. Chlorophyll's chromophore has an extended system of delocalized π electrons. In a molecule with conjugated double bonds, the π electrons are not confined to one individual carbon–carbon bond. They occupy electronic states influenced by the entire connected system. Light absorption changes the electronic state of this system. The energy gap between accessible states is smaller than for many simple molecules with only isolated bonds, which helps explain why chlorophyll absorbs visible rather than exclusively ultraviolet light. This does not mean that any molecule with conjugation necessarily has the same spectrum. Ring shape, substituents, metal coordination and interactions with the environment also determine electronic energies and transition probabilities.

Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b illustrate how a relatively small chemical difference can change a pigment's optical behavior. Chlorophyll a has a methyl group at the position where chlorophyll b has a formyl group. Both contain magnesium and a phytol chain and both participate in light harvesting in higher plants. The formyl substitution changes the electronic properties of the conjugated system and the local interactions available to the pigment. As a result, the spectra of the two pigments are different, although they overlap extensively. In diethyl ether, chlorophyll a shows prominent bands near 430 nm and 661–662 nm, whereas chlorophyll b has prominent bands near 453 nm and 642 nm. These values are representative rather than universal; solvent and protein binding affect the exact peaks and their relative strengths. The presence of both pigments enables a plant antenna to capture photons that would otherwise be absorbed less effectively.

It is useful to understand why the chemical identity of the substituent matters without memorizing an isolated list of wavelengths. A methyl group and a formyl group differ in polarity and in their electronic interaction with the macrocycle. They also provide different opportunities for interactions with surrounding amino acid side chains. A chlorophyll-binding pocket can therefore discriminate between pigment types and stabilize distinct orientations. Once bound, the pigment's properties reflect both the intrinsic molecular structure and the environment created by the protein. The same pigment species can have slightly different spectral characteristics in different binding sites of the same antenna. This heterogeneity becomes useful during excitation-energy transfer.

Chlorophyll a has two roles in oxygenic photosynthesis. It is a major antenna pigment and also participates in the photochemical machinery of both photosystems. Chlorophyll b functions primarily in light harvesting within antenna complexes of green plants and green algae. The primary photochemistry of the typical plant reaction centers is not assigned to chlorophyll b. This distinction explains why eliminating chlorophyll b is not equivalent to removing every reaction center, even though the resulting plant can have serious defects in antenna stability and light utilization. An experiment that observes decreased photosynthesis in a chlorophyll b-deficient mutant must determine whether the effect arises from reduced photon capture, altered antenna assembly, a secondary effect on center organization, or some combination of these.

The central magnesium is part of the intact chlorophyll chromophore. Removal of Mg²⁺ produces pheophytin, which retains the chlorin ring and phytol group but differs electronically and chemically. During acidification of a chlorophyll extract, pheophytin formation can change its color and absorption spectrum. In a living photosystem II, a specific pheophytin molecule serves as an electron acceptor in the charge-separation pathway. That is a particular functional use of the magnesium-free pigment in a defined protein environment, not evidence that all chlorophyll lacking magnesium is a useful antenna component. A pigment's name identifies a molecular structure; its biological function depends on the location and molecular partners of that structure.

The phytol chain also deserves careful interpretation. Removing a hydrophobic tail from a pigment may alter its association with membranes and proteins without completely eliminating the ability of its chromophore to absorb visible light. Chlorophyllide, for example, differs from chlorophyll in lacking the esterified phytol group. Such structural distinctions can be used in an examination to separate optical activity from proper biological localization. An extracted chromophore can still absorb light, yet an altered molecule may fail to be stably incorporated into the native photosynthetic apparatus. Absorption in solution is not a sufficient criterion for function in a thylakoid.

In plants, chlorophyll production is coordinated with assembly of the photosynthetic machinery. Chlorophyll biosynthesis draws on the tetrapyrrole pathway; its Mg-containing branch differs from the iron-containing heme branch. In the chlorophyll branch, magnesium is inserted into protoporphyrin IX, and subsequent transformations lead through chlorophyllide intermediates to chlorophyll. The full sequence of biosynthetic enzymes is not needed to understand antenna function. The important physiological principle is that chlorophyll must be made and incorporated in a regulated manner. Free excited chlorophyll can interact with oxygen and cause damage rather than perform organized photochemistry. A leaf therefore cannot safely maximize its supply of unbound chlorophyll independently of the capacity to assemble pigment–protein complexes.

Chlorophyll b is produced through oxidation of a methyl substituent in the chlorophyll pathway, with chlorophyllide a oxygenase playing a central role. Loss of this activity can result in a marked deficiency of chlorophyll b. In a typical chlorophyll b-deficient green plant, many major antenna proteins are reduced or unstable, while some core chlorophyll a-containing photosystem components persist. Such a mutant is useful for studying the contribution of peripheral antennae, but it is not a perfectly clean subtraction of the chlorophyll b absorption spectrum from an otherwise unaltered chloroplast. The quantity of chlorophyll b and the abundance of the proteins that bind it are physiologically linked.

A plant can change the relative amounts of chlorophyll a and b as it acclimates to its light environment. Since much of the chlorophyll b in higher plants occurs in peripheral antennae, a lower chlorophyll a:b ratio often indicates a larger contribution of chlorophyll b-containing antenna proteins relative to chlorophyll a-rich cores. The inference is most defensible for comparable green plant material and must be interpreted alongside the abundance of PSI, PSII and other pigments. A chlorophyll a:b ratio alone does not uniquely determine the antenna size of either photosystem. It is a compositional measurement, not a direct count of functional connections.

These molecular details lead to a general principle of structure–function reasoning. Conjugation makes visible-light absorption possible; the substitution pattern modifies spectral and binding properties; the hydrophobic tail contributes to incorporation into the photosynthetic apparatus; and the protein environment determines how a pigment communicates with neighboring molecules. None of these features alone constitutes a complete antenna. The biological function emerges from their combination.

5. Carotenoids: supplementary harvesting and protection

A second major group of pigments in photosynthetic tissues is the carotenoids. These compounds generally contain extended conjugated chains and absorb strongly in parts of the visible spectrum where chlorophyll absorption is less effective. Many common plant carotenoids are C₄₀ isoprenoid compounds derived from eight five-carbon isoprene units. Carotenoids that contain only carbon and hydrogen are called carotenes, while oxygen-containing members are called xanthophylls. β-Carotene is a familiar carotene; lutein, violaxanthin, neoxanthin and zeaxanthin are important xanthophylls in green plant photosynthetic membranes. The chemical distinction is based on oxygen-containing functional groups, not simply on whether a pigment appears yellow or orange.

The extended conjugated system of carotenoids explains their absorption in the blue to blue-green region. The spectrum depends on the length and geometry of conjugation, the end groups of the molecule and its environment. Carotenoids often appear yellow or orange because they preferentially absorb shorter visible wavelengths and transmit or reflect longer wavelengths. In a normally green leaf, their color is masked by the abundant chlorophylls. When chlorophyll is degraded during senescence, remaining carotenoids can become more visually conspicuous. A yellow leaf can therefore reveal pigments that were present before it changed color. The observation alone does not establish that new carotenoids were synthesized during yellowing.

Within photosynthetic complexes, some carotenoids can transfer absorbed excitation to neighboring chlorophylls. Their contribution is especially valuable where their absorption complements that of chlorophyll. This is not simply the additive optical effect of mixing colored molecules. A carotenoid must be positioned and oriented so that excitation can be transferred before it is lost by relaxation. The electronic states of carotenoids and the couplings to neighboring chlorophylls differ from those of a pair of chlorophyll molecules. The simple picture of excitation transfer as an uphill or downhill movement between two visible absorption maxima is therefore insufficient to calculate the efficiency of carotenoid-to-chlorophyll transfer. At examination level, it is enough to recognize that pigment identity, excited-state lifetime, energetic compatibility and molecular interactions determine whether the absorbed energy can be harvested.

Some carotenoids have primarily protective roles in particular sites. Chlorophyll that remains excited can occasionally form a triplet excited state. This is potentially harmful in an oxygen-rich environment because transfer of energy to ground-state molecular oxygen can generate singlet oxygen, a reactive oxygen species. A nearby carotenoid can intercept excitation from triplet chlorophyll, forming a carotenoid triplet state that typically dissipates its energy without producing singlet oxygen. Carotenoids can also participate in quenching singlet oxygen itself. The transfer of triplet excitation requires suitable close molecular interactions; it should not be assumed to follow the same simple long-range Förster model commonly used to introduce singlet excitation transfer.

The protective function is particularly important in a light-harvesting apparatus that concentrates pigments near one another. High pigment density improves the probability of light absorption and excitation transfer, but it also places electronically excited molecules in a membrane containing oxygen and oxidation-sensitive lipids and proteins. A chlorophyll molecule that has lost its normal protein environment may generate reactive species. By placing carotenoids alongside chlorophylls, the antenna and photosystem cores combine absorption with local protection. Different carotenoids are favored at different binding sites because their size, polarity and orientation affect both packing and their capacity to interact with excited chlorophyll.

The xanthophylls of the major plant antenna complexes are not interchangeable merely because they all contain oxygen. Hydroxyl groups and other oxygen-containing substituents can interact with the surrounding protein or membrane interface, while the conjugated hydrocarbon backbone interacts with neighboring pigment molecules. An altered carotenoid composition can therefore change antenna assembly, excited-state interactions and protective capacity at the same time. It is not always possible to identify the primary defect from a measurement of total carotenoid content. A mutation that removes a specific xanthophyll may be partly compensated by another pigment, and a plant may alter gene expression or antenna organization in response.

Carotenoid functions can appear contradictory if one considers only a single outcome of excitation. In weak light, absorption by a carotenoid and transfer to chlorophyll may improve productive capture. Under excessive light, specific pigment arrangements and regulatory changes can favor dissipation of excitation. These are not mutually exclusive functions. Excitation can follow multiple kinetic pathways, and the molecular state of a complex determines the relative probability of each pathway. The detailed role of the xanthophyll cycle and other rapidly regulated energy-dissipating mechanisms belongs to chapter 6.A.05. Here, the essential point is that absorption and protection arise from the same need to manage electronically excited states.

β-Carotene is also found in photosystem cores, where it participates in structural and protective interactions. Peripheral green plant antenna complexes characteristically bind several xanthophylls. These distributions illustrate why a carotenoid in the thylakoid cannot automatically be described as a peripheral antenna pigment. As with chlorophyll, the function must be assigned to the molecular location. The observation that a pigment absorbs light outside the strongest chlorophyll absorption bands demonstrates potential supplementary absorption, not the fraction of its absorbed excitations that actually reach a reaction center. That fraction must be investigated experimentally.

A simple thought experiment separates these possibilities. Suppose a purified antenna containing carotenoids and chlorophylls is illuminated in a band absorbed predominantly by carotenoids. If chlorophyll fluorescence is observed even after correcting for direct chlorophyll excitation, the observation supports energy transfer from carotenoids to chlorophyll. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that oxygen evolution would occur in a leaf. The isolated antenna may lack a complete reaction center. A second experiment using intact photosystems or chloroplasts could test whether the transferred energy promotes productive charge separation or electron flow. Evidence for excitation transfer and evidence for photochemistry answer related but distinct questions.

A different experiment compares a normal leaf with a carotenoid-deficient mutant. If the mutant loses chlorophyll and photosystem activity during strong illumination, the result is consistent with impaired protection. Yet it would be premature to infer that the entire defect results from singlet oxygen formation. Changes in antenna assembly, thylakoid development and pigment biosynthesis may contribute. The most convincing interpretation would combine pigment analysis, structural or protein abundance measurements, photochemical activity, and comparison under weak and strong light. A phenotype that appears mainly under high excitation pressure provides a useful clue to a protective function, while a defect that appears before substantial light exposure may indicate an assembly or developmental requirement.

Carotenoids thus broaden the effective absorption of the photosynthetic machinery, contribute to the stability of pigment–protein complexes, and help manage the potentially destructive consequences of chlorophyll excitation. Their value is not captured by an absorption spectrum alone. It is the integration of these roles that makes them indispensable partners of chlorophyll in oxygenic photosynthesis.

6. Absorption spectra, action spectra and experimental evidence

A central achievement of early photosynthesis research was to connect the color properties of biological pigments with the rates of biological reactions. If a pigment absorbs light, does that absorption actually help photosynthesis? A simple answer based on color is unreliable, because different molecules may absorb light for different purposes. The decisive comparison is between an absorption spectrum, which describes optical interaction, and an action spectrum, which describes the wavelength dependence of a chosen biological response.

An action spectrum is obtained by exposing a system to different wavelengths and measuring a defined response under comparable conditions. In photosynthesis the response can be oxygen evolution, carbon dioxide uptake, or another suitable measure of photochemical activity. The experimenter must specify how the illumination is normalized. If the number of incident photons per unit area per unit time differs substantially between wavelengths, differences in response can reflect the number of photons supplied instead of the biological effectiveness of each wavelength. Normalization to equal incident photon flux is a useful convention for comparing wavelength effectiveness. A separate calculation can then estimate the response per absorbed photon if absorptance is known.

The classic experiments of Theodor Wilhelm Engelmann in the early 1880s provided a striking biological demonstration of wavelength dependence. He illuminated filamentous green algae with dispersed light so that different parts of the algal filament received different wavelength bands. Aerobic motile bacteria served as indirect indicators of the oxygen produced by the illuminated algal cells. Bacteria tended to accumulate near regions illuminated by red and blue-violet light, consistent with enhanced oxygen production in those regions. The experiment related the spatial pattern of biological activity to the wavelength dependence of illumination. Its historical importance lies in connecting absorption by photosynthetic pigments to oxygen-evolving activity in living material.

Engelmann's method requires an understanding of its assumptions. The bacteria were not directly measuring the number of photons absorbed by chlorophyll. They were responding to the local oxygen environment. Oxygen can diffuse away from the site of production, and bacterial movement can be influenced by other conditions. The spectral distribution and intensity of light incident on the algal filament also influence the response. The spatial density of bacteria should therefore be understood as an ingenious qualitative indicator rather than as an exact modern measurement of absolute photosynthetic quantum efficiency. A contemporary experiment would control photon flux and measure oxygen exchange quantitatively, but it would ask essentially the same question.

A useful negative control would involve a preparation in darkness or a preparation lacking functioning photosynthetic cells. If bacterial accumulation in the original arrangement depended on light-driven oxygen production by the algal filament, the characteristic pattern should be weakened or absent when this source is removed. An additional control could test whether the bacteria themselves move toward particular colors independently of oxygen production. Without such a control, one might confuse bacterial phototaxis with a response to oxygen. These considerations demonstrate the logic of indirect biological assays. The observed organism reports an environmental variable, which in turn is influenced by the mechanism of interest.

An action spectrum need not coincide exactly with the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a isolated from a leaf. Chlorophyll b and carotenoids can contribute excitation to the photosystems, and protein binding modifies the spectral properties of pigments. Furthermore, a whole-leaf action spectrum is influenced by absorptance, the distribution of illumination within the leaf, the excitation balance between PSI and PSII, and the efficiency of electron transport and carbon assimilation under the measuring conditions. The prediction is broad correspondence in spectral regions where the relevant pigments harvest light effectively, not exact identity of every peak and valley.

Suppose an experiment shows strong oxygen evolution under blue and red illumination but a lower, clearly nonzero rate under green light. That observation is consistent with the absorption properties of the plant pigments and with the ability of a leaf to absorb part of the incident green spectrum. It does not imply that each green photon has a lower quantum efficiency after absorption. If the tissue absorbs fewer green photons, the rate per incident photon can be lower even when the rate per absorbed photon is similar. Conversely, a large difference in rate that persists after correction for total absorptance suggests that something beyond optical capture differs. It might involve nonphotosynthetic absorption, a change in excitation distribution between photosystems, or downstream physiology.

Another important distinction is between net and gross rates. A leaf consumes oxygen in respiration even while producing oxygen through photosynthesis. Net oxygen exchange is the algebraic result of production and consumption. Under some conditions the net rate can be close to zero despite ongoing photochemical oxygen production. If an action spectrum is measured using net oxygen exchange, changes in oxygen-consuming processes may influence the resulting curve. Investigators can estimate gross rates by suitable methods or use carefully justified corrections. A dark respiration rate is not always an exact representation of oxygen consumption in illuminated tissue. The correct experimental interpretation depends on what the method measures, not on the convenient label assigned to a graph.

The choice of light level is equally important. At high intensity, the response may become limited by processes other than the arrival of excitation at the reaction centers. A strongly absorbed wavelength can then fail to produce a proportionally greater measured rate. At low intensity, before significant saturation, comparisons more directly reflect the effectiveness of light capture and energy conversion. An action spectrum measured at one intensity is not necessarily identical to that measured at another. In an examination problem that supplies a saturation curve as well as spectral data, the curve helps determine whether a difference in response should be attributed primarily to light limitation.

Modern spectrophotometers and fluorescence measurements extend the logic of the early experiments. A pigment extract can be separated into components and each component analyzed for its own absorption bands. An intact complex can be excited at selected wavelengths while its fluorescence is recorded. If selective excitation of an accessory pigment results in chlorophyll fluorescence, after accounting for direct chlorophyll absorption, this supports excitation transfer. An intact photosynthetic preparation can additionally be assessed for oxygen evolution or another photochemical product. Comparing all three types of observations distinguishes the presence of an absorber, communication between pigments, and successful use of the excitation in photosynthetic chemistry.

The historical development of photosynthesis research also revealed that the number of chlorophyll molecules engaged in harvesting light is much larger than the number of photochemical centers. Emerson and Arnold's flashing-light experiments in the early 1930s, followed by the photosynthetic-unit concept developed by Gaffron and Wohl, helped establish the need for an antenna serving a limited number of photochemical sites. These experiments will be considered more closely when we examine antenna organization. Their conceptual implication is that a pigment molecule does not have to carry out a chemical turnover each time it absorbs light. It can perform its biological task by transferring excitation to a different pigment.

Absorption and action spectra are consequently not competing descriptions of the same measurement. They are complementary windows on the pathway from light to biological activity. An absorption spectrum asks which photons are captured. An excitation spectrum of a chosen fluorescence emission asks which absorbed wavelengths can populate the emitting state. A photosynthetic action spectrum asks which incident wavelengths promote a specified biological response. The differences between these spectra guide the development of mechanistic hypotheses, and the agreement between appropriately controlled measurements supports the connection between pigments and photosynthesis.

7. Thylakoid organization and the physical context of light harvesting

The absorption and transfer processes described so far occur within a specialized membrane system of the chloroplast. The chloroplast envelope surrounds the stroma, an aqueous compartment containing many of the enzymes of carbon metabolism. Within the stroma, the thylakoid membrane encloses a separate aqueous interior called the thylakoid lumen. The membrane contains the protein complexes of photosynthetic electron transport as well as the major antenna complexes. Light-harvesting pigments are primarily integrated into those complexes rather than suspended independently in the lumen or stroma. The distinction between the envelope and the thylakoid membrane is essential because their protein compositions and physiological functions are different.

In higher plant chloroplasts, regions of thylakoid membrane form stacks known as grana, and other regions remain relatively unstacked, including the stromal lamellae that connect the membrane system. The appressed regions of grana contain much of the PSII and LHCII population. PSI and ATP synthase are enriched in nonappressed regions, where their relatively large stromal-facing domains have more space. The distribution is a matter of enrichment and spatial organization, not an absolute rule assigning every molecule of a given complex to a single named compartment. Grana margins and other membrane regions participate in the dynamic organization of the photosynthetic apparatus.

The stacked and unstacked regions belong to an interconnected membrane system. A proton released into the lumen by a light-driven reaction is not being released into the stroma simply because the photosystem is in a granum. Nor does movement of a photosynthetic complex between membrane regions necessarily involve its extraction into an aqueous compartment. Lateral rearrangements within the membrane can change which complexes are close enough to interact while preserving membrane topology. These facts are particularly relevant when considering regulation of the allocation of excitation between PSI and PSII.

The thylakoid is a lipid bilayer containing a substantial population of integral membrane proteins. Chlorophyll molecules bind to specific sites in these proteins, and carotenoids associate with other defined positions. Interactions of the phytol chains and nonpolar protein surfaces with the hydrophobic environment help stabilize the assemblies. The arrangement places the absorbing chromophores close to one another while allowing control of their distances, orientations and electronic interactions. It would be much more difficult to achieve the same organization with freely diffusing pigments in an aqueous solution. Pigments would not only have poor solubility but would lack a stable architecture directing excitation toward photochemical centers.

An antenna protein can span the membrane using hydrophobic α-helical segments. Amino acid side chains and backbone groups in and around these segments form a three-dimensional environment for the pigments. Certain groups can coordinate the magnesium of chlorophyll, while other interactions stabilize the macrocycle, its substituents or its tail. The protein is not simply an inert container. It helps define pigment geometry and local electrostatic conditions, and thus influences absorption energies, the strength of electronic coupling, and the likelihood of excitation transfer. Even two chlorophyll a molecules within one complex may perform slightly different optical roles because their binding sites are different.

The physical continuity of the membrane does not imply unrestricted excitation sharing between every chlorophyll in a chloroplast. Excitation typically exists only briefly, and transfer to a distant pigment must occur through a suitable network before competing processes remove the excitation. The effective antenna of a photosystem consists of the pigments that are functionally connected to its center over this relevant timescale. Two pigments situated in the same membrane but separated by a large distance or by an unfavorable arrangement may not communicate efficiently. Conversely, separate protein complexes that associate closely can provide an effective path of energy migration across their interface.

The organization of photosystems also establishes the spatial orientation of subsequent chemical reactions. PSII derives electrons from water through a lumen-facing donor apparatus, while PSI ultimately supplies reducing equivalents on the stromal side. These orientations are integral to the development of the proton motive force and the supply of reducing power for stromal metabolism. An antenna does not itself need to move protons across the membrane to perform its main function; it must deliver excitation to a center whose cofactors are arranged to carry out oriented electron transfer. The separation between the optical and chemical tasks helps clarify why the topology of the reaction center matters even though an excitation can migrate laterally among antenna pigments.

A structural image of a thylakoid or a purified photosystem reveals the positions of complexes and cofactors, but a static image cannot by itself establish the route or rate of excitation transfer. A structural model is a basis for hypotheses: close pigments with favorable orientation and appropriate energies might interact strongly; a gap between complexes might limit transfer. Spectroscopic measurements test these predictions. A separation of several nanometers between two pigment groups can be significant on the scale of energy transfer, even though it is far below the resolution of conventional light microscopy. Different experimental techniques therefore address different aspects of the architecture.

The dynamic character of thylakoid organization is particularly evident when plants experience changes in illumination. Protein complexes may alter their associations, and the amount of antenna material relative to reaction-center material can change over longer periods. These adjustments influence how much light each photosystem intercepts and how the excitation is distributed. A diagram showing a PSII complex surrounded by a fixed number of LHCII trimers is a useful representative structure, not a promise that every chloroplast maintains exactly that arrangement throughout development and across environmental conditions. The diagrams in this chapter emphasize functional organization rather than universal stoichiometry.

This chapter concentrates on the thylakoid because it supplies the physical setting for light capture. The complete structure and biogenesis of the chloroplast were considered in cellular organization. Keeping the two topics distinct allows us to focus on the causal relationship between protein architecture, pigment placement and the conversion of sunlight into excitation energy that is available to a reaction center.

8. Photosystem organization: peripheral antennae, cores, LHCI and LHCII

A photosystem is an organized molecular assembly with a photochemical reaction center and associated light-harvesting pigments. In green plants, photosystem II and photosystem I are the two principal types of reaction-center complex engaged in oxygenic photosynthesis. Each contains a core antenna associated with the proteins and cofactors of the reaction center. In addition, peripheral antenna complexes can enlarge the effective region over which photons are intercepted. The names PSI and PSII identify distinct photosystems and do not imply the order in which electrons encounter them. During conventional linear electron flow, PSII acts upstream of PSI.

The concept of a photosynthetic unit emerged before the detailed molecular structures of these complexes were known. Emerson and Arnold exposed photosynthetic material to brief flashes of light and investigated how much oxygen could be produced per flash under conditions permitting recovery between flashes. As the energy of a sufficiently brief flash was increased, the response could approach a limit even though the sample contained a large amount of chlorophyll. The relationship between chlorophyll content and the limited photochemical response supported the existence of functional units in which many pigments contributed energy to a more restricted number of photochemical sites. Gaffron and Wohl subsequently developed an explicit photosynthetic-unit interpretation. These classical observations helped replace the idea that every chlorophyll molecule independently performs the entire chemical process.

The logic of the flash experiment is worth examining. During a very short flash, a photochemical center may be able to perform only a limited number of productive events before it must return to a state capable of another event. A flash containing enough photons to excite all available units cannot make each center operate an unlimited number of times. Consequently, the amount of product obtained per flash can approach a maximum. If the number of total chlorophyll molecules is known, the ratio of pigments to a suitable measure of productive units can indicate that numerous pigments serve each unit. Quantitative conversion of oxygen yield into the number of centers requires assumptions about electron turnovers, oxygen evolution and recovery. One must not directly equate one molecule of oxygen per flash with one photon or with one chlorophyll molecule.

The outer antenna associated principally with PSII in higher plants is called light-harvesting complex II, or LHCII. The major LHCII antenna is commonly present as trimers assembled from Lhcb-family proteins, particularly Lhcb1, Lhcb2 and Lhcb3 in representative higher plants. Each monomer binds multiple chlorophyll a and b molecules and several carotenoids, predominantly xanthophylls. A commonly described LHCII monomer contains fourteen chlorophylls, including eight chlorophyll a and six chlorophyll b molecules, and four carotenoid-binding sites. This structural example is useful because it demonstrates the high pigment density achieved by a single relatively small protein. It should not be generalized as an invariant stoichiometry for every Lhcb-family protein or every physiological preparation.

Several minor monomeric antenna proteins, often known as CP29, CP26 and CP24, help connect peripheral antenna material with the PSII core in typical higher plants. These antenna proteins are distinct from the core antenna proteins CP43 and CP47. The numbers in their conventional names do not directly state the number of chlorophylls bound by each protein. CP43 and CP47 are largely associated with chlorophyll a and are integral parts of the PSII core. They deliver excitation toward the reaction-center region formed by the D1 and D2 proteins and their cofactors. Together, the core and associated peripheral complexes form arrangements often termed PSII supercomplexes. A supercomplex represents an organized association of complexes rather than a new kind of chlorophyll molecule.

The organization allows energy absorbed at a distant peripheral pigment to reach the center in a sequence of transfer events. If light is absorbed by a chlorophyll b located within an LHCII trimer, the excitation may be transferred to a nearby chlorophyll a in that complex, then migrate within or between antenna assemblies and enter the core antenna. Eventually it can populate an excited state that is coupled to charge separation. The actual sequence is probabilistic and involves multiple possible paths. The pathway is not a mechanical rail with one pigment designated as the obligatory next stop for every excitation. Energetic connectivity and the relative rates of transfer determine which paths contribute most strongly.

The major peripheral antenna associated with PSI is called light-harvesting complex I, or LHCI. In a representative higher plant, several Lhca-family proteins form an antenna belt along one side of the PSI core. The PSI core contains numerous chlorophyll a molecules associated with its reaction-center proteins, particularly PsaA and PsaB, as well as other subunits. LHCI binds both chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, although its pigment composition and organization differ from those of major LHCII. A typical higher-plant PSI–LHCI complex contains a large collection of pigments, many of which serve light harvesting rather than carrying out primary electron transfer. As with PSII, the effective absorption cross-section depends on both the number of pigments and the efficiency of their communication with the center.

Certain chlorophylls of PSI and LHCI have absorption characteristics shifted toward the long-wavelength, lower-energy part of the spectrum. These are often called red forms. They show why the energy landscape of an antenna cannot be represented accurately by a perfectly smooth staircase in which every excited state is at a higher energy than the next state toward the center. Some red antenna states are lower in energy than states associated with productive trapping. At physiological temperature, thermal energy and reversible excitation exchange can assist transfer from such lower-energy states toward the trapping network. These states extend the wavelengths available for light harvesting, but their presence can influence transfer and trapping times. The exam-level lesson is that spectral overlap, thermal population and kinetics matter in addition to simple energy ordering.

The distinction between LHCI and LHCII is primarily one of molecular family, organization and preferential association. It does not mean that excitation absorbed by every LHCII molecule must always go to PSII. Under some conditions, plants redistribute a fraction of LHCII so that its excitation can contribute to PSI. Such state transitions provide a way to adjust the relative excitation of the two photosystems. The major LHCII antenna does not become a different reaction center during this redistribution. It changes functional association and hence the probability that its excitation is trapped by a particular photosystem.

The term antenna size is often used as if it were an easily counted structural property, but two meanings must be distinguished. Structural antenna size is the number of pigments associated with a photosystem or complex. Functional antenna size describes the effective capacity to absorb light and deliver excitation to a specified center. A pigment that absorbs strongly but is disconnected from the center adds to the structural pigment count without making an equivalent contribution to functional antenna size. An increase in the number of attached pigments can also have different effects at different wavelengths because pigments have distinct absorption cross-sections. Experimental estimates of functional antenna size are therefore conditional on the wavelength, physiological state and measurement method.

Consider two photosynthetic preparations containing the same number of active PSII centers. If preparation A has more efficiently coupled antenna pigments per center than preparation B, the rate of center excitation at low incident photon flux may be greater in A. Under saturating light, however, both preparations may reach similar maximum turnover rates if their centers and downstream reactions have the same capacities. A difference in the initial slope of a light-response curve and a similar high-light plateau would be consistent with this hypothesis. If the plateau also changes, altered downstream activity, center abundance or photodamage may contribute. Such a comparison is far more informative than a conclusion drawn solely from the amount of green pigment in the preparations.

Antenna complexes are also an important demonstration of the relationship between genetic information and cellular architecture. In plants many peripheral Lhca and Lhcb proteins are encoded by nuclear genes, synthesized outside the chloroplast and delivered to their sites of assembly. Pigments are synthesized and handled within the plastid environment. Core complexes include proteins encoded by both nuclear and chloroplast genomes. Successful photosystem assembly depends on the coordination of these contributions. The absence of one antenna protein can change the stability or composition of associated proteins and pigments, so the response to a mutation may not be restricted to a single missing absorption band.

The names LHCII, LHCI, CP43 and CP47 become easier to remember when they are associated with this architectural logic. Peripheral antennae intercept photons over a wider area; core antenna pigments provide optical connectivity near the reaction center; the reaction-center cofactors convert excitation into a charge-separated state. A diagram or a list of subunit names is valuable only to the extent that it helps us follow the functional route from absorption to photochemistry.

9. Excited-state dynamics and resonance energy transfer

When a chlorophyll molecule absorbs a photon, it is promoted from its ground electronic state to an excited state. The excited molecule differs from the ground-state molecule in its electronic energy and distribution of charge, but it need not have lost or gained an electron. This is one of the most important distinctions in photosynthetic biophysics. Excitation can be transferred between pigments without either pigment undergoing a permanent oxidation or reduction. Electron transfer, in contrast, changes the oxidation state of the electron donor and acceptor at least transiently. The light-harvesting stage and the primary photochemical stage must be distinguished even when both occur in the same protein complex.

A useful starting model involves two pigments called a donor and an acceptor. In this context donor means an excitation donor, not necessarily an electron donor. The donor has already absorbed a photon and reached an excited state. The acceptor begins in its ground state. If their electronic transitions are suitably coupled, the donor can return to its ground state while the acceptor becomes excited. The excitation has changed location, but the donor has not exported one of its electrons and the acceptor has not acquired that electron. Each molecule retains its own electrons. The energy transfer results from interaction between their electronic transitions.

For many pairs of sufficiently separated pigments, Förster resonance energy transfer provides a useful model. The excited donor has a transition dipole associated with the possibility of returning to its ground electronic state. The acceptor has a transition dipole associated with excitation. The coupling between these transitions can enable transfer of energy without a freely propagating emitted photon. The phrase resonance refers to energetic compatibility of the transitions. An acceptor with an appropriate absorption band overlapping the donor's emission band can be excited by the interaction. The transfer is nonradiative in the sense that the donor does not first need to emit a real photon that subsequently travels through the medium and is absorbed by the acceptor.

The donor emission spectrum is important because it describes the energies available when the excited donor returns toward its ground state. The acceptor absorption spectrum describes the energies at which its electronic excitation is possible. Spectral overlap between these two functions contributes to the probability of transfer. One cannot establish the strength of Förster transfer by comparing only the two absorption maxima. Even pigments with similar absorption maxima may interact poorly if their separation is large or their transition dipoles are arranged unfavorably. Conversely, pigments with somewhat different maxima may show efficient transfer when their relevant spectral bands overlap and their geometrical arrangement is favorable.

Distance has a particularly strong effect in the idealized Förster model. The transfer rate k_ET can be expressed as (1/τ_D)(R₀/r)⁶, where τ_D is the donor excited-state lifetime in the absence of the acceptor, r is the distance between donor and acceptor, and R₀ is the Förster radius for the given donor–acceptor pair and environment. R₀ is the separation at which the transfer rate equals the donor's combined rate of decay through competing pathways in this simple model. The transfer efficiency is consequently η = 1/[1 + (r/R₀)⁶]. The expression assumes a suitable dipole–dipole approximation, a defined pair, and fixed relevant parameters; it is not a general formula for the whole photosystem.

To derive the expression, define k_D = 1/τ_D as the total first-order donor decay rate without transfer. With an acceptor present, excitation can leave the donor either by the original processes or by transfer at rate k_ET. For independent competing first-order pathways, the probability that transfer occurs first is the transfer rate divided by the sum of the rates. Thus η = k_ET/(k_ET + k_D). Replacing k_ET by k_D(R₀/r)⁶ and dividing numerator and denominator by k_ET gives the stated efficiency. The dimensions are consistent because k_ET and k_D have units of inverse time and r/R₀ is dimensionless.

At a separation equal to R₀, the efficiency is one half. At half that separation, the transfer rate is sixty-four times the original donor decay rate, and the efficiency is 64/65, approximately 98.5%. At twice R₀, the efficiency falls to 1/65, approximately 1.54%. These values illustrate why protein architecture is so significant. A change in pigment separation that seems small compared with an entire photosystem may have a large influence on transfer between a particular pair. They also illustrate the distinction between the transfer rate and transfer efficiency. Halving separation multiplies the idealized rate by sixty-four, but efficiency cannot exceed unity.

Orientation affects the coupling. Transition dipoles have directions, and the relative alignment of the two dipoles and their separation vector influences the interaction. A model for an isolated pair in freely rotating solution may use an averaged orientation factor, but pigments constrained within a protein cannot automatically be assigned that average. The local refractive environment, donor fluorescence quantum yield and spectral overlap also affect R₀. A student interpreting a mutation should therefore avoid assuming that distance is the only possible cause of altered transfer. A mutation may change pigment orientation, spectral energy, donor lifetime or binding occupancy even when the apparent center-to-center distance is nearly unchanged.

For pigments that are very close or electronically strongly coupled, the description of an excitation as localized on a single donor followed by a simple Förster jump may become inadequate. Electronic interactions can produce excited states distributed across more than one pigment. These collective excited states are commonly described using the concept of an exciton. In a real antenna, excitation may migrate within closely coupled pigment groups and between groups through different regimes of interaction. An advanced examination question may require recognition that the Förster expression is a useful conceptual approximation, not proof that every excitation-transfer step in LHCII or LHCI obeys the same distance law.

A further complication is that energy transfer need not proceed irreversibly from a single high-energy pigment to one lower-energy pigment. Forward and reverse transfer can both occur when energetics and coupling permit. The distribution of excitation among pigments depends on their energies, the number of available states, temperature and the rates of interconversion. A low-energy pigment can favor occupancy of its excited state without becoming an irreversible trap. The reaction center provides a productive trap because charge separation creates a different kind of state that may persist long enough for further chemistry. In an efficient antenna network, this photochemical pathway competes successfully with excitation losses.

The biological importance of transfer kinetics becomes clear when we compare timescales. The intrinsic lifetime of an excited chlorophyll can be on the order of nanoseconds in appropriate isolated conditions. Many energy-transfer processes in antenna complexes take place over much shorter times, on femtosecond to picosecond scales. The exact values depend on the pigments and preparations, and trapping by a complete photosystem involves a network rather than one pair. The shorter transfer times help make it possible for excitation to migrate through several steps before it is lost. If each step were much slower than competing decay processes, a large antenna would provide little benefit because excitations would disappear before reaching the center.

Multiple transfer pathways can also provide robustness. If one path becomes less favorable because a pigment is missing, another route may still contribute. However, the existence of alternatives does not mean that no pigment is important. A pigment at a major connection between a peripheral antenna and the core can have a disproportionate effect on transfer efficiency. Experiments that compare the total absorption spectrum with excitation-transfer kinetics can reveal this. A mutant may have nearly unchanged total absorption but slower delivery of excitation to a reaction center. Its optical capture is intact, while the functional connectivity has deteriorated.

This reasoning explains why a protein–pigment architecture is more useful than an unorganized mixture with the same composition. The protein establishes favorable distances, orientations and local energies, while the collective architecture brings the peripheral complexes into communication with a reaction center. The resulting energy migration is rapid enough to compete with excitation loss. The antenna therefore solves a problem of probability and kinetics as well as a problem of molecular absorption.

10. The transition from excitation migration to reaction-center charge separation

In a photosystem, the light-harvesting antenna directs excitation toward a region that can carry out the first chemical step of photosynthesis. This region is called the reaction center. Its pigments and other cofactors are arranged so that an excited electronic state can lead to the transfer of an electron from a donor to an acceptor. This event produces a state in which positive and negative charges are separated. The separation establishes a form of chemical potential energy that can be used in subsequent electron-transfer reactions. The reaction center does not make ATP or carbohydrate merely by absorbing one photon. It initiates a sequence of chemical events from which those products can eventually arise.

The conventional names for the primary reaction-center donors of PSII and PSI are P680 and P700, respectively. The numbers refer to characteristic optical properties near 680 and 700 nm rather than to a requirement that only those exact wavelengths can drive the photosystems. Energy absorbed by pigments at many different wavelengths can reach a reaction center through its antenna. Even direct excitation of reaction-center pigments can occur over a range of wavelengths because their absorption bands have finite widths. In both photosystems the donor is associated with chlorophyll a, while the complete reaction center contains several interacting cofactors. P680 and P700 are useful functional designations, not labels for two different freely dissolved pigment species.

A simplified reaction-center model can be written using D for an electron donor and A for an electron acceptor. Absorption or delivery of excitation produces D*. Electron transfer then gives D⁺ and A⁻. The asterisk in the first expression identifies an electronically excited state; the superscript charges in the second identify a change in electron distribution. An electron has moved from one molecular component to another. In a real photosystem the excited state and the first charge-separated states can involve multiple coupled pigments, and the electron-transfer sequence is more detailed than this simple equation. The model is nevertheless sufficient for distinguishing excitation transfer from charge separation.

Why is excitation needed? The energy of an electronic excited state can change the tendency of a molecule to participate in oxidation–reduction reactions. The excited state of a reaction-center donor is generally a more powerful electron donor than its ground state. Transfer of an electron to a suitably positioned acceptor can then occur under conditions in which ground-state transfer would not be favored in the same way. In terms of free-energy reasoning, the absorbed photon supplies the initial energy required to access an electronic state capable of producing a charge-separated product. In terms of kinetics, the proteins must hold the donor and acceptor in a geometry that allows electron transfer to compete effectively with the relaxation of the excited state.

The charge-separated state is not automatically permanent. An electron can return to an oxidized donor through charge recombination, and some of the initially absorbed energy can be lost. Productive photochemistry depends on additional reactions that move the electron to further acceptors and replenish the electron donor, thereby reducing the probability of immediate reversal. The initial separation is therefore only the beginning of a controlled sequence. If an electron acceptor downstream is unable to receive another electron, earlier acceptors can remain reduced and the reaction center may become less able to trap new excitations productively. This situation helps explain why the capacity for light harvesting is linked to the state of the electron-transport chain.

The distinction between an open and a closed reaction center is a useful physiological shorthand. In the case of PSII, an open center is generally one in which the primary stable quinone acceptor QA is oxidized and the center can perform productive photochemical turnover. A closed center has QA reduced and cannot accept another electron through its normal forward pathway until the acceptor system is reoxidized. This description does not require a physical door that opens or closes within the antenna. It describes a redox and functional state. Different experimental methods may define operational degrees of closure, so the simple distinction is most useful as a starting model.

When a large fraction of PSII centers is closed, the average probability of productive trapping of an arriving excitation decreases. Other possible fates of excitation consequently become more significant unless regulatory mechanisms adjust energy dissipation. If a pigment is excited at the same rate but the probability of photochemistry decreases, more of its excitations may be lost through fluorescence or heat. The relative changes in these pathways depend on their kinetics and on the regulatory state. A rise in fluorescence after experimental closure of centers is consistent with reduced photochemical quenching, but an unchanged fluorescence signal does not necessarily mean that centers remained open because heat dissipation may also have increased.

The use of two photosystems allows oxygenic photosynthesis to accomplish an energetically demanding overall electron-transfer process. PSII supplies the initial light-driven charge separation coupled to the oxidation of water. Electrons move through intermediate carriers toward PSI, where another excitation can promote further electron transfer to high-energy reducing equivalents. The detailed molecular carriers and chemical reactions of both photosystems belong to the next chapters. The important connection here is that an antenna serves a particular trapping system, and the useful output of the two systems must be coordinated. Extra excitation of one system cannot necessarily compensate for a persistent lack of excitation of the other.

An instructive spectral experiment is the red drop, in which photosynthetic efficiency declines at sufficiently long red wavelengths when those wavelengths are supplied alone. Emerson and colleagues subsequently demonstrated that combining far-red light with shorter-wavelength light could produce a photosynthetic rate greater than the sum of the rates observed under the separate beams in appropriate conditions. This enhancement provided evidence for cooperation between two light reactions. Its interpretation is consistent with the modern understanding that the photosystems differ in spectral excitation preferences and must operate together for sustained linear electron flow. Far-red light can preferentially excite PSI under many conditions; shorter wavelengths can help relieve a relative PSII excitation limitation. Neither statement implies that every photon below or above a single numerical cutoff belongs exclusively to one photosystem.

A researcher measuring the effect of an accessory pigment must therefore consider whether its removal changes the balance of excitation between PSII and PSI. A small decrease in photon capture by one antenna could have a substantial effect on the combined rate if that photosystem was already relatively underexcited. Conversely, reducing an excessively large antenna could sometimes improve the distribution of excitation without increasing total absorption. The rate of a coupled pathway is constrained by the operation of its connected steps, not simply by the sum of photons absorbed by all pigments. The cooperation of the photosystems connects antenna organization directly to the physiology of the whole chloroplast.

11. Competition between photochemistry, fluorescence and heat

The excited state of chlorophyll is transient. Once formed, it can return toward its ground state through several alternative routes. In an isolated pigment, some energy may be emitted as fluorescence, and some may be converted into molecular motion through nonradiative relaxation. Other excited states can also be populated. In a photosynthetic complex, excitation may migrate to neighboring pigments, be used in productive reaction-center photochemistry, or be dissipated through regulated protective mechanisms. To understand the efficiency of the system, it is necessary to think in terms of competing pathways rather than to treat any one measured fate as inevitable.

Fluorescence is the emission of light as an excited molecule returns toward a lower electronic state. Chlorophyll fluorescence generally occurs at longer wavelengths than the photons that produced the excitation, because some energy is lost during relaxation before emission. A red fluorescence signal can therefore be obtained after exciting a chlorophyll-containing preparation with blue light. The fluorescence photon is not an additional energy source created by the pigment; it is an alternative fate of some of the initially absorbed radiant energy. Its energy is less than or at most comparable to that of the absorbed photon in ordinary fluorescence processes.

Consider a model in which an excited pigment or a simplified equilibrated antenna population has three independent first-order fates: photochemical trapping at rate k_P, fluorescence at rate k_F, and nonradiative energy loss at rate k_N. If the total population of excitations at time t is X(t), then its decay obeys dX/dt = −(k_P + k_F + k_N)X. For a single initial population and constant rates, the solution is X(t) = X(0) exp[−(k_P + k_F + k_N)t]. The mean lifetime is the reciprocal of the summed rate. Integrating the number of excitations that decay through each pathway gives the yield for that pathway as its rate divided by the total decay rate. Thus a change in a single rate can alter all three yields.

For example, suppose the rates in a teaching model are k_P = 8 ns⁻¹, k_F = 1 ns⁻¹ and k_N = 1 ns⁻¹. The total decay rate is 10 ns⁻¹, the mean lifetime is 0.1 ns, and the photochemical, fluorescence and nonradiative yields are 0.8, 0.1 and 0.1. If the photochemical rate falls to zero while the other two remain unchanged, the lifetime rises to 0.5 ns and the fluorescence yield becomes 0.5. If protective dissipation subsequently increases k_N to 9 ns⁻¹, the fluorescence yield falls to 0.1 despite continued absence of photochemical trapping. The numbers are hypothetical and do not represent a measured plant. The important inference is that the same fluorescence yield can occur in very different physiological states.

Chlorophyll fluorescence is particularly useful in experiments because it can be measured without directly collecting every chemical product of photosynthesis. In dark-adapted material, a weak measuring light can estimate the minimum fluorescence level F₀ when most PSII centers are open under the measurement conditions. A brief saturating pulse can transiently close the centers and estimate maximum fluorescence Fₘ under the prevailing dissipative conditions. The variable component is Fᵥ = Fₘ − F₀. The ratio Fᵥ/Fₘ is widely used as an estimate of the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII photochemistry in appropriately dark-adapted material. The interpretation assumes that the measurement states have been achieved and that relevant quenching processes have relaxed to the intended extent.

A hypothetical sample with F₀ = 200 arbitrary detector units and Fₘ = 1000 units has Fᵥ/Fₘ = 800/1000 = 0.80. If another sample has F₀ = 300 and Fₘ = 750, the ratio is 450/750 = 0.60. This comparison is consistent with reduced maximum PSII photochemical efficiency in the second sample under valid conditions, but the ratio does not uniquely diagnose a particular molecular defect. Persistent nonphotochemical quenching, photodamage, antenna disconnection or other factors may affect the signal. The raw fluorescence units also depend on measuring geometry and instrumentation. The ratio is not a direct measurement of whole-leaf carbon assimilation or the fraction of all incident sunlight captured.

Changes in antenna connectivity can alter fluorescence even without changing the absorption spectrum substantially. If a peripheral chlorophyll population becomes poorly connected to a functional reaction center, its excitations may remain available for fluorescence longer. If the same pigments aggregate into a strongly quenched configuration, they might instead show lower fluorescence and shorter lifetimes. The direction of the fluorescence change therefore depends on the alternative pathways introduced or removed by the structural alteration. This is a valuable distinction in data interpretation: a measured increase in fluorescence is an observation, not a complete mechanistic explanation.

Antenna regulation also helps balance excitation of PSI and PSII. A classical description of state transitions begins with preferential excitation of PSII, which can increase reduction of the plastoquinone pool. In green plants a redox-sensitive regulatory process involving the STN7 kinase promotes phosphorylation of a fraction of LHCII under appropriate conditions. This can favor the functional association of mobile LHCII with PSI, increasing its relative excitation. When the relevant redox and regulatory conditions reverse, dephosphorylation, involving the TAP38/PPH1 phosphatase, can favor reassociation with PSII. The details depend on light intensity, organism and physiological state. It would be inaccurate to conclude that every molecule of LHCII moves during every state transition or that plastoquinone reduction necessarily induces unrestricted kinase activity under all conditions.

State transitions illustrate the difference between the chemical identity of an antenna complex and its immediate function. The Lhcb proteins and pigments of a mobile LHCII fraction can remain essentially the same while their relationship to PSI and PSII changes. An experiment might therefore observe redistribution of fluorescence or a change in the functional excitation of PSI without a large change in total pigment content. Detecting protein phosphorylation and changes in associations provides evidence for the regulatory mechanism, while measurements of photochemical activity establish whether the redistribution has the predicted physiological effect.

Longer-term acclimation can modify pigment content, the chlorophyll a:b ratio, antenna-protein abundance and the relative amounts of photosystems. A shaded environment often favors a greater investment in light harvesting, whereas a strongly illuminated environment may favor a smaller effective antenna relative to photochemical or metabolic capacity. These are common physiological trends rather than universal rules. Nutrient availability, species, leaf development and the spectral composition of the light environment influence the outcome. A comparison between a shade-grown leaf and a sun-grown leaf should therefore be made on a defined basis, such as leaf area, chlorophyll content or number of active reaction centers.

When light exceeds the capacity for safe photochemistry, regulated nonphotochemical quenching helps reduce the lifetime and concentration of excited chlorophyll states. This can decrease fluorescence and protect the photosynthetic machinery without requiring every excess photon to be converted into a chemical product. Photoprotection is not the same as complete cessation of photosynthesis: productive trapping and energy dissipation can occur simultaneously among different excitations. Chapter 6.A.05 will develop the molecular pH-dependent regulatory processes and repair mechanisms. The foundation established here is the kinetic competition that makes such regulation both possible and necessary.

12. Comparative light harvesting and environmental adaptation

Green plant antennae are one evolutionary solution to the problem of capturing light. The underlying principle is more widespread than their particular chlorophyll a/b protein architecture. Cyanobacteria also perform oxygenic photosynthesis using two photosystems, and many possess large peripheral antenna assemblies called phycobilisomes. These complexes contain phycobiliproteins whose chromophores are linear tetrapyrroles known as phycobilins. Their spectral properties provide efficient absorption in wavelength regions that differ from the strongest bands of chlorophyll a. Energy can move through the phycobilisome and be transferred to photosystem-associated chlorophyll. In many cyanobacteria and red algae, the organization therefore differs substantially from the membrane-embedded Lhcb and Lhca systems typical of land plants.

The chemical difference between a phycobilin and chlorophyll is informative. Phycobilins do not possess the same closed magnesium-containing chlorin macrocycle and hydrophobic phytol arrangement as chlorophyll. Their attachment to phycobiliproteins and the spatial arrangement of the protein complexes provide the molecular context for light absorption and transfer. Nevertheless, excitation must ultimately reach the photochemical machinery. A phycobilisome illustrates that the absorption properties and architecture of a peripheral antenna can evolve independently of many conserved features of its reaction centers. A student should not infer that all photosynthetic organisms possess chlorophyll b or LHCII simply because these are prominent in land plants.

Diatoms and many brown algae use chlorophyll a, chlorophyll c and carotenoids such as fucoxanthin in distinctive antenna proteins. These pigments are suited to capturing light in aquatic environments where the available spectral distribution can differ from direct sunlight at the surface. The exact spectral composition of underwater light depends on water depth, dissolved substances, particles and other conditions. There is no single universal rule that one pigment makes an organism superior at every depth or in every habitat. Adaptation is better understood as a relationship between available photons, pigment absorption, transfer efficiency and the organism's other physiological requirements.

Some anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria use bacteriochlorophyll-containing antennae and reaction centers. Their ability to absorb longer wavelengths demonstrates that the spectral limits of photosynthesis depend on the particular pigment–protein system. These organisms are not simply green plants operating beyond the conventional PAR range. Their reaction-center chemistry and electron sources differ from those of oxygenic photosynthesis. The comparative example helps prevent two common errors: assuming that visible-light absorption is a universal requirement for every form of photosynthesis, and assuming that every photosynthetic reaction produces oxygen. The complete physiology of these bacterial systems belongs elsewhere in the curriculum.

Within terrestrial plant canopies, light availability varies even over short vertical distances. Upper leaves intercept much of the incoming red and blue light, while the light transmitted and scattered into lower layers can be enriched in other wavelengths, including far-red. Lower leaves often face a combination of reduced photon flux and altered spectral composition. Their acclimation can affect both total light harvesting and the relative excitation of PSI and PSII. This situation connects the spectroscopy of pigment–protein complexes to the ecology of plants. The light signal perceived by photoreceptors can also influence plant architecture in response to neighboring vegetation, but that response must be distinguished from the direct use of photons in photosynthetic photochemistry.

The size of an antenna has potential consequences beyond an individual leaf. In dense algal cultures or plant canopies, very large antennas in the most strongly illuminated cells may intercept more light than those cells can use productively, leaving less light for cells deeper in the population. Under particular conditions, reducing excessive antenna size could improve the distribution of light and collective productivity even if the initial absorption rate of an individual surface cell decreases. The opposite could occur under weak illumination, where reducing the antenna would further limit useful light capture. These predictions are conditional and require measurements of whole-population performance. They provide a simple example of why molecular engineering must be evaluated at the appropriate biological scale.

Biotechnological approaches to antenna modification may alter expression of pigment-binding proteins or the regulation of energy allocation. The aim is not necessarily to increase total chlorophyll content. It may instead be to optimize the relationship between incident light, effective antenna size, photochemical capacity and photoprotection. A change that improves productivity in a controlled light environment may be disadvantageous in the field, where light fluctuates and stress is variable. A convincing comparison should measure growth or carbon assimilation over meaningful intervals, monitor photosystem function and account for possible changes in pigment composition or stress sensitivity. A biochemical measurement on a purified complex is useful mechanistic evidence but does not establish improved agricultural performance by itself.

Comparative biology consequently clarifies which features are essential and which are contingent. Some form of effective light absorption, a means of communicating excitation to photochemical sites and a mechanism of charge separation are necessary for the type of light-driven energy conversion considered here. The identities of accessory pigments, the size and location of peripheral antennae, and the regulation of their associations vary. Understanding that distinction is more useful for interpreting unfamiliar examples than memorizing an unqualified claim that chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids constitute every possible photosynthetic antenna.

13. Integrating experiments: measuring capture, transfer and useful output

A robust investigation of an antenna begins by defining the step to be tested. If the hypothesis concerns photon capture, absorption or absorptance is the appropriate starting measurement. If the hypothesis concerns delivery of excitation, a spectroscopic or time-resolved measurement of energy transfer is more informative. If the question concerns the success of charge separation, measurements of photochemical products or suitable changes in reaction-center redox state are needed. If the question concerns physiological productivity, gas exchange, growth and other organism-level outcomes become relevant. Each level is constrained by earlier events but also by mechanisms that are absent from an isolated pigment preparation.

A simple experimental design for testing the function of chlorophyll b would compare a normal green plant with a mutant impaired in chlorophyll b synthesis and, ideally, a complemented line in which the relevant gene function is restored. The three lines should be grown under comparable conditions and measured at similar developmental stages. Pigment analysis could establish the loss or recovery of chlorophyll b. Measurements of Lhcb protein abundance and photosystem-core abundance would help determine whether peripheral complexes were altered and whether active center numbers remained comparable. Comparing response under selected wavelengths at low photon flux could test changes in functional antenna capture. Measurements at saturating light could determine whether the maximum photochemical capacity was also affected.

If the mutant shows a substantial reduction in low-light activity but a similar maximum activity per active center under saturating light, the result is compatible with a smaller or less efficient antenna. If the same mutant has a lower saturating activity even after normalizing to active center number, altered chemistry or downstream limitations may also be involved. If complementation restores the pigment composition and functional phenotype, the causal association with the mutated gene is strengthened. Complementation does not prove that the pigment itself is the only affected component because restoration of the pigment can also stabilize proteins and modify complex assembly. A careful mechanistic conclusion distinguishes the initiating genetic change from its multiple physiological consequences.

A related question asks whether two pigments in an antenna exchange excitation energy. Donor-only and acceptor-containing preparations can be illuminated at a wavelength chosen to favor absorption by the donor. If the acceptor emits fluorescence under these conditions, direct excitation of the acceptor must first be accounted for. A reduction in donor excited-state lifetime in the presence of the acceptor provides complementary evidence of an additional donor decay pathway. If an acceptor fluorescence signal appears at the same time, the interpretation of excitation transfer is stronger. However, changes in donor lifetime can also result from quenching unrelated to the intended acceptor, and acceptor emission can be influenced by its own fluorescence yield. A combination of observations is more reliable than any single signal.

When the donor lifetime is τ_D in the absence of transfer and τ_DA in the presence of an acceptor, the idealized efficiency can be calculated as 1 − τ_DA/τ_D, provided that the donor's other decay rates are unchanged and the observed lifetimes correspond to the same donor population. To derive this relation, the total donor decay rate in the acceptor-containing system is 1/τ_DA, while the original decay rate is 1/τ_D. Their difference is the transfer rate. Dividing the difference by the total decay rate gives 1 − τ_DA/τ_D. A lifetime reduction from 4 ns to 1 ns would therefore correspond to an idealized transfer efficiency of 75%. The calculation is not valid if the acceptor also changes the donor's nontransfer quenching rate or if the measured decay represents heterogeneous populations that cannot be described by one lifetime.

An experiment comparing two wavelengths should likewise be explicit about its normalization. Suppose a leaf absorbs 90% of incident red photons and 50% of incident green photons. Under equal incident flux of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, suppose gross oxygen-evolution rates are 9 and 5 µmol O₂ m⁻² s⁻¹, respectively. The yields per incident photon are 0.09 and 0.05 O₂ molecules per photon, whereas the yields per absorbed photon are both 0.10. The apparent spectral difference in oxygen production is explained entirely by capture within this hypothetical dataset. If the green-light oxygen-evolution rate were instead 2.5 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, its yield per absorbed photon would be 0.05, and differences after absorption would require investigation. The calculation would not by itself determine whether the discrepancy was due to a nonphotosynthetic absorber, excitation imbalance or another limitation.

A graph of oxygen evolution versus photon flux can help distinguish light harvesting from capacity. At low flux, the initial slope represents the change in rate per unit change in photon flux under the experimental conditions. At sufficiently high flux the rate may approach a plateau as reaction-center turnover or later processes become limiting. Comparing slopes and plateaus for matched preparations is more informative than comparing a single arbitrary intensity. A mutant with half the slope but the same plateau may have a capture defect, while a mutant with an unchanged low-light slope but a reduced plateau might have a limitation that emerges at higher flux. These are hypotheses rather than unique diagnoses because multiple mechanisms can produce similar curve shapes.

The same care is needed when interpreting photographs of pigment extracts separated by chromatography. Bands at different positions indicate that components have different interactions with the stationary and mobile phases under the experimental conditions. A band color provides an initial clue to pigment identity, but mobility is not a universal constant independent of the solvent system. Authentic standards or spectral analysis improve identification. The intensity or area of a band cannot automatically be translated into the number of functional photosystem units. Extraction breaks the molecular architecture through which pigments normally transfer excitation. Chromatography can describe chemical composition, while functional assays are required to establish the role of those pigments in intact complexes.

Taken together, these experimental approaches show why light harvesting is a mechanistic subject rather than a collection of colors, wavelengths and pigment names. It requires coordinated optical, structural, kinetic and physiological reasoning. A student who can follow the difference between photon interception, excited-state transfer, trapping and downstream turnover is equipped to interpret new data even when the organism, pigment or experimental arrangement is unfamiliar. The next chapter develops the chemistry of PSII, beginning at precisely the point reached here: a light-excited reaction center capable of transferring an electron to its first acceptors.

Example 1. Photon energy and power

For a 700 nm beam, Eₘ = 119626.6/700 = 170.895 kJ mol⁻¹. At 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, power density = 170895 × 0.000200 = 34.18 W m⁻². At 80% absorptance, 160 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ are absorbed. No photochemical yield is implied.

An extract has A = 0.80. Assuming negligible reflection and scattering, T = 10⁻⁰·⁸ = 0.1585, so absorptance = 0.8415. Doubling concentration gives A = 1.60 and absorptance = 0.9749, not 1.683.

Let R₀ = 5 nm and r = 6 nm. (r/R₀)⁶ = 1.2⁶ = 2.985984; η = 1/3.985984 = 0.2509. This is an isolated-pair prediction, not a whole-antenna yield.

For τD = 3.2 ns and τDA = 0.8 ns, η = 1 − 0.8/3.2 = 0.75. kET = 1/0.8 − 1/3.2 = 0.9375 ns⁻¹. Assumes intrinsic donor decay unchanged.

From the hypothetical table, blue yield = 6/80 = 0.075 O₂ per photon, compared with red 9/90 = 0.10. Absorption difference alone cannot explain this normalized contrast.

Part B — Conceptual questions

Question 1. Which molecular feature of chlorophyll contributes most directly to visible-light absorption?

  1. The esterified phytol chain
  2. The thylakoid lipid bilayer alone
  3. The extended conjugated chlorin system
  4. The magnesium ion alone
Answer and explanation

C. The extended conjugated chlorin system

The delocalized electronic states of the conjugated macrocycle permit visible transitions. Magnesium modifies its electronic properties, but is not the sole chromophore. Phytol mainly contributes hydrophobic association; lipids are the assembly environment.

Question 2. The structural substitution distinguishing chlorophyll b from chlorophyll a is principally:

  1. Loss of Mg²⁺
  2. Replacement of phytol by a sugar
  3. Replacement of a methyl group by a formyl group
  4. Addition of an oxygen atom to the central metal
Answer and explanation

C. Replacement of a methyl group by a formyl group

The formyl group in chlorophyll b changes the electronic and binding properties of the macrocycle. Loss of magnesium instead produces pheophytin; both a and b normally contain magnesium and phytol.

Question 3. For two monochromatic photons in vacuum, one at 450 nm and one at 675 nm, which relationship is correct?

  1. The 675 nm photon has 1.5 times the energy
  2. Both have equal energy because both are visible
  3. The 450 nm photon has 1.5 times the energy
  4. The energy depends only on photon flux
Answer and explanation

C. The 450 nm photon has 1.5 times the energy

E is inversely proportional to wavelength, so E450/E675 = 675/450 = 1.5. Flux determines total delivered energy, not the energy of a single photon.

Question 4. A green leaf appears green primarily because:

  1. Green photons have insufficient energy for any photosynthesis
  2. Green light is relatively enriched in reflected and transmitted light
  3. Chlorophyll emits mainly green fluorescence
  4. The leaf absorbs only red wavelengths
Answer and explanation

B. Green light is relatively enriched in reflected and transmitted light

The green appearance reflects the outgoing spectrum, not a complete absence of green absorption. Chlorophyll fluorescence is largely red/far-red, and chlorophyll also absorbs strongly in blue.

Question 5. Which statement distinguishes an action spectrum from an absorption spectrum?

  1. An action spectrum is always measured from purified chlorophyll
  2. Absorption spectra report oxygen evolution
  3. An action spectrum measures the wavelength dependence of a biological response
  4. Both spectra must have exactly identical peaks
Answer and explanation

C. An action spectrum measures the wavelength dependence of a biological response

An action spectrum measures a response such as oxygen evolution, whereas absorption describes optical capture. Accessory pigments, tissue optics and physiological yield can make their curves differ.

Question 6. In Engelmann's classic experiment, the motile aerobic bacteria served mainly as indicators of:

  1. The chlorophyll concentration of each algal cell
  2. The electrical potential across the thylakoid
  3. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the cytoplasm
  4. Local oxygen availability
Answer and explanation

D. Local oxygen availability

The bacteria accumulated preferentially where oxygen production was high. They did not measure chlorophyll or membrane voltage, and their distribution is an indirect qualitative assay.

Question 7. Which plant pigment is a major constituent of peripheral antennae but not normally assigned the primary reaction-center photochemistry?

  1. Chlorophyll a
  2. Pheophytin a in PSII
  3. Chlorophyll b
  4. P700-associated chlorophyll a
Answer and explanation

C. Chlorophyll b

Chlorophyll b is an accessory pigment predominantly in peripheral antennae. Chlorophyll a occurs in both antennae and reaction centers; pheophytin is a PSII electron acceptor.

Question 8. Which of the following is a xanthophyll?

  1. Lutein
  2. β-Carotene
  3. Chlorophyll a
  4. Pheophytin
Answer and explanation

A. Lutein

Lutein is an oxygenated carotenoid. β-Carotene is a hydrocarbon carotene. Chlorophyll and pheophytin are tetrapyrrole-derived pigments.

Question 9. Which statement about the LHCII trimer is correct for representative higher plants?

  1. It is a pigment-binding assembly of Lhcb-family proteins
  2. Each monomer is the complete PSII reaction center
  3. LHCII contains only chlorophyll a
  4. The three monomers are electron-transfer quinones
Answer and explanation

A. It is a pigment-binding assembly of Lhcb-family proteins

Major LHCII is commonly trimeric and binds chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids. The D1/D2 region of the PSII core, not an LHCII monomer, houses the primary electron-transfer cofactors.

Question 10. CP43 and CP47 are chiefly:

  1. PSI terminal acceptors
  2. Soluble enzymes of the Calvin cycle
  3. PSII core antenna proteins
  4. Two chlorophyll b biosynthetic enzymes
Answer and explanation

C. PSII core antenna proteins

Both proteins bind core antenna chlorophylls associated with PSII. They do not function as soluble carbon-fixation enzymes or PSI acceptors.

Question 11. What distinguishes the stroma from the thylakoid lumen?

  1. The stroma is the inside of each thylakoid
  2. The lumen lies outside the chloroplast envelope
  3. The lumen is enclosed by the thylakoid membrane, whereas the stroma surrounds thylakoids
  4. They are identical aqueous spaces in stacked regions
Answer and explanation

C. The lumen is enclosed by the thylakoid membrane, whereas the stroma surrounds thylakoids

The thylakoid membrane separates the lumen from the stroma. Stacking changes membrane arrangement but does not abolish the compartment distinction.

Question 12. LHCI is preferentially associated with:

  1. Photosystem I
  2. Mitochondrial complex I
  3. Photosystem II exclusively
  4. The chloroplast envelope
Answer and explanation

A. Photosystem I

LHCI refers to the Lhca-family peripheral antenna of PSI; LHCII is principally associated with PSII and can contribute to PSI under some regulatory conditions.

Question 13. In ordinary resonance excitation transfer from D* to A, the product is best represented as:

  1. D + A*
  2. D⁺ + A⁻
  3. D⁻ + A⁺
  4. D* + A* with two independent excitations
Answer and explanation

A. D + A*

The donor relaxes as the acceptor becomes excited without obligatory electron transfer. Charged products represent a different process, and two independent excitations cannot ordinarily arise from one transferred excitation.

Question 14. Which pair of spectra is particularly relevant for Förster transfer?

  1. Donor emission and acceptor absorption
  2. Donor absorption and acceptor reflection only
  3. Acceptor emission and its own absorption only
  4. The net oxygen evolution curves of two leaves
Answer and explanation

A. Donor emission and acceptor absorption

Donor emission and acceptor absorption overlap helps establish energetic compatibility. Orientation, separation and other factors also matter; whole-leaf oxygen curves do not define the molecular overlap integral.

Question 15. The ideal Förster transfer rate has which dependence on donor–acceptor separation r, if other parameters are fixed?

  1. r²
  2. r⁻¹
  3. r⁻⁶
  4. Independent of r
Answer and explanation

C. r⁻⁶

Dipole–dipole Förster transfer has a steep inverse-sixth-power distance dependence. Neither an inverse-linear relation nor distance independence describes this idealized model.

Question 16. At r = R₀, the transfer efficiency of the ideal Förster pair is:

  1. 100%
  2. 0%
  3. 50%
  4. 25%
Answer and explanation

C. 50%

At the Förster radius, transfer and original donor decay have equal rates. The probability of transfer is therefore one half.

Question 17. Which statement about P680 and P700 is most accurate?

  1. They are names of carotenoids
  2. They absorb exclusively one exact wavelength
  3. P700 belongs to PSII and P680 to PSI
  4. They designate characteristic reaction-center donor systems of PSII and PSI, respectively
Answer and explanation

D. They designate characteristic reaction-center donor systems of PSII and PSI, respectively

The names reflect characteristic spectral properties and functional assignment, not exclusive excitation wavelengths. Antennae deliver excitations from a broad spectral range.

Question 18. Which mechanism directly establishes the first charge-separated state?

  1. Reflection of a photon from the leaf
  2. Electron transfer from an excited donor to an acceptor
  3. Antenna excitation migration without redox change
  4. The emission of a fluorescence photon
Answer and explanation

B. Electron transfer from an excited donor to an acceptor

Primary photochemistry redistributes electrons and creates charge separation. Antenna transfer can supply the excitation but does not itself have to change oxidation states.

Question 19. A chlorophyll triplet can be hazardous primarily because it can:

  1. Generate ATP without electron flow
  2. Convert carbon dioxide into carotenoid
  3. Remove the phytol chain by fluorescence
  4. Transfer energy to oxygen and produce reactive singlet oxygen
Answer and explanation

D. Transfer energy to oxygen and produce reactive singlet oxygen

Triplet chlorophyll may sensitize molecular oxygen to form singlet oxygen. Nearby carotenoids can reduce this risk. ATP generation and carbon fixation are separate reactions.

Question 20. The primary role of chlorophyll fluorescence in experimental analysis is to provide:

  1. A direct count of all carbon atoms fixed
  2. An exact measure of all carotenoid molecules
  3. A probe of excited-state fate and photosystem function
  4. A direct measurement of stromal pH in every condition
Answer and explanation

C. A probe of excited-state fate and photosystem function

Fluorescence reports a competing excitation-decay pathway. Its interpretation depends on photochemistry, heat dissipation and measuring conditions, so it cannot directly quantify all other physiological variables.

Part C — Analytical questions

Question 21. Two equal-power monochromatic lamps emit at 450 nm and 675 nm. Ignoring optical losses, what is the photon-number ratio N675/N450?

  1. 2/3
  2. 1.5
  3. 1
  4. 2.25
Answer and explanation

B. 1.5

At equal power, photon rate is proportional to wavelength because each photon carries hc/λ. Thus 675/450 = 1.5. The inverse ratio applies to photon energies, not photon numbers.

Question 22. An intact leaf has reflectance 0.12 and transmittance 0.18 at 550 nm. At 200 µmol incident photons m⁻² s⁻¹, what is absorbed flux?

  1. 60 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹
  2. 176 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹
  3. 140 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹
  4. 164 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. 140 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹

Absorptance is 1 − 0.12 − 0.18 = 0.70, so 200 × 0.70 = 140. Sixty is the unabsorbed flux; the other options ignore one outgoing component.

Question 23. A dilute extract has absorbance 0.30. Its concentration doubles with path length fixed and Beer–Lambert behavior maintained. Approximate absorbed fraction, neglecting reflection?

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.60
  3. 0.50
  4. 1.00
Answer and explanation

A. 0.75

New absorbance is 0.60; T = 10⁻⁰·⁶ ≈ 0.251, and absorption ≈ 0.749. Absorbance is logarithmic and cannot be read directly as fraction.

Question 24. Red and green beams supply 100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ each. Red absorptance is 0.8 and gross O₂ rate 8; green absorptance is 0.4 and O₂ rate 4, in matching molar units. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. Both beams give the same gross O₂ yield per absorbed photon
  2. Green photons cannot reach a center
  3. Red has twice the postabsorption efficiency
  4. Green yields more O₂ per incident photon
Answer and explanation

A. Both beams give the same gross O₂ yield per absorbed photon

Red 8/80 and green 4/40 both equal 0.10 O₂ per absorbed photon. Red is more effective per incident photon because more photons are absorbed; the data do not imply a lower green postabsorption yield.

Question 25. A carotenoid-containing antenna emits chlorophyll fluorescence when excited at a wavelength where direct chlorophyll absorption is negligible. What is the strongest justified inference?

  1. The preparation necessarily evolves oxygen
  2. Every carotenoid transfers with unit efficiency
  3. The data support energy transfer from carotenoid to chlorophyll
  4. The carotenoid has donated an electron to NADP⁺
Answer and explanation

C. The data support energy transfer from carotenoid to chlorophyll

Sensitized chlorophyll emission supports excitation transfer after direct excitation and other artifacts are excluded. It does not demonstrate downstream oxygen evolution, NADP⁺ reduction or a particular absolute efficiency.

Question 26. Two antenna samples have identical pigment absorbance, but the second shows a longer chlorophyll excited-state lifetime and lower photochemical yield with unchanged active-center abundance. Which hypothesis fits best?

  1. Higher productive trapping rate
  2. Higher absorption cross-section demonstrated by the data
  3. A larger donor decay rate from heat alone
  4. Weaker excitation coupling to productive traps
Answer and explanation

D. Weaker excitation coupling to productive traps

Reduced access to a photochemical trap can lengthen the excited-state lifetime while lowering photochemical yield. A higher trapping or heat-decay rate by itself would shorten the lifetime. Absorbance is unchanged.

Question 27. An ideal donor–acceptor pair has r = 1.5R₀. Approximately what fraction of donor excitations transfer?

  1. 0.50
  2. 0.088
  3. 0.67
  4. 0.081
Answer and explanation

D. 0.081

1.5⁶ = 11.390625, so η = 1/(1+11.390625) ≈ 0.0807. The value 0.088 approximates the relative rate (1/1.5⁶), not the yield.

Question 28. A donor lifetime changes from 5 ns to 2 ns after addition of an acceptor. With unchanged intrinsic decay, transfer efficiency is:

  1. 40%
  2. 60%
  3. 150%
  4. 30%
Answer and explanation

B. 60%

η = 1 − 2/5 = 0.60. Forty percent is the remaining lifetime fraction; 150% cannot be an efficiency.

Question 29. With the same R₀ and donor lifetime, doubling r changes the ideal Förster transfer rate by what factor?

  1. 1/64
  2. 1/2
  3. 1/4
  4. 64
Answer and explanation

A. 1/64

The rate varies as r⁻⁶, so doubling separation multiplies it by 2⁻⁶. The efficiency itself is not necessarily reduced by exactly this factor because it is a ratio of competing rates.

Question 30. The donor and acceptor are at an unchanged distance, but a mutation decreases donor-emission/acceptor-absorption overlap. In an otherwise unchanged weak-coupling pair, what is predicted?

  1. Unchanged transfer because distance is unchanged
  2. Lower Förster transfer rate
  3. Greater transfer under all conditions
  4. Immediate formation of two charged pigments
Answer and explanation

B. Lower Förster transfer rate

Spectral overlap contributes to R₀ and hence to the rate. Distance alone does not fix transfer; no obligatory electron transfer follows.

Question 31. A mutant lacking chlorophyll b shows half the low-light O₂ rate per active PSII center but the same saturating rate. Which conclusion is most consistent?

  1. PSII water oxidation must be completely absent
  2. The Calvin cycle is absent
  3. Chlorophyll b normally provides all reaction-center electrons
  4. Functional light harvesting per center is reduced
Answer and explanation

D. Functional light harvesting per center is reduced

The lower initial slope with preserved maximum suggests reduced capture/delivery rather than loss of all PSII chemistry. The mutation may also change antenna protein abundance, so it is not a pure pigment-subtraction experiment.

Question 32. A PSII preparation initially has kP = 7, kF = 1, kN = 2 ns⁻¹. Closure makes kP = 0 without changing other rates. Fluorescence yield changes from:

  1. 0.1 to 1/3
  2. 0.1 to 0.5
  3. 0.7 to 0
  4. 0.2 to 2/3
Answer and explanation

A. 0.1 to 1/3

Initial yield is 1/(7+1+2)=0.1. After closure it is 1/(1+2)=1/3. The other fractions refer to different decay rates or assume heat loss disappears.

Question 33. The same closed preparation subsequently increases kN to 9 ns⁻¹ while kF remains 1 ns⁻¹. Which observation is expected?

  1. Photochemistry is restored to 90%
  2. Fluorescence must increase to 90%
  3. Excited-state lifetime increases indefinitely
  4. Fluorescence yield falls to 0.10 although photochemistry remains zero
Answer and explanation

D. Fluorescence yield falls to 0.10 although photochemistry remains zero

The new total rate is 10 ns⁻¹, with fluorescence yield 1/10. kP remains zero; increased nonradiative decay shortens lifetime rather than restoring photochemistry.

Question 34. A dark-adapted leaf gives F₀ = 240 and Fₘ = 960. The estimate Fᵥ/Fₘ is:

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.80
  3. 4.0
  4. 0.75
Answer and explanation

D. 0.75

Fᵥ = 960−240=720, and 720/960=0.75. F₀/Fₘ is 0.25, and Fₘ/F₀ is 4; neither equals the requested ratio.

Question 35. The same total amount of chlorophyll is present in two leaves, but leaf X has twice as many active centers as leaf Y. All else is unknown. Which claim is justified?

  1. X must absorb half as many photons
  2. Y always has twice the quantum yield
  3. Both leaves must have the same maximum rate
  4. Pigment amount alone is insufficient to infer functional antenna size or rate
Answer and explanation

D. Pigment amount alone is insufficient to infer functional antenna size or rate

Total pigment content does not specify allocation, coupling or activity. The number of centers and pigments per center affect capture and capacity, but their precise functional properties remain unmeasured.

Question 36. An algal sample evolves 3 units of O₂ with far-red and 5 units with shorter red light separately, but 12 units when combined under comparable conditions. What does the superadditive result support?

  1. Every antenna pigment is a separate photochemical center
  2. Cooperation between differently excited photochemical systems
  3. A complete absence of PSI activity
  4. The inability of far-red light to participate in photosynthesis
Answer and explanation

B. Cooperation between differently excited photochemical systems

The combined response exceeds 3+5=8, supporting complementary light reactions in the classical Emerson enhancement interpretation. It is not evidence that far-red is useless or that each antenna pigment is a center.

Question 37. A mutant has strongly reduced STN7 activity. Under conditions normally inducing state 2, which prediction is most appropriate?

  1. Phosphorylation-dependent redistribution of mobile LHCII is impaired
  2. All chlorophyll a becomes chlorophyll b
  3. PSI ceases to contain any LHCI
  4. PSII is physically removed from the thylakoid
Answer and explanation

A. Phosphorylation-dependent redistribution of mobile LHCII is impaired

STN7 participates in LHCII phosphorylation during state transitions. A loss can impair redistribution without eliminating PSI or chemically converting pigments.

Question 38. Under suitable state-transition conditions, loss of TAP38/PPH1 would most directly impair:

  1. Formation of the Mg chlorin ring by direct catalysis
  2. LHCII dephosphorylation and return toward state 1
  3. Water splitting at every PSII center
  4. Carbon incorporation into RuBP by Rubisco
Answer and explanation

B. LHCII dephosphorylation and return toward state 1

TAP38/PPH1 catalyzes LHCII dephosphorylation. The other processes belong to pigment biosynthesis, PSII chemistry or carbon metabolism.

Question 39. A mutant deficient in a carotenoid shows normal initial absorbance but rapid high-light decline in PSII function. Which additional evidence best tests a protective role?

  1. Compare oxidative damage and recovery in mutant, wild type and complemented line under weak and strong light
  2. Only a photograph of the leaf color
  3. An exact count of stomata without light treatments
  4. Measure the same absorbance again in darkness only
Answer and explanation

A. Compare oxidative damage and recovery in mutant, wild type and complemented line under weak and strong light

Light-dependent damage and rescue help test the protective hypothesis. Controls and matched growth conditions are needed because carotenoids also affect assembly. Color and repeated absorbance alone cannot establish the cause.

Question 40. A highly red-shifted PSI antenna pigment has an excited state below some states involved in productive trapping. Why need it not permanently immobilize excitation?

  1. Energy transfer is always strictly downhill
  2. Every pigment simultaneously loses an electron
  3. Its photon energy is necessarily twice that of blue light
  4. Thermal population and reversible excitation exchange can allow access to trapping states
Answer and explanation

D. Thermal population and reversible excitation exchange can allow access to trapping states

Antenna energy migration can be reversible and thermally assisted. Strictly downhill irreversible transfer is an oversimplification; the red photon has lower energy than a blue one.

Question 41. A researcher measures gross O₂ yield per incident photon without controlling leaf absorptance. Which additional measurement is essential to estimate yield per absorbed photon?

  1. Only chloroplast diameter
  2. Only the color of the incident lamp housing
  3. The number of mitochondria
  4. Reflectance and transmittance at the measurement wavelength
Answer and explanation

D. Reflectance and transmittance at the measurement wavelength

Absorptance = 1−R−T provides the necessary denominator correction. The other measurements do not establish the fraction of incident photons absorbed.

Question 42. An antenna complex lacks its reaction center but still shows rapid transfer between bound pigments. Which conclusion follows?

  1. ATP synthesis must be occurring in the isolated antenna
  2. Each transfer event is a transmembrane electron transfer
  3. O₂ evolution proves the center is still present
  4. Excitation migration and reaction-center charge separation are separable processes
Answer and explanation

D. Excitation migration and reaction-center charge separation are separable processes

Energy migration can occur within a functional antenna without the redox cofactors needed for primary photochemistry. It does not establish ATP production, water oxidation or electron movement.

Additional advanced formats

Question 43. Assertion–reason: A: Absorption and photosynthetic action spectra can differ. R: Productive photosynthesis depends on processes after absorption, including excitation trapping.

  1. A and R true; R does not explain A
  2. A false; R true
  3. A true; R false
  4. Both true and R explains A
Answer and explanation

D. Both true and R explains A

Both statements are correct; postabsorption yield and accessory systems explain why the action spectrum need not match an isolated-pigment spectrum. The assertion is not a claim of no correlation.

Question 44. Assertion–reason: A: LHCII may contribute to PSI excitation during state transitions. R: Every LHCII chlorophyll becomes a new PSI electron donor when phosphorylated.

  1. Both true and R explains A
  2. A true; R false
  3. Both false
  4. A false; R true
Answer and explanation

B. A true; R false

A mobile LHCII fraction can associate functionally with PSI; phosphorylation does not convert its chlorophylls into primary electron donors. The reason is false.

Question 45. Multiple statements: I. Chlorophyll b contains Mg. II. β-Carotene is a xanthophyll. III. Pheophytin lacks Mg. IV. Phytol is the principal visible chromophore. Select all correct.

  1. I and III
  2. II and IV
  3. I, II and III
  4. III and IV
Answer and explanation

A. I and III

I and III are correct. β-Carotene is a hydrocarbon carotene, and the chlorin ring rather than the phytol chain supplies the principal chromophore.

Question 46. Matching: 1. P680; 2. P700; 3. CP43; 4. Lhca. Which set is correct?

  1. 1 PSI donor, 2 PSII donor, 3 peripheral PSI, 4 water-splitting complex
  2. 1 PSII donor, 2 PSI donor, 3 PSII core antenna, 4 PSI peripheral antenna
  3. 1 carotenoid, 2 xanthophyll, 3 Calvin enzyme, 4 lumen proton
  4. 1 PSII donor, 2 PSI donor, 3 PSI core, 4 PSII peripheral antenna
Answer and explanation

B. 1 PSII donor, 2 PSI donor, 3 PSII core antenna, 4 PSI peripheral antenna

P680 and P700 designate PSII and PSI donor systems, respectively. CP43 is a PSII core antenna; Lhca proteins comprise LHCI. The alternatives swap associations or confuse pigments with enzymes.

Question 47. A diagram shows D* + A → D + A*. Which observation distinguishes this step from D* + A → D⁺ + A⁻?

  1. Only the latter involves formation of an oxidized donor and reduced acceptor
  2. Both molecules must emit photons
  3. Both processes necessarily move a proton
  4. Only the former conserves energy
Answer and explanation

A. Only the latter involves formation of an oxidized donor and reduced acceptor

The first diagram represents excitation transfer, the latter electron transfer. Energy conservation applies to both; neither requires emission or proton movement by itself.

Question 48. A set of relative light-response curves follows V=Vmax(1−e⁻ᵏᴵ). With Vmax fixed and k reduced by half, which change is predicted?

  1. The asymptote halves
  2. Initial slope halves; the asymptote is unchanged
  3. Both initial slope and asymptote double
  4. Activity becomes negative at zero irradiance
Answer and explanation

B. Initial slope halves; the asymptote is unchanged

Differentiating gives initial slope Vmax k. At high I, the exponential tends to zero, leaving Vmax. This simple hypothetical curve isolates capture sensitivity, not all physiological processes.

Question 49. A 650 nm beam delivers 0.020 mol photons m⁻² in one exposure. What incident energy is supplied per area?

  1. 0.00368 kJ m⁻²
  2. 3.68 kJ m⁻²
  3. 36.8 kJ m⁻²
  4. 184 kJ m⁻²
Answer and explanation

B. 3.68 kJ m⁻²

Molar energy is 119626.6/650 ≈ 184.04 kJ/mol. Multiplying by 0.020 gives 3.68 kJ/m². The 184 value is per mole, not per area for this dose.

Question 50. Which comparison best discriminates sensitized fluorescence from direct excitation of the acceptor?

  1. Compare the combined sample with an unrelated green leaf only
  2. Measure acceptor-only, donor-only and combined samples with matched excitation and detection
  3. Record only absorbance of the donor at a different wavelength
  4. Use a single total fluorescence intensity without controls
Answer and explanation

B. Measure acceptor-only, donor-only and combined samples with matched excitation and detection

The separate preparations allow correction for direct acceptor excitation and donor emission overlap. Matched measurement conditions are crucial; a lone intensity measurement cannot identify the mechanism.

Question 51. Consider phycobilisome-containing cyanobacteria and green land plants. Which generalization is sound?

  1. Both necessarily contain LHCII
  2. Neither performs oxygenic photosynthesis
  3. Different peripheral antenna structures can supply excitation to photosystems
  4. Phycobilins and chlorophyll b have identical molecular structure
Answer and explanation

C. Different peripheral antenna structures can supply excitation to photosystems

Many cyanobacteria use phycobilisomes and plants use Lhca/Lhcb antennae. Both can perform oxygenic photosynthesis; their accessory pigments and proteins need not be identical.

Question 52. An experimental treatment doubles leaf chlorophyll content and leaves incident flux fixed, but the oxygen rate increases by only 5%. Which explanation remains possible?

  1. The data violate conservation of energy
  2. Absorption or downstream photochemistry was already near saturation
  3. Every added pigment must be an electron acceptor
  4. The treatment proves chlorophyll is not involved in light harvesting
Answer and explanation

B. Absorption or downstream photochemistry was already near saturation

Both optical capture and metabolic response can saturate. A small rate gain is compatible with more pigment without proportional functional benefit. The other conclusions do not follow from the data.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Green light is useless
Green wavelengths can be absorbed and support photosynthesis, especially deeper in tissue. The color of outgoing light is not an absolute absorption rule.
Blue photons always produce more ATP per photon
Excess excitation energy may relax as heat before trapping; downstream stoichiometry is not determined by incident photon energy alone.
Equal watts means equal photons
Photon energy varies inversely with wavelength. At equal power, longer wavelengths deliver more photons.
Absorbance of 0.5 means 50% absorption
Absorbance is logarithmic; neglecting reflection, absorptance is 1−10⁻ᴬ.
Action spectrum equals the chlorophyll a extract spectrum
Accessory pigments, tissue optics, excitation balance and physiology modify biological response.
Engelmann measured absolute oxygen yield
Bacterial distribution was an indirect qualitative indicator, subject to diffusion and movement.
Chlorophyll b is a reaction-center donor
Chlorophyll b is principally peripheral antenna pigment in green plants; chlorophyll a participates in primary photochemistry.
Phytol is the main visible chromophore
The conjugated chlorin ring is responsible for most visible absorption; phytol contributes hydrophobic association.
Carotenoids only protect and never harvest
Some carotenoids transfer excitation; others or the same pigments in other states support protection.
CP43 and CP47 are peripheral LHCI proteins
They are PSII core antenna components.
LHCII cannot ever serve PSI
State transitions can allow a mobile LHCII fraction to contribute to PSI excitation.
Excitation transfer is electron transfer
Resonance energy transfer redistributes excitation without requiring oxidation or reduction of either molecule.
Förster transfer is controlled only by distance
Orientation, spectral overlap and donor/environmental properties also matter.
All antenna energy moves strictly downhill
Reversible exchange and thermal effects can allow transfer from low-energy antenna states to trapping states.
P680 only absorbs exactly 680 nm
Names indicate characteristic spectra; antennae and broad absorption bands permit excitation by multiple wavelengths.
Higher fluorescence always means higher photosynthesis
Fluorescence competes with trapping and nonradiative dissipation; closure can increase fluorescence while reducing photochemistry.
Fᵥ/Fₘ equals whole-leaf carbon fixation efficiency
It estimates maximum PSII photochemical efficiency under suitable dark-adapted conditions, not carbon gain or total solar conversion.
More antenna pigments always improve growth
Light saturation, protection, center capacity and shading of other cells create conditional tradeoffs.

Integrated revision and mastery

Photosynthetic light harvesting begins with wavelength-selective absorption by pigments. Chlorophyll a and b have different electronic and binding properties; carotenoids supplement capture and manage excited-state risks. The thylakoid protein architecture positions these pigments so that excitation can migrate rapidly through peripheral and core antennae. LHCI and LHCII enlarge the functional absorption cross-section of PSI and PSII. Productive trapping differs from antenna transfer because it creates a charge-separated state. Fluorescence and nonradiative relaxation compete with photochemistry; regulation changes that competition and can alter excitation allocation between the photosystems. The central experimental task is to distinguish incident light, absorbed light, transfer efficiency and photochemical yield.

High-yield relationships

  • E = hc/λ; Eₘ ≈ 119626.6/λ(nm) kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Absorptance = 1 − R − T; in a nonreflecting solution T = 10⁻ᴬ.
  • η = 1/[1+(r/R₀)⁶] for a suitable Förster pair.
  • η = 1 − τDA/τD if other donor decay rates are unchanged.
  • Yield of a constant first-order route = its rate / total rate.
  • Fᵥ/Fₘ = (Fₘ−F₀)/Fₘ, under appropriate dark-adapted measuring conditions.
