Light as directional information

A young shoot growing beside a window provides a striking example of a plant responding to the spatial distribution of light. The shoot may initially be almost vertical, but its axis gradually curves toward the brighter region. This change in orientation is not simply a consequence of photosynthesis occurring faster on one side. A seedling grown in darkness, with very little functional photosynthetic machinery in its elongating tissues, can already detect unilateral blue light. The ability to turn therefore depends on a sensory mechanism that converts an external physical cue into an internal difference in growth.

This distinction between using light and sensing light is essential. A photosystem uses excitation to initiate electron transfer that supports energy conversion. A sensory photoreceptor instead changes its biochemical activity after absorbing light. The resulting signal may alter the activity of another protein, the distribution of a membrane transporter, the rate of expansion of a cell, or the position of an organelle. Light provides information about the surroundings even when the eventual movement requires chemical energy obtained by metabolism.

Phototropism is a change in growth direction determined by the direction of incident light. A shoot exhibiting positive phototropism grows toward the source; a negatively phototropic organ grows away. Because this definition concerns the relation between stimulus direction and response direction, not the identity of the pigment, a bending response cannot by itself establish that a particular photoreceptor is involved. Temperature gradients, gravity and differences in developmental competence can all modify the observed curvature. Experimental controls must separate these influences.

A tropism is also distinct from a nastic movement. For example, a leaf may open after illumination, but if the direction of opening is determined by its anatomy rather than by the direction from which the light arrives, the response is photonastic rather than phototropic. A circadian leaf movement can continue with an approximately daily period under constant environmental conditions. Chloroplast relocation is a further category: individual organelles move within a cell without requiring a differential increase in the lengths of the two sides of an entire shoot. The same family of photoreceptors can participate in different kinds of outputs without making those outputs biologically equivalent.

The chapter develops two linked problems. First, a cell must detect light using a molecule whose activity can change reversibly. Second, the plant must use the information about where and how intensely the light is arriving. The first problem is solved by the flavin-binding sensory domains of phototropins. The second involves networks of signaling proteins and transport systems that operate differently in elongating shoots and in chloroplast-containing cells.

From the coleoptile experiment to the receptor

Classical phototropism experiments used the coleoptile, the protective sheath that surrounds the emerging shoot of a grass seedling. Coleoptiles are particularly convenient because their geometry makes curvature readily visible and their tip can be treated separately from their growing base. In the nineteenth century Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin observed that covering or removing the tip could prevent normal curvature toward unilateral illumination. An uncovered tip could support bending in a region farther down the organ, even when that basal region was shielded from direct light. The logic of the result is that the perception of light and the execution of differential growth can occur in spatially separated regions.

An opaque tip cap intercepts photons before they reach the underlying tissue, but it can also exert physical pressure, alter humidity or interfere with normal development. A transparent cap of the same shape provides an important comparison. Removal of the tip is more disruptive because it simultaneously removes a source of growth signals and introduces a wound. A single tip-removal result is therefore less informative than a set of complementary manipulations that preserve ordinary elongation while changing the opportunity for light perception. The classic studies support the existence of communication between the tip and the elongation region; they did not identify a specific receptor protein.

The subsequent identification of auxin and investigations of growth-promoting substances from coleoptile tips supplied a plausible means for this communication. If growth-promoting activity becomes stronger on one side of the elongation region, cells on that side can extend faster. For a positively phototropic shoot, the shaded side generally lengthens more than the lit side. Consequently it forms the outer, longer arc of the bend. The tip and the growing region are parts of a connected system, but the hormone distribution is not identical to the original photon distribution.

Molecular genetics made it possible to look upstream of hormone action. One strategy was to screen Arabidopsis seedlings for individuals that continued to elongate but failed to bend normally under unilateral blue light. Mutants of this sort distinguish a defect in sensory signaling from a complete failure of cell growth. Identification of the NPH1 gene and its light-responsive protein connected the bending phenotype with a receptor having flavin-binding domains and a protein kinase region. NPH1 is the historical name associated with Arabidopsis phototropin 1. A related receptor, phototropin 2, revealed that the light response could be distributed across members of a receptor family rather than controlled by one molecule in every intensity range.

A genetic result alone establishes that the corresponding gene is needed for an output under the tested conditions, not necessarily that its product absorbs the initiating photon. Direct light-dependent changes in the isolated protein, the presence of a bound light-absorbing cofactor and the disruption of signaling by alterations to the sensory domains supply additional evidence for the receptor's identity. Rescue of the mutant phenotype by an appropriate wild-type gene further links the molecular lesion to the physiological response. Together these independent levels of evidence are stronger than an action spectrum or a mutant phenotype considered separately.

Measuring a blue-light sensory response

When an organism responds to illumination, the wavelengths that cause the strongest response provide a useful clue about its sensory apparatus. An absorption spectrum describes the amount of light absorbed by a chemical species as a function of wavelength. An action spectrum instead describes the effectiveness of different wavelengths in producing a chosen biological outcome. In a phototropism assay, that outcome might be a particular bend angle measured after a standardized recovery interval. The distinction matters because several events intervene between absorption of a photon and the measured angle. A wavelength that efficiently produces the initial photochemical state may not yield the largest final curvature if the experiment also activates a second signaling pathway that modifies the growth response.

The experimental definition of effectiveness must be chosen before the measurement is made. Suppose a fixed dose of light produces nearly maximum curvature over a broad band of wavelengths. A graph of angle against wavelength will then have a flat summit, even if the underlying absorption spectrum has well-defined peaks. The flat summit might indicate saturation of a downstream response rather than broad absorption by the receptor. Conversely, choosing a dose that is too weak may produce angles too small to distinguish from spontaneous curvature. One approach is to measure the light dose needed to produce a modest, fixed response at each wavelength. The reciprocal of that dose provides a measure of relative effectiveness. This approach requires a response that changes monotonically over the selected dose range.

A colored filter placed in front of a lamp does not automatically create a spectrally controlled stimulus. Filters can transmit small quantities of unintended wavelengths, and different filters may differ greatly in total transmission. A student who compares a dim blue beam with a bright red beam cannot determine whether the difference in curvature reflects wavelength or photon flux. An appropriate instrument or a calibrated set of light sources provides the relevant photon flux at the level of the seedling. To test direction separately from wavelength, the beam should be delivered from the same angle and through a comparable illumination geometry for every wavelength.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which equally oriented seedlings receive monochromatic light at three wavelengths. A small fixed bend of ten degrees requires 8 micromoles of photons per square meter at 450 nm, 40 micromoles per square meter at 520 nm, and more than 160 micromoles per square meter at 660 nm. Dividing the smallest required dose by each measured dose yields relative sensitivities of 1, 0.2 and less than 0.05, respectively. The inequality is important: the third treatment did not establish the actual dose needed for ten degrees, so the student cannot invent an exact sensitivity at 660 nm. The result is compatible with a blue-light receptor, but a matching spectral preference alone would not distinguish phototropin from all other flavin-containing receptors.

An action spectrum can be interpreted more confidently when paired with a receptor mutant. Suppose the normal seedlings respond most efficiently in blue light and the phot1 phot2 mutant shows a greatly diminished response across this range while maintaining comparable growth potential. Complementation with a functional phototropin restores a response with the expected spectral preference. The evidence now connects the receptor family with the measured output. Even so, the experiment does not show that every absorbed blue photon is captured by phototropin; chlorophyll, flavoproteins and other cellular components also absorb wavelengths in this region.

The relation between wavelength and photon energy can be calculated without introducing a separate course in photophysics. With Planck's constant approximately 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule seconds, the speed of light approximately 3.00 × 10⁸ meters per second, and a wavelength of 450 × 10⁻⁹ meters, one photon has an energy of about 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. Multiplying by Avogadro's constant gives roughly 266 kilojoules per mole of photons. At 660 nm the corresponding value is about 181 kilojoules per mole. Thus an energy-matched red beam contains about 1.47 times as many photons as the blue beam. This is a comparison of physical input, not a statement about how many receptor molecules are activated. The quantum yield of a photochemical step is an additional factor.

An experiment that aims to relate response to wavelength should usually distinguish the light used to stimulate the tissue from the light used to observe it. Illuminating dark-grown seedlings repeatedly with a bright inspection lamp can activate sensory pathways before the intended pulse. A supposedly dark control can therefore become a pretreated control without the experimenter noticing. Imaging with appropriate nonstimulating conditions, standardizing handling time and including an unilluminated group help address this problem. The same concern applies to microscopic observations of moving chloroplasts.

Spectral response is also inseparable from the initial biological condition. A dark-grown seedling and a green seedling differ in tissue optics, elongation capacity and signaling state. A green leaf contains abundant photosynthetic pigments that alter the spectrum reaching photoreceptors deeper in the tissue. Accordingly, a difference between two action spectra is not automatically evidence for a change in the chromophore molecule. The position and state of the receptor, the penetration of light and the choice of output all matter. Exam questions often become straightforward once these different explanatory levels are separated.

Phototropin architecture and chromophore chemistry

Arabidopsis thaliana possesses two principal phototropins, phot1 and phot2. Both are blue-light-responsive serine/threonine protein kinases. Their architecture is modular: the amino-terminal sensory region contains two LOV domains, named LOV1 and LOV2, and the carboxyl-terminal portion contains the catalytic kinase domain. The term LOV derives from light, oxygen or voltage, the name of a family of sensory modules; in plant phototropins these domains are adapted for light sensing. A domain is a structurally organized part of a protein that can contribute a distinguishable function within the full-length molecule.

Each LOV domain binds a flavin mononucleotide molecule, abbreviated FMN. FMN is derived from riboflavin and contains the conjugated isoalloxazine ring system that can absorb blue and near-ultraviolet light. The protein portion provides the binding pocket and the environment that governs the excited chromophore's subsequent reactions. The bound FMN is a chromophore rather than an ordinary kinase substrate: its role is to receive photons and initiate a structural transition. Phototropins do not use FMN as a permanent source of phosphate for the reactions they catalyze.

A protein that contains two chromophores is not necessarily a detector that requires two photons at exactly the same instant. LOV1 and LOV2 are individually capable of photochemistry, and their contributions to the function of the intact protein differ. In the common activation model, LOV2 is the major regulatory switch coupling illumination to kinase activation. LOV1 can modulate sensitivity, receptor association or signaling properties, depending on the receptor and response examined. Mutations that inactivate one domain therefore need not produce the same physiological effect as complete removal of the whole phototropin.

The receptor is often associated with the cytoplasmic surface of the plasma membrane. This location is compatible with interactions with signaling proteins and with responses involving membrane transporter localization. It is not equivalent to the architecture of a conventional receptor that spans the membrane and binds an extracellular soluble ligand. Light can reach the internal chromophore directly, and the protein changes activity as a result. Some receptor populations change their cellular localization following illumination, so membrane association should not be described as an irreversible attachment.

The catalytic region uses ATP and transfers its terminal phosphate to suitable hydroxyl-containing amino-acid residues. Its activity is regulated by the sensory region; the presence of a kinase domain does not imply that it is fully active in darkness. A useful structural experiment is to compare an intact wild-type protein with one carrying a light-insensitive LOV2 and another carrying a catalytically defective kinase domain. In principle, the first mutant disrupts the coupling of photon perception to catalysis, while the second can still undergo chromophore photochemistry but fails at phosphate transfer. If both produce a similar growth phenotype, molecular measurements are required to identify the distinct failures.

LOV photochemistry and activation of kinase signaling

In the dark-adapted state, FMN is held noncovalently in the LOV binding pocket. Absorption of an appropriate photon promotes the flavin into an electronically excited state. A transient triplet state can participate in the chemical reaction that forms a bond between a conserved cysteine side chain and the C4a position of the flavin ring. The resulting cysteinyl-flavin adduct represents a light-generated state of the sensory domain. It is not an irreversible lesion in the protein; thermal reactions can return the chromophore and cysteine to their dark-adapted configuration.

The photochemical event changes interactions within the domain. In the LOV2 module the structural change can weaken interactions with the adjacent J-alpha helix. This helix is an important component of the coupling between the sensory region and the kinase. Light-induced changes in the helix and related protein interfaces relieve inhibition of the catalytic domain. The general causal sequence is thus photon absorption, chromophore reaction, structural rearrangement and kinase activation. These are distinct events and should be measured by different experimental readouts when testing the model.

Autophosphorylation occurs when a kinase transfers phosphate to residues within the same protein or to a receptor molecule of the same type. Increased phototropin phosphorylation after blue light is a useful biochemical readout of activation. It must not be confused with the formation of the FMN–cysteine bond, because the latter is a photochemical reaction without ATP-dependent phosphate transfer. A kinase inhibitor can in principle suppress phosphorylation while leaving the upstream optical change intact. Conversely a light-insensitive chromophore pocket can abolish the usual light-induced rise in catalytic activity without destroying the kinase's amino-acid sequence.

Dark recovery helps explain why a phototropin reports recent light exposure rather than accumulating a permanent record of all past illumination. Consider a simplified population of receptor molecules with a light-dependent activation rate kL and a dark recovery rate kd, both measured in inverse seconds. Let a be the fraction in the activated sensory state. The rate of change is kL(1 − a) − kd a. Under constant light, the population reaches a steady state when the forward and reverse rates balance, giving a = kL/(kL + kd). This is an illustrative two-state model, not a fitted physiological phototropin response curve. The model predicts that increasing irradiance increases the active fraction until the available receptors approach saturation.

If illumination ends, kL becomes zero in this simplified model. The remaining activated fraction then decreases exponentially with a half-life of ln(2)/kd. For an illustrative recovery rate of 0.02 per second, the half-life is approximately 34.7 seconds. A hypothetical receptor with a recovery rate of 0.01 per second has twice that half-life. If both receptors are exposed to an identical short pulse that produces the same initial active fraction, the slower recovering receptor remains activated longer. However, prolonged biochemical activation does not guarantee proportionally greater bending because downstream pathways can adapt, saturate or be limited by available growth.

Photon fluence rate is the number of photons delivered per area per time, often expressed in micromoles of photons per square meter per second in plant physiology. Fluence is the accumulated number per area. A constant pulse of 2 micromoles per square meter per second for 30 seconds delivers 60 micromoles per square meter. A pulse of 6 for 10 seconds delivers the same fluence. Equal fluence does not necessarily yield equal receptor activation because recovery and downstream signaling occur during illumination. This is why an experiment should report both the intensity and the duration of a light treatment rather than only a total dose.

A related distinction concerns equal radiant energy and equal photon number. The energy of one photon is hc divided by wavelength, where h is Planck's constant and c is the speed of light in vacuum. Blue photons have greater individual energy than red photons. Consequently equal radiant power at two wavelengths does not imply equal photon flux. Even at equal photon flux, a receptor can respond much more strongly to one wavelength because of its absorption spectrum and the efficiency of productive photochemistry. Greater photon energy by itself is not sufficient to predict phototropin activation.

Testing the sensor-to-kinase coupling

It is useful to distinguish a protein's amino-acid sequence, its three-dimensional conformation and its biochemical activity. A mutation can leave the overall fold largely intact but change the contact between two domains. A protein that binds FMN normally might still fail to activate its kinase if a regulatory interface cannot rearrange. Conversely, a receptor might lose normal dark-state inhibition while remaining catalytically capable. These possibilities are more informative than a simple classification into light-responsive and light-insensitive proteins because they reveal distinct functions of the modular receptor.

One type of molecular investigation compares the optical behavior of an isolated LOV domain with that of free FMN. The spectrum of the bound chromophore reflects the environment created by the surrounding amino acids. After blue-light excitation, time-dependent spectral changes reveal formation and decay of new photochemical states. Observing a reversible optical transition establishes that the domain is capable of sensing light at the molecular level. However, an isolated LOV domain contains no catalytic kinase region. The experiment cannot, on its own, demonstrate that phosphate transfer occurs in the full-length receptor. It addresses the first part of the proposed causal chain.

To establish coupling, one may compare a full-length receptor in dark-adapted and illuminated states using a phosphorylation assay. ATP supplies the transferable phosphate; the receptor or another suitable protein can act as substrate. If the reaction increases after illumination, the result provides evidence that the catalytic state is regulated by light. A control lacking active kinase distinguishes enzymatic incorporation of phosphate from unrelated changes in the sample. A control lacking the normal LOV photochemical response tests whether kinase activation depends on the sensory region. It is important to compare equal amounts of protein, because doubling receptor abundance can increase total phosphate incorporation without increasing activity per molecule.

The LOV2–J-alpha interface provides an especially instructive perturbation. Harper, Christie and Gardner (2004) described substitutions disrupting the contact between the sensory domain and its associated helix; when introduced into intact phototropin, selected substitutions promoted kinase activity even without blue-light exposure. This supports a model in which the dark-state interface restrains catalysis. In this type of reasoning, an amino-acid change that bypasses normal stimulus dependence is valuable because it identifies an inhibitory interaction rather than merely demonstrating that some region is required. A light-induced structural rearrangement may release a restraint instead of creating an enzyme active site from nothing.

Consider an illustrative set of normalized biochemical activities for four constructs, using the illuminated wild-type protein as one arbitrary activity unit. Wild type gives 0.08 in darkness and 1.00 after illumination; a chromophore-reaction-deficient construct gives 0.06 and 0.07; a catalytically inactive construct gives 0.01 and 0.01; a regulatory-interface mutant gives 0.72 and 0.80. These values are invented for reasoning practice and are not reported measurements from the Harper study. The last construct provides evidence for altered basal regulation. Its smaller fold induction in light is not evidence of low catalytic capacity: much of the activation is present before illumination. A student who computes only light-to-dark ratios could misinterpret this pattern.

Additional measurements would clarify the meaning of each row. If the chromophore-reaction-deficient receptor is properly folded, abundant and able to bind ATP, failure of light-induced activity is consistent with disruption of the upstream sensory step. If it fails to bind FMN at all, the explanation differs from a receptor that binds FMN but fails to form the light-generated covalent adduct. Likewise, the catalytic mutant may have normal optical transitions. Optical and enzymatic measurements are therefore complementary. The appropriate next experiment depends on the particular molecular defect rather than on the fact that all of these mutants might show abnormal growth.

Autophosphorylation is not a single uniform modification. Proteins often carry several phosphorylatable residues, and the functions of those residues may differ. A mutation that prevents one regulatory phosphorylation can impair signaling without eliminating every light-associated change in receptor phosphorylation. A bulk band shift on a gel summarizes changes in the mobility of a protein population and does not necessarily identify which individual residues were modified. The combination of mutational analysis and functional readouts distinguishes regulatory sites from phosphorylation events that are incidental to a particular physiological output.

A normal receptor kinase need not remain maximally active forever after illumination. Sensory recovery, protein phosphatases and interactions with other signaling components can all contribute to resetting the system. When a tissue moves from shade into sun, its prior illumination may affect its response to a new stimulus. Molecular recovery and physiological adaptation must therefore be distinguished. The first refers to return of a molecular state toward its initial condition; the second refers to the history-dependent change in the responsiveness of a biological system. A receptor may recover its dark absorbance while the tissue retains a downstream adaptation.

An informative experiment involving two light pulses separates these possibilities. Deliver an initial pulse and then deliver an identical second pulse after different dark intervals. Measure a rapid receptor-associated signal immediately after the second pulse and a slower physiological response afterward. If the biochemical signal recovers more quickly than the physiological response, some persistence exists beyond the receptor's sensory photocycle. If both recover on similar timescales, receptor recovery is a plausible contributing limitation, but coincidence of timescales does not establish causation. The direction of the second light pulse can also be reversed to test whether previous stimulation has altered spatial responsiveness.

Such experiments illustrate why studying a photoreceptor as a light-activated kinase is more informative than simply memorizing its domain names. Its modular architecture permits separate manipulation of photon sensing, regulatory coupling, catalysis and interactions with downstream partners. These experimentally separable stages provide a framework for interpreting many apparently conflicting mutant phenotypes.

Converting asymmetric activation into growth

A single phototropin molecule cannot measure a macroscopic angle between a shoot and a lamp. Direction is represented in the tissue by the unequal activation of receptor populations in different positions. In a unilateral illumination experiment, cells near the illuminated side can encounter a higher local photon flux than cells at corresponding positions on the opposite side. Absorption and scattering by tissue create a spatial pattern that is translated into a spatial pattern of biochemical activity. Uniform illumination may produce strong receptor activation everywhere while providing little left–right difference to orient a shoot.

NON-PHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3, or NPH3, is an important signaling component that interacts with phototropin pathways. ROOT PHOTOTROPISM 2, RPT2, belongs to the related NPH3/RPT2-like family and contributes to appropriate phototropic signaling over a range of light conditions. These proteins help connect the activation of the photoreceptor with downstream responses. Their regulatory properties include changes in phosphorylation and association or localization, but the pathway should not be reduced to a universal linear series in which every downstream protein is directly phosphorylated by the previous one.

The name NPH3 is derived from the mutant phenotype, not from a particular enzymatic reaction. A mutant lacking functional NPH3 can retain phototropin protein and some upstream biochemical activation but show defective hypocotyl curvature. This observation demonstrates why measuring phosphorylated receptor is not enough to claim the whole signaling pathway has worked. RPT2 can alter response sensitivity and contributes to the behavior of phototropic pathways at different irradiances. Its function is particularly useful when considering why the magnitude and duration of the output need not be determined by receptor occupancy alone.

The PIN family of auxin efflux carriers provides a mechanism for establishing directional auxin transport. The polarity of a transporter refers to its preferential distribution on one region of the cell membrane. A cell can therefore export auxin more effectively toward one neighbor than toward another even if its internal concentration is not strongly asymmetric. Changes in PIN membrane trafficking can redirect the net movement of the hormone across a tissue. PIN3-mediated transport is implicated in the lateral auxin redistribution of Arabidopsis hypocotyl phototropism, although other transporters and regulatory processes also participate under particular conditions.

The hormone redistribution model predicts an increase in auxin response on the shaded side of a positively phototropic shoot. Auxin can promote wall loosening and cell extension in suitable shoot tissues; faster length increase on the shaded side generates curvature toward the lit side. A fluorescent transcriptional auxin-response reporter may provide evidence for unequal signaling, but its intensity is not a direct chemical measurement of free IAA concentration. The response depends on hormone concentration, sensitivity, transcription, fluorophore maturation and degradation. It is consequently important to distinguish a reporter gradient from a calibrated hormone concentration gradient.

An inhibitor of auxin transport may abolish curvature even when a receptor still absorbs blue light and undergoes phosphorylation. This result places the transport-dependent process somewhere in the causal chain leading to growth, provided the inhibitor has not generally killed the tissue or suppressed every form of elongation. A control showing that the treated tissue can still extend in response to another suitable stimulus strengthens the interpretation. The strongest demonstration uses complementary approaches: genetic alterations, spatial reporters, biochemical measurements and phenotypic rescue.

The mechanics of curvature can be understood using a simple geometric approximation. Imagine an elongating strip of a shoot with two sides separated by a distance d. If the curved segment has an angle θ in radians and each side follows a circular arc, the difference between the outer and inner arc lengths is dθ. For a separation of 0.8 mm and a measured length difference of 0.4 mm, the predicted bend is 0.5 radian, or approximately 28.6 degrees. The calculation assumes a uniform circular bend and approximately constant separation of the two sides. Real tissues can change diameter, bend nonuniformly or have elastic constraints, so this expression is a teaching model rather than a universal measurement protocol.

Roots need not show the same growth response to auxin as shoots. Depending on concentration, tissue and developmental context, an increase in auxin can inhibit root elongation. The distribution of the signal and the growth response to that distribution jointly determine the direction of bending. Accordingly, a student should not transfer the simple positive-shoot rule to every root or infer that all organs turn toward blue light. Gravity and other directional cues further complicate root responses.

From membrane polarity to a lateral signal

To understand the directional transport of auxin, imagine a row of adjacent cells extending from the illuminated side of a hypocotyl toward the shaded side. Each cell has several membrane faces through which a small molecule may enter or leave. If exit is equally probable through every face, a population of molecules may spread but will not exhibit persistent net transport in one direction in a symmetrical environment. If exit carriers become enriched on one face, the probabilities become unequal. The tissue can sustain a net flux even without requiring a new localized source of the molecule. The location of a transporter is thus as important as its abundance.

The term polar auxin transport is sometimes misunderstood as a description of the molecular polarity of auxin itself. Here polarity primarily refers to the directionality generated by transport machinery organized across cells. A transporter embedded in the outward-facing side of a cell exports hormone toward the neighboring space on that side. If the same protein is relocated to an inward-facing membrane, the direction of export changes. A tissue-level pattern can emerge from coordinated changes in multiple cell layers. Such changes provide a means by which a spatial difference in phototropin activation could be converted into a different spatial pattern of growth.

Ding and colleagues' 2011 analysis of Arabidopsis hypocotyl phototropism connected light-dependent PIN3 localization with the redistribution of auxin signaling across the organ. The important experimental insight is not that every auxin carrier always moves toward the shaded side. Instead, the location of specific carriers within particular cell types changes in a manner compatible with redirected hormone flow. In a cross section, an inward-facing surface of a cell on the illuminated side points toward the center of the organ. The orientation of the corresponding surface on the other side is different. Interpreting microscopy therefore requires a map of the tissue's geometry, not just an observation that a fluorescent membrane signal becomes asymmetric.

Consider two otherwise identical hypocotyls. In one, PIN3 becomes polarized after unilateral blue light; in the other, PIN3 protein is present at the same total abundance but cannot undergo normal localization changes. It is possible for both to show an indistinguishable protein band on a whole-organ immunoblot, yet have different phototropic behavior. This example illustrates the difference between protein amount and protein distribution. It also explains why a bulk expression measurement cannot replace cell-specific localization analysis in a problem about directional growth.

A fluorescent fusion protein is often useful for determining transporter location. However, its presence must be interpreted carefully. The fluorescent tag could alter the transporter's ability to move through membrane trafficking pathways or to transport its substrate. If the fusion protein restores normal function in an otherwise deficient plant, it is more likely to provide a physiologically meaningful localization pattern. An apparent movement of a fluorescent signal could also result from changes in cell geometry or imaging conditions. Comparison of the signal at defined membrane faces, with appropriate normalization and controls, is more convincing than an unquantified image.

Auxin response reporters address a complementary question. In a transcription-based reporter, a regulatory DNA sequence drives the production of a fluorescent or enzymatic signal in cells responding to auxin. Reporter activity reflects an integrated consequence of signaling and transcription. It may become visible only after protein synthesis or maturation. A reporter designed around hormone-dependent degradation of a sensor has a different time dependence and may show an inverse relation between signal intensity and hormone exposure. A student should read the definition of the reporter before interpreting whether a brighter region has higher or lower auxin signaling.

A useful model of two interacting tissue compartments treats the illuminated side and shaded side as separate pools of free hormone. Let the amounts be A and B, measured in arbitrary but consistent units. A net lateral flux J from the illuminated pool to the shaded pool removes J units per time from A and adds J units per time to B. If there is no synthesis, degradation or longitudinal transport during the selected interval, the sum A + B is conserved. Starting from A = B = 20 units, a redistribution of 5 units yields A = 15 and B = 25. The total remains 40 while the shaded-to-lit amount ratio rises to 25/15, or about 1.67. Unequal distribution does not require an increase in total auxin.

Now consider a different experiment in which both sides start at 20 units, but the shaded side synthesizes 5 additional units while the lit side remains unchanged. The resulting amounts are 20 and 25, giving a total of 45 and a ratio of 1.25. Both models create a difference of some kind, but they have different consequences for the total pool and the source of the asymmetry. This simplified example clarifies why evidence for a lateral gradient alone cannot determine whether redistribution, synthesis, degradation or longitudinal transport was responsible. Actual tissues have simultaneous fluxes and metabolism, so conservation of the two measured pools is an assumption of the model, not a general biological law.

The rate of cell elongation depends on the cell's mechanical properties as well as its hormone response. Auxin-responsive growth involves regulation of cell-wall extensibility and the capacity for water uptake under suitable conditions. A cell that has completed elongation cannot necessarily respond to a new auxin distribution by becoming substantially longer. This explains why the region that detects the light and the region that produces visible bending need not be identical. If an experiment demonstrates a reporter asymmetry in a mature region without curvature, a defect in mechanical competence is one possible explanation; it does not establish that photoreceptor activation failed.

Growth in the outer side of the curved segment is an accumulated outcome. If the rates on the lit and shaded sides are initially different for ten minutes and then become equal, an existing difference in length can remain. The angle does not instantly return to zero when the signaling difference disappears. A second stimulus arriving from the opposite direction could create a new growth asymmetry that gradually modifies the previous bend. Thus time-integrated curvature is not an instantaneous assay of receptor activation. Direct comparison of two time courses, one measuring a signaling marker and another measuring curvature, is more useful than expecting the curves to match exactly.

The existence of a hormone transport mechanism does not remove the need for upstream spatial perception. Applying the same auxin concentration to both sides of an organ may restore general growth but not directionality. Applying growth-promoting activity to one side may generate curvature even in a receptor-deficient plant, provided downstream growth machinery is intact. This is a form of pathway bypass, not proof that the mutant's endogenous photoreceptor has been repaired. Bypass experiments are especially valuable for placing a perturbation upstream or downstream of a physiological effector.

Phot1 and phot2 across different light regimes

Phot1 contributes strongly to phototropism under weak directional blue light, whereas phot2 provides overlapping function especially as light intensity rises. This relationship is demonstrated by comparing single and double loss-of-function mutants with wild type under the same conditions. At an appropriate low fluence rate, the loss of phot1 causes a larger bending defect than the loss of phot2. Under stronger light, the remaining phot2 in a phot1 mutant can support a response. Removing both receptors eliminates much of the canonical phototropin-dependent response. Exact response magnitudes and light thresholds depend on the plant material and assay.

Redundancy does not mean the receptors have identical biochemical properties or are expressed at identical levels in every tissue. Partial redundancy means that one gene product can support a function that can also be performed by another gene product under at least some conditions. A mutant phenotype may be absent in an assay that allows compensation yet pronounced in a different assay. For example, testing only high irradiance can obscure a phot1-dependent weak-light phenotype. Testing only low irradiance can obscure a contribution from phot2.

A useful experimental design combines four genotypes with at least three light conditions: darkness, weak unilateral blue light and stronger unilateral blue light. Dark controls establish the baseline orientation of each genotype. Seedlings should be age-matched and initially aligned. The lamp geometry and gravity vector must be held constant or randomized in a controlled way. Measuring the time course of curvature as well as its final angle distinguishes a delay in signaling from a persistent inability to bend. A second assay of overall elongation helps ensure that a smaller curvature is not merely a consequence of a stunted hypocotyl.

The distinction between blue-light sensing and other photoreceptor pathways also matters. Cryptochromes bind flavin but are not LOV-domain serine/threonine kinases of the phototropin type. Their major roles include transcriptional regulation associated with de-etiolation and developmental timing. Phytochromes instead contain a bilin chromophore and are particularly important for red and far-red responses. Other photoreceptors can modulate phototropic sensitivity, so an absence of a strong response to a red-only beam does not imply that red light has no influence over subsequent blue-light behavior.

Light history, directional sensitivity and response dynamics

In natural conditions the light environment is rarely a constant beam delivered to a motionless seedling. Leaves above a young plant may shift with the wind, exposing it to short intervals of brighter light. The sun's position changes over hours, and the illuminated surface of an elongating shoot changes as the shoot bends. A useful sensory system must therefore respond to both spatial contrast and recent light history. If every momentary fluctuation produced an irreversible large change in growth, the plant might expend resources turning in response to brief stimuli that do not predict future light availability.

Classic phototropism studies distinguish pulse-induced curvature from responses to prolonged illumination. A pulse can initiate a change that becomes visible after the light has been removed. Continuous stimulation, in contrast, provides new spatial information while growth proceeds. In etiolated seedlings, different dose regimes can elicit different forms of positive phototropism. A response curve can be nonmonotonic across these regimes, because the processes governing sensitivity and adaptation are not captured by a single saturating receptor occupancy curve. Terms such as first positive and second positive phototropism refer to experimentally distinguished response regimes, not simply to phot1 and phot2 respectively.

The difference between a transient and a sustained response can be understood by comparing two hypothetical readouts. Suppose receptor activation rises to a plateau within a minute of illumination, while curvature increases only after a ten-minute delay. This delay is compatible with downstream signaling, auxin transport and the accumulation of differential extension. If the curvature rate then declines despite a stable stimulus, adaptation or a change in the spatial gradient is possible. As the shoot turns, the initially shaded side may receive more light, reducing the original difference in activation. The changing shape of the organ can act as part of a feedback loop between light input and growth output.

A simple geometrical feedback model helps clarify this principle. Consider a straight shoot initially at an angle of sixty degrees relative to the direction it would adopt if it fully aligned with the light. Let the effective left–right contrast be proportional, for illustration, to the sine of this angular deviation. The contrast is then about 0.87 of its maximum at sixty degrees, 0.50 at thirty degrees and 0.17 at ten degrees. As orientation improves, the signal driving further turning can become smaller. This is a conceptual model, not an empirical formula for every tissue. Diffuse light, leaf optics and the gravity vector can make the real relation quite different.

Phot1 and phot2 overlap in function across light regimes, but their sensitivity cannot be inferred simply by counting photons absorbed by one LOV domain. Differences in receptor expression, photocycle behavior, protein interactions and downstream regulation can influence which receptor dominates a given output. A low-intensity phot1-dependent response is not necessarily replaced completely by phot2 at the next higher intensity. Instead, the contribution of each receptor changes with the assay. One should use the relevant comparison: wild type versus a single mutant versus the double mutant at the same fluence rate and exposure duration.

NPH3 and RPT2 are important in understanding how phototropin activation is converted into a physiologically appropriate response. A direct phosphorylation event and a change in the overall phosphorylation pattern of a signaling protein can coexist. For example, phot1-dependent phosphorylation at a conserved NPH3 region has been linked to phototropic function, whereas other NPH3 phosphorylation changes reflect additional regulatory events. A diagram that labels NPH3 solely as phosphorylated in light or solely as dephosphorylated in light is therefore too coarse to capture the underlying biology. The exam-relevant principle is that modification state and molecular interactions regulate the transmission of information from the light-activated kinase.

RPT2 illustrates why signal transducers can influence light sensitivity. A transducer might alter the duration of signaling, the receptor's accessibility to its partners or the efficiency with which a downstream step follows activation. These changes can be particularly important under prolonged or intense illumination. A receptor that remains present and capable of absorbing photons is not automatically able to drive a normal output across all environmental conditions. A mutant affecting adaptation could show reasonable early curvature but abnormal continued growth, or a response that depends unusually strongly on prior illumination.

To distinguish response sensitivity from maximum response capacity, it is helpful to construct a dose–response curve. A leftward shift means a particular response is achieved at a lower dose, provided the response is compared over the same output scale. A reduced maximum means the system cannot attain the former response under the conditions tested, even when the stimulus is increased. A delayed response measured at a fixed early time can mimic either pattern. Therefore a suitable dataset includes enough time points to establish whether the compared systems have reached comparable physiological stages.

For an illustrative example, imagine that wild-type seedlings exposed to weak, medium and strong blue light reach bend angles of 18, 35 and 37 degrees after two hours, while a mutant reaches 4, 22 and 36 degrees. The mutant eventually approaches the wild-type response at the highest intensity, suggesting that its sensitivity differs under the tested conditions but substantial growth capacity is retained. A second mutant giving 2, 3 and 4 degrees may have a more severe sensory or downstream defect. The numbers do not establish the genotype; a transport defect, altered adaptation or impaired growth can also change the curve. They demonstrate how to extract a limited conclusion from response patterns before identifying their molecular cause.

The light-response system also interacts with gravitational orientation. Positive shoot phototropism may turn the axis away from its initial upward direction, while negative shoot gravitropism favors upward growth. A final oblique orientation can result from the combined effects of different directional inputs, rather than incomplete recognition of the light source. In an experiment, keeping the angle between the light vector and gravity constant enables more reliable comparisons. A change in that angle between treatment groups introduces a new biological variable even when wavelength and photon flux have been standardized.

Intracellular chloroplast relocation

Within a mature leaf, chloroplasts are positioned near the walls of mesophyll cells rather than floating randomly throughout the entire cell volume. Their location affects how much light they intercept and how strongly they shade one another. Under weak directional light, increasing the projected chloroplast area perpendicular to the beam can increase interception. Under intense light, decreasing this projected area can reduce exposure to excess excitation. A plant can therefore adjust its optical geometry without growing a new leaf.

In the accumulation response, chloroplasts move toward illuminated cell surfaces or illuminated regions. Under high-intensity blue light, chloroplasts undergo avoidance and relocate away from strongly irradiated regions, often occupying anticlinal walls that are more nearly parallel to the incident light in the standard leaf-illumination arrangement. Periclinal walls run approximately parallel to the leaf surface; anticlinal walls run across the leaf thickness. These anatomical terms describe orientation relative to the organ surface, not the chemical composition of the walls.

Both phot1 and phot2 can contribute to the Arabidopsis accumulation response. Phot2 has the principal role in sustaining the classic high-light avoidance response. Consequently a phot2 mutant can retain low-light accumulation through phot1 but fail to execute normal sustained avoidance under conditions in which wild-type chloroplasts retreat. A phot1 phot2 double mutant lacks normal phototropin-controlled movement. Other species and experimental conditions can show different wavelength dependencies or transient movements; the Arabidopsis genetic model should not be presented as an exhaustive description of all photosynthetic eukaryotes.

CHUP1, CHLOROPLAST UNUSUAL POSITIONING 1, is a protein associated with the chloroplast outer envelope and the positioning machinery that connects chloroplasts with the cell periphery. Chloroplast-associated actin filaments reorganize during movement. The spatial organization of these actin structures is important for directed relocation. This process is not equivalent to the long-distance transport of auxin in a growing hypocotyl. A defective positioning protein can impair chloroplast relocation even if the upstream phototropin perceives light normally.

The physiological significance can be studied by exposing wild type and an avoidance-defective mutant to strong light and assessing chlorophyll fluorescence or photosynthetic performance before and after exposure. A greater decline in maximum photosystem II efficiency in the mutant is consistent with the idea that avoidance helps reduce damage. However, it is necessary to control for baseline photosynthetic differences, differences in leaf pigment content and other protective pathways. A mutant's increased susceptibility does not prove that chloroplast relocation is the only mechanism of protection.

In a leaf transmittance assay, accumulation typically causes more incoming measuring light to be intercepted by chloroplasts, decreasing transmittance. Avoidance reduces projected interception and commonly increases transmittance. Optical measurements offer a useful population-level readout, while time-lapse microscopy provides direct spatial evidence about chloroplast position. Combining both measurements makes it easier to distinguish chloroplast movement from a change in pigment abundance or tissue optics.

Chloroplast avoidance and nonphotochemical quenching address related physiological problems by different means. Avoidance adjusts where the organelles are positioned and thus how much incident light they intercept. Nonphotochemical quenching adjusts the dissipation of absorbed excitation within the photosynthetic apparatus. The two processes can operate together. Neither process completely eliminates the danger of photoinhibition, and avoiding excessive exposure entails a tradeoff if light subsequently becomes limiting.

Phototropins also participate in leaf flattening, leaf positioning and the blue-light-dependent opening of stomata. These responses improve the conditions for photosynthesis through different tissues and mechanisms. In guard cells the signal leads to regulation of plasma membrane proton pumps and ion transport; changes in turgor alter pore aperture. This output illustrates the versatility of the same receptor family. Its detailed osmotic and electrical mechanism belongs to the following chapter on stomatal movement.

How a chloroplast changes its position

A mesophyll cell is a mechanically constrained environment. A large central vacuole occupies much of its volume, leaving the chloroplasts in a layer of cytoplasm near the cell periphery. An organelle moving from one wall region to another must therefore navigate the geometry of the cell while maintaining its integrity. This is a different mechanical problem from the expansion of a hypocotyl. The organelle remains inside the same cell, and its envelope, thylakoids and photosynthetic enzymes are not ordinarily dismantled and rebuilt at the new position. Relocation depends on controlled movement and anchoring within the cortical cytoplasm.

The cytoskeleton provides a dynamic organizational system rather than a rigid scaffold. Actin filaments are polymers of actin subunits that can assemble, disassemble and interact with proteins connecting cellular structures. In the context of chloroplast photorelocation, particularly relevant structures are short chloroplast-associated actin filaments at the interface between the organelle and the plasma membrane. The organization of these filaments differs from the broad networks of cytoplasmic actin visible throughout a living cell. A perturbation that affects the chloroplast-associated structures need not abolish every actin-dependent process in that cell.

CHUP1 provides an example of a positioning factor with a location appropriate for its biological role. It associates with the outer chloroplast envelope and participates in the actin-dependent machinery that establishes normal chloroplast positioning. This envelope is the external boundary of the organelle, not the thylakoid membrane where photosynthetic electron transfer occurs. The distinction helps explain why a chloroplast positioning mutant can retain at least some photosynthetic capacity while exhibiting abnormal location and light-driven movement. A primary defect in photosystem assembly would be a different molecular problem.

Kadota and colleagues (2009) combined observations of actin organization and chloroplast movement in Arabidopsis. They found that the short chloroplast-associated filaments were absent in the relevant CHUP1-deficient material even though ordinary cytoplasmic actin structures could still be observed. In wild-type cells, redistribution of the short filaments accompanied light-directed movement, with enrichment on the advancing side. The observations linked the specialized actin organization to chloroplast displacement. They did not require the conclusion that a chloroplast is simply pulled along a conventional long actin track by a specified motor. For examination purposes, the robust conclusion concerns dynamic local actin organization and its dependence on the positioning machinery.

In reasoning about this system, the advancing side of a chloroplast is defined by its direction of displacement, not automatically by the direction from which the stimulus originates. During an accumulation response, a chloroplast may advance toward an illuminated area. During avoidance, the same type of organelle may advance away from that area. An image showing greater actin signal at the advancing edge does not mean that actin invariably accumulates on the side of a chloroplast nearest the light source. The relation between light position and leading edge changes when the response changes sign.

Consider a mesophyll cell with a patch of strong blue light placed on its left half. A chloroplast initially inside that patch begins moving to the right. If an actin reporter becomes enriched at the right-hand edge of the organelle, that pattern is consistent with avoidance and the association of chloroplast actin with the advancing edge. In a second trial, the same patch is weakly illuminated and a chloroplast initially outside it moves left toward the patch. Its advancing edge is now the left-hand edge. A comparison of these trials is more useful than memorizing a fixed relation such as light on the left means actin on the right. The experiment asks how the receptor selects an appropriate direction under different intensity conditions.

A chloroplast positioning mutant may display an abnormal distribution even before stimulation. This starting configuration can affect the apparent size of a subsequent relocation response. Suppose wild-type chloroplasts start spread across a periclinal wall, while a mutant has many organelles already clustered along a side wall. After strong illumination, the wild type shows a large shift in the fraction of organelles near the side walls. The mutant shows a small shift. The small change could reflect impaired movement, but it might also reflect a reduced distance from the endpoint at the beginning of the experiment. Recording both the initial distribution and the subsequent trajectory is essential.

Microscopic analysis can quantify movement in ways that bulk transmittance cannot. The position of the center of each chloroplast can be recorded relative to a reference in the cell. A displacement vector includes direction and magnitude. Summing the magnitudes of successive displacements gives path length, whereas the distance from the initial to final position gives net displacement. A chloroplast that moves around a cell corner can travel a substantial distance while exhibiting a smaller net displacement. The distinction is useful when comparing responses that involve different starting locations or trajectories.

For a worked example, suppose the center of an organelle is initially at coordinates (4, 6) micrometers relative to a fixed cell corner. After 30 seconds it is at (7, 10) micrometers. Its net displacement is the vector (3, 4) micrometers, with magnitude five micrometers by the Pythagorean relation. If the motion were straight, the average speed would be five divided by thirty, or approximately 0.167 micrometers per second. If intermediate images show that the chloroplast actually traveled eight micrometers along a curved route, the mean path speed is about 0.267 micrometers per second. Neither value should be reported as a published chloroplast velocity; these are hypothetical coordinates used to teach interpretation.

In another illustrative dataset, three chloroplasts move by 4, 5 and 6 micrometers toward a newly illuminated weak-light patch, while three others move by 4, 5 and 6 micrometers away from it. If the experimenter reports only unsigned displacement magnitudes, both groups have the same mean of five micrometers. A signed displacement along the stimulus axis would instead show the difference between accumulation and avoidance. Measurements used to describe directional behavior must preserve directional information. This is the same principle that applies to bending angles when positive and negative phototropism are compared.

An actin-disrupting treatment can impair organelle motion, but it may also impair cytoplasmic organization, cell viability or unrelated trafficking processes. A more specific genetic defect with normal general actin organization provides a different type of evidence. The two approaches are complementary. Chemical perturbation can demonstrate rapid dependence on an intact cellular structure; genetic perturbation can identify a protein required for a defined part of the movement machinery. Rescue with a functional positioning protein and recovery of the local actin pattern strengthen the causal connection. Neither perturbation should be interpreted without checking that the organelles remain intact and the cells are physiologically competent.

The distinction between upstream reception and downstream positioning becomes particularly clear when a single chloroplast mutant is compared with a phototropin double mutant. Both may fail to relocate normally, but the phototropin defect affects the receipt of blue-light information, whereas a positioning defect can interrupt the translation of that information into displacement. A readout such as blue-light-dependent receptor phosphorylation would be expected to distinguish some of these cases, provided it is measured in the relevant tissue. Additional outputs such as hypocotyl bending or stomatal opening can help test whether the defect is broadly shared or relatively specific to organelle positioning.

Optical consequences of accumulation and avoidance

The optical properties of a leaf depend on the organization of cells, air spaces, pigments and chloroplasts. A photon entering a leaf may be reflected, transmitted or absorbed. Absorption is a necessary preliminary step for photosynthetic use of the light, but absorption alone does not establish that the energy was converted into useful chemical products. Some absorbed energy can be emitted as fluorescence or dissipated as heat. The positioning of chloroplasts changes the probability of interception before the subsequent fate of the excitation is determined.

For a simple optical thought experiment, imagine a chloroplast as a flattened ellipsoid. When its broad face lies perpendicular to a beam, its projected area is large. When its edge faces the beam, the projected area is smaller. If an idealized thin disk has area A and the angle between its surface normal and the direction of the beam is θ, its projected area is A times the absolute value of cos θ. A disk with area 30 square micrometers facing the beam at zero degrees presents 30 square micrometers of projected area. At sixty degrees it presents approximately 15 square micrometers. At ninety degrees the mathematical thin disk would present zero area. Real chloroplasts have finite thickness and scatter light, so the last result is not literally applicable to a living organelle.

In a leaf receiving predominantly perpendicular illumination, chloroplasts distributed along the periclinal surfaces can present more pigment-bearing area toward the incident beam. Relocation toward anticlinal surfaces can reduce the projected area and increase mutual shading. The exact optical effect depends on the cell geometry and the distribution of incoming light. If a beam arrives obliquely, the relation between the anatomical walls and the beam changes. An exam question should specify the illumination geometry when it asks a student to infer the arrangement that presents the largest projected area.

Projected area is also affected by overlap. Imagine four chloroplasts each presenting ten square micrometers in a plane perpendicular to the light. If their projected outlines do not overlap, their total projected coverage is forty square micrometers. If two pairs overlap completely in projection, the union of the outlines covers only twenty square micrometers, although the total amount of chloroplast material is unchanged. Light transmitted through the first chloroplast may still be absorbed by another, so the geometric reduction in coverage is not identical to the reduction in total photon absorption. The model explains why chloroplast distribution can matter even when pigment content is constant.

Leaf transmittance is the fraction of incident light that emerges on the opposite side of the leaf. If incident measuring light has intensity I0 and transmitted intensity is It, the fractional transmittance is It/I0. In an illustrative leaf sample receiving 100 arbitrary intensity units, a transmitted signal of 20 corresponds to a transmittance of 0.20, or 20 percent. After accumulation, transmission might fall to 14 units; after avoidance it might rise to 28 units. These values illustrate the expected direction of change under suitable measurement conditions, not universal values for a leaf. Reflectance can change as well, so the fraction that does not pass through the sample is not necessarily all absorbed.

To make this distinction quantitative, define fractional reflectance R, fractional transmittance T and fractional absorptance A. Under a simple energy-balance description at a fixed wavelength, and neglecting other complicating emission terms for the measurement, A = 1 − R − T. If T decreases from 0.20 to 0.14 while R remains 0.10, calculated absorptance rises from 0.70 to 0.76. If reflectance instead rises from 0.10 to 0.18, absorptance after the change is 0.68. In the second case a fall in transmission accompanies a slight fall, not a rise, in absorption. The calculation demonstrates why transmittance is most useful as a calibrated proxy for chloroplast position and not as an unqualified direct measure of photosynthetic energy capture.

A leaf transmittance experiment can often use a measuring wavelength that does not itself strongly drive the blue-light movement response. The blue stimulus is then applied separately, and the transmitted monitoring signal is recorded at successive times. A dark-adapted baseline, a weak-blue-light interval and a stronger-blue-light interval can reveal a sequence of optical changes. For the canonical Arabidopsis response, a fall in transmittance during weak illumination followed by an increase during sufficiently strong illumination is consistent with accumulation followed by avoidance. The order of these treatments matters because the initial chloroplast distribution influences the subsequent optical response.

If the experimenter applies strong light immediately to a sample whose chloroplasts are already largely in an avoidance position, the increase in transmittance may be small even if the movement machinery is functional. Conversely, beginning from a strongly accumulated arrangement can produce a larger optical transition. To compare genotype effects, the samples should begin from a defined common light history or their initial distributions should be documented independently. Standardization of initial state is particularly important when a response is reversible and state dependent.

A useful design for measuring recovery alternates weak and strong light while keeping a record of the response after each switch. If wild-type chloroplasts move back toward a weakly illuminated region after the strong stimulus ends, the recovery provides evidence that the preceding displacement was a regulated response rather than a permanent destruction of photosynthetic tissue. A failure to return could reflect persistent activation, loss of positioning competence or tissue injury. The time course and independent measurements of physiological state help distinguish these possibilities. The possibility of injury is particularly important if strong illumination is applied for a long duration.

At intermediate fluence rates, competing movement responses may produce complex patterns. A chloroplast may initially retreat and later accumulate, or different chloroplasts in one cell may experience different local conditions. Averaging a transmittance trace over many cells can obscure such heterogeneity. A flat average trace does not necessarily prove that no organelles moved; opposite optical effects may cancel. Imaging individual cells, or restricting the stimulus to a well-defined region, provides additional information. This is an example of the general limitation of interpreting population averages as though every individual component behaved identically.

The ecological value of repositioning is best understood as conditional. In a shaded environment, enhancing interception can benefit photosynthesis when light is limiting and other required resources are available. Under very strong illumination, reducing exposure can lower the burden on photosynthetic protective and repair mechanisms. If carbon dioxide, water or temperature limits photosynthesis, extra light interception may provide little additional carbon gain. Thus a response that optimizes photon capture does not guarantee maximal growth in all conditions. The plant's overall performance depends on the constraints operating at the same time.

Testing the protective significance of chloroplast avoidance

A mechanistic response and its physiological benefit are separate questions. Identifying phot2 as an important receptor for avoidance tells us how the high-light relocation response is initiated. It does not by itself demonstrate that the response increases survival or photosynthetic efficiency. To test function, researchers must compare the consequences of light exposure in plants that differ in their ability to relocate chloroplasts. Ideally the relevant comparison isolates the movement defect from unrelated changes in light sensing, development or photosynthetic machinery.

Excessive excitation can become problematic when the rate of absorbed light exceeds the rate at which the photosynthetic system can safely use or dissipate it. Under such conditions, photochemical reactions can contribute to the formation of reactive oxygen species, and photosynthetic components may undergo damage. Photosystem II is especially useful as a target of physiological assessment because its activity responds to light stress and recovery. Chloroplast avoidance reduces the incoming optical burden; it does not directly repair every damaged photosystem. Mechanisms of repair and dissipation are taught in the photosynthesis subunit and should be distinguished from changes in organelle positioning.

Kasahara and colleagues (2002) compared plants with different defects affecting chloroplast avoidance and observed greater susceptibility to high-light damage than in normal plants. The use of more than one type of defective plant strengthens the physiological interpretation. If every result came from only one phototropin mutant, a skeptic could propose that the susceptibility arose entirely from another output of the receptor. Comparing independent lesions associated with defective relocation helps connect the common physiological deficit to the common movement phenotype. Even this strategy benefits from controls for baseline development and photosynthesis.

One common measure in such reasoning is maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II, estimated after suitable dark adaptation from chlorophyll fluorescence. In the usual notation, F0 is the minimum fluorescence measured when photosystem II reaction centers are largely open and Fm is the maximum fluorescence reached during an appropriate saturating pulse. Variable fluorescence Fv equals Fm minus F0. The ratio Fv/Fm equals (Fm − F0)/Fm. This dimensionless ratio is not a measurement of the total amount of ATP in the leaf, nor is it the instantaneous carbon-fixation rate. It reflects a photochemical property under the specified measurement conditions.

Suppose an illustrative wild-type leaf has F0 = 180 and Fm = 900 before strong-light exposure. Its initial Fv/Fm is 720/900 = 0.80. After exposure and standardized dark adaptation, F0 = 200 and Fm = 800, giving 600/800 = 0.75. A mutant starting with the same initial fluorescence values changes to F0 = 280 and Fm = 700, giving 420/700 = 0.60. Under these hypothetical conditions, the decrease is larger in the mutant. If the mutant also lacks normal chloroplast avoidance, the pattern is compatible with a protective role for movement. It does not show that the whole decrease is due to irreversible damage; the interpretation also depends on the recovery interval and other physiological readouts.

The distinction between absolute decrease and proportional decrease is worth retaining. The wild-type ratio falls by 0.05, which is 6.25 percent of its initial 0.80. The mutant falls by 0.20, which is 25 percent of its initial ratio. Reporting that the decrease in the ratio is four times as large in the mutant is mathematically correct for this example. Reporting that the mutant lost four times as much total photosynthetic carbon fixation would not follow from these data. Each biological measurement has its own interpretation and must not be silently replaced by a different quantity.

A second control concerns light dose. If the movement-defective mutant absorbs more incident light than wild type because its chloroplasts remain in a high-interception arrangement, that difference is part of the proposed protective mechanism. Equal incident fluence does not necessarily mean equal absorbed fluence. A comparison at equal incident illumination tests performance in the same environment; a comparison at matched absorbed energy could address a different question about intrinsic susceptibility of the photosynthetic apparatus. The distinction is essential when deciding whether avoidance operates by reducing exposure or by changing the chemistry of damage resistance.

Long-term survival and growth measurements are also informative, but they integrate many variables. A leaf that suffers greater short-term photoinhibition may still recover if the stress is brief and repair is efficient. Conversely, a modest repeated loss of photosynthetic efficiency over many days may reduce growth even without obvious leaf death. When a study compares final biomass under different light conditions, that result can reflect stomatal conductance, leaf positioning, chloroplast relocation and developmental differences simultaneously. It cannot be attributed to one output simply because that output is the most familiar phototropin response.

Protection is therefore best treated as a hierarchy of observations: receptor-dependent signaling, organelle relocation, optical consequences, physiological changes during light stress and longer-term plant performance. Evidence at one level supports but does not automatically establish every later level. The hierarchy provides a framework for a Part C question in which a mutant is normal for some readouts but defective in others. A student should identify which link in the hierarchy has actually been tested and what additional comparison would help establish the proposed mechanism.

An avoidance response also entails an opportunity cost. If an organelle remains in a low-interception arrangement after light becomes scarce, photosynthetic light capture may be reduced. The biological importance of a reversible photocycle and movement recovery is therefore not simply to turn protection on; it also allows the plant to return toward an efficient low-light arrangement when conditions change. The balance between light capture and damage prevention is dynamic rather than a choice between permanently maximizing and permanently minimizing absorption.

A receptor family with several tissue-specific outputs

A plant may use phototropins in a seedling hypocotyl, in the petiole of a leaf, in a mesophyll cell and in guard cells. The underlying sensory modules can share the same photochemical principle while the downstream response differs according to the identity of the tissue. This organization is a recurring theme in cell signaling: an activated receptor has consequences determined by the proteins and structures available to receive its signal. A phototropin cannot induce differential growth in a cell type lacking an appropriate growth program simply because it has absorbed blue light.

Leaf positioning and flattening illustrate an organ-level output related to, but not identical with, ordinary seedling phototropism. A petiole connects the leaf blade to the stem and contributes to the angle at which the blade intercepts light. Changes in petiole growth and blade shape can alter the orientation of a large photosynthetic surface. A leaf that remains curled may have regions shaded by other regions of the same blade. A flatter blade can intercept more light under suitable conditions, but the optimal angle depends on the position of the light source and the environment. Not every change in leaf angle is a direct phototropic curvature response.

The distinction can be tested by defining two measurements: the angle between the petiole and a fixed reference axis, and the degree of curvature of the blade across its width. A mutant might have a normal petiole angle but abnormal blade flattening. Another mutant might have the opposite pattern. Combining these measurements into one vague category called leaf movement would conceal a potentially important difference. A precise phenotypic description allows investigators to ask whether signaling components are shared or whether one branch of the pathway has been selectively altered.

Studies of leaf positioning under blue illumination have found roles for phototropins and selected phototropin signaling factors, with the relative contributions depending on light conditions. This supports an important distinction between a receptor family and the specific network in which the receptor operates. A signaling protein indispensable for one response under a weak-light condition may be less important for another response or under a condition in which a second receptor pathway contributes. The presence of a normal chloroplast movement response in a mutant does not necessarily establish that every leaf-developmental response is also normal.

Guard-cell opening supplies a particularly clear contrast in timescale and mechanism. Guard cells alter their shape as their solute and water content changes, modifying the size of the stomatal pore. A reversible pore movement is not the same as an irreversible difference in extension of two sides of a growing hypocotyl. Kinoshita and colleagues demonstrated a role for both Arabidopsis phototropins in the blue-light component of stomatal opening. The fact that the same receptor family regulates this response does not require the conclusion that NPH3-dependent auxin redistribution is the mechanism of guard-cell opening. Guard cells contain a specialized signaling and ion-transport apparatus that receives the receptor input.

The response to blue light is also not the entire explanation of stomatal regulation. A plant must balance entry of carbon dioxide for photosynthesis with loss of water vapor. Red light can affect photosynthesis-associated stomatal responses, and water deficit signals can favor closure even when blue light is present. An open pore is therefore an integrated physiological state rather than a direct indicator of phototropin activity. This distinction prepares the analysis of guard-cell proton pumping, membrane potential, solute flux and turgor in Node 6.E.05 without importing the full mechanism into the present chapter.

Imagine two mutants with a similar lack of blue-light-induced stomatal opening. One lacks both major phototropins and the other lacks a signaling factor expressed predominantly in guard cells. Both may show a defective aperture response, but their predictions for hypocotyl curvature and chloroplast relocation differ. The receptor double mutant is expected to affect multiple phototropin outputs, while a guard-cell-specific downstream defect could leave other tissues substantially normal. A tissue-specific complementation experiment is one way to test this distinction. Restoring a functional protein only in guard cells might rescue aperture without restoring curvature if the other tissues remain deficient.

Cryptochromes provide a useful comparison because their blue-light sensitivity might lead a student to confuse them with phototropins. Blue-light-induced changes in seedling morphology are not all evidence of phototropin-dependent directional growth. Cryptochromes are particularly associated with developmental signaling and control of growth programs during de-etiolation. A hypocotyl that becomes shorter under uniform blue light exhibits a developmental response to light, but shortening alone does not demonstrate phototropism because the direction of growth has not been compared with the light vector. Some rapid growth effects may also involve phototropins, so the assay and genetic evidence determine the receptor assignment.

Phytochrome-mediated red-light effects can likewise change the context in which phototropism occurs. A seedling's prior exposure to red light can modify developmental state and sensitivity to blue illumination. The final response to a combined red and blue treatment need not equal the sum of responses to each color separately. An interaction between pathways can change expression, protein activity or growth competence. To test an interaction, the appropriate comparison includes darkness, red alone, blue alone and both together, with photon fluxes and timing specified. A greater response in the combined treatment than in blue alone shows an effect of red pretreatment or simultaneous red signaling; it does not establish that phototropin itself has become a red-light receptor.

Photosynthetic pigments are a third source of overlap. A green leaf absorbs blue light through chlorophyll and accessory pigments as well as through sensory photoreceptors. If photosynthesis changes the internal concentration of carbon dioxide or the energy status of a tissue, it can alter physiological behavior independently of a direct sensory switch. Measuring a rapid blue-light-specific response at low stimulus intensity, using receptor mutants and controlling photosynthetic background illumination can help separate these effects. The purpose is not to deny that energy metabolism supports movement, but to determine which light-dependent process initiates the particular signal being studied.

The plant-level contribution of the receptor family is therefore best regarded as coordination of several optical and physiological adjustments. Phototropism alters the orientation of a growing axis. Leaf positioning and flattening influence the geometry of the photosynthetic surface. Chloroplast relocation adjusts light interception at the cellular level. Stomatal opening regulates the aperture through which gases exchange. These responses are complementary but not interchangeable. Their shared receptor input provides an opportunity for coordinated adaptation without requiring one identical downstream mechanism in every tissue.

Interpreting experiments and model limitations

The strongest reasoning begins by specifying the level at which each measurement is made. Absorbance spectroscopy can reveal a chromophore transition. An immunoblot or mobility shift can report phosphorylation. A reporter for auxin-responsive transcription can reveal a spatial pattern of hormone response. Microscopy can reveal changes in transporter polarity or chloroplast location. Whole-organ curvature is a final integrated outcome. A failure in an early readout is compatible with failure of all later readouts, while normal early readouts together with defective later ones point toward downstream defects.

A complementation experiment can test whether a particular phototropin domain is necessary for signaling. Introduce the wild-type receptor and a version with altered LOV2 photochemistry separately into a receptor-deficient background. Match their expression as closely as possible and verify protein accumulation. If wild type restores blue-light curvature but the altered version does not, the experiment supports a necessary role for the intact sensory module. If the mutant protein is unstable, however, its inability to restore curvature cannot be assigned solely to failure of photochemistry. Measuring receptor accumulation is therefore an essential control.

A related experiment compares illumination of a narrow region of a leaf with uniform illumination at the same local fluence rate. A microscopic time course can distinguish movement into the illuminated region from movement away. At weak light a local accumulation pattern is expected; at strong light a phot2-dependent avoidance pattern is expected in a suitable wild-type Arabidopsis preparation. The spatial distribution of the stimulus must be recorded, because the word strong alone does not specify which parts of a cell received light. The imaging light must also be chosen so that it does not inadvertently induce or modify the movement under investigation.

Mechanistic models are useful when they generate distinguishable predictions. A model in which blue light changes auxin synthesis only on one side and a model in which it changes lateral auxin transport can both predict a final reporter asymmetry. Measuring transporter distribution and using perturbations of directional transport provide ways to discriminate between these explanations. It is possible that more than one process contributes, so a result supporting transport need not rule out every change in metabolism. The exam-level conclusion should track what the experiment directly establishes rather than claiming a stronger result than the measurements permit.

Reasoning from experiments: controls, rescue and genetic interactions

A strong experimental explanation identifies the variable being changed, the variable being measured and the biological conditions that must remain comparable. For a light response, relevant independent variables include wavelength, incident photon flux, pulse duration and direction. Genotype is another independent variable when mutations are compared. Dependent variables can include the fraction of active receptor, the extent of phosphorylation, a spatial reporter ratio, bend angle, chloroplast displacement or leaf transmittance. Confounding variables include seedling age, tissue size, previous illumination, temperature and mechanical orientation. Merely listing a mutant and a light condition is not a complete experimental design.

Consider an experiment aimed at testing whether loss of phototropin function specifically affects directional growth rather than overall elongation. Begin with dark-grown wild type and a receptor-deficient genotype of similar initial length. Divide each into unilaterally illuminated and dark groups. Record hypocotyl length as well as bending angle after a fixed interval. Suppose the illuminated wild type and mutant both extend by three millimeters, while the wild type bends forty degrees and the mutant bends five degrees. Such an illustrative result supports a defect in the regulation of direction rather than a general inability to grow. It does not identify the exact affected molecular stage. A receptor-associated biochemical assay would help localize the defect.

A control using equal illumination from opposite directions addresses the possibility that light simply accelerates or inhibits growth rather than instructing its orientation. If the opposing beams have equal local intensity and produce approximately equal stimulation on both sides, the net directional response may be small even though signaling is active. The total incident photons should not be inadvertently doubled when comparing the bilateral and unilateral treatments unless the study explicitly asks about that difference. A careful comparison separates spatial pattern from total dose by controlling both.

Genetic complementation is particularly powerful when the aim is to connect a phenotype to a gene. A mutant defective in a known receptor can be transformed with a functional copy of that receptor gene. Restoration of the expected response supports the conclusion that the receptor lesion contributed to the defect. Expression level and tissue specificity remain important. A highly overexpressed gene can produce effects that do not faithfully reproduce its native regulation. Comparing expression and including an appropriate vector control help distinguish genuine rescue from an unrelated consequence of the transformation.

A genetic interaction can also reveal partial redundancy. Suppose a weak-light treatment yields large curvature in wild type, substantially reduced curvature in a phot1 single mutant, near-normal curvature in a phot2 single mutant and almost no curvature in the double mutant. A higher-intensity treatment yields more substantial curvature in the phot1 single mutant while the double mutant remains severely defective. The changing pattern is consistent with an overlapping contribution from phot2 that becomes more evident in the higher-intensity treatment. It is not evidence that the two genes encode identical proteins or that one receptor stops functioning whenever the other becomes active.

The relative contribution of two receptors should not be estimated by subtracting the two single-mutant responses from the wild-type response without examining the assumptions. A pathway may saturate, and the measured final angle is limited by tissue geometry and growth. For instance, if one receptor alone is sufficient to drive a near-maximal response, both single mutants might look nearly normal even though each receptor makes a substantial contribution when both are present. Under a weaker stimulus, the same genetic deletions might reveal more obvious phenotypes. Functional redundancy is thus a property of a particular biological output under particular test conditions rather than a fixed arithmetic percentage assigned to a gene.

Epistasis analysis compares phenotypes produced by combinations of mutations. In a simple linear pathway, the phenotype of a downstream lesion may mask the effect of an upstream perturbation. However, an equal absence of bending in two single mutants and the double mutant is not sufficient to establish their order when the measured output has reached its lower limit. Both mutations may independently prevent essential processes. A bypass experiment or a biochemical intermediate can provide more information. If one mutant lacks receptor phosphorylation and another shows normal receptor phosphorylation but fails to redistribute auxin, the intermediate readouts support different positions in the causal network.

An example involving receptor activation and tissue specificity is instructive. A hypothetical mutant exhibits normal blue-induced phototropin autophosphorylation, normal chloroplast relocation and impaired hypocotyl phototropism. The first two findings argue against a complete loss of phototropin function. A defect in a hypocotyl-specific signaling or transport process is plausible. A second mutant lacks both curvature and chloroplast movement, but maintains normal growth in darkness. This pattern is compatible with a shared upstream receptor defect, although other broadly required factors might produce similar phenotypes. The point of the comparison is to narrow candidate explanations rather than to identify a specific gene from two observations alone.

Time-dependent measurements can separate two apparently similar phenotypes. Suppose wild-type seedlings reach thirty degrees by one hour and forty degrees by three hours. Mutant A reaches five degrees by one hour but thirty-eight degrees by three hours. Mutant B reaches five degrees by one hour and eight degrees by three hours. Both mutants appear strongly defective in the early measurement, but the later results distinguish delayed response from persistent impairment under the selected conditions. This is why a graph with several time points is often more informative than an endpoint bar chart. Changes in growth capacity across time must also be considered.

The design of a chloroplast movement experiment benefits from the same logic. A researcher can measure the fraction of organelles in an illuminated patch before and after a blue-light stimulus. If the patch contains four of twenty chloroplasts before treatment and twelve of twenty afterward, the local fraction rises from 0.20 to 0.60. The increase is forty percentage points, or a threefold increase relative to the initial fraction. If a second treatment starts with twelve organelles in the patch, reaching sixteen afterward represents a smaller increase even though the final fraction is greater. Reporting starting and ending states avoids confusion between response magnitude and endpoint occupancy.

Replication matters because individual plants and cells vary. Multiple chloroplasts within one cell are not necessarily independent biological replicates; they share the same cellular signaling environment. Several cells within a single leaf may likewise share developmental conditions. For a study of genotype-level differences, independent plants provide an important level of replication. Measurements of many organelles can characterize within-cell variation, but treating every organelle as an independent plant would exaggerate the amount of independent evidence. Students should identify the experimental unit before interpreting sample size and uncertainty.

A mean response can conceal considerable variability. For illustration, four wild-type seedlings show angles of 20, 25, 35 and 40 degrees, averaging 30 degrees. Four mutant seedlings show 0, 0, 40 and 40 degrees, averaging 20 degrees. Although the mutant mean is lower, the individual values indicate a heterogeneous response. It would be misleading to describe every mutant as having a uniformly reduced angle of twenty degrees. The spread may reflect developmental differences, incomplete penetrance or experimental variability; distinguishing those possibilities requires additional data. Reporting individual measurements or an appropriate measure of variation makes the comparison more interpretable.

Finally, an exam dataset may contain observations that are insufficient to choose a unique model. When a receptor mutant lacks curvature, one cannot conclude from that observation alone that auxin synthesis is abolished. When a chloroplast mutant transmits more measuring light, one cannot conclude without additional evidence that it has more photosynthetic pigment. Recognizing the limit of an inference is part of mechanistic understanding. The most useful next experiment is one whose possible outcomes would distinguish between the remaining explanations, not merely repeat the same ambiguous measurement.

Connecting photoreception to plant performance

The sequence from light perception to plant performance can be summarized as a hierarchy of state changes. At the molecular level, a chromophore enters a light-generated state and the receptor's catalytic activity changes. At the cellular level, signaling partners and transport or cytoskeletal machinery alter their behavior. At the tissue level, different cell populations can acquire different growth rates or reposition their photosynthetic apparatus. At the whole-plant level, these changes influence light interception and the exchange of resources with the environment. Each level requires its own evidence and operates over its own timescale.

These levels also help explain why responses need not be proportional to one another. A doubling of incident photon flux does not necessarily double receptor activation because a finite receptor population can approach saturation. A doubling of receptor activation does not necessarily double lateral hormone transport because transporters can saturate or signaling pathways can adapt. A doubling of the difference in growth rates does not necessarily double the final angle because the stimulus geometry changes as the plant turns. Similarly, moving twice as many chloroplasts to a leaf surface does not necessarily double photosynthetic carbon gain because other resources or photochemical capacity may be limiting.

An illustrative combined scenario considers a young plant emerging beneath a larger plant's leaf. The open region to one side of the canopy offers a brighter direction. The seedling can use unequal blue-light input across its growing tissue to change its orientation. Once its own leaves are exposed, phototropin-dependent positioning and flattening may improve their interception of the available light. Within the mesophyll, accumulation can favor interception while the environment remains dim, whereas a sudden strong sunfleck may initiate avoidance. The scenario connects the responses across spatial scales without implying that each step must finish before another begins. These processes can operate simultaneously in different tissues.

If the plant is also water limited, opening stomata in response to light may increase water loss. Hormonal signals favoring closure can therefore counterbalance the light input. A photosynthetic tissue experiencing temperature stress may not be able to use every photon captured even if its chloroplasts accumulate normally. These examples demonstrate why a receptor-mediated response cannot be labeled universally advantageous independent of context. Sensory signaling supplies information to an integrated physiological system; the consequences depend on the environment and the plant's other regulatory processes.

The key distinction for CSIR-NET and SET reasoning is between detecting the stimulus and executing the response. The same upstream blue-light receptor family contributes to several outputs, but disruption of one downstream effector need not remove every output. Conversely, different sensory receptors can affect the same broad outcome, such as growth, through different signaling mechanisms. An experimental observation becomes meaningful when the student identifies the relevant tissue, spatial geometry, light history and level of measurement. This framework makes it possible to interpret unfamiliar mutant, reporter and physiological datasets without relying on memorization of a rigid pathway.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All light-induced bending is photosynthesis-driven.

Correction: Phototropism can occur in etiolated tissue using sensory photoreceptors. Why it is tempting: Both processes involve light, which encourages conflating energy capture with information. Examination reasoning: Use receptor mutants and growth controls rather than assuming a photosynthetic cause.

2. Phototropin contains FAD because cryptochrome does.

Correction: Phototropin LOV domains bind FMN; cryptochromes typically bind FAD. Why it is tempting: Both are flavin receptors. Examination reasoning: Match cofactor to domain architecture.

3. LOV2 binds ATP to make the light adduct.

Correction: FMN–cysteine adduct formation is a photochemical process; the kinase uses ATP. Why it is tempting: Both events occur in the activated receptor. Examination reasoning: Distinguish optical readouts from phosphorylation assays.

4. LOV1 and LOV2 must absorb two simultaneous photons.

Correction: Each LOV module can undergo photochemistry; LOV2 is a principal catalytic regulatory switch. Why it is tempting: Two domains may be mistaken for a two-photon requirement. Examination reasoning: Predict domain-specific rather than all-or-none defects.

5. Phototropin is a transmembrane extracellular ligand receptor.

Correction: It is a light-sensitive kinase commonly associated with the plasma membrane. Why it is tempting: Membrane association is often confused with membrane-spanning topology. Examination reasoning: Identify the internal FMN chromophore as the photosensor.

6. The lit side always grows faster.

Correction: In typical positive shoot phototropism the shaded side extends more and becomes the outer arc. Why it is tempting: Students infer that greater illumination directly promotes extension. Examination reasoning: Draw the two arcs before assigning bend direction.

7. Auxin always promotes elongation.

Correction: The growth response depends on dose and tissue; roots can respond differently from shoots. Why it is tempting: A common shoot model is overgeneralized. Examination reasoning: Use the specified organ's auxin sensitivity.

8. A bright DR5 reporter is a direct IAA assay.

Correction: A transcriptional reporter reflects auxin response and reporter kinetics. Why it is tempting: Fluorescence looks like a concentration map. Examination reasoning: Distinguish concentration, signaling and reporter output.

9. Normal total PIN3 means normal auxin polarity.

Correction: Transporter location can change independently of abundance. Why it is tempting: Bulk immunoblots omit spatial information. Examination reasoning: Inspect membrane-face localization and directional flux.

10. phot1 functions exclusively in low light and phot2 exclusively in strong light.

Correction: The receptors partially overlap; phot1 can function across intensities. Why it is tempting: Simplified comparisons are mistaken for exclusive roles. Examination reasoning: Compare single and double mutants at matched fluence rates.

11. Phot2 is needed for all chloroplast accumulation.

Correction: Both phot1 and phot2 contribute to accumulation; phot2 dominates sustained avoidance. Why it is tempting: The importance of phot2 in avoidance is applied to every response. Examination reasoning: Identify the intensity regime and output.

12. Chloroplast movement is a type of shoot phototropism.

Correction: It is intracellular relocation; shoot phototropism involves differential organ growth. Why it is tempting: Both movements can be directional. Examination reasoning: Ask what physically moves and how.

13. Actin always accumulates on the side nearest light.

Correction: Chloroplast-associated actin is associated with the advancing edge, whose orientation differs between accumulation and avoidance. Why it is tempting: Weak-light behavior is overgeneralized. Examination reasoning: Identify the movement vector first.

14. More transmittance always means higher photosynthesis.

Correction: Higher transmittance can indicate reduced optical interception during avoidance; reflectance and physiology also matter. Why it is tempting: Optical and photosynthetic measures are conflated. Examination reasoning: Use transmittance as a conditional movement proxy, not a direct carbon fixation assay.

15. A normal kinase signal proves all receptor outputs are functional.

Correction: Downstream transport, positioning or tissue-specific signaling can still fail. Why it is tempting: A proximal readout is confused with the final response. Examination reasoning: Trace molecular, cellular and physiological intermediates separately.

16. Equal fluence guarantees equal bending.

Correction: Intensity, duration, recovery and adaptation can alter responses at equal dose. Why it is tempting: Fluence is treated as the sole variable. Examination reasoning: Compare rate and time as well as accumulated photons.

17. Failure of two single mutants to bend establishes their gene order.

Correction: A shared floor phenotype alone cannot order genes. Why it is tempting: Simple linear epistasis rules are applied without intermediate readouts. Examination reasoning: Use molecular measurements or bypass and complementation.

18. Avoidance and nonphotochemical quenching are the same mechanism.

Correction: Avoidance changes optical exposure; quenching dissipates absorbed excitation. Why it is tempting: Both help limit light stress. Examination reasoning: Distinguish interception from excitation fate.

Original examination practice

These original questions are arranged by level. Attempt each question before opening its explanation; the first questions in each level may require a short written response rather than an option choice.

1. Part B — Which cofactor is directly associated with phototropin LOV domains?

Answer and explanation: FMN, rather than the bilin of phytochrome or FAD usually associated with cryptochrome. The sensory chromophore initiates light-dependent conformational signaling.

2. Part B — What process is directly catalyzed by the phototropin kinase?

Answer and explanation: ATP-dependent phosphorylation of suitable protein residues. Adduct formation within a LOV domain is a separate photochemical step.

3. Part B — Which Arabidopsis phototropin is especially important for sustained high-light chloroplast avoidance?

Answer and explanation: Phot2. Both phot1 and phot2 can contribute to accumulation, so a phot2 mutant may still show the low-light response.

4. Part B — Which ordering correctly represents the major phototropin domains from N to C terminus?
  1. Kinase–LOV2–LOV1
  2. LOV1–LOV2–kinase
  3. LOV2–phytochromobilin–kinase
  4. FAD photolyase–kinase

Answer: B. The amino-terminal sensory half contains LOV1 followed by LOV2; the carboxyl-terminal region is the serine/threonine kinase. The reversed order is incorrect, phytochromobilin belongs to phytochrome, and the photolyase-like domain is characteristic of cryptochromes rather than phototropin.

5. Part B — What is the principal chemical change of the LOV light state?
  1. FMN reduction to permanently free riboflavin
  2. Phosphorylation of the flavin by ATP
  3. Reversible cysteinyl–FMN adduct formation
  4. Conversion of Pr to Pfr

Answer: C. The light state involves a reversible covalent bond between a conserved cysteine and the flavin. ATP-dependent phosphorylation belongs to kinase activity, Pr/Pfr is phytochrome photoconversion, and FMN is not permanently released.

6. Part B — Which structural element is especially associated with LOV2-mediated release of kinase inhibition?
  1. Jα helix
  2. Thylakoid lumen
  3. CO transcription factor
  4. PIN3 transport pore

Answer: A. Reorganization of the LOV2-associated Jα helix helps couple sensory excitation to the catalytic region. Thylakoid lumen is a chloroplast compartment, CO is a photoperiodic regulator, and PIN3 participates downstream in auxin transport.

7. Part B — Which statement best describes phototropin localization?
  1. An obligatory extracellular receptor with seven membrane-spanning helices
  2. An exclusively thylakoid lumen protein
  3. An exclusively soluble nuclear DNA-binding protein
  4. A light-sensitive kinase commonly associated with the cytoplasmic face of the plasma membrane

Answer: D. Phototropins are plasma-membrane-associated light-sensitive kinases and may show light-dependent localization changes. They are neither canonical seven-transmembrane receptors nor thylakoid lumen enzymes nor exclusively nuclear transcription factors.

8. Part B — Which condition provides the clearest directional stimulus for shoot phototropism?
  1. Uniform bilateral blue light of equal strength
  2. A unilateral blue-light beam
  3. Constant darkness
  4. Uniform red light with no direction contrast

Answer: B. Unilateral blue illumination creates lateral contrast in phototropin activation. Equal bilateral or uniform illumination lacks a comparable left–right cue, and darkness provides no phototropic light signal.

9. Part B — During conventional positive shoot phototropism, which side forms the longer arc?
  1. Shaded side
  2. Illuminated side
  3. Both sides equally
  4. Only the central vascular cylinder

Answer: A. The shaded side generally extends more and forms the outer arc. Faster growth on the lit side would tend to bend the axis away; equal extension changes length rather than generating this curvature.

10. Part B — Which factor is a member of the NPH3/RPT2-like phototropin signaling family?
  1. Rubisco
  2. DELLA
  3. RPT2
  4. Phytochromobilin

Answer: C. RPT2 belongs to the NPH3/RPT2-like family. Rubisco is a carbon fixation enzyme, DELLA regulates gibberellin responses, and phytochromobilin is a chromophore.

11. Part B — PIN3 contributes to hypocotyl phototropism primarily through which function?
  1. FMN binding
  2. Direct photon absorption
  3. Chloroplast actin nucleation
  4. Directional auxin efflux

Answer: D. PIN3 is an auxin efflux carrier whose membrane localization can influence directional transport. It is not a photoreceptor, flavin-binding sensory domain or chloroplast actin organizer.

12. Part B — Which assay directly reports organelle relocation at single-cell resolution?
  1. Time-lapse chloroplast microscopy
  2. Whole-leaf total protein concentration
  3. A whole-organ length measurement
  4. A measurement of total ATP without imaging

Answer: A. Tracking chloroplast positions over time directly reveals displacement. Bulk protein, ATP and organ length may describe other cellular properties but do not resolve individual chloroplast locations.

13. Part B — Which response is expected when a suitably dark-adapted Arabidopsis leaf receives weak blue illumination?
  1. Permanent chloroplast destruction
  2. Accumulation toward illuminated regions
  3. Obligatory stomatal closure
  4. Conversion of chlorophyll into FMN

Answer: B. Weak blue light can elicit phototropin-dependent chloroplast accumulation, improving light interception. The other options describe neither the normal movement nor the relevant photochemistry.

14. Part B — In a standard leaf illuminated perpendicular to its surface, which walls commonly become occupied during avoidance?
  1. Only the nuclear envelope
  2. Only the vacuolar membrane
  3. Anticlinal walls
  4. Only the thylakoid membrane

Answer: C. Chloroplasts tend to move toward side-wall regions (anticlinal relative to the leaf surface), decreasing direct exposure. The listed organelle membranes are not alternative cell-wall orientations.

15. Part B — Which protein is associated with the chloroplast envelope and normal actin-dependent chloroplast positioning?
  1. PIF4
  2. GID1
  3. HY5
  4. CHUP1

Answer: D. CHUP1 is a chloroplast positioning factor associated with the outer envelope. PIF4 and HY5 are transcriptional regulators, while GID1 is a gibberellin receptor.

16. Part B — Which describes a photonastic response?
  1. Movement whose direction is determined by organ anatomy rather than the direction of light
  2. Growth specifically toward a unilateral beam
  3. Movement exclusively determined by gravity
  4. A reversible cysteinyl–FMN reaction

Answer: A. Nastic response direction is not set by the direction of the inducing stimulus. Phototropism is directional growth in relation to light; gravitropism responds to gravity; photochemistry is molecular rather than a nastic organ movement.

17. Part B — What is the unit of photon fluence rate commonly used in plant photobiology?
  1. Micromoles per liter
  2. Micromoles of photons per square meter per second
  3. Joules per mole of auxin
  4. Degrees per millimeter

Answer: B. Fluence rate describes photons incident per area per time. Concentration, molar chemical energy and curvature per length quantify different properties.

18. Part B — What is the usual effect of dark recovery on the LOV cysteinyl–flavin adduct?
  1. Permanent conversion into chlorophyll
  2. Accumulation of an irreversible cross-link
  3. Return toward the dark-adapted noncovalent state
  4. Production of NADPH by the receptor

Answer: C. Thermal recovery breaks the reversible adduct and restores the dark state. The sensory cycle is not chlorophyll synthesis, irreversible cross-linking or photosynthetic electron transfer.

19. Part B — Which contrast between phototropins and cryptochromes is most accurate?
  1. Both are identical catalytic kinases
  2. Neither binds a chromophore
  3. Phototropin is a red-light bilin receptor
  4. Phototropin has LOV–FMN sensory domains and a kinase, whereas plant cryptochromes have a different flavin-based architecture

Answer: D. They share sensitivity to blue wavelengths but have distinct domain architectures and predominant functions. A bilin is characteristic of phytochrome, not phototropin.

20. Part B — Which statement about chloroplast avoidance and nonphotochemical quenching is correct?
  1. They can act together at different levels of photoprotection
  2. Both physically relocate chloroplasts
  3. Both are identical phototropin-catalyzed phosphorylation events
  4. Avoidance directly repairs every damaged PSII reaction center

Answer: A. Avoidance adjusts optical exposure; nonphotochemical quenching dissipates absorbed excitation. Neither is equivalent to PSII repair, and quenching does not require relocating the whole organelle.

21. Part B — A dark-grown seedling displays blue-light-dependent shortening but no directional comparison was made. Which description is justified?
  1. Positive phototropism is proven
  2. A light-dependent growth response was observed; tropism is not yet established
  3. Chloroplast avoidance is proven
  4. The phototropin genotype is identified

Answer: B. Directional dependence is required to diagnose a tropism. Shortening alone neither reveals organelle relocation nor identifies the gene involved.

22. Part B — What does the ratio Fv/Fm primarily describe under suitable dark-adapted measurement conditions?
  1. Absolute leaf carbon-fixation rate
  2. Chloroplast displacement distance
  3. Maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II photochemistry
  4. Phototropin concentration

Answer: C. Fv/Fm is a chlorophyll fluorescence estimate of PSII maximum quantum efficiency. It is not a direct measurement of carbon fixation, displacement or receptor abundance.

23. Part B — Which statement best explains why guard-cell and hypocotyl responses differ despite shared phototropins?
  1. The receptors absorb different photons solely because the cells have different names
  2. Guard cells contain no signaling proteins
  3. Hypocotyls contain no ATP
  4. Downstream tissue-specific machinery determines the physiological output

Answer: D. A shared receptor can engage distinct signaling networks and effectors in different tissues. Guard cells and hypocotyls both contain metabolic and signaling machinery; naming does not determine photon energy.

24. Part C — A LOV2 mutant displays normal protein abundance but no light-induced kinase activation. A kinase-dead mutant still forms a cysteinyl–FMN adduct. Which stages are affected?

Answer and explanation: The first mutation affects light-to-kinase coupling, and the second affects catalytic signaling downstream of sensory activation. Both can impair curvature despite different molecular defects.

25. Part C — Uniform and unilateral blue light produce the same total receptor phosphorylation, but only unilateral light produces directional curvature. Explain.

Answer and explanation: Total phosphorylation does not indicate the spatial distribution of activity. The unilateral stimulus can impose a lateral difference in signaling, auxin transport and growth; uniform illumination need not establish this directional difference.

26. Part C — A mutant has normal phototropin phosphorylation and low-light chloroplast accumulation, but lacks high-light avoidance. Is a defect in all FMN binding the simplest explanation?

Answer and explanation: No. The intact accumulation response and receptor phosphorylation argue against a universal sensory defect. A phot2-specific or downstream avoidance defect is a more targeted hypothesis; gene identification requires additional evidence.

27. Part C — A shoot segment has an outer–inner arc length difference of 0.6 mm and diameter 1.2 mm. Estimate the bend.

Answer and explanation: Under the circular-arc approximation, θ = 0.6/1.2 = 0.5 radians, or approximately 28.6 degrees. The outer side is the longer side; the estimate assumes constant separation.

28. Part C — Two light pulses deliver 60 micromoles per square meter: 2 for 30 s and 6 for 10 s. Must they produce equal curvature?

Answer and explanation: No. The doses match, but photochemical dark recovery and signaling adaptation can operate during the longer pulse. Curvature additionally depends on spatial gradients and growth capacity.

29. Part C — Equal protein amounts of WT and a mutant are examined. Both show the same light-induced LOV absorbance transition, but only WT increases phosphorylation. What is the best conclusion?
  1. The mutant must lack FMN
  2. The mutant has enhanced photon absorption
  3. The defect is compatible with catalytic activity or sensor-to-kinase coupling
  4. The mutant necessarily lacks all PIN proteins

Answer: C. A normal optical transition argues that at least the measured sensory chemistry remains intact. Loss of phosphorylation points to coupling or catalysis. FMN absence contradicts the normal optical transition, and PIN abundance is not determined by these receptor measurements.

30. Part C — A regulatory-interface mutant has high kinase activity in darkness but does not generate directional curvature under uniform dark conditions. Which interpretation is strongest?
  1. Kinase activity alone may not provide a directional difference
  2. The LOV domain must absorb red light
  3. ATP-dependent catalysis is absent
  4. Uniform darkness necessarily creates a lateral auxin gradient

Answer: A. Constitutive activation can bypass a regulatory restraint without supplying spatial asymmetry. The other options infer an unsupported chromophore shift, contradict measured catalysis, or assume an asymmetry that has not been demonstrated.

31. Part C — Two constructs have light/dark activities of 1.0/0.1 and 0.8/0.7 respectively. Which is the correct comparison?
  1. The second has no kinase activity
  2. The first has lower absolute light activity
  3. Both have identical fold induction
  4. The second shows lower fold induction but higher basal activity

Answer: D. The first induces tenfold; the second about 1.14-fold. The second nevertheless has substantial basal and illuminated activity. Fold induction alone is therefore a poor measure of maximum catalytic competence.

32. Part C — Under a simplified two-state model, kL = 0.06 s⁻¹ and kd = 0.02 s⁻¹. What is the steady active fraction?
  1. 0.25
  2. 0.75
  3. 3.0
  4. 0.08

Answer: B. At equilibrium, kL(1−a)=kda, hence a=kL/(kL+kd)=0.06/0.08=0.75. 0.25 is the inactive fraction, 3.0 is a ratio of rates, and 0.08 s⁻¹ is their sum, not a fraction.

33. Part C — A receptor has an activated-state half-life of 40 s in darkness. Starting at active fraction 0.80, what fraction remains after 80 s in the simple first-order recovery model?
  1. 0.40
  2. 0.60
  3. 0.20
  4. 0.05

Answer: C. Eighty seconds equals two half-lives, so 0.80 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 0.20. This calculation assumes no further light activation and first-order recovery; the other values do not represent two halvings.

34. Part C — A 450-nm treatment and a 660-nm treatment deliver equal radiant energy. What is true about photon numbers?
  1. The red treatment supplies approximately 1.47 times as many photons
  2. The blue treatment supplies 1.47 times as many photons
  3. The photon numbers must be identical
  4. Photon number is independent of wavelength at equal energy

Answer: A. Since photon energy is hc/λ, photon number at fixed total energy is proportional to wavelength. The ratio is 660/450≈1.47. Equal radiant energy is therefore not equivalent to photon-flux matching.

35. Part C — The doses needed for a fixed response at three wavelengths are 5, 25 and 100 μmol m⁻². Normalized to the most effective wavelength, what are the relative sensitivities?
  1. 1, 5, 20
  2. 5, 25, 100
  3. 0.05, 0.25, 1
  4. 1, 0.20, 0.05

Answer: D. Relative sensitivity at a fixed effect is inversely related to required dose: 5/5, 5/25, 5/100. The other options use direct dose or reverse normalization.

36. Part C — A phot1 mutant bends weakly at low blue irradiance but substantially at higher irradiance. The phot1 phot2 double mutant remains nearly straight at both. Which inference is justified?
  1. Phot1 is unnecessary for weak-light responses
  2. Phot2 can contribute to curvature when phot1 is absent, especially in the higher-light treatment
  3. Phot2 is the only receptor at all intensities
  4. Both mutations must eliminate auxin synthesis

Answer: B. The single-to-double contrast reveals an overlapping phot2 contribution under the higher-light condition. The weak-light defect demonstrates a phot1 role, and neither auxin synthesis nor exclusive phot2 function is established.

37. Part C — A mutant exhibits normal overall hypocotyl extension, normal receptor phosphorylation and no lateral auxin-reporter asymmetry. Which next experiment is most targeted?
  1. Measure total chlorophyll only
  2. Increase temperature without controls
  3. Examine PIN polarity and downstream phototropin signaling in the elongating tissue
  4. Measure photon energy using a different wavelength only

Answer: C. The observations localize the defect after receptor activation but before or at establishment of a hormone response difference. PIN localization and signaling intermediates address that interval; chlorophyll, temperature and photon energy do not discriminate the leading hypotheses.

38. Part C — In an isolated two-pool model, lit and shaded pools each contain 24 units of auxin. Six units move from lit to shaded without other fluxes. What is the new shaded-to-lit ratio?
  1. 30/18 = 1.67
  2. 24/24 = 1.00
  3. 18/30 = 0.60
  4. 36/12 = 3.00

Answer: A. The lit pool loses six to become eighteen, and the shaded pool gains six to become thirty. Total stays forty-eight. Reversing the ratio or assuming twice the specified transfer produces the incorrect alternatives.

39. Part C — A tagged PIN protein is abundant but a tagged construct does not rescue a PIN-deficient line. How should the localization image be treated?
  1. As definitive proof of normal transporter function
  2. As proof that no auxin is present
  3. As direct measurement of IAA concentration
  4. With caution because the tag or construct may impair normal protein function

Answer: D. Protein abundance and fluorescence do not establish biological activity. Failure of functional complementation raises concern that the construct is not a faithful reporter; neither hormone absence nor concentration follows from its image.

40. Part C — A response reporter is twice as bright on the shaded side. A student concludes that IAA concentration is precisely twice as high. What is the principal problem?
  1. Brightness cannot be spatially measured
  2. Reporter output need not be linearly proportional to free IAA
  3. Auxin never changes reporter expression
  4. The shaded side cannot contain auxin

Answer: B. Reporter output depends on signaling, kinetics and potentially nonlinear expression. A twofold fluorescence ratio is not a calibrated twofold concentration ratio; the other options deny known assay capabilities or make unsupported biological assertions.

41. Part C — A shoot segment is approximated as circular with sides separated by 1.5 mm. The shaded side length increases by 0.9 mm more than the lit side. What bend angle follows?
  1. 0.6 degrees
  2. 90 degrees
  3. 0.6 radians, about 34.4 degrees
  4. 1.67 radians

Answer: C. Using ΔL=dθ gives 0.9/1.5=0.6 rad, or 34.4°. The result is in radians before conversion. 1.67 reverses the ratio; 90° does not follow from the given dimensions.

42. Part C — Two genotypes have angles of 5° at one hour. At three hours, A reaches 39° and B remains at 6°, while WT reaches 40°. What conclusion is best?
  1. A is delayed under these conditions; B shows a persistent defect over the measured interval
  2. Both genotypes have identical response capacity
  3. Both are proven phot2 nulls
  4. A is incapable of cell growth

Answer: A. The later measurements separate delayed near-normal response from sustained impairment. An identical early endpoint is insufficient to determine genotype, growth capacity or final behavior.

43. Part C — After strong local blue illumination, a chloroplast moves rightward away from a lit patch. Which actin pattern is most consistent with the leading-edge model?
  1. All actin must disappear from the cell
  2. Actin always enriches on the lit side
  3. No actin change can occur in avoidance
  4. Local chloroplast-associated actin enriches at the right-hand advancing edge

Answer: D. The advancing edge is defined by movement, so a rightward avoidance trajectory is associated with the right edge. General actin disappearance and a fixed relationship to the lit side are not required.

44. Part C — A mutant retains ordinary cytoplasmic actin but lacks short chloroplast-associated filaments and shows abnormal positioning. What candidate function best fits?
  1. Phytochrome Pr/Pfr conversion
  2. A CHUP1-associated positioning defect
  3. Carbon fixation by Rubisco
  4. A general absence of actin protein from all cells

Answer: B. The phenotype specifically implicates local chloroplast positioning machinery, consistent with CHUP1 involvement. Normal general actin argues against total actin absence; phytochrome conversion and Rubisco activity are not direct explanations.

45. Part C — A leaf transmits 0.25 and reflects 0.15 of incident measuring light. Assuming a simple energy balance, what fraction is absorbed?
  1. 0.40
  2. 0.85
  3. 0.60
  4. 0.10

Answer: C. A=1−T−R=1−0.25−0.15=0.60. Adding the transmitted and reflected fractions gives the nonabsorbed 0.40; ignoring one term or subtracting them from each other is incorrect.

46. Part C — After low blue light, transmittance falls; after stronger blue light it rises in WT, but stays low in a mutant. Microscopy confirms retained accumulation and failed sustained avoidance. Which receptor defect is most plausible?
  1. phot2 loss
  2. Complete loss of both phototropins
  3. Universal abolition of FMN binding
  4. Loss of every chloroplast

Answer: A. The preserved low-light response with selectively impaired sustained avoidance is consistent with phot2 deficiency in the classic Arabidopsis model. Double loss or universal chromophore loss would predict a broader defect; microscopy excludes chloroplast absence.

47. Part C — A leaf shows F0=210 and Fm=700 after strong light. What is Fv/Fm?
  1. 0.30
  2. 0.49
  3. 3.33
  4. 0.70

Answer: D. Fv=700−210=490, and Fv/Fm=490/700=0.70. The 0.30 option is F0/Fm; 0.49 mistakes the variable fluorescence for a normalized ratio; 3.33 is Fm/F0.

48. Part C — A receptor double mutant and a guard-cell-specific signaling mutant both lack blue-light opening. Only the receptor double mutant also lacks normal chloroplast relocation. What conclusion is supported?
  1. Both mutants necessarily have identical molecular lesions
  2. The receptor is shared across outputs, whereas a downstream lesion may be tissue specific
  3. Chloroplast movement is caused by stomatal opening
  4. All blue light responses require one identical downstream chain

Answer: B. The phenotype pattern supports shared upstream reception and distinct downstream tissue pathways. It does not establish identical gene lesions or a causal dependence of chloroplast movement on pore opening.

49. Other — Assertion–reason: Assertion: An opaque coleoptile-tip cap can prevent phototropic curvature. Reason: A light-perceiving tip can provide signals influencing a separate elongation region. Choose the relationship.
  1. Both true, and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, but reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Answer: A. The classic experimental logic separates perception at the tip from growth farther down. Blocking the perception site can interrupt the signaling required for directional elongation. A transparent-cap control helps exclude mechanical interference.

50. Other — Assertion–reason: Assertion: High-light chloroplast avoidance directly dissipates excess excitation as heat in antenna complexes. Reason: Avoidance can reduce light interception by relocating chloroplasts.
  1. Both true and linked
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false

Answer: C. Avoidance modifies light interception, whereas nonphotochemical quenching dissipates excitation within the photosynthetic apparatus. The reason is true but describes a different mechanism from the assertion.

51. Other — State SET-style multi-statement item: I. LOV domains bind FMN. II. PIN3 can contribute to directional auxin flux. III. Every phototropin output is a form of differential shoot growth. IV. Phot2 is important for sustained high-light avoidance in Arabidopsis. Which are correct?
  1. I and III only
  2. II and III only
  3. III and IV only
  4. I, II and IV only

Answer: D. I, II and IV are supported by receptor chemistry, transport and mutant physiology. III is false because chloroplast relocation, leaf positioning and stomatal aperture involve different physical outputs.

52. Other — Experimental reasoning: A researcher counts 100 chloroplasts in one mutant leaf and 100 in one WT leaf, then claims 100 independent plant replicates per genotype. What is the main flaw?
  1. Chloroplast positions cannot be measured
  2. The independent experimental unit is not each chloroplast when organelles are nested within cells and plants
  3. Every chloroplast is an independent plant
  4. The sample must always contain exactly 50 organelles

Answer: B. The observations share cellular and plant environments. Many organelles characterize within-leaf behavior, but independent plants are needed to support genotype-level inference. The other alternatives misidentify the experimental unit or impose an arbitrary sample size.

Revision and mastery

Phototropins are sensory blue-light kinases. FMN-bound LOV1 and LOV2 domains precede the C-terminal serine/threonine kinase; the LOV2 cysteinyl–flavin photocycle and conformational coupling regulate catalysis. A lateral activation difference, appropriate signaling through NPH3/RPT2-related proteins, directional auxin transport and unequal shoot extension can generate phototropism. In mesophyll cells, partially redundant phototropins control accumulation, while phot2 is particularly important for sustained high-light avoidance. CHUP1-associated short actin filaments participate in the movement machinery. Guard-cell opening and leaf positioning use related receptors but tissue-specific output systems.

Equations to reconstruct

  • Photon energy E = hc/λ; compare photon flux at equal radiant power using wavelength.
  • Fluence = constant fluence rate × duration.
  • Two-state illustrative model: active fraction at equilibrium = kL/(kL+kd); dark recovery half-life = ln(2)/kd.
  • Circular-arc approximation: differential length ΔL = dθ, with θ in radians.
  • Optical energy balance: absorptance = 1 − reflectance − transmittance under the stated assumptions.
  • Dark-adapted PSII maximum efficiency estimate: Fv/Fm = (Fm − F0)/Fm.
