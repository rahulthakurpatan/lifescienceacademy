Light as directional information

A young shoot growing beside a window provides a striking example of a plant responding to the spatial distribution of light. The shoot may initially be almost vertical, but its axis gradually curves toward the brighter region. This change in orientation is not simply a consequence of photosynthesis occurring faster on one side. A seedling grown in darkness, with very little functional photosynthetic machinery in its elongating tissues, can already detect unilateral blue light. The ability to turn therefore depends on a sensory mechanism that converts an external physical cue into an internal difference in growth.

This distinction between using light and sensing light is essential. A photosystem uses excitation to initiate electron transfer that supports energy conversion. A sensory photoreceptor instead changes its biochemical activity after absorbing light. The resulting signal may alter the activity of another protein, the distribution of a membrane transporter, the rate of expansion of a cell, or the position of an organelle. Light provides information about the surroundings even when the eventual movement requires chemical energy obtained by metabolism.

Phototropism is a change in growth direction determined by the direction of incident light. A shoot exhibiting positive phototropism grows toward the source; a negatively phototropic organ grows away. Because this definition concerns the relation between stimulus direction and response direction, not the identity of the pigment, a bending response cannot by itself establish that a particular photoreceptor is involved. Temperature gradients, gravity and differences in developmental competence can all modify the observed curvature. Experimental controls must separate these influences.

A tropism is also distinct from a nastic movement. For example, a leaf may open after illumination, but if the direction of opening is determined by its anatomy rather than by the direction from which the light arrives, the response is photonastic rather than phototropic. A circadian leaf movement can continue with an approximately daily period under constant environmental conditions. Chloroplast relocation is a further category: individual organelles move within a cell without requiring a differential increase in the lengths of the two sides of an entire shoot. The same family of photoreceptors can participate in different kinds of outputs without making those outputs biologically equivalent.

The chapter develops two linked problems. First, a cell must detect light using a molecule whose activity can change reversibly. Second, the plant must use the information about where and how intensely the light is arriving. The first problem is solved by the flavin-binding sensory domains of phototropins. The second involves networks of signaling proteins and transport systems that operate differently in elongating shoots and in chloroplast-containing cells.

From the coleoptile experiment to the receptor

Classical phototropism experiments used the coleoptile, the protective sheath that surrounds the emerging shoot of a grass seedling. Coleoptiles are particularly convenient because their geometry makes curvature readily visible and their tip can be treated separately from their growing base. In the nineteenth century Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin observed that covering or removing the tip could prevent normal curvature toward unilateral illumination. An uncovered tip could support bending in a region farther down the organ, even when that basal region was shielded from direct light. The logic of the result is that the perception of light and the execution of differential growth can occur in spatially separated regions.

An opaque tip cap intercepts photons before they reach the underlying tissue, but it can also exert physical pressure, alter humidity or interfere with normal development. A transparent cap of the same shape provides an important comparison. Removal of the tip is more disruptive because it simultaneously removes a source of growth signals and introduces a wound. A single tip-removal result is therefore less informative than a set of complementary manipulations that preserve ordinary elongation while changing the opportunity for light perception. The classic studies support the existence of communication between the tip and the elongation region; they did not identify a specific receptor protein.

The subsequent identification of auxin and investigations of growth-promoting substances from coleoptile tips supplied a plausible means for this communication. If growth-promoting activity becomes stronger on one side of the elongation region, cells on that side can extend faster. For a positively phototropic shoot, the shaded side generally lengthens more than the lit side. Consequently it forms the outer, longer arc of the bend. The tip and the growing region are parts of a connected system, but the hormone distribution is not identical to the original photon distribution.

Molecular genetics made it possible to look upstream of hormone action. One strategy was to screen Arabidopsis seedlings for individuals that continued to elongate but failed to bend normally under unilateral blue light. Mutants of this sort distinguish a defect in sensory signaling from a complete failure of cell growth. Identification of the NPH1 gene and its light-responsive protein connected the bending phenotype with a receptor having flavin-binding domains and a protein kinase region. NPH1 is the historical name associated with Arabidopsis phototropin 1. A related receptor, phototropin 2, revealed that the light response could be distributed across members of a receptor family rather than controlled by one molecule in every intensity range.

A genetic result alone establishes that the corresponding gene is needed for an output under the tested conditions, not necessarily that its product absorbs the initiating photon. Direct light-dependent changes in the isolated protein, the presence of a bound light-absorbing cofactor and the disruption of signaling by alterations to the sensory domains supply additional evidence for the receptor's identity. Rescue of the mutant phenotype by an appropriate wild-type gene further links the molecular lesion to the physiological response. Together these independent levels of evidence are stronger than an action spectrum or a mutant phenotype considered separately.

Phototropin architecture and chromophore chemistry

Arabidopsis thaliana possesses two principal phototropins, phot1 and phot2. Both are blue-light-responsive serine/threonine protein kinases. Their architecture is modular: the amino-terminal sensory region contains two LOV domains, named LOV1 and LOV2, and the carboxyl-terminal portion contains the catalytic kinase domain. The term LOV derives from light, oxygen or voltage, the name of a family of sensory modules; in plant phototropins these domains are adapted for light sensing. A domain is a structurally organized part of a protein that can contribute a distinguishable function within the full-length molecule.

Each LOV domain binds a flavin mononucleotide molecule, abbreviated FMN. FMN is derived from riboflavin and contains the conjugated isoalloxazine ring system that can absorb blue and near-ultraviolet light. The protein portion provides the binding pocket and the environment that governs the excited chromophore's subsequent reactions. The bound FMN is a chromophore rather than an ordinary kinase substrate: its role is to receive photons and initiate a structural transition. Phototropins do not use FMN as a permanent source of phosphate for the reactions they catalyze.

A protein that contains two chromophores is not necessarily a detector that requires two photons at exactly the same instant. LOV1 and LOV2 are individually capable of photochemistry, and their contributions to the function of the intact protein differ. In the common activation model, LOV2 is the major regulatory switch coupling illumination to kinase activation. LOV1 can modulate sensitivity, receptor association or signaling properties, depending on the receptor and response examined. Mutations that inactivate one domain therefore need not produce the same physiological effect as complete removal of the whole phototropin.

The receptor is often associated with the cytoplasmic surface of the plasma membrane. This location is compatible with interactions with signaling proteins and with responses involving membrane transporter localization. It is not equivalent to the architecture of a conventional receptor that spans the membrane and binds an extracellular soluble ligand. Light can reach the internal chromophore directly, and the protein changes activity as a result. Some receptor populations change their cellular localization following illumination, so membrane association should not be described as an irreversible attachment.

The catalytic region uses ATP and transfers its terminal phosphate to suitable hydroxyl-containing amino-acid residues. Its activity is regulated by the sensory region; the presence of a kinase domain does not imply that it is fully active in darkness. A useful structural experiment is to compare an intact wild-type protein with one carrying a light-insensitive LOV2 and another carrying a catalytically defective kinase domain. In principle, the first mutant disrupts the coupling of photon perception to catalysis, while the second can still undergo chromophore photochemistry but fails at phosphate transfer. If both produce a similar growth phenotype, molecular measurements are required to identify the distinct failures.

LOV photochemistry and activation of kinase signaling

In the dark-adapted state, FMN is held noncovalently in the LOV binding pocket. Absorption of an appropriate photon promotes the flavin into an electronically excited state. A transient triplet state can participate in the chemical reaction that forms a bond between a conserved cysteine side chain and the C4a position of the flavin ring. The resulting cysteinyl-flavin adduct represents a light-generated state of the sensory domain. It is not an irreversible lesion in the protein; thermal reactions can return the chromophore and cysteine to their dark-adapted configuration.

The photochemical event changes interactions within the domain. In the LOV2 module the structural change can weaken interactions with the adjacent J-alpha helix. This helix is an important component of the coupling between the sensory region and the kinase. Light-induced changes in the helix and related protein interfaces relieve inhibition of the catalytic domain. The general causal sequence is thus photon absorption, chromophore reaction, structural rearrangement and kinase activation. These are distinct events and should be measured by different experimental readouts when testing the model.

Autophosphorylation occurs when a kinase transfers phosphate to residues within the same protein or to a receptor molecule of the same type. Increased phototropin phosphorylation after blue light is a useful biochemical readout of activation. It must not be confused with the formation of the FMN–cysteine bond, because the latter is a photochemical reaction without ATP-dependent phosphate transfer. A kinase inhibitor can in principle suppress phosphorylation while leaving the upstream optical change intact. Conversely a light-insensitive chromophore pocket can abolish the usual light-induced rise in catalytic activity without destroying the kinase's amino-acid sequence.

Dark recovery helps explain why a phototropin reports recent light exposure rather than accumulating a permanent record of all past illumination. Consider a simplified population of receptor molecules with a light-dependent activation rate kL and a dark recovery rate kd, both measured in inverse seconds. Let a be the fraction in the activated sensory state. The rate of change is kL(1 − a) − kd a. Under constant light, the population reaches a steady state when the forward and reverse rates balance, giving a = kL/(kL + kd). This is an illustrative two-state model, not a fitted physiological phototropin response curve. The model predicts that increasing irradiance increases the active fraction until the available receptors approach saturation.

If illumination ends, kL becomes zero in this simplified model. The remaining activated fraction then decreases exponentially with a half-life of ln(2)/kd. For an illustrative recovery rate of 0.02 per second, the half-life is approximately 34.7 seconds. A hypothetical receptor with a recovery rate of 0.01 per second has twice that half-life. If both receptors are exposed to an identical short pulse that produces the same initial active fraction, the slower recovering receptor remains activated longer. However, prolonged biochemical activation does not guarantee proportionally greater bending because downstream pathways can adapt, saturate or be limited by available growth.

Photon fluence rate is the number of photons delivered per area per time, often expressed in micromoles of photons per square meter per second in plant physiology. Fluence is the accumulated number per area. A constant pulse of 2 micromoles per square meter per second for 30 seconds delivers 60 micromoles per square meter. A pulse of 6 for 10 seconds delivers the same fluence. Equal fluence does not necessarily yield equal receptor activation because recovery and downstream signaling occur during illumination. This is why an experiment should report both the intensity and the duration of a light treatment rather than only a total dose.

A related distinction concerns equal radiant energy and equal photon number. The energy of one photon is hc divided by wavelength, where h is Planck's constant and c is the speed of light in vacuum. Blue photons have greater individual energy than red photons. Consequently equal radiant power at two wavelengths does not imply equal photon flux. Even at equal photon flux, a receptor can respond much more strongly to one wavelength because of its absorption spectrum and the efficiency of productive photochemistry. Greater photon energy by itself is not sufficient to predict phototropin activation.

Converting asymmetric activation into growth

A single phototropin molecule cannot measure a macroscopic angle between a shoot and a lamp. Direction is represented in the tissue by the unequal activation of receptor populations in different positions. In a unilateral illumination experiment, cells near the illuminated side can encounter a higher local photon flux than cells at corresponding positions on the opposite side. Absorption and scattering by tissue create a spatial pattern that is translated into a spatial pattern of biochemical activity. Uniform illumination may produce strong receptor activation everywhere while providing little left–right difference to orient a shoot.

NON-PHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3, or NPH3, is an important signaling component that interacts with phototropin pathways. ROOT PHOTOTROPISM 2, RPT2, belongs to the related NPH3/RPT2-like family and contributes to appropriate phototropic signaling over a range of light conditions. These proteins help connect the activation of the photoreceptor with downstream responses. Their regulatory properties include changes in phosphorylation and association or localization, but the pathway should not be reduced to a universal linear series in which every downstream protein is directly phosphorylated by the previous one.

The name NPH3 is derived from the mutant phenotype, not from a particular enzymatic reaction. A mutant lacking functional NPH3 can retain phototropin protein and some upstream biochemical activation but show defective hypocotyl curvature. This observation demonstrates why measuring phosphorylated receptor is not enough to claim the whole signaling pathway has worked. RPT2 can alter response sensitivity and contributes to the behavior of phototropic pathways at different irradiances. Its function is particularly useful when considering why the magnitude and duration of the output need not be determined by receptor occupancy alone.

The PIN family of auxin efflux carriers provides a mechanism for establishing directional auxin transport. The polarity of a transporter refers to its preferential distribution on one region of the cell membrane. A cell can therefore export auxin more effectively toward one neighbor than toward another even if its internal concentration is not strongly asymmetric. Changes in PIN membrane trafficking can redirect the net movement of the hormone across a tissue. PIN3-mediated transport is implicated in the lateral auxin redistribution of Arabidopsis hypocotyl phototropism, although other transporters and regulatory processes also participate under particular conditions.

The hormone redistribution model predicts an increase in auxin response on the shaded side of a positively phototropic shoot. Auxin can promote wall loosening and cell extension in suitable shoot tissues; faster length increase on the shaded side generates curvature toward the lit side. A fluorescent transcriptional auxin-response reporter may provide evidence for unequal signaling, but its intensity is not a direct chemical measurement of free IAA concentration. The response depends on hormone concentration, sensitivity, transcription, fluorophore maturation and degradation. It is consequently important to distinguish a reporter gradient from a calibrated hormone concentration gradient.

An inhibitor of auxin transport may abolish curvature even when a receptor still absorbs blue light and undergoes phosphorylation. This result places the transport-dependent process somewhere in the causal chain leading to growth, provided the inhibitor has not generally killed the tissue or suppressed every form of elongation. A control showing that the treated tissue can still extend in response to another suitable stimulus strengthens the interpretation. The strongest demonstration uses complementary approaches: genetic alterations, spatial reporters, biochemical measurements and phenotypic rescue.

The mechanics of curvature can be understood using a simple geometric approximation. Imagine an elongating strip of a shoot with two sides separated by a distance d. If the curved segment has an angle θ in radians and each side follows a circular arc, the difference between the outer and inner arc lengths is dθ. For a separation of 0.8 mm and a measured length difference of 0.4 mm, the predicted bend is 0.5 radian, or approximately 28.6 degrees. The calculation assumes a uniform circular bend and approximately constant separation of the two sides. Real tissues can change diameter, bend nonuniformly or have elastic constraints, so this expression is a teaching model rather than a universal measurement protocol.

Roots need not show the same growth response to auxin as shoots. Depending on concentration, tissue and developmental context, an increase in auxin can inhibit root elongation. The distribution of the signal and the growth response to that distribution jointly determine the direction of bending. Accordingly, a student should not transfer the simple positive-shoot rule to every root or infer that all organs turn toward blue light. Gravity and other directional cues further complicate root responses.

Phot1 and phot2 across different light regimes

Phot1 contributes strongly to phototropism under weak directional blue light, whereas phot2 provides overlapping function especially as light intensity rises. This relationship is demonstrated by comparing single and double loss-of-function mutants with wild type under the same conditions. At an appropriate low fluence rate, the loss of phot1 causes a larger bending defect than the loss of phot2. Under stronger light, the remaining phot2 in a phot1 mutant can support a response. Removing both receptors eliminates much of the canonical phototropin-dependent response. Exact response magnitudes and light thresholds depend on the plant material and assay.

Redundancy does not mean the receptors have identical biochemical properties or are expressed at identical levels in every tissue. Partial redundancy means that one gene product can support a function that can also be performed by another gene product under at least some conditions. A mutant phenotype may be absent in an assay that allows compensation yet pronounced in a different assay. For example, testing only high irradiance can obscure a phot1-dependent weak-light phenotype. Testing only low irradiance can obscure a contribution from phot2.

A useful experimental design combines four genotypes with at least three light conditions: darkness, weak unilateral blue light and stronger unilateral blue light. Dark controls establish the baseline orientation of each genotype. Seedlings should be age-matched and initially aligned. The lamp geometry and gravity vector must be held constant or randomized in a controlled way. Measuring the time course of curvature as well as its final angle distinguishes a delay in signaling from a persistent inability to bend. A second assay of overall elongation helps ensure that a smaller curvature is not merely a consequence of a stunted hypocotyl.

The distinction between blue-light sensing and other photoreceptor pathways also matters. Cryptochromes bind flavin but are not LOV-domain serine/threonine kinases of the phototropin type. Their major roles include transcriptional regulation associated with de-etiolation and developmental timing. Phytochromes instead contain a bilin chromophore and are particularly important for red and far-red responses. Other photoreceptors can modulate phototropic sensitivity, so an absence of a strong response to a red-only beam does not imply that red light has no influence over subsequent blue-light behavior.

Intracellular chloroplast relocation

Within a mature leaf, chloroplasts are positioned near the walls of mesophyll cells rather than floating randomly throughout the entire cell volume. Their location affects how much light they intercept and how strongly they shade one another. Under weak directional light, increasing the projected chloroplast area perpendicular to the beam can increase interception. Under intense light, decreasing this projected area can reduce exposure to excess excitation. A plant can therefore adjust its optical geometry without growing a new leaf.

In the accumulation response, chloroplasts move toward illuminated cell surfaces or illuminated regions. Under high-intensity blue light, chloroplasts undergo avoidance and relocate away from strongly irradiated regions, often occupying anticlinal walls that are more nearly parallel to the incident light in the standard leaf-illumination arrangement. Periclinal walls run approximately parallel to the leaf surface; anticlinal walls run across the leaf thickness. These anatomical terms describe orientation relative to the organ surface, not the chemical composition of the walls.

Both phot1 and phot2 can contribute to the Arabidopsis accumulation response. Phot2 has the principal role in sustaining the classic high-light avoidance response. Consequently a phot2 mutant can retain low-light accumulation through phot1 but fail to execute normal sustained avoidance under conditions in which wild-type chloroplasts retreat. A phot1 phot2 double mutant lacks normal phototropin-controlled movement. Other species and experimental conditions can show different wavelength dependencies or transient movements; the Arabidopsis genetic model should not be presented as an exhaustive description of all photosynthetic eukaryotes.

CHUP1, CHLOROPLAST UNUSUAL POSITIONING 1, is a protein associated with the chloroplast outer envelope and the positioning machinery that connects chloroplasts with the cell periphery. Chloroplast-associated actin filaments reorganize during movement. The spatial organization of these actin structures is important for directed relocation. This process is not equivalent to the long-distance transport of auxin in a growing hypocotyl. A defective positioning protein can impair chloroplast relocation even if the upstream phototropin perceives light normally.

The physiological significance can be studied by exposing wild type and an avoidance-defective mutant to strong light and assessing chlorophyll fluorescence or photosynthetic performance before and after exposure. A greater decline in maximum photosystem II efficiency in the mutant is consistent with the idea that avoidance helps reduce damage. However, it is necessary to control for baseline photosynthetic differences, differences in leaf pigment content and other protective pathways. A mutant's increased susceptibility does not prove that chloroplast relocation is the only mechanism of protection.

In a leaf transmittance assay, accumulation typically causes more incoming measuring light to be intercepted by chloroplasts, decreasing transmittance. Avoidance reduces projected interception and commonly increases transmittance. Optical measurements offer a useful population-level readout, while time-lapse microscopy provides direct spatial evidence about chloroplast position. Combining both measurements makes it easier to distinguish chloroplast movement from a change in pigment abundance or tissue optics.

Chloroplast avoidance and nonphotochemical quenching address related physiological problems by different means. Avoidance adjusts where the organelles are positioned and thus how much incident light they intercept. Nonphotochemical quenching adjusts the dissipation of absorbed excitation within the photosynthetic apparatus. The two processes can operate together. Neither process completely eliminates the danger of photoinhibition, and avoiding excessive exposure entails a tradeoff if light subsequently becomes limiting.

Phototropins also participate in leaf flattening, leaf positioning and the blue-light-dependent opening of stomata. These responses improve the conditions for photosynthesis through different tissues and mechanisms. In guard cells the signal leads to regulation of plasma membrane proton pumps and ion transport; changes in turgor alter pore aperture. This output illustrates the versatility of the same receptor family. Its detailed osmotic and electrical mechanism belongs to the following chapter on stomatal movement.

Interpreting experiments and model limitations

The strongest reasoning begins by specifying the level at which each measurement is made. Absorbance spectroscopy can reveal a chromophore transition. An immunoblot or mobility shift can report phosphorylation. A reporter for auxin-responsive transcription can reveal a spatial pattern of hormone response. Microscopy can reveal changes in transporter polarity or chloroplast location. Whole-organ curvature is a final integrated outcome. A failure in an early readout is compatible with failure of all later readouts, while normal early readouts together with defective later ones point toward downstream defects.

A complementation experiment can test whether a particular phototropin domain is necessary for signaling. Introduce the wild-type receptor and a version with altered LOV2 photochemistry separately into a receptor-deficient background. Match their expression as closely as possible and verify protein accumulation. If wild type restores blue-light curvature but the altered version does not, the experiment supports a necessary role for the intact sensory module. If the mutant protein is unstable, however, its inability to restore curvature cannot be assigned solely to failure of photochemistry. Measuring receptor accumulation is therefore an essential control.

A related experiment compares illumination of a narrow region of a leaf with uniform illumination at the same local fluence rate. A microscopic time course can distinguish movement into the illuminated region from movement away. At weak light a local accumulation pattern is expected; at strong light a phot2-dependent avoidance pattern is expected in a suitable wild-type Arabidopsis preparation. The spatial distribution of the stimulus must be recorded, because the word strong alone does not specify which parts of a cell received light. The imaging light must also be chosen so that it does not inadvertently induce or modify the movement under investigation.

Mechanistic models are useful when they generate distinguishable predictions. A model in which blue light changes auxin synthesis only on one side and a model in which it changes lateral auxin transport can both predict a final reporter asymmetry. Measuring transporter distribution and using perturbations of directional transport provide ways to discriminate between these explanations. It is possible that more than one process contributes, so a result supporting transport need not rule out every change in metabolism. The exam-level conclusion should track what the experiment directly establishes rather than claiming a stronger result than the measurements permit.

Measuring a blue-light sensory response

When an organism responds to illumination, the wavelengths that cause the strongest response provide a useful clue about its sensory apparatus. An absorption spectrum describes the amount of light absorbed by a chemical species as a function of wavelength. An action spectrum instead describes the effectiveness of different wavelengths in producing a chosen biological outcome. In a phototropism assay, that outcome might be a particular bend angle measured after a standardized recovery interval. The distinction matters because several events intervene between absorption of a photon and the measured angle. A wavelength that efficiently produces the initial photochemical state may not yield the largest final curvature if the experiment also activates a second signaling pathway that modifies the growth response.

The experimental definition of effectiveness must be chosen before the measurement is made. Suppose a fixed dose of light produces nearly maximum curvature over a broad band of wavelengths. A graph of angle against wavelength will then have a flat summit, even if the underlying absorption spectrum has well-defined peaks. The flat summit might indicate saturation of a downstream response rather than broad absorption by the receptor. Conversely, choosing a dose that is too weak may produce angles too small to distinguish from spontaneous curvature. One approach is to measure the light dose needed to produce a modest, fixed response at each wavelength. The reciprocal of that dose provides a measure of relative effectiveness. This approach requires a response that changes monotonically over the selected dose range.

A colored filter placed in front of a lamp does not automatically create a spectrally controlled stimulus. Filters can transmit small quantities of unintended wavelengths, and different filters may differ greatly in total transmission. A student who compares a dim blue beam with a bright red beam cannot determine whether the difference in curvature reflects wavelength or photon flux. An appropriate instrument or a calibrated set of light sources provides the relevant photon flux at the level of the seedling. To test direction separately from wavelength, the beam should be delivered from the same angle and through a comparable illumination geometry for every wavelength.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which equally oriented seedlings receive monochromatic light at three wavelengths. A small fixed bend of ten degrees requires 8 micromoles of photons per square meter at 450 nm, 40 micromoles per square meter at 520 nm, and more than 160 micromoles per square meter at 660 nm. Dividing the smallest required dose by each measured dose yields relative sensitivities of 1, 0.2 and less than 0.05, respectively. The inequality is important: the third treatment did not establish the actual dose needed for ten degrees, so the student cannot invent an exact sensitivity at 660 nm. The result is compatible with a blue-light receptor, but a matching spectral preference alone would not distinguish phototropin from all other flavin-containing receptors.

An action spectrum can be interpreted more confidently when paired with a receptor mutant. Suppose the normal seedlings respond most efficiently in blue light and the phot1 phot2 mutant shows a greatly diminished response across this range while maintaining comparable growth potential. Complementation with a functional phototropin restores a response with the expected spectral preference. The evidence now connects the receptor family with the measured output. Even so, the experiment does not show that every absorbed blue photon is captured by phototropin; chlorophyll, flavoproteins and other cellular components also absorb wavelengths in this region.

The relation between wavelength and photon energy can be calculated without introducing a separate course in photophysics. With Planck's constant approximately 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule seconds, the speed of light approximately 3.00 × 10⁸ meters per second, and a wavelength of 450 × 10⁻⁹ meters, one photon has an energy of about 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. Multiplying by Avogadro's constant gives roughly 266 kilojoules per mole of photons. At 660 nm the corresponding value is about 181 kilojoules per mole. Thus an energy-matched red beam contains about 1.47 times as many photons as the blue beam. This is a comparison of physical input, not a statement about how many receptor molecules are activated. The quantum yield of a photochemical step is an additional factor.

An experiment that aims to relate response to wavelength should usually distinguish the light used to stimulate the tissue from the light used to observe it. Illuminating dark-grown seedlings repeatedly with a bright inspection lamp can activate sensory pathways before the intended pulse. A supposedly dark control can therefore become a pretreated control without the experimenter noticing. Imaging with appropriate nonstimulating conditions, standardizing handling time and including an unilluminated group help address this problem. The same concern applies to microscopic observations of moving chloroplasts.

Spectral response is also inseparable from the initial biological condition. A dark-grown seedling and a green seedling differ in tissue optics, elongation capacity and signaling state. A green leaf contains abundant photosynthetic pigments that alter the spectrum reaching photoreceptors deeper in the tissue. Accordingly, a difference between two action spectra is not automatically evidence for a change in the chromophore molecule. The position and state of the receptor, the penetration of light and the choice of output all matter. Exam questions often become straightforward once these different explanatory levels are separated.

Testing the sensor-to-kinase coupling

It is useful to distinguish a protein's amino-acid sequence, its three-dimensional conformation and its biochemical activity. A mutation can leave the overall fold largely intact but change the contact between two domains. A protein that binds FMN normally might still fail to activate its kinase if a regulatory interface cannot rearrange. Conversely, a receptor might lose normal dark-state inhibition while remaining catalytically capable. These possibilities are more informative than a simple classification into light-responsive and light-insensitive proteins because they reveal distinct functions of the modular receptor.

One type of molecular investigation compares the optical behavior of an isolated LOV domain with that of free FMN. The spectrum of the bound chromophore reflects the environment created by the surrounding amino acids. After blue-light excitation, time-dependent spectral changes reveal formation and decay of new photochemical states. Observing a reversible optical transition establishes that the domain is capable of sensing light at the molecular level. However, an isolated LOV domain contains no catalytic kinase region. The experiment cannot, on its own, demonstrate that phosphate transfer occurs in the full-length receptor. It addresses the first part of the proposed causal chain.

To establish coupling, one may compare a full-length receptor in dark-adapted and illuminated states using a phosphorylation assay. ATP supplies the transferable phosphate; the receptor or another suitable protein can act as substrate. If the reaction increases after illumination, the result provides evidence that the catalytic state is regulated by light. A control lacking active kinase distinguishes enzymatic incorporation of phosphate from unrelated changes in the sample. A control lacking the normal LOV photochemical response tests whether kinase activation depends on the sensory region. It is important to compare equal amounts of protein, because doubling receptor abundance can increase total phosphate incorporation without increasing activity per molecule.

The LOV2–J-alpha interface provides an especially instructive perturbation. Harper, Christie and Gardner (2004) described substitutions disrupting the contact between the sensory domain and its associated helix; when introduced into intact phototropin, selected substitutions promoted kinase activity even without blue-light exposure. This supports a model in which the dark-state interface restrains catalysis. In this type of reasoning, an amino-acid change that bypasses normal stimulus dependence is valuable because it identifies an inhibitory interaction rather than merely demonstrating that some region is required. A light-induced structural rearrangement may release a restraint instead of creating an enzyme active site from nothing.

Consider an illustrative set of normalized biochemical activities for four constructs, using the illuminated wild-type protein as one arbitrary activity unit. Wild type gives 0.08 in darkness and 1.00 after illumination; a chromophore-reaction-deficient construct gives 0.06 and 0.07; a catalytically inactive construct gives 0.01 and 0.01; a regulatory-interface mutant gives 0.72 and 0.80. These values are invented for reasoning practice and are not reported measurements from the Harper study. The last construct provides evidence for altered basal regulation. Its smaller fold induction in light is not evidence of low catalytic capacity: much of the activation is present before illumination. A student who computes only light-to-dark ratios could misinterpret this pattern.

Additional measurements would clarify the meaning of each row. If the chromophore-reaction-deficient receptor is properly folded, abundant and able to bind ATP, failure of light-induced activity is consistent with disruption of the upstream sensory step. If it fails to bind FMN at all, the explanation differs from a receptor that binds FMN but fails to form the light-generated covalent adduct. Likewise, the catalytic mutant may have normal optical transitions. Optical and enzymatic measurements are therefore complementary. The appropriate next experiment depends on the particular molecular defect rather than on the fact that all of these mutants might show abnormal growth.

Autophosphorylation is not a single uniform modification. Proteins often carry several phosphorylatable residues, and the functions of those residues may differ. A mutation that prevents one regulatory phosphorylation can impair signaling without eliminating every light-associated change in receptor phosphorylation. A bulk band shift on a gel summarizes changes in the mobility of a protein population and does not necessarily identify which individual residues were modified. The combination of mutational analysis and functional readouts distinguishes regulatory sites from phosphorylation events that are incidental to a particular physiological output.

A normal receptor kinase need not remain maximally active forever after illumination. Sensory recovery, protein phosphatases and interactions with other signaling components can all contribute to resetting the system. When a tissue moves from shade into sun, its prior illumination may affect its response to a new stimulus. Molecular recovery and physiological adaptation must therefore be distinguished. The first refers to return of a molecular state toward its initial condition; the second refers to the history-dependent change in the responsiveness of a biological system. A receptor may recover its dark absorbance while the tissue retains a downstream adaptation.

An informative experiment involving two light pulses separates these possibilities. Deliver an initial pulse and then deliver an identical second pulse after different dark intervals. Measure a rapid receptor-associated signal immediately after the second pulse and a slower physiological response afterward. If the biochemical signal recovers more quickly than the physiological response, some persistence exists beyond the receptor's sensory photocycle. If both recover on similar timescales, receptor recovery is a plausible contributing limitation, but coincidence of timescales does not establish causation. The direction of the second light pulse can also be reversed to test whether previous stimulation has altered spatial responsiveness.

Such experiments illustrate why studying a photoreceptor as a light-activated kinase is more informative than simply memorizing its domain names. Its modular architecture permits separate manipulation of photon sensing, regulatory coupling, catalysis and interactions with downstream partners. These experimentally separable stages provide a framework for interpreting many apparently conflicting mutant phenotypes.

From membrane polarity to a lateral signal

To understand the directional transport of auxin, imagine a row of adjacent cells extending from the illuminated side of a hypocotyl toward the shaded side. Each cell has several membrane faces through which a small molecule may enter or leave. If exit is equally probable through every face, a population of molecules may spread but will not exhibit persistent net transport in one direction in a symmetrical environment. If exit carriers become enriched on one face, the probabilities become unequal. The tissue can sustain a net flux even without requiring a new localized source of the molecule. The location of a transporter is thus as important as its abundance.

The term polar auxin transport is sometimes misunderstood as a description of the molecular polarity of auxin itself. Here polarity primarily refers to the directionality generated by transport machinery organized across cells. A transporter embedded in the outward-facing side of a cell exports hormone toward the neighboring space on that side. If the same protein is relocated to an inward-facing membrane, the direction of export changes. A tissue-level pattern can emerge from coordinated changes in multiple cell layers. Such changes provide a means by which a spatial difference in phototropin activation could be converted into a different spatial pattern of growth.

Ding and colleagues' 2011 analysis of Arabidopsis hypocotyl phototropism connected light-dependent PIN3 localization with the redistribution of auxin signaling across the organ. The important experimental insight is not that every auxin carrier always moves toward the shaded side. Instead, the location of specific carriers within particular cell types changes in a manner compatible with redirected hormone flow. In a cross section, an inward-facing surface of a cell on the illuminated side points toward the center of the organ. The orientation of the corresponding surface on the other side is different. Interpreting microscopy therefore requires a map of the tissue's geometry, not just an observation that a fluorescent membrane signal becomes asymmetric.

Consider two otherwise identical hypocotyls. In one, PIN3 becomes polarized after unilateral blue light; in the other, PIN3 protein is present at the same total abundance but cannot undergo normal localization changes. It is possible for both to show an indistinguishable protein band on a whole-organ immunoblot, yet have different phototropic behavior. This example illustrates the difference between protein amount and protein distribution. It also explains why a bulk expression measurement cannot replace cell-specific localization analysis in a problem about directional growth.

A fluorescent fusion protein is often useful for determining transporter location. However, its presence must be interpreted carefully. The fluorescent tag could alter the transporter's ability to move through membrane trafficking pathways or to transport its substrate. If the fusion protein restores normal function in an otherwise deficient plant, it is more likely to provide a physiologically meaningful localization pattern. An apparent movement of a fluorescent signal could also result from changes in cell geometry or imaging conditions. Comparison of the signal at defined membrane faces, with appropriate normalization and controls, is more convincing than an unquantified image.

Auxin response reporters address a complementary question. In a transcription-based reporter, a regulatory DNA sequence drives the production of a fluorescent or enzymatic signal in cells responding to auxin. Reporter activity reflects an integrated consequence of signaling and transcription. It may become visible only after protein synthesis or maturation. A reporter designed around hormone-dependent degradation of a sensor has a different time dependence and may show an inverse relation between signal intensity and hormone exposure. A student should read the definition of the reporter before interpreting whether a brighter region has higher or lower auxin signaling.

A useful model of two interacting tissue compartments treats the illuminated side and shaded side as separate pools of free hormone. Let the amounts be A and B, measured in arbitrary but consistent units. A net lateral flux J from the illuminated pool to the shaded pool removes J units per time from A and adds J units per time to B. If there is no synthesis, degradation or longitudinal transport during the selected interval, the sum A + B is conserved. Starting from A = B = 20 units, a redistribution of 5 units yields A = 15 and B = 25. The total remains 40 while the shaded-to-lit amount ratio rises to 25/15, or about 1.67. Unequal distribution does not require an increase in total auxin.

Now consider a different experiment in which both sides start at 20 units, but the shaded side synthesizes 5 additional units while the lit side remains unchanged. The resulting amounts are 20 and 25, giving a total of 45 and a ratio of 1.25. Both models create a difference of some kind, but they have different consequences for the total pool and the source of the asymmetry. This simplified example clarifies why evidence for a lateral gradient alone cannot determine whether redistribution, synthesis, degradation or longitudinal transport was responsible. Actual tissues have simultaneous fluxes and metabolism, so conservation of the two measured pools is an assumption of the model, not a general biological law.

The rate of cell elongation depends on the cell's mechanical properties as well as its hormone response. Auxin-responsive growth involves regulation of cell-wall extensibility and the capacity for water uptake under suitable conditions. A cell that has completed elongation cannot necessarily respond to a new auxin distribution by becoming substantially longer. This explains why the region that detects the light and the region that produces visible bending need not be identical. If an experiment demonstrates a reporter asymmetry in a mature region without curvature, a defect in mechanical competence is one possible explanation; it does not establish that photoreceptor activation failed.

Growth in the outer side of the curved segment is an accumulated outcome. If the rates on the lit and shaded sides are initially different for ten minutes and then become equal, an existing difference in length can remain. The angle does not instantly return to zero when the signaling difference disappears. A second stimulus arriving from the opposite direction could create a new growth asymmetry that gradually modifies the previous bend. Thus time-integrated curvature is not an instantaneous assay of receptor activation. Direct comparison of two time courses, one measuring a signaling marker and another measuring curvature, is more useful than expecting the curves to match exactly.

The existence of a hormone transport mechanism does not remove the need for upstream spatial perception. Applying the same auxin concentration to both sides of an organ may restore general growth but not directionality. Applying growth-promoting activity to one side may generate curvature even in a receptor-deficient plant, provided downstream growth machinery is intact. This is a form of pathway bypass, not proof that the mutant's endogenous photoreceptor has been repaired. Bypass experiments are especially valuable for placing a perturbation upstream or downstream of a physiological effector.

Light history, directional sensitivity and response dynamics

In natural conditions the light environment is rarely a constant beam delivered to a motionless seedling. Leaves above a young plant may shift with the wind, exposing it to short intervals of brighter light. The sun's position changes over hours, and the illuminated surface of an elongating shoot changes as the shoot bends. A useful sensory system must therefore respond to both spatial contrast and recent light history. If every momentary fluctuation produced an irreversible large change in growth, the plant might expend resources turning in response to brief stimuli that do not predict future light availability.

Classic phototropism studies distinguish pulse-induced curvature from responses to prolonged illumination. A pulse can initiate a change that becomes visible after the light has been removed. Continuous stimulation, in contrast, provides new spatial information while growth proceeds. In etiolated seedlings, different dose regimes can elicit different forms of positive phototropism. A response curve can be nonmonotonic across these regimes, because the processes governing sensitivity and adaptation are not captured by a single saturating receptor occupancy curve. Terms such as first positive and second positive phototropism refer to experimentally distinguished response regimes, not simply to phot1 and phot2 respectively.

The difference between a transient and a sustained response can be understood by comparing two hypothetical readouts. Suppose receptor activation rises to a plateau within a minute of illumination, while curvature increases only after a ten-minute delay. This delay is compatible with downstream signaling, auxin transport and the accumulation of differential extension. If the curvature rate then declines despite a stable stimulus, adaptation or a change in the spatial gradient is possible. As the shoot turns, the initially shaded side may receive more light, reducing the original difference in activation. The changing shape of the organ can act as part of a feedback loop between light input and growth output.

A simple geometrical feedback model helps clarify this principle. Consider a straight shoot initially at an angle of sixty degrees relative to the direction it would adopt if it fully aligned with the light. Let the effective left–right contrast be proportional, for illustration, to the sine of this angular deviation. The contrast is then about 0.87 of its maximum at sixty degrees, 0.50 at thirty degrees and 0.17 at ten degrees. As orientation improves, the signal driving further turning can become smaller. This is a conceptual model, not an empirical formula for every tissue. Diffuse light, leaf optics and the gravity vector can make the real relation quite different.

Phot1 and phot2 overlap in function across light regimes, but their sensitivity cannot be inferred simply by counting photons absorbed by one LOV domain. Differences in receptor expression, photocycle behavior, protein interactions and downstream regulation can influence which receptor dominates a given output. A low-intensity phot1-dependent response is not necessarily replaced completely by phot2 at the next higher intensity. Instead, the contribution of each receptor changes with the assay. One should use the relevant comparison: wild type versus a single mutant versus the double mutant at the same fluence rate and exposure duration.

NPH3 and RPT2 are important in understanding how phototropin activation is converted into a physiologically appropriate response. A direct phosphorylation event and a change in the overall phosphorylation pattern of a signaling protein can coexist. For example, phot1-dependent phosphorylation at a conserved NPH3 region has been linked to phototropic function, whereas other NPH3 phosphorylation changes reflect additional regulatory events. A diagram that labels NPH3 solely as phosphorylated in light or solely as dephosphorylated in light is therefore too coarse to capture the underlying biology. The exam-relevant principle is that modification state and molecular interactions regulate the transmission of information from the light-activated kinase.

RPT2 illustrates why signal transducers can influence light sensitivity. A transducer might alter the duration of signaling, the receptor's accessibility to its partners or the efficiency with which a downstream step follows activation. These changes can be particularly important under prolonged or intense illumination. A receptor that remains present and capable of absorbing photons is not automatically able to drive a normal output across all environmental conditions. A mutant affecting adaptation could show reasonable early curvature but abnormal continued growth, or a response that depends unusually strongly on prior illumination.

To distinguish response sensitivity from maximum response capacity, it is helpful to construct a dose–response curve. A leftward shift means a particular response is achieved at a lower dose, provided the response is compared over the same output scale. A reduced maximum means the system cannot attain the former response under the conditions tested, even when the stimulus is increased. A delayed response measured at a fixed early time can mimic either pattern. Therefore a suitable dataset includes enough time points to establish whether the compared systems have reached comparable physiological stages.

For an illustrative example, imagine that wild-type seedlings exposed to weak, medium and strong blue light reach bend angles of 18, 35 and 37 degrees after two hours, while a mutant reaches 4, 22 and 36 degrees. The mutant eventually approaches the wild-type response at the highest intensity, suggesting that its sensitivity differs under the tested conditions but substantial growth capacity is retained. A second mutant giving 2, 3 and 4 degrees may have a more severe sensory or downstream defect. The numbers do not establish the genotype; a transport defect, altered adaptation or impaired growth can also change the curve. They demonstrate how to extract a limited conclusion from response patterns before identifying their molecular cause.

The light-response system also interacts with gravitational orientation. Positive shoot phototropism may turn the axis away from its initial upward direction, while negative shoot gravitropism favors upward growth. A final oblique orientation can result from the combined effects of different directional inputs, rather than incomplete recognition of the light source. In an experiment, keeping the angle between the light vector and gravity constant enables more reliable comparisons. A change in that angle between treatment groups introduces a new biological variable even when wavelength and photon flux have been standardized.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which cofactor is directly associated with phototropin LOV domains?

FMN, rather than the bilin of phytochrome or FAD usually associated with cryptochrome. The sensory chromophore initiates light-dependent conformational signaling.

2. Part B — What process is directly catalyzed by the phototropin kinase?

ATP-dependent phosphorylation of suitable protein residues. Adduct formation within a LOV domain is a separate photochemical step.

3. Part B — Which Arabidopsis phototropin is especially important for sustained high-light chloroplast avoidance?

Phot2. Both phot1 and phot2 can contribute to accumulation, so a phot2 mutant may still show the low-light response.

4. Part C — A LOV2 mutant displays normal protein abundance but no light-induced kinase activation. A kinase-dead mutant still forms a cysteinyl–FMN adduct. Which stages are affected?

The first mutation affects light-to-kinase coupling, and the second affects catalytic signaling downstream of sensory activation. Both can impair curvature despite different molecular defects.

5. Part C — Uniform and unilateral blue light produce the same total receptor phosphorylation, but only unilateral light produces directional curvature. Explain.

Total phosphorylation does not indicate the spatial distribution of activity. The unilateral stimulus can impose a lateral difference in signaling, auxin transport and growth; uniform illumination need not establish this directional difference.

6. Part C — A mutant has normal phototropin phosphorylation and low-light chloroplast accumulation, but lacks high-light avoidance. Is a defect in all FMN binding the simplest explanation?

No. The intact accumulation response and receptor phosphorylation argue against a universal sensory defect. A phot2-specific or downstream avoidance defect is a more targeted hypothesis; gene identification requires additional evidence.

7. Part C — A shoot segment has an outer–inner arc length difference of 0.6 mm and diameter 1.2 mm. Estimate the bend.

Under the circular-arc approximation, θ = 0.6/1.2 = 0.5 radians, or approximately 28.6 degrees. The outer side is the longer side; the estimate assumes constant separation.

8. Part C — Two light pulses deliver 60 micromoles per square meter: 2 for 30 s and 6 for 10 s. Must they produce equal curvature?

No. The doses match, but photochemical dark recovery and signaling adaptation can operate during the longer pulse. Curvature additionally depends on spatial gradients and growth capacity.
