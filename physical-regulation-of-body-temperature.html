1. Heat balance: from energy conservation to body temperature

A lizard lying on a sun-warmed rock and a mammal walking in a cool forest face the same physical problem: energy continually crosses the boundary between an animal and its surroundings. Their sources of heat differ, but the laws determining heat exchange are identical. Physical thermoregulation changes the rate of heat transfer rather than directly increasing biochemical heat production. The distinction matters because an animal can defend its temperature by reducing loss even when metabolism does not rise.

Recall from Node 7.G.01 that endothermy describes the predominant source of body heat, while homeothermy describes relative temperature stability. Both ectotherms and endotherms use physical regulation. A basking reptile changes exposure to solar radiation; a mammal redistributes blood between core and skin; a bird fluffs its feathers. In each case a change in conductance, exposed surface, local temperature or water loss alters the energy budget.

Constructing a signed heat budget

Define M as metabolic energy release, W as useful mechanical work exported from the body, K, C and R as net conductive, convective and radiative heat loss, respectively, and E as evaporative heat loss. Express all rates in watts (joules per second). Under this convention a dry heat loss becomes negative when energy enters the animal. The storage rate is:

S = M − W − K − C − R − E

For an approximately uniform body of mass m and specific heat c, S ≈ mc × dT/dt. Positive storage raises average temperature, negative storage lowers it, and zero storage means no net change in stored thermal energy.

Respiratory sensible and latent heat exchanges may be grouped under convection and evaporation or reported separately; do not count them twice. Heat balance is not equivalent to thermal comfort, nor does zero storage imply that the animal is inactive. A running animal at constant temperature can have very large heat production and loss. Core temperature can also change differently from skin temperature, so the single-compartment approximation is best used for short quantitative questions with stated assumptions.

How the idea developed

Classical calorimetry established the connection between oxidation and animal heat production. Fourier's treatment of conduction, Newton's cooling approximation and the Stefan–Boltzmann description of thermal radiation supplied distinct physical laws. Physiologists combined these concepts with measurements of metabolism, surface temperature and water loss to explain animal energy budgets. The modern approach is to measure separate fluxes rather than infer the mechanism merely from body temperature.

2. Conduction: heat exchange by contact

Conduction is transfer of thermal energy through matter without macroscopic movement of the material. Faster molecular motion on the warmer side transfers energy to adjacent cooler material. When a snake contacts a rock, the direction of exchange depends on rock temperature relative to the animal's contacting surface, not the air temperature. A cold floor can therefore cool a resting animal even inside a warm room.

For steady, one-dimensional transport through a uniform layer of thickness L, thermal conductivity k and cross-sectional area A, Fourier's law gives K = kA(Thot − Tcold)/L in watts. Here k has units W m−1 K−1. In an animal use skin-to-substrate temperature difference for a simple external contact model. Real contact involves irregular surfaces, compressed fur, contact resistance and potentially multiple layers. Their resistances in series add: Rth = Σ L/(kA), and heat rate equals temperature difference divided by total resistance.

Air trapped between feathers or hairs conducts poorly compared with water. When plumage becomes wet, water may replace insulating air and increase heat transfer; wetting may additionally increase evaporation. Blubber provides a low-conductivity barrier between internal tissues and cold water and does not depend on maintaining a dry external air layer. Thickness matters, but circulation through insulation may also carry heat around that resistance.

Worked example

Consider a simplified insulating layer, k = 0.04 W m−1 K−1, A = 0.20 m², L = 0.02 m and a temperature difference of 20 K. Conductive loss is 0.04 × 0.20 × 20 / 0.02 = 8 W. Doubling thickness to 0.04 m halves it to 4 W if all other quantities remain fixed. Halving contact area has the same effect in this idealized model. These are illustrative values, not measurements from a specific species.

Experimental control requires equal temperature gradients, area and contact pressure. Measuring cooling of an insulated model and an uninsulated model under these conditions tests the insulation effect more directly than comparing two unrelated animals.

3. Convection: boundary layers, wind and water

Convection exchanges heat between a surface and moving fluid. Air or water touching the skin approaches its temperature; replacement of that fluid carries energy away or brings new energy in. Natural convection results from buoyancy differences produced by heating or cooling. Forced convection occurs when wind, swimming, running or a fan drives fluid movement.

A useful engineering approximation is C = hA(Ts − Ta), where h is the convective heat transfer coefficient in W m−2 K−1, Ts is surface temperature, and Ta is bulk fluid temperature. The coefficient is not a universal constant: it varies with flow velocity, body shape, fluid properties and boundary-layer structure. A thick layer of relatively stationary air reduces effective heat transfer. Wind typically thins that layer and increases h.

For skin at 33°C, air at 23°C, exposed area 0.5 m² and h = 6 W m−2 K−1, C = 30 W. If h increases to 12 while surface temperature stays unchanged, C = 60 W. In an actual animal skin may cool, thereby reducing the gradient, and vasoconstriction may reduce heat delivery. The simple calculation therefore predicts an immediate tendency rather than a complete physiological steady state.

When moving air warms the animal

If air temperature exceeds skin temperature, the sign reverses. At 38°C air and 34°C skin, faster wind increases convective heat gain if other conditions are fixed. A breeze may still cool an animal through enhanced evaporation, so the total effect depends on humidity, skin wetness and radiation. Wind chill is a description of enhanced heat loss in a cold setting, not a reduction in the actual air temperature.

Water generally carries heat away from a warm body much more effectively than still air at the same temperature. Its thermal properties and fluid movement explain why water immersion is a major thermal challenge for many terrestrial mammals. Aquatic mammals counter this with blubber, fur in some species, controlled circulation and countercurrent exchangers. The precise rate depends on flow, posture and insulation, not fluid identity alone.

4. Radiation: long-wave exchange and solar exposure

Radiation transports energy by electromagnetic waves and does not require contact or a material medium. All bodies above absolute zero emit thermal radiation. An animal also absorbs radiation from surrounding surfaces and may receive short-wave solar energy. These pathways explain why air temperature alone fails to characterize a thermal environment.

For a gray surface exchanging long-wave radiation with a large isothermal enclosure, the approximate net outward rate is R = εσA(Ts4 − Tr4). Emissivity ε lies between zero and one, σ ≈ 5.67 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4, and absolute temperatures must be expressed in kelvin. Effective area and geometric view factors matter for realistic surroundings. Short-wave solar absorption should be included separately, for example Rnet = Rlong-wave − Qsolar absorbed in our outward-positive convention.

Near ordinary biological temperatures, linearizing the fourth-power difference can yield an effective radiative coefficient hr ≈ 4εσT̄³. At T̄ = 300 K and ε = 0.95, hr is about 5.8 W m−2 K−1. With A = 0.5 m² and a 10 K difference, long-wave loss is approximately 29 W. The linear approximation is convenient for small gradients; use the full expression for large differences.

Behaviour and microclimate

A basking ectotherm can raise its body temperature above ambient air by orienting toward sunlight and exposing more projected area. Turning away, entering shade or retreating into a burrow reduces absorbed solar radiation. A clear night sky may be an effective cold radiative sink; a shelter limits the animal's view of that sink. Visible colour affects short-wave absorption but is not a reliable predictor of infrared emissivity. Dark and light surfaces can have similar long-wave emissivity.

Operative environmental temperature is a useful way to combine dry heat exchange in a specified physical model. It depends on radiation, air movement, geometry and material properties. It is not simply the arithmetic mean of air and ground temperatures and does not by itself capture evaporative potential.

5. Evaporation: latent heat and humidity

Evaporation is the conversion of liquid water to water vapour. Molecules leaving the liquid carry energy, and the latent heat required for this phase change is drawn partly from the animal or its immediate surroundings. At ordinary physiological temperatures the latent heat of vaporization of water is approximately 2.4 kJ per gram. If evaporation is supplied entirely by body heat, E = λṁevap, where λ is latent heat in J g−1 and ṁ is the evaporation rate in g s−1.

Unlike dry heat exchange, evaporation can remove energy from an animal even when air is warmer than the skin, provided the vapour-pressure gradient permits net evaporation. The driving force is related to the difference between water vapour pressure near a wet surface and that of the surrounding air. Relative humidity is the ratio of actual vapour pressure to saturation vapour pressure at the specified air temperature. At different temperatures identical relative humidities need not imply identical evaporative capacity.

A simple wet-surface model treats the evaporative rate as proportional to exposed wet area and the vapour-pressure difference, with a coefficient determined by air movement and transport conditions. Clothing or plumage may impede vapour movement. High humidity reduces the gradient, and air movement can increase transport when a gradient remains. When sweat drips away without evaporating, the corresponding latent heat is not removed from the body. Sweat secretion and effective evaporation are therefore different quantities.

Water and energy trade-off

If 120 g of water evaporates per hour, the cooling rate is 120 × 2400 / 3600 = 80 W. If 120 g is secreted but only half evaporates, the evaporative component is 40 W. Other pathways, including sensible heat transfer to runoff, can exist but are not counted as latent heat. Evaporative cooling imposes a water cost and, for sweating, often electrolyte loss. Desert animals consequently balance heat defence with conservation of body water.

Condensation on a cooler surface releases latent heat rather than extracting it. The usual biological assumption E ≥ 0 is appropriate for ordinary net evaporative loss but should not be blindly applied under conditions favouring condensation.

6. Surface area, body size and physical insulation

For geometrically similar animals of constant density, area scales with linear dimension squared and volume or mass with linear dimension cubed. Consequently A ∝ m2/3 and A/m ∝ m−1/3. A small animal has more external exchange area per unit mass and, at a given surface flux and tissue heat capacity, may change temperature faster than a larger one. If mass increases eightfold, area increases fourfold and area per mass halves.

The relationship is a geometric null model, not a universal empirical rule for metabolic rate. Shape, fur, feather structure, perfusion, behavior and appendage size modify effective heat exchange. A sphere minimizes area for its volume, whereas elongated appendages provide relatively large area. Curling into a compact posture decreases exposed area; spreading limbs or wings can increase it. Huddling reduces exposed group area and may create a warmer local microclimate, although individuals at the edge receive less protection.

Insulation reduces dry heat conductance. Fur and feathers trap air; piloerection and feather fluffing increase the thickness of that trapped layer when conditions allow. Flattening the coat can facilitate heat loss. Human clothing creates similar thermal resistance. Subcutaneous fat and blubber contribute resistance, particularly in aquatic mammals. Insulation is useful not only for retaining internal heat in cold settings but also for reducing external dry heat gain in hot surroundings when the gradient reverses.

Ecogeographic patterns

Bergmann's rule describes a tendency toward larger body size in colder climates within some related endotherms; Allen's rule describes shorter exposed appendages in cold climates. Both can be interpreted partly through heat exchange, but neither is a universal law. Food availability, phylogeny, development and water balance are competing explanations. Tests should compare appropriately related populations and distinguish correlations from mechanisms.

For an approximately isothermal body cooling in a constant environment with total conductance G, mc dT/dt = −G(T − Tenv). The difference decays exponentially with time constant τ = mc/G. Larger heat capacity or smaller conductance slows cooling. The model omits heat production and changing perfusion; living animals can deviate strongly from a passive exponential curve.

7. Skin blood flow and regulated thermal conductance

The body's core is separated from the environment by tissues whose thermal conductance changes. Blood carries sensible heat from warmer regions to cooler regions through bulk transport. Cutaneous vasodilation increases blood flow near the surface, tending to raise skin temperature and facilitate outward dry heat transfer when the surroundings are cooler. Vasoconstriction reduces warm blood delivery to the skin, lowers its temperature relative to the core, and conserves central heat.

Heat carried by a blood stream can be approximated by Q = ṁbcb(Tin − Tout). This represents internal heat transport, not an additional environmental heat loss to add to radiation or convection. Blood transports energy to the skin; that energy subsequently crosses the animal's boundary through physical pathways. Counting both as external losses would violate energy conservation.

Consider two serial thermal resistances: core-to-skin and skin-to-environment. Vasoconstriction increases effective internal resistance, while vasodilation reduces it. Wind reduces external convective resistance, and fur increases external resistance. In a simplified steady dry model, Q = (Tcore − Tenv)/(Rinternal + Rexternal). A cold skin can coexist with a stable warm core because temperature gradients develop across the shell.

In hot environments vasodilation cannot ensure outward dry heat flow if the environment exceeds skin temperature. However, perfusing wet skin can supply heat for evaporation. Large cutaneous blood flow also competes with circulatory demands of exercising muscles and may contribute to cardiovascular strain when body water is depleted. The neural control circuits responsible for these changes are treated in 7.G.04; here the focus is on the physical effect.

Comparative examples

Rabbit ears and elephant ears provide vascular surfaces that can enhance heat transfer under favourable gradients. Their effectiveness depends on blood flow, area, wind and radiant environment. A warm infrared image of an appendage indicates high surface temperature but cannot alone quantify heat loss without environmental and emissivity information.

8. Sweating, panting and other evaporative mechanisms

Humans rely strongly on eccrine sweating during heat stress. Sweat is transported to the skin surface, where evaporation removes heat. Sympathetic cholinergic innervation is unusual compared with many sympathetic effector pathways. Sweat output varies across body regions and with acclimatization, hydration, exercise intensity and ambient conditions. Sweat gland ducts reabsorb part of the secreted salt, although the extent depends on flow and hormonal regulation.

Many mammals and birds rely substantially on respiratory evaporation. Panting increases ventilation of moist respiratory surfaces. Rapid shallow panting can increase ventilation of anatomical dead space while limiting changes in alveolar ventilation compared with equally large tidal breaths. In some species or conditions, intense panting does increase alveolar ventilation and can lower arterial CO₂, producing respiratory alkalosis. Therefore it is incorrect to say panting never affects acid–base balance.

Some birds use gular flutter: rapid oscillation of the floor of the mouth or throat moves air over moist surfaces. In certain species this provides substantial evaporative cooling with comparatively low muscular cost. Saliva spreading in some mammals and cutaneous water loss in some birds provide further routes. The dominant pathway is species-specific; the presence of sweat glands does not establish that sweating is the principal cooling mechanism.

Comparative experiment

Measure respiratory and cutaneous water loss separately in a chamber while maintaining controlled air temperature, humidity and flow. An increase in respiratory evaporation with breathing frequency supports a panting mechanism; an increase in cutaneous evaporation with skin wetness supports a sweating or skin pathway. Simultaneous oxygen consumption helps assess the metabolic cost of the response. Compare within species and account for body mass, hydration and activity. Chamber water vapour concentration alone cannot distinguish the source.

Both respiratory and cutaneous cooling lose water. Maintaining effective cooling in dry heat may be easier physically than in humid heat, but water limitation remains a central biological constraint. The optimum strategy depends on heat load, available water, salt balance and circulatory capacity.

9. Countercurrent heat exchange and regional heterothermy

In a countercurrent exchanger, two streams move in opposite directions while exchanging heat across adjacent surfaces. Warm arterial blood travelling from the body core toward a cold appendage can transfer energy to cooler venous blood returning from it. This warms returning blood and cools arterial blood before it reaches the distal tissues. Less heat reaches the environment while the core remains warm.

Opposite flow maintains a temperature difference along much of the exchange length. With parallel flow, the streams enter together at one end and approach a common temperature as they travel; the driving gradient tends to collapse. In an ideal balanced countercurrent exchanger, the cold outlet can become warmer than the hot outlet without exceeding the hot inlet temperature. This does not violate the second law because heat passes locally from the warmer stream to the cooler stream at every position.

In birds' legs and some marine mammal appendages, close arterial and venous vessels reduce peripheral heat loss. A cool foot is not necessarily evidence of failed regulation; tolerating a cooler periphery can conserve core energy while maintaining enough blood supply for tissue viability. Some fish, including tunas, use countercurrent vascular arrangements to retain metabolic heat in selected regions, producing regional endothermy. The exchange redistributes existing heat; it does not create heat.

For illustration, equal blood heat-capacity rates of 2 W K−1 enter an idealized exchanger at 38°C (arterial) and 12°C (venous). If arterial blood leaves at 18°C, 2 × (38 − 18) = 40 W has transferred internally. Neglecting environmental losses from the exchanger, venous blood leaves at 32°C. The maximum possible transfer is 2 × (38 − 12) = 52 W, giving effectiveness 40/52 ≈ 0.77. Actual flow rates, vascular geometry and bypass pathways complicate the idealized calculation.

Compare this mechanism with the renal countercurrent exchanger from Node 7.F.05. Both use opposite flow to preserve gradients, but the exchanged quantity and physiological purpose differ. The renal countercurrent multiplier generates an osmotic gradient through active and passive solute transport; a passive heat exchanger does not multiply energy.

10. Integrated predictions and experimental interpretation

Physical mechanisms operate simultaneously. A cold mammal reduces skin blood flow and exposed surface, increases insulation and seeks shelter; dry heat loss falls. A hot mammal may increase skin perfusion, spread its limbs, seek shade and increase evaporation. An ectotherm adjusts behaviour to gain or lose heat and may exploit contact with warm or cool surfaces. These are not rigid sequences: the relevant gradient, water availability and the species' physiology determine the most effective combination.

How to test the mechanisms

Direct calorimetry measures heat exchange with the environment; indirect calorimetry estimates metabolic energy release from gas exchange using assumptions about substrate oxidation. Neither alone establishes all heat pathways. A complete experiment may combine oxygen consumption, temperature probes, skin blood flow, chamber humidity, airflow and radiant temperature. Thermal imaging measures surface radiation and requires an appropriate emissivity assumption. A black-globe or physical animal model can help estimate radiant or operative environmental conditions, but the model needs suitable geometry and calibration.

Use a factorial design to separate airflow and humidity: low versus high wind crossed with low versus high humidity, with radiant and air temperatures controlled. Dry and wetted physical models can separate sensible heat transfer from evaporative effects. For live-animal experiments, use ethically approved non-harmful conditions and account for individual differences and acclimation. If wind increases cooling only in the wet model at air temperature above surface temperature, evaporation is a plausible explanation; direct water-loss measurements strengthen the inference.

When analyzing graphs, identify the dependent variable first: heat flux, temperature, conductance or metabolic rate are not interchangeable. Examine whether the surface-to-environment gradient changes and whether an apparent plateau indicates steady state, saturation of a response or insufficient measurement sensitivity. A causal conclusion requires the appropriate intervention and controls.

Original examination practice

These original questions are designed for conceptual and analytical practice; they are not claimed to be previous-year questions. Attempt each before opening its answer.

1. MCQ — A reptile contacts a rock warmer than its ventral skin. Which flux is immediately directed into the animal?

A. Conduction   B. Evaporation   C. Metabolic heat   D. None

Show answer and explanation

A. Heat travels from the warmer rock through the contact surface. The signed conductive loss is negative.

2. MCQ — An increase in wind speed raises convective heat transfer. When can this increase dry heat gain?

A. Air colder than skin   B. Air warmer than skin   C. Equal temperatures   D. Always

Show answer and explanation

B. The convective coefficient increases, but the temperature gradient is inward when air is warmer.

3. MCQ — Which change most directly reduces conductive heat loss through dry fur?

A. Decrease layer thickness   B. Increase k   C. Increase trapped air thickness   D. Increase area

Show answer and explanation

C. Fourier's law predicts lower transfer with increased insulation thickness for fixed k, area and gradient.

4. MCQ — For geometrically similar animals, an eightfold mass increase changes area per mass by what factor?

A. 8   B. 4   C. 2   D. 1/2

Show answer and explanation

D. A/m scales as m to the power −1/3; 8 to that power equals one-half.

5. MCQ — What explains the cooling advantage of countercurrent over parallel flow?

A. Active heat pumping   B. Maintained local gradients   C. Heat production   D. Complete blood mixing

Show answer and explanation

B. Opposite flow sustains local differences along the exchanger. No heat pump or mixing is required.

6. MCQ — Which factor determines the latent cooling component of sweating most directly?

A. Total sweat secreted   B. Sweat evaporated   C. Sweat salt concentration alone   D. Skin blood pressure

Show answer and explanation

B. The phase change of evaporated water determines latent heat removal; runoff is not equivalent.

7. MCQ — Which measurement is most useful for distinguishing radiative from convective heat exchange?

A. Body mass alone   B. Mean radiant and air temperatures plus airflow   C. Heart rate alone   D. Urine volume

Show answer and explanation

B. The relevant environmental gradients and airflow determine distinct sensible heat pathways.

8. MCQ — In an animal with core 38°C and skin 25°C during cold exposure, which interpretation is most defensible?

A. All tissues are at 38°C   B. Skin temperature alone proves hypothermia   C. A core-shell gradient exists   D. Evaporation is absent

Show answer and explanation

C. A cool shell may protect a warm core; no conclusion about evaporation follows from temperatures alone.

9. Conceptual — Explain why an animal may reduce exposure to sun yet increase exposure to wind.

Show answer and explanation

Shade reduces absorbed short-wave radiation; wind increases convective exchange and may increase evaporation. If air is warmer than skin, increased wind can increase dry heat gain, so the net benefit depends on evaporation.

10. Conceptual — Why does water replace air in wet fur and impair insulation?

Show answer and explanation

Water increases thermal coupling relative to the trapped air layer and may collapse the insulating structure. Evaporation adds another cooling route. These effects depend on the coat and wetting conditions.

11. Conceptual — Distinguish thermal conductance from thermal conductivity.

Show answer and explanation

Conductivity k is a material property in W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; conductance G describes a particular pathway in W K⁻¹ and depends on geometry, material and sometimes flow. For a uniform slab G = kA/L.

12. Conceptual — Why is a higher infrared surface temperature not sufficient to quantify total heat loss?

Show answer and explanation

Radiative flux depends on emissivity, surrounding radiant temperature and area. Convection, evaporation and conduction also contribute; an image measures surface temperature rather than all fluxes.

13. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: Evaporation may cool a mammal in air warmer than its skin. Reason: A favourable water vapour pressure gradient can drive evaporation independently of the sign of the dry temperature gradient.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion   B. Both true, reason not explanatory   C. Assertion true, reason false   D. Assertion false, reason true

Show answer and explanation

A. Latent heat transfer is driven by water vapour transport rather than solely by the sensible temperature difference.

14. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: A countercurrent exchanger produces additional metabolic heat. Reason: Heat passes between closely associated blood streams.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion   B. Both true, reason not explanatory   C. Assertion true, reason false   D. Assertion false, reason true

Show answer and explanation

D. The mechanism redistributes already existing heat; the second statement describes the physical exchange.

15. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: All panting leaves arterial CO₂ unchanged. Reason: Panting can preferentially ventilate dead space through shallow breaths.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion   B. Both true, reason not explanatory   C. Assertion true, reason false   D. Assertion false, reason true

Show answer and explanation

D. Some panting patterns preserve alveolar ventilation, but stronger or different patterns can alter CO₂.

16. Numerical — M = 160 W, W = 30 W, K = 5 W, C = 30 W, R = 45 W and E = 50 W. Find storage.

Show answer and explanation

S = 160 − 30 − 5 − 30 − 45 − 50 = 0 W. Metabolic and external heat flows balance.

17. Numerical — For k = 0.05 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, A = 0.4 m², L = 0.02 m and ΔT = 12 K, find conductive loss.

Show answer and explanation

K = 0.05 × 0.4 × 12 / 0.02 = 12 W. Increasing thickness threefold would reduce it to 4 W at fixed gradient.

18. Numerical — Skin is 34°C and air is 39°C. A = 0.6 m² and h = 10 W m⁻² K⁻¹. Find signed convective loss.

Show answer and explanation

C = 10 × 0.6 × (34 − 39) = −30 W; the animal gains 30 W.

19. Numerical — An animal must remove 180 W by evaporation. Assuming λ = 2400 J/g, calculate water evaporated in one hour.

Show answer and explanation

180 × 3600 / 2400 = 270 g. If only 75% of secreted sweat evaporates, secretion must be 360 g under the simplified assumptions.

20. Numerical — A 20 kg animal with effective specific heat 3500 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ stores 70 W for 10 minutes. Estimate mean body temperature change.

Show answer and explanation

ΔT = 70 × 600 / (20 × 3500) = 0.60 K. This is a lumped average assuming constant storage.

21. Analytical — At constant air and radiant temperature, wind increases cooling in a dry model but has a much larger effect in a wet model. What does the difference suggest?

Show answer and explanation

Both models experience enhanced sensible transfer. Additional wet-model cooling suggests evaporation. Measure vapour output and monitor surface temperature to quantify the contribution.

22. Analytical — Two animals of equal mass and identical metabolic heat have different core temperatures in the same cold chamber. Can insulation be inferred as the sole cause?

Show answer and explanation

No. Differences may involve surface area, perfusion, activity, evaporation and initial conditions. Measure conductance, fluxes and temperature gradients under controlled conditions.

23. Analytical — A graph shows skin temperature rising during heat stress while core temperature is unchanged. Explain a plausible mechanism and a limitation.

Show answer and explanation

Vasodilation can increase skin temperature and external heat loss in a favourable gradient. A stable core implies approximate balance but does not prove vasodilation; direct blood-flow measurement is needed.

24. Analytical — A countercurrent exchanger receives warm blood at 36°C and cool blood at 10°C; outlet temperatures are 17°C and 29°C. Equal heat-capacity rates are 3 W/K. Verify energy balance.

Show answer and explanation

Hot stream loses 3 × 19 = 57 W; cold stream gains 3 × 19 = 57 W. The outlet ordering is possible in countercurrent flow; local gradients remain outward from warm to cool stream.

One-minute heat balance map

Storage changes average body temperature. Area, insulation, blood flow and behaviour regulate exchange without necessarily changing metabolism.
