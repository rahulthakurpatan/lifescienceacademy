The animal as a thermal system

A resting mammal in a cool room, a lizard on a sunlit rock and a bird standing on snow face the same physical requirement: their tissues continually exchange energy with the surroundings. The biological consequences depend not merely on air temperature but on the rate and direction of the exchange. A lizard may become warmer than the air by absorbing solar radiation, whereas a resting mammal may cool despite producing heat metabolically. Physical thermoregulation concerns the mechanisms that alter these exchanges. Chemical thermoregulation, discussed in the next chapter, concerns changes in metabolic heat production. In a living animal the two processes overlap, but separating them initially makes it possible to reason clearly about the cause of a temperature change.

Temperature and heat must first be distinguished. Temperature describes the thermal state of a material and determines the direction of spontaneous heat transfer between two bodies in thermal contact. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference, not a substance contained within an animal. The internal energy of a body depends partly on its temperature, its mass and its composition. A large animal may have a greater thermal energy content than a small animal even if its temperature is lower. A small bird and a large mammal at the same body temperature therefore do not require the same quantity of heat to raise their temperatures by one degree. The rate of exchange and the capacity to store energy are distinct properties.

An animal is not a uniformly warm object. Deep organs, actively contracting muscles, skin, fur and exposed appendages may have different temperatures. Blood transports thermal energy between regions, while conduction through the tissues moves energy along local temperature gradients. A thermometer measuring the skin cannot automatically be used as a substitute for a measurement of deep body temperature. A fall in skin temperature during cold exposure may indicate that less warm blood reaches the surface, even while the core remains stable. Conversely, during exercise the temperature of a working muscle may increase before a distant core measurement changes. These spatial differences are part of the mechanism of regulation, not simply measurement error.

The outer boundary of the animal is a useful place to construct a heat budget. Energy crosses it by several pathways. Metabolic reactions release chemical energy, some of which becomes external mechanical work, and much of the remainder becomes heat. Heat may pass between the animal and a contacting solid, the surrounding fluid and radiative surfaces. Water that changes from liquid to vapour removes energy from the region where it evaporates. A description of any one pathway is insufficient to predict body temperature because gains and losses occur simultaneously. A wet animal in a breeze may gain heat from warm air but lose even more through evaporation; a dry animal in that same breeze may gain heat overall.

Physiologists distinguish thermal steady state from the absence of energy transfer. At thermal steady state, the mean body temperature does not change appreciably during the chosen observation interval. Metabolic heat production and environmental gains are balanced by heat losses, so energy is still being exchanged. An animal in thermal steady state is not necessarily at thermal equilibrium with its surroundings. For instance, a bird at a core temperature above ambient temperature can maintain that difference through continuous metabolism. The energy required to sustain this nonequilibrium state ultimately derives from food or stored substrates.

The skin and integument are especially important because they regulate the connection between the internally warm tissues and the environment. An animal can change skin blood flow within minutes, adopt a new posture in seconds, and modify the thickness of an insulating air layer by altering the position of its fur or feathers. Some responses act without changing metabolic rate substantially. Others carry costs, such as muscular work during panting or loss of water through evaporation. The correct question in comparative physiology is therefore not simply which mechanism cools an animal, but how the mechanism modifies the heat budget and what additional resources it consumes.

A quantitative heat budget

Conservation of energy provides the foundation for interpreting every physical thermoregulatory response. Define M as the rate of metabolic energy liberation and W as the rate of useful mechanical work performed on the environment. M minus W is the net rate of energy made available as heat within the animal in a simplified whole-body budget. Define K, C and R as net outward heat transfer by conduction, convection and radiation respectively, and E as outward evaporative heat loss. With these conventions, heat storage rate S equals M minus W minus K minus C minus R minus E. All rates must be expressed in consistent units, commonly watts, where one watt is one joule per second. Respiratory dry and evaporative components may be included in C and E respectively or counted separately, but they must not be counted twice.

A positive value of K means net conductive heat leaves the animal; a negative value means the animal gains heat from a warmer contact surface. The same sign convention applies to C and R. E is normally positive during evaporation from the animal. A humid environment can reduce E without making dry heat exchange disappear. Condensation releases latent heat rather than removing it, and would need to be treated as a gain if relevant. A frequent error is to substitute the magnitude of heat gained as a positive loss. When an examination gives an explicitly inward flux, assign the negative sign before subtracting the term or use a separate incoming-energy sum.

An illustrative resting animal liberates 120 W metabolically and performs no external mechanical work. Its net conduction, convection, radiation and evaporation are 5 W, 25 W, 55 W and 35 W outward. The losses total 120 W and heat storage is zero. If the surrounding surfaces become warmer while all other terms remain fixed and net radiation falls to 15 W outward, total loss becomes 80 W. Storage is now 40 W. The reduced radiative loss has created a positive energy imbalance even though the animal still loses heat by radiation. It may restore steady state by increasing another route of loss, reducing metabolic heat production where possible or changing exposure.

In a second illustrative case an exercising animal has M equal to 350 W, W equal to 50 W, conduction negligible, convection 30 W inward, radiation 20 W inward and evaporation 280 W outward. The dry terms are C = −30 W and R = −20 W. Substituting gives S = 350 − 50 − 0 − (−30) − (−20) − 280 = 70 W. To achieve zero storage without changing the other terms, evaporation must reach 350 W. This example shows why an apparently large sweat evaporation rate may still be insufficient in a hot radiant environment. The water demand for heat balance depends on environmental gains as well as metabolic heat.

Heat storage can be related approximately to the rate of change in mean body temperature. If the mass is m kilograms and the effective specific heat capacity is c joules per kilogram per kelvin, the thermal capacity is mc joules per kelvin. For approximately uniform body temperature and constant composition, S = mc dT/dt. Over an interval of constant S, the temperature change is S times elapsed seconds divided by mc. Suppose the animal in the previous example has mass 60 kg and an illustrative effective specific heat of 3500 J per kg per kelvin. After 600 seconds at 70 W it stores 42,000 J. Dividing by 210,000 J/K gives a mean temperature increase of 0.20 K. This is an idealized estimate: real tissues differ in temperature and the balance terms change as the animal responds.

The heat budget is useful even when individual fluxes cannot all be measured directly. If an animal maintains a stable mean temperature and metabolic heat is known, the sum of all net heat exchanges must balance it. However, this observation alone does not distinguish radiation from convection or evaporation. A measurement of body temperature provides evidence about the sum of the terms, not their partition. To infer the mechanism, investigators must measure water vapour exchange, environmental temperatures, heat flux or other relevant variables. The distinction between a conservation constraint and an independently measured mechanism is central to experimental reasoning.

Conduction and thermal resistance

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through matter without requiring bulk motion of the material. In an animal lying on a cold surface, energy passes from the warmer tissues through the contact region into the cooler material. Conduction also occurs within the animal, for example across layers of skin and adipose tissue. The direction is determined by the local temperature gradient. A warm rock may supply heat to a basking lizard, and the same rock can draw heat from the animal after it cools below the skin temperature.

A simple model for steady conduction through a flat layer of homogeneous material is K = kA(T1 − T2)/L. Here k is thermal conductivity in W per metre per kelvin, A is cross-sectional area in square metres, L is thickness in metres and T1 − T2 is the temperature difference in kelvin. The sign is positive when the first surface is warmer. This equation assumes steady transfer in one dimension, constant conductivity, and negligible lateral losses and heat generation within the layer. A larger contact area or conductivity increases the rate of transfer; a thicker layer decreases it. The units reduce to watts because metres squared divided by metres, combined with conductivity and temperature difference, yields joules per second.

The resistance form is often more intuitive. Define thermal resistance Rt = L/(kA), with units K/W. Then K = (T1 − T2)/Rt. Two layers placed in series carry the same steady heat flow if there is no internal source or sink between them. Their resistances add, just as pressure drops add along sequential resistances to fluid flow. A layer with high resistance supports a large temperature difference for a given heat flux. The analogy with electrical resistance is mathematical rather than anatomical; it does not imply that the tissues contain an electrical circuit responsible for heat conduction.

Consider a simplified dry insulating layer 0.02 m thick with effective conductivity 0.04 W per metre per kelvin and area 0.50 square metres. If the temperatures at its boundaries are 32 and 12 degrees Celsius, its resistance is 0.02/(0.04 × 0.50) = 1 K/W. The heat transfer is 20 W. Doubling the thickness while preserving the same effective conductivity and boundary temperatures doubles resistance and halves heat flow to 10 W. An actual coat cannot always be treated as a homogeneous layer because it contains air, fibres and potentially moisture, but the model correctly illustrates why thickness and trapped air can matter.

Contact geometry is also important. A curled mammal reduces the area of its body exposed to a cold floor compared with lying fully extended. A lizard may press its ventral surface against a heated rock to increase thermal contact. Even when the apparent geometric area is large, actual microscopic contact depends on roughness, compression and intervening air. Thus two materials at the same measured temperature may feel different to a human hand because their thermal conductivity and thermal capacity differ. A metal surface can take heat away rapidly, whereas a low-conductivity dry wooden surface transfers it more slowly.

Water usually transfers heat away from an unprotected warm animal much more effectively than still air at the same temperature, in part because of differences in thermal properties and because fluid movement renews the thermal boundary layer. Aquatic mammals do not depend solely on a layer of dry external air for insulation. Subcutaneous fat and, in some species, specialized fur that retains air reduce thermal exchange. The comparative question is whether the insulation retains its properties in the actual environment. A terrestrial coat that becomes soaked may lose much of the resistance provided by trapped air. This is a change in heat-transfer conditions, not a direct consequence of the water being intrinsically colder.

Convection and the moving boundary layer

Convection is heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid. Molecules next to the skin initially exchange energy locally, and movement of the surrounding air or water transports warmed or cooled fluid away. The fluid adjacent to a warm animal may become warmer than the bulk fluid farther away. If the warmed layer is not continually replaced, it reduces the effective temperature difference at the boundary. A breeze, current or movement of the animal through the fluid can renew this boundary layer and alter the rate of exchange.

Natural convection arises from buoyancy forces associated with temperature-dependent density differences. Warmed air generally becomes less dense and tends to rise in a gravitational field, while cooler air moves towards the warm surface. Forced convection occurs when an external mechanism drives the flow, such as wind, a swimming animal's motion or a fan. Both may operate simultaneously. The rate depends on the geometry of the animal, fluid properties, flow velocity and orientation; it is not determined by temperature difference alone.

For a simplified convective model, C = hA(Ts − Ta), where h is a convective heat transfer coefficient in W per square metre per kelvin, A is the effective exchange area, Ts is the surface temperature and Ta is the bulk fluid temperature. This equation is an empirical representation of a boundary process, not a claim that h has a universal value. A change in airflow can change h, while altered skin circulation can change Ts. The sign of the exchange is determined by the surface-to-fluid temperature difference. Core temperature should not be substituted automatically for surface temperature, particularly when fur, clothing or peripheral vasoconstriction produces a substantial thermal gradient.

For an illustrative skin surface of 1.5 square metres at 34 degrees Celsius in air at 24 degrees Celsius, h = 5 W per square metre per kelvin gives an outward convective loss of 75 W. If airflow increases h to 10 with the temperatures held fixed, loss becomes 150 W. If the air is instead 40 degrees Celsius, the same h of 10 gives C = −90 W. The stronger airflow now increases sensible heat gain. If the skin is wet, the breeze may simultaneously enhance evaporation, and the net thermal effect depends on the relative magnitudes of sensible gain and latent loss. This is why the statement that wind always cools is physically false.

Animals change effective convective exposure by behaviour. A bird sheltering behind vegetation encounters a lower air speed than it would in an exposed location. An animal entering a flowing stream experiences both water contact and movement. A small mammal crouching close to the ground may occupy a slower-moving layer of air, although ground temperature and radiation can alter the net benefit. A change of microhabitat therefore modifies multiple heat budget terms at once. Field observations of air temperature measured well above an animal may fail to represent the local fluid temperature or airspeed experienced by its body.

When an animal has insulating fur, the outer fur surface rather than naked skin is the interface with the moving air. Wind may penetrate or compress the coat, changing the coat's effective resistance as well as the external convective coefficient. A single h value is then not sufficient to describe the entire pathway from core to atmosphere. A series model containing internal resistance, coat resistance and external convective resistance gives a more useful conceptual picture. The largest resistance often dominates the total temperature drop, and changing a much smaller resistance may have relatively little effect on overall heat flow.

Thermal radiation and the radiant environment

Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves. Unlike conduction and convection it does not require material contact or a moving fluid, and it can cross a vacuum. Every ordinary animal surface emits thermal radiation because of its temperature. The animal also absorbs radiation emitted or reflected by surrounding surfaces, and it may absorb shortwave solar radiation. Net radiative exchange is the difference between these outgoing and incoming transfers. Radiation can therefore be a major heat gain even when the surrounding air is cooler than the animal.

For a small grey surface in a simplified large isothermal enclosure, the net longwave radiant loss is R = εσA( Ts⁴ − Tr⁴ ). Emissivity ε is dimensionless, σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant approximately 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W per square metre per kelvin to the fourth power, A is the effective radiating area, and Ts and Tr are absolute temperatures in kelvin. The enclosure model assumes an appropriate radiative view of the surroundings and neglects complicated geometry and spectral properties. Temperatures must be converted from Celsius before raising them to the fourth power. A temperature difference in kelvin has the same numerical magnitude as one in Celsius, but the fourth powers of absolute temperatures do not.

If the skin is 307 K and the surroundings have an effective radiant temperature of 297 K, the net longwave flux for a surface with emissivity 0.95 is approximately 59 W per square metre. This follows from the fourth-power difference or from a linearized coefficient near 302 K of roughly 6 W per square metre per kelvin. A nearby heated wall can raise the effective radiant temperature and reduce or reverse this loss without changing measured air temperature. Conversely, exposure to a clear night sky may enhance radiant cooling relative to air temperature. The sky is not a material surface at a single physical temperature; an effective radiant temperature represents the intensity of incoming longwave radiation.

Direct sunlight adds a separate term governed by the projected area, solar irradiance and the fraction absorbed. If a lizard presents 0.01 square metres of projected area to 600 W per square metre of incident sunlight and absorbs 70 percent, it receives 4.2 W of shortwave energy. This hypothetical calculation does not establish how fast the lizard warms; convection, conduction, longwave emission, evaporation and thermal capacity determine the result together. Changing posture relative to the sun can alter the projected area without changing total skin area.

Surface colour and thermal emissivity must not be treated as interchangeable. Dark pigmentation often increases absorption of visible sunlight, but the infrared emissivity of a biological surface depends on material properties and wavelength. A dark-coated animal is not guaranteed to have a larger net heat gain under all conditions. Fur thickness, how deeply solar energy penetrates, airflow through the coat, and the temperature of the coat surface determine how much absorbed energy reaches the skin. In examination problems, distinguish a stated difference in shortwave absorptivity from an assumed difference in longwave emissivity.

Radiative and convective fluxes may have opposite directions. In a shaded enclosure with warm air and cool walls an animal could gain heat by convection while losing it by radiation. In a cool outdoor environment under intense sunshine, solar gain may outweigh convective loss. A thermometer that is shielded from direct radiation measures air temperature, not the full thermal environment. To compare microhabitats, physiological studies often consider both radiant and convective effects. The value of such measurements is that they connect environmental energy exchange with the animal's actual thermal exposure rather than assuming a single environmental temperature describes everything.
