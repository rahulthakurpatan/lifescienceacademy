1. The intestine as a shared metabolic compartment

A mammal can digest cooked starch efficiently, yet much of the carbohydrate contained in plant cell walls escapes its own digestive enzymes. This observation poses a physiological question: is food that remains chemically intact after passage through the small intestine necessarily lost to the animal? The answer depends on the organisms occupying its digestive tract. Many of the bonds that mammalian enzymes cannot hydrolyse can be cleaved by microbial enzymes. The products of the resulting microbial metabolism may then become available to the host. Digestion is consequently not restricted to the secretions and absorptive surfaces described in conventional accounts of animal physiology. It includes chemical transformations carried out by other organisms within a compartment whose physical conditions are partly established by the animal.

The intestinal lumen is topologically continuous with the environment. A metabolite inside that lumen has not yet entered the internal fluid environment of the host. To influence the liver directly through the portal circulation, it ordinarily must first cross the intestinal epithelium. To influence an epithelial receptor at the luminal surface, however, it may not need to enter the circulation at all. This distinction explains why microbial physiology cannot be inferred merely by counting microorganisms. One must determine what molecules they make, where those molecules are found, whether they cross an epithelial boundary, and which cells can detect or utilise them.

The word microbiota refers to the organisms in a particular environment; microbiome can include their collective genetic and functional context. Here the emphasis is on the activities that link these organisms to host physiology. A genetic capability for degrading a plant polymer is not the same thing as an ongoing degradation reaction. The gene must be expressed, the encoded enzyme must encounter an accessible substrate, and environmental conditions must permit the resulting organism or community to continue metabolising it. Similarly, the appearance of a microbial product in stool is evidence of its presence in the sampled material, but does not by itself measure production throughout the intestine.

Three categories of interaction organise this chapter. In a nutritional interaction, microbes alter the quantity or chemical form of substrates available to the host. In a signalling interaction, microbial products are detected by cellular receptors or affect regulatory proteins. In an ecological interaction, microbes and host tissues alter the availability of space, electron acceptors, nutrients and protective substances for other organisms. These categories overlap. Butyrate, for example, can act as a metabolic fuel, as a regulator of gene expression and as an indirect determinant of oxygen availability in the intestinal lumen. Understanding such overlapping functions is more informative than treating each microbial product as exclusively beneficial or harmful.

The distribution of functions along the gut reflects physiological differences between compartments. A rapid flow of material through an absorptive segment can limit the time available for microbial growth. Acidity, bile salts, digestive enzymes and oxygen exposure also vary along the tract. The distal colon offers prolonged access to residual material under conditions favourable to many anaerobic organisms. This does not imply an absence of function in the small intestine; rather, the local microbial density, available substrates and exposure to host secretions differ. It is the conjunction of chemical conditions and microbial capability that determines the dominant reactions at each site.

The host spends resources to sustain this arrangement. The epithelium is continually renewed, mucus is secreted and immune surveillance is maintained. Nutrient recovery may offset some of the cost of maintaining an inhabited digestive tract, but the host is not guaranteed a net benefit from every individual microbe. Microbial growth can consume compounds that the animal might otherwise absorb, and microbial enzymes can generate products whose effects depend on concentration. When evaluating a proposed symbiotic function, compare the benefit to the host with the consequences of a relevant alternative, rather than assuming that residence alone proves mutualism.

Consider a substance released into the distal colon in an experimental animal. A researcher detects it in intestinal contents and finds that animals with more of the substance have higher expression of an epithelial gene. These observations establish two associations but leave several causal alternatives. The substance may directly regulate transcription; the same microbial population may make another active product; a host change may increase both gene expression and metabolite retention. To distinguish these alternatives, a suitable comparison must manipulate the substance independently or alter the host's capacity to detect it. This elementary distinction between source, exposure and response underlies almost every microbiome physiology question.

A useful way to describe the intestinal system is to draw a boundary around the host and account for what crosses it. Carbon entering in food can be absorbed directly, converted into microbial biomass, retained temporarily in a metabolite pool, expelled as gas or recovered in faecal material. A host–microbe interaction becomes a nutritional function only when it changes the fate of such material in a way that is relevant to the animal. This approach prevents a common confusion between disappearance of a substrate from the lumen and appearance of usable material in host tissue. The substrate may have disappeared because it was consumed by a bacterium, without the animal receiving its carbon directly.

The importance of a microbial transformation also depends on the host's physiological state. A metabolite that contributes only a small fraction of whole-body energy may still be a substantial fuel for the cell population immediately adjacent to its production site. Conversely, a substance that is quantitatively important in energy recovery need not reach high enough concentrations in every organ to act as a regulator there. Local physiological significance, systemic flux and whole-animal energy contribution are separate quantities. They should be compared using the appropriate tissue and time scale.

Classical nutrition experiments first established the importance of nutrient intake, digestion and balance. Studies in animals raised without microorganisms subsequently provided a way to examine whether some processes ordinarily regarded as intrinsic to the host actually require microbial contributions. Modern chemical and genetic measurements then made it possible to test individual metabolic pathways within a community. These approaches complement one another: balance studies quantify overall consequences, controlled colonisation changes the community, and molecular measurements help identify the intervening reaction. The progression from a whole-animal observation to a specific mechanism is a recurring feature of physiological research.

2. Substrate availability and microbial carbohydrate degradation

Digestion in the upper gastrointestinal tract reduces many dietary macromolecules to absorbable units. Human salivary and pancreatic alpha-amylases hydrolyse internal alpha-1,4 glycosidic linkages in accessible starch, whereas intestinal brush-border enzymes complete the processing of many smaller carbohydrates. Cellulose contains beta-1,4 linkages between glucose residues, a structural difference that changes the enzymes required to hydrolyse it. Humans do not secrete a digestive cellulase capable of efficiently converting dietary cellulose into absorbable glucose. Microbial species equipped with suitable carbohydrate-active enzymes can attack some otherwise inaccessible polysaccharides, although the extent of degradation depends strongly on the physical organisation of the substrate.

Dietary fibre is not one chemically uniform substance. Pectins, hemicelluloses, fructans, resistant starches and cellulose differ in solubility, molecular structure, particle arrangement and susceptibility to microbial attack. Resistant starch is starch that resists digestion in the small intestine under particular conditions; this can reflect crystalline structure, retrogradation after processing, or physical encapsulation. Therefore the classification of a carbohydrate as resistant is partly a property of its interaction with the digestive system rather than simply the presence of one particular bond.

Microbial degradation can require extracellular enzymes, cell-surface complexes and transport systems that bring released fragments into the cell. One bacterial population may adhere to a particle and release oligosaccharides; another may grow using the smaller compounds without being capable of initiating breakdown itself. The structure of the original food thus shapes not only total fermentation but the spatial arrangement of microbial metabolism. Grinding or cooking can change accessibility without changing the identity of every sugar residue.

A decrease in fermentable substrate delivery to the colon may arise even when the total mass of food eaten is unchanged. Imagine replacing a poorly absorbed carbohydrate with an equal mass of glucose that is efficiently absorbed proximally. The latter provides energy to the host directly, but less carbon reaches the distal microbial community. Conversely, a rise in fermentable substrate may stimulate selected microorganisms rather than all organisms equally. If one required degradative function is absent, the remaining organisms may respond slowly or not at all. The prediction therefore depends on both the substrate and the resident functional community.

The host also supplies substrates. Mucins contain extensively glycosylated protein domains, and shed epithelial cells and digestive secretions contribute additional material. A bacterium that uses mucin-derived carbohydrates does not necessarily cause barrier damage under normal conditions, because mucus is continuously renewed and microbial utilisation can occur in a spatially controlled outer layer. The outcome changes if degradation exceeds renewal or if protective separation between bacteria and epithelium becomes compromised. A low dietary substrate supply can alter the relative importance of host-derived substrates, but the magnitude and consequences vary by animal, community and diet.

Plant tissues present a physical as well as a chemical obstacle. Cellulose microfibrils are embedded in matrices containing other polysaccharides and, in some tissues, lignin. An enzyme may hydrolyse a purified polymer in solution yet have little access to the same linkage inside an intact cell wall. Microbial cooperation may therefore involve sequential removal of structural components that expose previously shielded substrates. A student who predicts fermentation solely from the molecular formula of a carbohydrate misses the effects of particle surface, porosity and accessibility.

A classic example of a specialised degrader is Ruminococcus bromii. Ze and colleagues studied the contribution of this organism to resistant starch utilisation and showed enhanced fermentation when it was introduced into a particular mixed faecal community with limited starch degradation [9]. The design addressed a mechanistic question that simple abundance correlations could not resolve: could adding a population with the relevant function change substrate disappearance? The result supports the idea that a specialised member can enable community-level processing of a particulate carbohydrate. It does not demonstrate that this one species is necessary for every resistant starch in every individual. Other communities may contain organisms with overlapping capabilities.

Different chemical forms of the same nutrient can create distinct physiological outcomes. A small soluble carbohydrate may be readily absorbed or rapidly fermented in the proximal colon, while a slowly accessible particulate carbohydrate can persist farther distally. Two meals with equal total fermentable carbon could therefore differ in the location and timing of organic acid production. If the study endpoint is a distal mucosal cell response, the spatial delivery of the substrate may matter as much as its total mass. This principle also explains why an in vitro fermentation vessel cannot reproduce every spatial feature of the intestinal tract.

Substrate identity is not restricted to carbohydrates. Proteins and amino acids that reach the distal intestine may be metabolised by microbes, yielding ammonia, branched-chain fatty acids and a variety of other compounds. Some products can be further used by neighbouring organisms, while others enter the host or are eliminated. Because the assigned scope centres on the physiological contribution of carbohydrate fermentation, it is sufficient to recognise that organic acid production is not an exclusive marker of carbohydrate processing. Branched-chain acids can be useful clues to amino acid metabolism, but their measured concentrations still reflect multiple rates of production and removal.

The residence time of a substrate can determine the fraction degraded even when microbial enzyme capacity remains unchanged. Consider a simplified first-order model in which the amount of accessible substrate S decreases according to dS/dt = −kS, where k is a positive rate constant with units of inverse time. Integration gives S(t) = S(0)e to the power −kt. If k is 0.2 per hour and a particle remains in a compartment for five hours, the model predicts that about 37% of its initially accessible substrate remains. At ten hours, about 14% remains. This does not describe all fibre fermentation, because microbial growth, saturation and changes in accessibility can make the rate nonconstant. It illustrates why alterations in transit can produce different fermentation outcomes even at equal intake.

Spatial differences in substrate availability also influence which microbial populations encounter one another. An organism attached to a food particle experiences a different environment from an organism living predominantly in the liquid phase. If products diffuse away or are rapidly consumed, the organisms contributing successive reactions need not be present in equal abundance. Some degradation products may be captured close to their source, whereas others spread into the surrounding lumen. A stool sample integrates material shed or transported from many locations; it does not necessarily preserve the microscopic associations that made a metabolic interaction efficient.

The accessibility of a microbial enzyme's substrate is also affected by the host's earlier digestive reactions. For example, incomplete hydrolysis of a starch particle can expose internal surfaces to microbial enzymes. The quantity reaching the large intestine is determined by upper intestinal processes, not only by the original composition of food. In comparing two animals, apparent differences in microbial fermentation can therefore be caused upstream by differences in host digestion or absorption. Directly measuring the residual material entering the fermentation compartment helps distinguish a difference in microbial capacity from a difference in substrate delivery.

3. Anaerobic fermentation and metabolic cross-feeding

A cell obtains usable energy by coupling favourable chemical transformations to ATP synthesis. When oxygen is scarce, many intestinal microorganisms cannot rely on oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor. Fermentation provides a means of maintaining redox balance while obtaining ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation. Glycolysis converts hexose into pyruvate and produces reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Unless the reduced cofactor is reoxidised, glycolytic reactions cannot continue indefinitely. Different fermentation pathways use organic intermediates as electron acceptors, generating products that differ in oxidation state and structure.

Acetate, propionate and butyrate are the principal short-chain fatty acids considered here. They contain two, three and four carbon atoms respectively. Lactate and succinate are important intermediate or alternative products; they need not accumulate if other community members consume them. Carbon dioxide and hydrogen can also arise. No single equation represents the combined metabolism of the entire colon because different bacteria use different substrates, routes and end products. Even within one population the partition of carbon can respond to growth conditions.

Cross-feeding occurs when the metabolic product of one organism serves as a substrate for another. Consider a primary degrader that releases sugars from resistant starch and ferments them to lactate and acetate. A second organism can use these compounds in a butyrate-producing pathway. In such an arrangement, butyrate production depends on the activity of more than one metabolic participant. Removal of the primary degrader can reduce butyrate even if the terminal butyrate producer survives. Removal of the terminal producer can instead allow intermediate compounds to accumulate. Measuring the abundances of these organisms without measuring metabolites cannot distinguish these alternatives reliably.

Some microbial fermentation reactions generate hydrogen. Accumulation of hydrogen can constrain the thermodynamics of reactions that release it. Hydrogen-consuming methanogenic archaea, acetogenic bacteria or sulfate-reducing organisms can alter these conditions. The ecological outcome depends on available electron acceptors and substrates. For instance, methanogens may use hydrogen and carbon dioxide to generate methane, whereas sulfate reducers can generate sulfide. The same carbon supply can therefore produce different gas and acid profiles in communities that differ in their hydrogen-consuming partners.

Cross-feeding illustrates why microbial functional redundancy is neither absolute nor absent. Two taxonomically distinct organisms may possess related biochemical functions, allowing one to replace another after a perturbation. However, organisms that produce the same terminal compound may differ in their requirements for substrates, spatial niches and cofactors. Loss of a rare but specialised primary degrader can eliminate the supply of a substrate needed by several abundant organisms. A physiologist must therefore distinguish redundancy at the level of an enzyme, redundancy at the level of a pathway and redundancy of the community-level output under a specified environment.

The biochemical diversity of organic acid formation can be understood by comparing branch points rather than memorising a single universal fermentation pathway. Pyruvate may be converted to acetyl-CoA, from which acetate production through acetyl phosphate can support substrate-level phosphorylation. Butyrate formation involves the assembly of two acetyl-CoA-derived units into a four-carbon intermediate, followed by reduction steps. Some gut bacteria use a butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase reaction at the terminal stage, coupling butyrate release to acetate utilisation. Other organisms use alternative terminal reactions. Thus acetate can be both a fermentation product and a substrate supporting further product formation.

Propionate can be formed through several microbial routes. One representative route passes through succinate-related intermediates, whereas other organisms can use lactate-related or deoxy-sugar-derived routes. The existence of multiple pathways means that an increase in propionate does not uniquely identify a particular organism. A community can shift the pathway used to make a molecule without a proportional change in the total molecule recovered. At the exam level, it is more useful to recognise precursor-product relationships and alternative routes than to memorise every microbial enzyme.

The term anaerobic does not imply that all intestinal organisms obtain ATP exclusively by fermentation. Some organisms carry out respiration using electron acceptors other than oxygen when these are available. Methanogenic archaea conserve energy through reactions distinct from bacterial carbohydrate fermentation. A facultative anaerobe may switch between respiratory and fermentative modes as the local environment changes. These distinctions explain why changing electron acceptor availability can change community composition even when the supply of organic carbon is unchanged.

In an illustrative two-species culture, organism X consumes polymer and releases lactate, while organism Y consumes lactate and produces butyrate. If X is grown alone, one expects substrate disappearance and lactate accumulation. Y alone, supplied only with intact polymer it cannot hydrolyse, may show little growth. The mixed culture can consume polymer and produce more butyrate than X alone. To distinguish a nutritional cross-feeding mechanism from an unknown growth-promoting signal, provide Y with the proposed intermediate in the absence of X. Restoration of growth and butyrate synthesis would support the intermediate as a sufficient substrate under those conditions. Direct transfer can be tested by isotopic labelling. The design remains an illustrative reasoning exercise rather than a report of measured rates.

A biochemical distinction worth emphasising is the fate of reducing equivalents during production of the terminal metabolites. Complete aerobic oxidation of carbohydrate can transfer electrons ultimately to oxygen, whereas fermentation retains considerable chemical energy in reduced organic products. The presence of energy in those products is precisely what makes them useful to another organism or to the host. Microbial fermentation does not merely destroy dietary carbohydrate; it partitions its carbon and free energy among microbial ATP production, biomass and secreted products. The host may subsequently recover part of the energy retained in those products by oxidising them.

In a representative acetate-forming branch, phosphorylation of an intermediate creates a compound capable of transferring a phosphoryl group to ADP. This is substrate-level phosphorylation: the immediate energy donor is a high-energy metabolic intermediate rather than a proton gradient across a respiratory membrane. The student should distinguish ATP production from regeneration of NAD+. The former provides usable phosphoryl-transfer energy; the latter allows oxidation of substrate to continue. A pathway that supports both functions can sustain metabolism under conditions in which another electron acceptor is unavailable.

The amount of acid produced per unit of substrate consumed can be expressed as a yield, such as millimoles of product per millimole of substrate. A higher yield of one product need not correspond to greater total metabolic activity if other product pathways become less active. Suppose one culture consumes 5 mmol of a hexose-equivalent substrate and produces 4 mmol acetate, whereas another consumes 8 mmol and produces 5 mmol acetate. The second produces more acetate in absolute amount, but its acetate yield is 0.625 compared with 0.8 for the first. Both the substrate flux and the partition of carbon matter. Measurements of the full product spectrum provide a more complete explanation than ranking communities by one yield.

4. Short-chain fatty acids: fate, transport and carbon accounting

Acetate, propionate and butyrate are weak acids whose carboxyl groups can donate protons. At a pH above their pKa, a large fraction exists in the negatively charged form. Passive movement of the undissociated acid and carrier-mediated transport both contribute to epithelial handling of these molecules. Monocarboxylate transporters and sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporters provide examples of mechanisms by which cells move organic acid equivalents across membranes. A measured concentration of a short-chain fatty acid does not identify which of these mechanisms dominates under every experimental condition.

The fate of an absorbed short-chain fatty acid depends on its chemical identity and the cells that encounter it. Much butyrate is metabolised locally by differentiated colonocytes. Propionate reaching the liver can contribute carbon to gluconeogenic pathways; acetate can enter acetyl-CoA-dependent metabolism in several tissues. These are characteristic metabolic tendencies rather than exclusive destinations. The fraction extracted by epithelium and liver influences how much reaches systemic tissues. For this reason a luminal metabolite concentration is not a reliable substitute for its systemic concentration.

Carbon accounting provides an elementary quantitative framework for studying fermentation. Consider an illustrative closed vessel containing labelled glucose-equivalent substrate. If two millimoles of hexose are metabolised, twelve millimoles of labelled carbon atoms have been supplied. Suppose two millimoles of acetate, one millimole of propionate and one millimole of butyrate are recovered. These correspond to four, three and four millimoles of carbon atoms respectively. Eleven of the twelve millimoles of carbon have been recovered in the three acids; the remaining one may occur in biomass, carbon dioxide or other products. This example is a carbon balance, not a claim that the listed products constitute a fully balanced biochemical reaction. A complete reaction must also conserve hydrogen, oxygen, charge and reducing equivalents.

A second quantitative relationship concerns a flowing intestinal compartment. The amount of a metabolite in a compartment changes with time according to production plus inflow minus uptake, outflow and microbial consumption. When the amount remains approximately constant, these rates balance, but individual rates can be large. Imagine that production rises from ten to sixteen arbitrary amount units per hour and epithelial uptake rises from eight to fourteen while outflow remains two. Both situations can maintain the same pool despite markedly different transfer to the host. Conversely, epithelial uptake can fall while production stays constant, causing the luminal pool to increase. Therefore a higher concentration is not sufficient evidence for greater microbial production.

Concentration also depends on volume. A stool sample with a different water content can exhibit a different concentration even if the excreted amount over the sampling interval is similar. A sound experimental design distinguishes concentration, total amount and flux. Measuring labelled substrate incorporation into metabolites helps identify carbon origin, whereas measuring portal blood or uptake helps estimate transfer. These measurements answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The acid-base properties of SCFAs provide another quantitative example. For a monoprotic weak acid HA in equilibrium with H+ and A−, the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation is pH = pKa + log10([A−]/[HA]), provided activities can be approximated by concentrations. Taking an illustrative pKa of 4.8, at pH 6.8 the ratio of dissociated to undissociated acid is 10 squared, or 100. The undissociated fraction is therefore 1/(100+1), approximately 0.99%. At pH 5.8 the ratio is 10 and the undissociated fraction is about 9.1%. A fall in pH can consequently increase the fraction of acid in a form more able to cross a lipid bilayer by simple diffusion. This prediction does not itself quantify total epithelial transport because carriers, gradients and metabolism also contribute.

A second consideration is the relationship between epithelial utilisation and blood concentration. If an intestinal region absorbs ten micromoles of a compound each minute and metabolises six micromoles locally, only four micromoles per minute remain available to leave the region as that unchanged compound, assuming no other sources or sinks. If blood flow through the region is 20 millilitres per minute, the resulting arteriovenous concentration difference would be 0.2 micromoles per millilitre, or 0.2 millimoles per litre, under the simplifying assumption of steady state and a well-mixed blood compartment. This calculation illustrates why blood concentration alone does not reveal epithelial uptake: local transformation can substantially alter the net export of the parent molecule.

Acetate provides two-carbon units that can enter acetyl-CoA metabolism, but mammals cannot obtain net glucose from acetyl-CoA through the ordinary citric acid cycle. The cycle releases carbon dioxide and regenerates oxaloacetate rather than providing a net accumulation of oxaloacetate from acetyl-CoA alone. Propionate can enter metabolism through propionyl-CoA, methylmalonyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, providing a route toward gluconeogenic intermediates in suitable tissues. These carbon pathway distinctions are particularly important in foregut fermenters, where propionate is an important gluconeogenic precursor. Detailed enzyme regulation belongs to biomolecular metabolism, but the differing physiological fate of fermentation products follows directly from their carbon skeletons.

An acid appearing in the portal circulation may be converted by the liver into other molecules. If an isotope is subsequently detected in a host lipid, it is not necessary for the intact acid to have reached the final tissue. The label can pass through an intermediate metabolic pool. Conversely, detection of labelled carbon in a metabolite does not show that the metabolite entered host cells unchanged. Interpretation depends on which compound is measured, the time of collection and whether the labelled atom remains in its original chemical context.

Host extraction of a metabolite can be understood using a simplified fractional model. If the intestinal epithelium removes 70% of an absorbed product through local metabolism and the liver removes 50% of what remains, then 15% of the original absorbed amount reaches the systemic circulation unchanged: the remaining fractions multiply, 0.30 × 0.50. If the local fraction is instead only 20%, systemic delivery becomes 0.80 × 0.50 = 40%. These hypothetical values demonstrate how a change at one tissue can strongly change systemic exposure without any change in microbial production. They are not universal extraction percentages for short-chain fatty acids.

The biological effect of acidification also depends on buffering. Adding an organic acid to a strongly buffered environment may cause only a modest pH shift compared with adding the same amount to a poorly buffered environment. Bicarbonate and other solutes contribute to acid-base conditions in intestinal compartments. Consequently, a study that observes a microbial growth response after adding an acid should distinguish an effect of the acid molecule from an effect of the resulting pH. A pH-matched control and a control containing an equivalent concentration of a suitable salt can help disentangle the alternatives. The controls must be chosen with attention to ion composition and osmotic effects.

Short-chain fatty acids contribute to the mixture of osmotic and chemical signals encountered by epithelial cells. Their movement can be accompanied by changes in proton or sodium handling, depending on the transport mechanism. One cannot infer the net direction of water movement from an increase in acid concentration alone because other solute concentrations, epithelial transport and luminal water content also influence the gradients. Host digestion and absorptive mechanisms are treated in the preceding subunit; the important connection is that microbial products participate in the same physical constraints as other intestinal solutes.

5. Butyrate and the energetics of the colonic epithelium

The colonic epithelium consists of a continuously renewed sheet of cells that separates the microbial lumen from underlying tissue. Differentiated absorptive colonocytes expend ATP on ion transport, maintenance of intracellular composition and epithelial integrity. Although many mammalian tissues use glucose extensively, differentiated colonocytes can derive substantial energy from microbial butyrate. Butyrate is converted to an activated acyl-CoA derivative and oxidised through mitochondrial metabolism. The acetyl-CoA formed can enter the citric acid cycle; reducing equivalents support oxidative phosphorylation when oxygen is available at the cellular level.

The juxtaposition of an anaerobic lumen and oxygen-consuming epithelial cells is physiologically significant. Oxygen is supplied from the blood side of the intestinal wall. Its consumption by cells helps maintain the gradient towards the lumen. A colonocyte that increases oxidative metabolism can consume more oxygen, potentially reducing the amount available to facultative anaerobes near the epithelial surface. The absence of abundant oxygen in the lumen does not mean that epithelial mitochondria have no oxygen supply.

Donohoe and colleagues studied the relation between microbial colonisation and epithelial energetics in mammalian colon. Comparisons of germ-free and colonised animals helped distinguish effects of microbial absence from ordinary dietary energy provision. Their work showed that provision of butyrate could rescue aspects of the energetic disturbance in germ-free colonocytes, supporting a fuel-dependent explanation [1]. This evidence is stronger than an observation that an abundance of a bacterial species correlates with epithelial ATP. It connects a defined microbial metabolite to a functional cellular outcome in an experimental model. Nevertheless, a germ-free animal develops in a different microbial environment from birth, and the experiment does not quantify the contribution of butyrate to every human epithelial state.

Cell differentiation is central to interpreting the biological effects of this metabolite. A mature colonocyte with active oxidative metabolism can remove substantial butyrate before it reaches cells deeper in crypts. A rapidly proliferating cell with a different metabolic programme can experience different intracellular exposure. Thus a concentration that supplies fuel to one cell type may alter proliferation or gene regulation in another. The existence of an effect on epithelial proliferation does not invalidate the nutritional role of butyrate; it reveals that tissue architecture and metabolic state modify the effective signal.

A useful experimental distinction is between energy rescue and signalling rescue. If an intervention restores ATP while leaving a particular receptor inactive, a fuel pathway remains plausible. If another intervention activates a receptor without providing appreciable oxidisable carbon, it can test a distinct signalling mechanism. Neither observation automatically excludes additional effects. For example, mitochondrial metabolism, membrane receptor signalling and changes in chromatin can occur in parallel. Appropriate controls must separate the endpoints rather than expecting one metabolic product to have only one mode of action.

Energy demand in the epithelium is continuous because absorptive transport must operate against gradients. ATP hydrolysis by the sodium-potassium pump supports ionic conditions that indirectly drive many transport systems. Membrane turnover, protein synthesis and cellular repair also require energy. An intervention that changes availability of oxidative fuel may therefore affect barrier properties indirectly through energetic constraints even if it never binds a receptor. To demonstrate this chain experimentally, it is important to measure both metabolic endpoints and barrier outcomes rather than infer the latter from ATP alone.

Mitochondrial oxidation of a fatty acid involves more than converting its carbon skeleton into acetyl-CoA. Electrons transferred to NAD+ and flavin-linked carriers ultimately enter respiratory pathways. Oxygen reduction at the terminal step supports maintenance of a proton electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. ATP synthase uses that gradient to couple proton movement to ATP formation. This chain clarifies why an anaerobic microbial product can support an aerobic host process: fermentation in the lumen and oxidative phosphorylation in the epithelium occur in distinct cellular compartments with different electron acceptor availability.

The energy-related effects of butyrate need to be distinguished from its effects on gene regulation. A nonmetabolisable compound that inhibits a histone deacetylase can serve as a conceptual comparator for butyrate in a cell model. If both compounds alter a transcriptional endpoint but only butyrate restores oxidative energy production, the result supports separation of those endpoints. A second comparator might provide an alternative mitochondrial substrate without reproducing receptor activation. Such controls allow a physiologist to ask whether an observed effect depends on metabolism or ligand detection. Actual experimental interpretation must account for off-target effects, effective intracellular concentration and cell viability.

The colonic crypt creates spatial variation in exposure. Cells near the luminal surface and cells deeper in the crypt differ in differentiation and access to luminal compounds. Consumption of a metabolite by surface cells can reduce its delivery to deeper cells, just as extraction of a circulating hormone by tissue can change its downstream availability. If the surface layer is damaged, both the physical diffusion distance and the metabolic consumption along that route can change. An identical luminal concentration may then have a different effect on proliferative cells. The physiological state of the tissue is part of the explanation, not an incidental background condition.

The metabolic oxygen gradient can be analysed as the outcome of diffusion and consumption. Oxygen supplied by mucosal blood moves along a concentration gradient toward tissues with lower oxygen activity. If the epithelium consumes much of that oxygen, little may reach the luminal side. A reduction in epithelial consumption can therefore alter oxygen delivery to microbes even if blood oxygen concentration is unchanged. Conversely, reduced blood perfusion can lower tissue oxygen availability even when consumption is high. The direction of an observed change in luminal oxygen cannot be interpreted without considering both supply and removal.

Hypoxia-inducible factors participate in the response of cells to oxygen-dependent changes in protein stability and gene regulation. In the intestinal mucosa, physiological low-oxygen conditions can help support regulatory responses related to barrier protection. The word hypoxia must nevertheless be interpreted with care: a viable epithelium maintaining a normal oxygen gradient is not equivalent to an ischaemic epithelium experiencing insufficient oxygen delivery. A factor that stabilises a protective transcriptional programme under one condition may not compensate for severe failure of ATP supply under another.

In an experimental comparison, oxygen consumption rate can be measured alongside ATP content. A higher oxygen consumption rate does not necessarily imply an exactly proportional increase in ATP because mitochondrial coupling efficiency, substrate identity and energy demand may differ. If oxygen consumption rises but ATP remains unchanged, it is possible that ATP turnover has increased or that some respiration is not efficiently coupled to ATP synthesis. Measuring cell viability and other aspects of mitochondrial function helps interpret such a result. At this level the aim is not to master specialist metabolic instrumentation but to avoid treating a single readout as a complete account of cellular energetics.

6. SCFA sensing by receptors and chromatin-associated mechanisms

An organic acid can affect a target cell without being consumed as a major fuel. Cell-surface receptors detect extracellular ligands and change intracellular signalling. Free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2, also called GPR43) and free fatty acid receptor 3 (FFAR3, also called GPR41) are G-protein-coupled receptors that respond to short-chain fatty acids. Their ligand sensitivities overlap, but they are not identical. FFAR2 can couple to Gi/o and Gq/11 pathways in suitable cellular contexts, whereas FFAR3 is principally associated with Gi/o signalling. Gi/o signalling can suppress adenylyl cyclase activity, while Gq/11 signalling can increase phospholipase C-dependent signals. The net physiological effect depends on which receptor is present, the available downstream machinery and other simultaneous stimuli.

Enteroendocrine cells translate chemical information from the intestinal environment into endocrine signals. Some populations can respond to short-chain fatty acids by altering secretion of peptide hormones such as peptide YY or glucagon-like peptide-1. These hormones participate in communication between the gut and other organs. The full mechanisms of appetite and systemic energy balance belong to later nodes; the important principle here is that a bacterial fermentation product can become an upstream input to an animal endocrine circuit. Hormone release depends on ligand exposure and cell type, not simply on the number of bacteria in the intestine.

A second mechanism involves histone deacetylases, which remove acetyl groups from proteins including histones. Histone acetylation can influence chromatin organisation and accessibility, although the effect on transcription varies with locus and cellular context. Butyrate and, to a lesser extent under many experimental conditions, propionate can inhibit some histone deacetylase activities. This is an intracellular biochemical mechanism distinct from activation of a G-protein-coupled receptor. Delivery to the cell, intracellular metabolism and effective concentration are therefore essential considerations.

Furusawa and colleagues reported that microbe-derived butyrate promoted colonic regulatory T-cell differentiation in experimental systems and linked the result to histone acetylation at regulatory regions of Foxp3 [2]. The result illustrates a connection between a fermentation product and immune cell differentiation. It does not establish that any increase in faecal butyrate automatically improves human immune health or that every effect of butyrate is mediated through Foxp3. A mechanistic hypothesis is tested in specific cells under specified experimental conditions.

Consider receptor-deficient cells that retain the ability to take up butyrate. If a particular secretory response disappears after FFAR2 deletion but ATP production remains elevated, the observations support a receptor requirement for that secretory response and a distinct fuel contribution. If histone acetylation still increases, an intracellular action may remain intact. This type of experiment illustrates why the words necessary and sufficient must be attached to a specified endpoint: a receptor may be necessary for one response and unnecessary for another.

The distinction between membrane and intracellular action has consequences for experimental time courses. Receptor activation can alter second messenger concentrations within seconds or minutes. Hormone secretion can follow quickly if the target cell already contains stored peptide. A change in histone acetylation, transcription, protein abundance and cell differentiation can extend over a longer interval. These time scales overlap and are not universal; nevertheless, the order of observed changes can help identify a plausible mechanism. A rapid calcium signal before any measurable change in transcription is compatible with membrane receptor signalling.

Tolhurst and colleagues examined SCFA-dependent secretion of GLP-1 and investigated expression and function of SCFA receptors in enteroendocrine L-cell systems [10]. The work provides a teaching example of combining receptor expression data with functional cellular responses and genetic models. Expression alone establishes that the molecular machinery may be present. A ligand-induced response establishes that cells are capable of reacting under the experimental conditions. Altering the relevant receptor strengthens attribution of the response to that pathway. These levels of evidence should be distinguished when evaluating a claim about an endocrine signal.

A receptor concentration-response relation provides a useful mathematical model. Under simplifying equilibrium assumptions for a single independent binding site, fractional occupancy is L/(Kd+L), where L is free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant in the same units. At L = Kd, occupancy is one-half; at L = 3Kd, occupancy is three-quarters. Doubling ligand concentration does not necessarily double receptor occupancy, especially near saturation. In real cells with several ligands, receptor reserve and downstream amplification, the concentration producing half-maximal functional response need not equal Kd. The model clarifies why a change in microbial metabolite abundance may yield a small or large physiological response depending on the initial exposure and signalling state.

A histone modification is similarly not a universal switch. Acetylation at a particular regulatory region can be associated with an increased probability of transcription in a suitable regulatory context. But differentiation also depends on transcription factors, cytokine signals and developmental history. In a T-cell experiment, an increase in Foxp3-associated acetylation does not mean that histone acetylation alone creates the complete regulatory phenotype. Demonstrating a change in gene expression, cellular identity and suppressive function addresses different layers of evidence. The relevant conclusion is a contribution to differentiation under specified conditions, not a blanket classification of every SCFA as an immune suppressant.

A receptor can be present on only a subset of cells in an apparently uniform tissue. If two animals have identical average concentrations of a metabolite, differences in receptor-expressing cell populations may still produce different hormone outputs. Tissue-level measurements of receptor messenger RNA combine many cell types and do not necessarily indicate receptor density in the relevant secretory cells. A comparison between sorted cell populations, or another suitable cell-specific measurement, provides more direct information about the site of signalling. This is why the location of a receptor is part of the causal explanation.

Ligand concentration at a receptor need not equal the concentration in a bulk luminal sample. A metabolite may be absorbed, diluted in interstitial fluid or consumed by neighbouring cells before reaching a receptor on a particular membrane surface. An experiment that directly applies a high concentration to cultured cells bypasses several of these constraints. Such an experiment can establish that a response is possible, but its physiological relevance depends on whether comparable exposure occurs in the intact animal. The distinction between experimental potency and real physiological exposure is especially important for molecules that are rapidly metabolised.

A simplified antagonism experiment can help distinguish receptor pathways. Imagine measuring a calcium response after adding a short-chain fatty acid to a cell population. A selective receptor antagonist reduces the response, but a second pathway capable of changing calcium remains active. If the antagonist is truly selective and cell viability is preserved, the difference supports receptor participation. A genetic deletion provides a complementary test, yet long-term genetic changes can alter expression of other pathways. Agreement between independent approaches strengthens the inference. Disagreement does not automatically invalidate one experiment; it may reveal compensatory responses or differences in conditions.

7. Microbial vitamins and the difference between synthesis and host supply

Vitamins are organic micronutrients that support essential biochemical functions; many participate in coenzyme-mediated reactions. Animals cannot synthesise adequate quantities of all required vitamins, but their microbial partners possess a variety of vitamin biosynthetic pathways. Some intestinal bacteria can produce forms of vitamin K and several B-group vitamins, including thiamine, riboflavin, folate and biotin. Other microorganisms lack complete synthetic pathways and depend on environmental supply or products released by neighbouring species.

The benefit of microbial vitamin synthesis must be evaluated separately for the community and the host. Vitamin produced inside one bacterium may be retained for its own metabolism. It may be released through secretion, leakage or cell turnover, then consumed by another microorganism. Only the fraction that becomes accessible in an appropriate chemical form at a suitable intestinal absorptive surface can contribute to host nutrition. Thus production by an organism is not equivalent to net absorption by the animal.

Vitamin B12 provides a particularly instructive example. Dietary cobalamin absorption in humans depends on a specialised process involving intrinsic factor and receptor-mediated uptake in the terminal ileum. Much microbial cobalamin synthesis occurs in the colon, downstream from that major uptake site. In addition, some microbial corrinoids are not equivalent to the cobalamin forms required by human enzymes. It is therefore incorrect to conclude from the presence of B12 biosynthesis genes in faecal samples that dietary B12 requirements have disappeared. In animals that reingest faecal material, the spatial problem can be altered because microbial products can pass through the upstream absorptive tract again.

Vitamin K participates in the maturation of certain proteins through gamma-carboxylation, including proteins involved in haemostasis. Intestinal microorganisms can synthesise menaquinones, but their contribution to the host pool depends on chemical form, release and absorption, and cannot be assumed to replace dietary sources. The interaction between microbial micronutrient production and host uptake also differs among animal species, reflecting anatomical organisation and feeding behaviour.

A genetic inventory of vitamin pathways is best interpreted as functional potential. Measuring a vitamin in microbial cultures demonstrates production under those culture conditions. A labelled precursor that appears in a chemically identified vitamin recovered from host tissue provides stronger evidence of transfer. To determine whether that transfer has physiological significance, researchers must also assess a relevant endpoint such as a coenzyme-dependent reaction, biochemical status or restoration of function under carefully controlled nutrient supply. Different experiments answer successive links in the same causal chain.

Vitamin dependence can help explain community assembly independently of host nutrition. An organism that cannot synthesise a needed cofactor may survive where another species or the diet supplies it. Conversely, a biosynthetic pathway may impose an energetic cost on a producing organism. Under conditions with plentiful external vitamin, selection may favour organisms capable of using the available compound without making it themselves. This type of ecological dependence explains why the functional repertoire of the community cannot be obtained simply by summing the nutritional effects attributed to each species in isolation.

Folate illustrates the relationship between vitamin chemical identity and host biochemistry. Folate derivatives carry one-carbon units in biosynthetic reactions, including reactions relevant to nucleotide metabolism. A microorganism can synthesise folate-related compounds in forms that vary in cellular retention and accessibility. Detection of a compound that belongs to a vitamin family is therefore insufficient to quantify the usable cofactor pool in the host. Measurements should distinguish total family abundance from chemically defined forms, and they should identify whether the molecule has crossed the epithelial boundary.

The site of vitamin absorption is a particularly powerful comparative concept. A foregut microbial community releases compounds before material passes through much of the host's small intestine. A distal hindgut community releases compounds after several specialised uptake sites. In some animals, reingestion of material from the distal compartment provides another opportunity for absorption. This spatial difference does not alter the chemistry of a vitamin, but it can alter the nutritional value of its synthesis. It follows that the vitamin contribution of microbes cannot be generalised from one digestive architecture to another.

Consider a hypothetical stable-isotope experiment in which a precursor supplied to a colonised animal is incorporated into a vitamin molecule by microbial metabolism. The labelled vitamin is detected in stool but not in blood. That result supports production and excretion, but not measurable delivery to the systemic pool. If another experiment detects the labelled vitamin in the host, the strength of inference depends on excluding direct host synthesis from the precursor or alternative conversion routes. If the host is already vitamin-replete, an increase in transfer may not produce a measurable change in a physiological endpoint. Adequacy, transfer and functional limitation are separate questions.

A micronutrient balance must distinguish microbial synthesis from the host's requirements. Let M represent the amount of a chemically defined vitamin made by microbes over a day. If only a fraction r is released in accessible form and a fraction a of the released amount is absorbed, the host receives Mra from that source in a simplified model. For example, synthesis of 100 arbitrary units with 40% accessible release and 25% absorption produces 10 units of host delivery. A community synthesising 60 units with 80% release and 50% absorption produces 24 units of delivery. The larger biosynthetic output does not necessarily make the larger nutritional contribution.

Vitamin utilisation also depends on the host's pre-existing nutritional condition. A coenzyme-dependent enzyme may already be supplied with adequate cofactor, in which case a moderate increase in vitamin delivery has little effect on its activity. Under deficiency, a similar amount may have a different consequence. To show that a microbial product is important to a physiological function, it is therefore useful to consider the dose-response relation and starting nutritional state. These considerations do not support treating microbiome measurements as a clinical assessment of vitamin sufficiency.

The same chemical family can contain related molecules with different biological utility. This is especially relevant to microbial corrinoids, which can participate in diverse microbial reactions and can be exchanged within communities. Distinguishing a corrinoid's chemical identity from a generic signal of the vitamin family is necessary to assess compatibility with host enzymes. A bioassay using a microorganism with a particular cofactor requirement may respond to compounds that are not equally useful to mammalian metabolism. The measurement method should be aligned with the biological claim being made.

8. Bile salt transformation and signalling

Bile acids are steroid-derived molecules synthesised by the liver from cholesterol. The primary bile acids in humans include cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid. Conjugation with glycine or taurine produces bile salts that participate in the dispersion and absorption of lipids. Most bile acids are recovered in the distal small intestine and transported back to the liver through the portal circulation. A fraction nevertheless reaches the colon, where microbial enzymes alter their chemical structures. This cycle connects a liver product to microbial metabolism and then back to the host.

Bile salt hydrolase catalyses deconjugation by cleaving the amide linkage between a bile acid and its conjugated amino acid. This reaction does not itself remove a hydroxyl group from the steroid nucleus. A different microbial pathway, 7-alpha-dehydroxylation, converts cholic acid to deoxycholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid to lithocholic acid. Only particular organisms possess the relevant biochemical capacity. Deconjugation and secondary bile acid formation are therefore distinct functions that may be carried out by different members of a community.

Changing the structure of a bile acid alters more than its role in lipid emulsification. It can change solubility, interactions with membranes and ability to activate host receptors. Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a nuclear receptor sensitive to bile acids; in intestinal cells it contributes to regulation of signals involved in hepatic bile acid synthesis. TGR5, also called GPBAR1, is a membrane-associated G-protein-coupled receptor that commonly signals through Gs and cyclic AMP. These two receptors are neither structurally equivalent nor activated identically by all bile acids.

Intestinal FXR activation can promote production of fibroblast growth factor 19 in humans (the corresponding murine signal is FGF15). This endocrine signal travels to the liver, where it contributes to negative feedback on bile acid synthesis, including regulation of cholesterol 7-alpha-hydroxylase. Thus an alteration in microbial bile acid transformation can change the mixture of receptor ligands presented to the host and potentially modify feedback. The direction of the net effect cannot be predicted from the words secondary bile acid alone because individual bile acids have different receptor activities, and animal species have different bile acid pools.

Bile acids also influence microorganisms. Their detergent properties and other antimicrobial effects can constrain growth, while tolerant organisms may survive and modify them. A shift in host secretion or reabsorption can therefore change the microbial habitat, and altered microbial activity can in turn reshape the bile acid pool. This reciprocal interaction exemplifies host–microbe bidirectionality. It is an error to depict the animal as a passive recipient of microbial products.

Experimental separation of these mechanisms requires chemical specificity. Total bile acid concentration could remain constant even while the proportion of a receptor-active species changes. A researcher comparing communities should therefore distinguish conjugated primary acids, unconjugated acids and secondary products. Measuring a receptor-dependent gene or second messenger provides a more direct test of signalling than a measurement of total bile alone. Receptor-deficient models help test dependence, but changes in baseline bile acid physiology must be considered when interpreting them.

Conjugation of bile acids changes the relationship between their ionisation and digestive function. Conjugated bile salts remain relatively water compatible at intestinal pH and can participate in mixed micelle formation. Deconjugation changes their physicochemical behaviour and can affect the probability of reabsorption. Microbial transformation therefore has the potential to alter both the digestive pool and its signalling properties. However, most bile acids participate in repeated enterohepatic cycling; changes in one fraction can be buffered or amplified by hepatic synthesis and intestinal recovery.

A quantitative mass balance for the circulating bile acid pool distinguishes synthesis from repeated secretion. Suppose an illustrative pool contains 3 grams and undergoes five effective cycles during a day, giving 15 grams of cumulative intestinal delivery in the simplified model. If the net loss is 0.5 grams per day, maintenance of a stable pool requires synthesis of approximately 0.5 grams per day, not 15 grams. Cycling counts repeated use of the same molecules, whereas synthesis replaces losses. If a microbial reaction changes the efficiency of recovery, the liver may alter synthesis through feedback. The resulting pool size cannot be predicted solely from the amount initially transformed.

The FXR–FGF19 feedback loop illustrates an endocrine negative feedback mechanism. A change in receptor activation in the intestine may alter expression and secretion of a signal that influences enzyme activity or expression in the liver. If hepatic synthesis falls, the composition and amount of bile reaching the intestine may change in subsequent cycles. This creates a circuit in which the intestinal microbial environment can both influence and be influenced by a host-controlled chemical pool. In mice the related signal is FGF15, so an exam question that uses a murine model should not automatically substitute the human nomenclature.

TGR5-mediated signalling operates on a different timescale and through a different receptor architecture. Binding of appropriate bile acids can alter cyclic AMP signalling in receptor-expressing cells. In selected enteroendocrine contexts it can contribute to hormone release. A nuclear receptor reporter assay and a cyclic AMP assay consequently assess different proximal responses. If both responses change in an animal after altering the microbiota, it is possible that the new bile acid mixture affects both receptors, but the relative contributions require targeted testing. The coexistence of two sensors does not imply identical affinity, cellular distribution or downstream function.

Bile acids have a spatially distributed life cycle. The liver establishes much of the primary pool, the intestine receives that pool during digestion, and transport systems recover much of it for recirculation. Microbial transformation can occur where organisms encounter the molecules, including the portion that escapes early recovery. Consequently, a change in intestinal transit or in host transport can alter the opportunity for transformation independently of microbial enzyme expression. The availability of a substrate to a bacterial pathway is itself regulated by the host.

A simple experiment could compare two microbial communities exposed to equal amounts of a conjugated primary bile salt. The first produces a large amount of unconjugated primary acid but little secondary acid; the second produces both. The difference suggests that the first possesses effective deconjugation capacity but lacks, or does not express under these conditions, a downstream transformation. To determine whether the difference arises from distinct populations or different expression levels, measure the relevant functional capacity and the chemical products. The chemical profile is more informative than assigning both communities the general label bile acid metabolising.

Hydrophobicity can influence the effects of bile acids on cells. Some more hydrophobic bile acids interact strongly with membranes and may cause injury at sufficiently high exposures, yet they can also participate in normal physiological signalling at other concentrations. The biological effects are therefore concentration- and context-dependent. A receptor-mediated protective response observed at one exposure cannot be generalised into a claim that increasing the same molecule indefinitely is advantageous. Similarly, receptor activation does not by itself prove that the net effect on whole-animal metabolism is beneficial.

9. Epithelial and mucosal barrier physiology

The intestinal barrier is not a single membrane. It consists of an organised epithelial cell layer, junctional complexes connecting adjacent cells, overlying mucus, locally secreted protective molecules and immune components. Together these elements permit nutrient uptake while limiting inappropriate contact between luminal material and underlying tissues. A barrier is not maximally effective when it blocks all exchange; its physiological purpose is selective permeability.

Goblet cells secrete mucins, including the gel-forming mucin MUC2 that contributes to the colonic mucus architecture. The mucus provides a hydrated physical environment and a substrate for selected microbial activities. A relatively bacteria-excluding inner layer has been well characterised in healthy mouse distal colon; the arrangement differs along the gastrointestinal tract and across species. It is consequently misleading to imagine that the entire gut contains a uniform layer of mucus completely free from microorganisms.

Tight junctions near the apical region of epithelial cells regulate the paracellular movement of water, ions and other substances. Claudins are important determinants of permeability selectivity, and proteins such as occludin and zonula occludens-associated scaffolds contribute to junctional organisation. An alteration in junctional protein abundance does not necessarily predict the passage of every molecule, because localisation, assembly and specific pore properties also matter. A change in permeability to small ions may occur without equivalent leakage of large macromolecules.

Short-chain fatty acids can influence epithelial energetics and regulatory pathways that affect barrier function. Butyrate oxidation can sustain ATP-dependent functions, while regulatory responses may modify junctional organisation. Physiological hypoxia within the mucosa can participate in protective gene regulation, but severe oxygen deprivation or inflammatory injury has different consequences. A mechanistic account must distinguish a regulated oxygen gradient from pathological tissue hypoxia.

Transepithelial electrical resistance, commonly abbreviated TEER, measures resistance to ionic current across an epithelial preparation after appropriate correction for blank supports and surface area. A higher value generally indicates lower ionic conductance under the same measurement conditions. If measured resistance is 220 ohms, the blank support contributes 40 ohms and epithelial surface area is 0.5 square centimetres, the corrected area-normalised resistance is (220 − 40) × 0.5 = 90 ohm square centimetres. A comparison group with a corrected value of 45 ohm square centimetres has half the area-normalised resistance. This does not establish that the first group passes half as much of every solute.

A complementary tracer assay measures passage of a defined molecular probe across the barrier. If TEER increases but a large tracer crosses at an unchanged rate, the results need not contradict one another. They may reflect different permeability routes or limited sensitivity. Histological observations, junctional localisation, cell viability and metabolite measurements can strengthen interpretation. No single barrier measurement demonstrates all aspects of intestinal integrity.

Mucus thickness reflects the balance between synthesis, secretion, expansion, degradation and removal. If the measured mucus layer becomes thinner after a dietary change, the observation does not reveal which term in this balance has altered. Desai and colleagues used a defined microbiota in a mouse model to study the interaction between dietary fibre deprivation and mucus degradation [11]. Their findings support an ecological route connecting substrate deprivation with diminished protection. Other model conditions may reveal a stronger role for reduced mucus production [12]. The contrast is instructive: identical structural outcomes can arise through different changes in production and loss, and an appropriate mechanistic study measures both.

A mucosal barrier can be assessed from several directions. Morphology may reveal gaps, altered cell organisation or reduced mucus thickness; functional tracer experiments measure transport; localisation of junction-associated proteins can indicate changes in architecture; immune measurements can detect downstream exposure. A visually intact epithelium may still possess abnormal permeability, while a biochemical change in a junctional protein may not have a detectable functional consequence. Concordant changes across complementary measures increase confidence that the observed defect is physiologically meaningful.

For a simplified tracer experiment, define apparent permeability P = (dQ/dt)/(A × C0), where dQ/dt is the rate of appearance of tracer on the receiving side, A is surface area and C0 is the starting donor concentration. If 0.02 micromoles appear each minute across 1 square centimetre from a donor concentration of 2 micromoles per cubic centimetre, P = 0.01 centimetres per minute under the assumed constant concentration and sink conditions. This is a simplified flux-derived measure, not a general law valid for every intestinal solute. Active transport, metabolism, accumulation in the receiver and changing donor concentration can complicate interpretation. Unlike TEER, it refers to the particular tracer used.

When resistance and tracer measurements disagree, consider the physical size and charge of the tracer, the pathway used and the condition of the epithelial preparation. An increase in ion-selective pore resistance may have little effect on a larger molecule that crosses primarily through a different pathway. A damaged cell layer can change both measures, but the magnitude and timing can differ. The correct answer to a data interpretation problem is often that the observations support a specific change but do not justify a claim of complete barrier restoration.

Epithelial renewal places an additional constraint on barrier homeostasis. Cells lost from the surface must be replaced by newly differentiated cells while junctions and polarity are maintained. Damage that increases the rate of cell loss can challenge this balance even if mucus and antimicrobial peptide production remain initially unchanged. A metabolite that affects proliferation can therefore influence barrier repair differently from one that primarily alters the permeability of mature junctions. Measuring only cell division or only resistance misses the relationship between renewal and function.

Water movement and mucus hydration affect the observed geometry of the barrier. Mucin secretion does not automatically guarantee a layer with the same physical properties, because polymer expansion and hydration depend on the local chemical environment. A histological measurement of a fixed tissue may differ from the dimensions in living tissue. When comparing preparations, consistent sample handling and a functional measure are important. Such technical considerations are part of scientific interpretation rather than a reason to discard all morphological evidence.

An epithelial cell may encounter microbial components without bacterial entry into underlying tissue. Small molecules and soluble antigens can cross or be sampled by controlled routes, whereas whole microorganisms normally face a more substantial physical barrier. A report of increased microbial-derived material in blood therefore requires precise identification of what was measured. It does not necessarily establish the presence of live circulating bacteria. Distinguishing molecular exposure, viable organism translocation and a downstream inflammatory marker prevents overstatement of the physiological defect.

10. Immune maturation, tolerance and mucosal defence

The intestinal immune system confronts an unusual problem: abundant microorganisms inhabit a compartment directly adjacent to host tissues, yet continuous intense inflammatory activation would disrupt nutrient absorption and damage the epithelium. Immune development in the gut therefore involves both defensive capacity and regulation. Microbial signals contribute to the maturation and activity of immune populations, and the host simultaneously controls the location and abundance of its microbial partners.

Epithelial and innate immune cells possess pattern recognition receptors that can detect conserved microbial structures. The response to such signals depends on cellular location, receptor availability, barrier integrity and other contextual information. Microbial recognition is not synonymous with a pathological inflammatory response. A signal sensed in a controlled mucosal context can support protective mechanisms; exposure of the same microbial components to normally separated internal tissues can produce a different response. Compartmentalisation is therefore an essential aspect of mucosal immunity.

Antimicrobial peptides provide local chemical protection. Paneth cells in the small intestinal crypts are a representative source of defensins and other antimicrobial factors. Other epithelial populations also contribute to mucosal defence. These molecules do not sterilise the intestine; they can restrict access to sensitive regions, influence community organisation and provide protection against invading organisms. An antimicrobial factor may consequently alter both resident and pathogenic populations depending on their susceptibility.

Secretory immunoglobulin A provides another mechanism of control. Plasma cells in mucosal tissue produce polymeric IgA, which can be transported across epithelial cells by the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor. Released secretory IgA can bind luminal antigens and microbes, influence their retention in mucus and limit epithelial contact. This activity differs from simply killing all organisms recognised by the antibody. Binding can change microbial organisation while preserving a populated intestinal lumen.

Regulatory T cells help restrain inappropriate immune activation and maintain tolerance. Microbial antigens and metabolites can influence their development or activity. The butyrate study discussed earlier is a useful example of how a chemically defined microbial product can connect metabolism with immune differentiation. However, the microbiota also contributes to defensive immune maturation, so it should not be described as a system that only suppresses inflammation. An appropriate balance must permit responses to genuine invasion while limiting damaging responses to ordinary luminal exposure.

An exam question may compare germ-free animals, animals colonised with a defined community and germ-free animals exposed to a particular metabolite. If colonisation restores several immune endpoints but the metabolite restores only one, the metabolite may be sufficient for that measured response without reproducing the full effects of the community. Conversely, failure to reproduce an effect can reflect inadequate concentration, developmental timing or the need for multiple signals. This distinction prevents overinterpretation of a single rescue experiment.

The route by which luminal material is sampled influences the immune response. M cells in organised lymphoid regions facilitate transfer of particulate and soluble antigens toward underlying immune cells, whereas other epithelial and antigen-presenting cell populations participate in sampling through distinct mechanisms. These controlled routes allow information about luminal antigens to enter the immune system without requiring widespread loss of barrier integrity. Thus antigen recognition can coexist with maintenance of epithelial separation. A physiological immune response is not synonymous with the passage of large numbers of live microbes into systemic blood.

IgA function illustrates the relationship between specificity and spatial regulation. An antibody can bind a microbial surface molecule without triggering the same effector mechanisms as a circulating antibody in a different tissue. Secretory components can help protect mucosal antibody from degradation, and multivalent binding can affect the organisation of microbial particles. The resulting ecological effect depends on antibody specificity, affinity and the organism's surface properties. Some interactions restrict epithelial contact, whereas other interactions may facilitate persistence in a suitable mucosal niche [13]. The presence of IgA coating is not a simple classification of an organism as pathogenic.

Immune maturation depends on time as well as microbial exposure. A newly colonised animal may respond acutely to microbial molecules while its lymphoid tissue and cell populations change over a longer interval. A comparison of germ-free and conventionally raised animals therefore includes differences in developmental history. If a metabolite is supplied only in adulthood, restoration of an acute response may occur without full restoration of developmental features. The correct causal conclusion must specify the endpoint and time window. This idea prepares the more systematic treatment of gnotobiotic experiments in a later node.

The immune system also helps determine which microbial functions persist. Antimicrobial molecules can exclude organisms from specific locations, IgA can alter access to epithelial surfaces, and inflammatory mediators can change tissue and luminal chemistry. A community that supports a particular immune state may therefore be stabilised by that state, creating reciprocal causation. If inflammation is induced independently of the community, subsequent microbial shifts should not be mistaken for evidence that the community initiated the inflammation. Time-resolved measurements and independent perturbations of host and microbial variables are essential.

Microbial recognition and immune tolerance are dynamic rather than fixed states. The same epithelial population can respond differently to a microbial molecule when exposed simultaneously to tissue damage signals. Local cytokine environments influence antigen presentation and lymphocyte differentiation. Consequently, the physiological response to a microbial community depends not only on its antigens but also on the condition of the host tissue. An organism that is accommodated in an intact mucosal environment can become a source of inflammation when spatial separation or immune regulation changes.

A useful immune experiment compares animals with similar microbial burdens but altered antibody transport. If loss of polymeric immunoglobulin receptor increases microbial contact with epithelium without initially increasing the total number of organisms in the lumen, the result would support a role for secretory antibody in spatial organisation. A subsequent inflammatory response might arise from the changed location of organisms rather than from an initial increase in total load. Measurement of mucus-associated and luminal populations separately is therefore more informative than a single bulk count.

The establishment of a regulatory T-cell phenotype is not equivalent to complete suppression of all immune responses. Regulatory cells participate in control of excessive activation, while protective immunity still requires appropriately activated effector cells. The physiological outcome depends on the distribution and activity of several populations. If a metabolite increases the proportion of regulatory cells, the absolute number may increase, decrease or remain unchanged depending on the total lymphocyte population. Both proportions and absolute counts are relevant when assessing immune maturation. These considerations link intestinal physiology with the principles of cell population measurement addressed in the immune system and methods units.

11. Colonisation resistance and ecological feedback

Colonisation resistance describes the ability of an established microbial community and its host-associated environment to restrict establishment or expansion of other organisms. Several mechanisms may operate simultaneously. Resident organisms consume nutrients, occupy spatial niches, alter pH, produce inhibitory compounds and influence epithelial defences. The resulting resistance is an emergent property of an ecological and physiological system rather than a direct consequence of high species richness alone.

Competition requires overlap in limiting resources. A newly arriving organism may fail to grow because a resident population consumes its preferred substrate. If the invader uses a different substrate or can exploit an environmental change, competition may become less effective. Some organisms produce bacteriocins or other inhibitory molecules, but susceptibility depends on the target. Organic acid accumulation can change pH and impose metabolic constraints, although acid tolerance varies across species.

The host epithelial metabolism provides an example of indirect colonisation resistance. Byndloss and colleagues investigated a pathway in mice linking microbial signals, epithelial PPAR-gamma activity and restriction of respiratory resources that can support expansion of Enterobacteriaceae [3]. The key conceptual point is that a microbial community can influence the competitive environment through its effects on host metabolism. Loss of a resident microbial function can change oxygen or nitrate availability, creating conditions favourable to organisms able to use those electron acceptors. This is more than direct competition for a sugar.

Not all members of Enterobacteriaceae are pathogens, nor is an increase in their relative abundance proof of infection. The same physiological perturbation can affect organisms differently because their metabolic capabilities differ. In a controlled comparison, a strain capable of respiratory growth may gain an advantage over a strain deficient in the relevant respiration pathway when electron acceptors become available. Such an interaction between environment and genotype provides a mechanistic test of the proposed ecological advantage.

Bile acid transformations can also alter resistance to particular enteric organisms. Some bile acid species affect germination or growth of susceptible organisms, and community loss can change that inhibitory environment. The direction of effect depends on the organism, life-cycle stage and chemical composition. It is not scientifically justified to classify all primary acids as promoting pathogens or all secondary acids as inhibiting them. Detailed disease-related interventions belong to the later dysbiosis node.

The feedback is bidirectional. A shift in epithelial inflammation can change nutrient and electron acceptor availability, which can favour a different microbial community. That altered community can modify metabolites, antigen exposure and barrier function. When interpreting observational data, an increase in a microbial group may be a cause, a consequence or part of a reinforcing feedback loop. Establishing the order of events and perturbing a specific link is necessary to distinguish these explanations.

Colonisation resistance can be described through a simple population balance. If the number of an invading organism changes at a rate proportional to its population, dN/dt = (g − l)N, where g is the per-capita growth rate and l the per-capita loss rate, then a positive net rate permits expansion and a negative rate causes decline under constant conditions. Consider an illustrative growth rate of 0.3 per hour and loss rate of 0.5 per hour. The net rate is −0.2 per hour, so the population remaining after five hours is exp(−1), or approximately 37% of the initial value. If a physiological perturbation raises growth to 0.7 per hour while loss is unchanged, net growth becomes positive. Such a model is not intended to predict the complex kinetics of an actual infection; it shows why an ecological shift can reverse the sign of population change without changing every resident taxon.

Bile acid-dependent resistance offers a complementary example. Buffie and colleagues linked a bile acid-transforming organism, Clostridium scindens, to resistance against Clostridioides difficile in experimental systems and reported that secondary bile acid production contributed to the effect [14]. The physiological reasoning is that a community-produced modification of a host molecule changes conditions experienced by another organism. However, resistance can also depend on nutrient availability and other microbial products. The observation is not a general claim that administering one organism is safe or effective for every disease.

A selective perturbation differs from indiscriminate community depletion. If one removes many microorganisms with a broad treatment, changes in pathogen growth may arise from loss of nutrients competitors, changes in bile acid chemistry, altered pH or altered epithelial responses. To identify one route, compare an organism or community with a particular functional capacity to a closely matched comparator lacking it. Measure the proposed intermediate and assess the downstream effect. A rescue using the missing intermediate can provide additional evidence, although it may not reproduce the spatial or temporal pattern of production by living organisms.

Resistance also includes the host's ability to tolerate normal microbial occupancy without excessive injury. The two concepts should not be conflated. Resistance reduces growth or establishment of an organism; tolerance reduces damage for a given microbial burden. In an experiment, two animals may carry similar microbial loads but show different epithelial injury. This is not evidence that the healthier animal necessarily has stronger resistance to colonisation. Conversely, a lower burden need not imply better tissue protection if the host is more sensitive to inflammatory signals. Distinguishing load from physiological damage is an important component of data interpretation.

A resident population can change the invasion threshold for another organism by altering a resource concentration. Consider a microbe whose growth rate depends on a limiting nutrient R in a saturating way: g = gmaxR/(K+R), where gmax is maximal growth rate and K is the resource concentration giving half-maximal growth in this simplified model. If resource concentration is K/3, growth is one-quarter of gmax; if it is 3K, growth is three-quarters of gmax. Under the same removal rate, these two conditions can lead to opposite population outcomes. Resource competition therefore has a mechanistic interpretation in terms of growth and loss rather than a vague statement that resident organisms occupy space.

Spatial resistance matters even when total growth is unchanged. A bacterium can survive within the lumen but fail to establish contact with a particular epithelial niche because mucus, antibodies or competing organisms restrict access. If the scientific question concerns colonisation of a surface, total faecal abundance is an imperfect endpoint. Assessing the relevant spatial compartment and tissue response better tests the mechanism. Colonisation resistance can consequently be expressed at several biological levels: exclusion from a niche, suppression of net growth, or restriction of spread to another compartment.

Ecological recovery after a perturbation need not restore the original taxonomic composition exactly. If alternative organisms re-establish the required metabolic function, a protective physiological property may recover despite differences in community membership. Conversely, a community resembling the original composition can retain a functional defect if gene expression, substrate access or host conditions have changed. Recovery of a particular physiological endpoint and recovery of a taxonomic profile are separate definitions of restoration. A robust experiment specifies which of these outcomes is measured.

12. Integration across animal species and interpretation of physiological experiments

The role of fermentation is especially clear in animals adapted to diets rich in structural polysaccharides. In ruminants, a large microbial fermentation compartment precedes the main absorptive regions of the small intestine. Volatile fatty acids generated in the rumen are absorbed and make an important contribution to energy metabolism. Microbial cells passing farther along the digestive tract can become a nutritional resource when digested. In hindgut fermenters, extensive fermentation occurs after the small intestine, changing the availability of microbial biomass and products to the host. Some animals compensate for this spatial arrangement through reingestion of specialised faecal material. These comparisons explain why the same microbial biochemical capacity can have different nutritional significance in different digestive architectures.

Human and laboratory mouse gut physiology also differ. The size and relative metabolic roles of intestinal compartments, bile acid composition, diet and behaviour are not identical. A result obtained in a mouse model can reveal a plausible mammalian mechanism while leaving uncertain its magnitude in humans. Translation requires measurements appropriate to the target species, not just the presence of similarly named microbial taxa or host receptors.

An integrative physiological experiment might begin with a clearly defined hypothesis that increased delivery of a fermentable carbohydrate improves epithelial barrier function through microbial butyrate production. Groups would need to distinguish substrate exposure, the presence of an appropriate microbial community and the activity of a proposed host pathway. A well-matched control carbohydrate and measurement of intake would help distinguish direct nutritional changes from microbial mediation. Labelled carbon could connect substrate to butyrate, while epithelial ATP, resistance and tracer permeability would provide different physiological endpoints.

Consider four illustrative groups: baseline diet with a conventional community, fermentable diet with that community, fermentable diet without a required butyrate-producing function, and the third group with a controlled butyrate rescue. If the second and fourth groups show higher corrected TEER than the first and third, the results support a contribution of butyrate under those conditions. They would not establish that butyrate is the only mediator unless alternatives are tested. If ATP increases in the rescue group but tracer permeability remains abnormal, the data separate metabolic rescue from complete barrier rescue.

Functional redundancy can be tested by comparing communities with different species compositions that produce similar metabolite flux under matched conditions. Similar measured physiological responses would support redundancy for that endpoint. A change of diet might remove this equivalence if the communities use different substrates. Thus functional redundancy must be qualified by substrate, environment, measurement and outcome. The distinction will become particularly important in the next node, where sequencing and metabolite measurements are evaluated as different forms of evidence.

The principal lesson is that gut microbial physiology is a network of connected transformations. A dietary polymer can supply carbon to a primary degrader, a cross-feeding organism can produce an organic acid, an epithelial cell can oxidise that acid or detect it through a receptor, and altered epithelial metabolism can change the growth environment of neighbouring microbes. Vitamin and bile acid transformations add further metabolic links; mucosal immunity and barrier maintenance regulate the interface. Understanding this network requires tracing actual molecules, compartments and causal steps rather than assigning a universal health value to particular species.

An experiment that relates dietary fibre to host physiology must control more than calories. Replacing one dietary component can alter texture, transit time, water retention, energy availability and host behaviour. Some changes can act independently of microbial fermentation. An appropriate control diet may therefore require careful matching of nutritional and physical properties. The strongest inference arises when the experiment combines dietary manipulation with a defined microbial functional perturbation and a direct measurement of the proposed product. Without such evidence, a difference between diets can establish a dietary effect but cannot uniquely assign it to the microbiota.

A useful distinction is between mediation and modification. Microbial butyrate might mediate part of the effect of a dietary substrate if the substrate changes butyrate production and butyrate contributes to the physiological endpoint. The host genotype can modify the effect if receptor expression or epithelial metabolism changes the response to the same exposure. An experimental interaction between diet and genotype can therefore arise even if microbial production is identical. Separating upstream changes in product supply from downstream differences in sensitivity requires measuring both exposure and response.

A hypothetical two-factor dataset illustrates the point. Suppose mean epithelial resistance is 80 units in control wild-type tissue and 120 in wild-type tissue supplied with a microbial product. In receptor-deficient tissue the corresponding means are 70 and 75. The treatment differences are 40 and 5 units. The difference between the treatment differences is 35 units. If variability and experimental design support the difference, the receptor appears to modify the response. The remaining five-unit response could reflect an alternative mechanism, experimental noise or incomplete receptor dependence. These means alone do not establish statistical significance; sample size and variability must be considered.

Finally, the appropriate interpretation depends on the biological level under study. A cultured bacterium can establish a biochemical capacity under defined conditions. A mixed microbial community can demonstrate cooperation or competition. Epithelial culture can reveal a direct cellular response. A whole animal can demonstrate integration across organs, though systemic feedback introduces additional variables. Human observational studies can reveal relevance in the population but may not identify the causal pathway. None of these approaches is universally superior; their value depends on the question. A coherent physiological explanation often requires evidence from several levels, each addressing a different link in the chain.

The difference between relative and absolute measurements can affect physiological predictions even before detailed sequencing methods are considered. Suppose a community contains 10 million cells of a product-forming population among 100 million total cells. After a perturbation, the same 10 million cells are present among 50 million total cells. Its relative abundance has increased from 10% to 20%, but its absolute abundance has not changed. Even absolute abundance does not reveal per-cell metabolic rate. If substrate delivery falls, product output could decline while relative abundance increases. The next node will examine methods for resolving these distinct measurements in greater detail.

In a time-series experiment, a product can change before or after a host response. If a metabolite rises before the measured response, it satisfies an important temporal condition for a proposed causal mechanism, but a common upstream change may still drive both. If the response clearly precedes the metabolite increase, the simple claim that the increase initiated the response is weakened. Feedback complicates such interpretation because later changes may reinforce an earlier event. Carefully timed interventions and repeated measurements help distinguish initiation from maintenance.

The integration of several physiological pathways is best understood by following a specific perturbation. A change in fermentable substrate can alter microbial products; those products can affect epithelial metabolism and hormone release; the host can respond through altered motility, secretion or nutrient handling; these changes feed back on the substrates and chemical conditions experienced by the microbes. No single component determines the final state in isolation. The central task in analytical questions is to locate the measured endpoint within this network and identify which connecting steps have been directly tested.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Microbial genes prove nutrient delivery

    Gene content indicates capability, not production, release or uptake.

    Gene inventories are often confused with direct metabolic measurements. In a question, locate the missing expression, release or uptake step.

  2. All dietary fibre is fermented equally

    Chemical linkage, physical access, community composition and residence time determine fermentation.

    The dietary category fibre hides chemical diversity. Compare accessibility and the required degradative enzymes.

  3. Fermentation requires oxygen

    Fermentation can regenerate oxidised cofactors without oxygen as terminal acceptor.

    Students often equate all energy metabolism with aerobic respiration. Identify the terminal redox process and ATP-generation route.

  4. A butyrate producer must degrade all polymers

    Cross-feeding allows the terminal producer to use products released by other organisms.

    Taxonomic labels obscure cooperation. Ask whether an upstream substrate supplier remains.

  5. High stool SCFA always means high production

    Absorption, water content, outflow and consumption also determine the measured concentration.

    Concentration is easier to measure than flux. Write the production–removal balance before interpreting it.

  6. All SCFA act identically

    Their metabolic destinations, ligand sensitivities and intracellular effects differ.

    Their shared classification obscures chemical and receptor differences. Compare carbon skeletons and cell-specific endpoints.

  7. Luminal anaerobiosis means epithelial mitochondria lack oxygen

    Blood supplies oxygen from the tissue side; epithelial consumption helps maintain the gradient.

    A compartment-level oxygen description is incorrectly applied to every cell. Trace oxygen supply from blood.

  8. HDAC inhibition requires FFAR2 activation

    Intracellular deacetylase modulation and receptor signalling are distinct mechanisms.

    Students may assume one ligand has only one mechanism. Distinguish membrane signalling from intracellular exposure.

  9. Microbial B12 removes the need for dietary B12

    Colonic synthesis may be downstream of ileal uptake and may produce alternative corrinoids.

    Synthesis is mistaken for absorption. Locate the uptake site and identify the chemical form.

  10. Bile salt hydrolase performs 7-alpha-dehydroxylation

    Deconjugation and removal of a steroid hydroxyl group are distinct reactions.

    Both reactions involve bile acids, but they remove different chemical groups. Trace the actual bond changed.

  11. FXR and TGR5 are both GPCRs

    FXR is a nuclear receptor, whereas TGR5 is a membrane GPCR.

    Both are bile acid sensors. Identify cellular location and mechanism before predicting the readout.

  12. A strong barrier blocks every molecule

    Selective absorption and controlled paracellular movement are physiological necessities.

    Barrier is mistaken for an impermeable wall. Distinguish regulated nutrient transport from unwanted exposure.

  13. TEER alone measures every barrier property

    Resistance chiefly measures ionic conductance; tracer permeability and other endpoints complement it.

    Electrical measurements appear comprehensive. Compare the property of ions with the size and route of the tracer.

  14. Secretory IgA sterilises the gut

    It can restrict microbial contact and organise populations without eliminating them.

    Antibody binding is confused with killing. Consider immune exclusion and microbial spatial organisation.

  15. Taxonomic diversity alone predicts colonisation resistance

    Resistance depends on functional competition, metabolites, spatial context and host responses.

    Species richness is an attractive summary statistic, but not a mechanistic measure of resource competition. Identify the limiting resource.

  16. A microbial association proves causality

    A controlled perturbation and appropriate endpoints are needed to test a causal mechanism.

    Association may arise from reverse causation or confounding. Seek a specific perturbation, temporal ordering and rescue.

Quantitative relationships

Carbon recovery = sum of product amount × carbon atoms per molecule. At steady state, production plus inflow equals uptake plus consumption plus outflow. Corrected TEER = (measured resistance − blank resistance) × area. Apparent tracer permeability = tracer appearance rate divided by area × donor concentration under simplified sink assumptions. Receptor occupancy = L/(Kd + L) under a single-site equilibrium model. For every equation, keep units and assumptions explicit.
