1. Reading the environment before making food

A germinating seed faces a choice that cannot be reversed without cost. If it remains dormant in a favorable environment, it loses a chance to establish itself. If it commits to germination in darkness beneath a dense canopy, its finite reserves may be exhausted before a shoot can support itself. The spectral composition of light provides one piece of evidence about the environment. The intensity of light alone cannot distinguish a cloudy open field from the light transmitted through neighboring leaves. Green foliage absorbs many red photons in photosynthetic pigments and transmits or reflects a larger fraction of far-red photons. Thus the ratio of red to far-red light can change even before the total photon supply falls dramatically. A seedling able to respond to this difference can modify development in anticipation of competition.

A phytochrome is a photoreceptor, a molecule that absorbs light and converts that physical input into a change in biological signaling. Its primary function in this chapter is not to collect the energy that drives carbon fixation. A single photochemical event can change the activity of a regulatory protein, and the regulatory change can influence the transcription of many genes. That amplification permits a small light input to have a large developmental effect. It also explains why the action of a brief light pulse cannot be evaluated solely in terms of the amount of sugar it would produce.

The developing shoot has several possible responses. A dark-grown seedling typically invests in hypocotyl extension, retains relatively closed cotyledons and has plastids that are not yet mature photosynthetic chloroplasts. During the transition to light, extension slows and cotyledons expand while chloroplast differentiation accelerates. A seedling already growing in light can instead respond to a decrease in the red to far-red ratio by increasing stem or petiole elongation. These examples are not contradictory: their starting states and ecological problems differ. The signaling network interprets light in the context of previous illumination, developmental age and tissue identity.

Wavelength is the spatial period of an electromagnetic wave. In the approximate regions of interest, red light is represented by 660 nm and far-red by 730 nm. Neither number is a sharp border beyond which phytochrome becomes insensitive. The receptor absorbs across bands of wavelengths, and those bands overlap. Photon fluence is the total number of incident photons per area, whereas photon fluence rate is the number arriving per area per unit time. The red to far-red ratio is dimensionless only when the two measurements are expressed in compatible units and the spectral bands are defined consistently. In experiments, one should always specify the spectrum, intensity, duration and prior light history.

2. From a germination experiment to a reversible receptor

In the classic lettuce seed study published by Borthwick and colleagues in 1952, the experimental problem was whether radiation could govern germination through a reversible reaction. A useful design begins by placing seeds from the same lot under equivalent moisture and temperature conditions. Some remain in darkness and others receive brief red or far-red treatments. The critical comparison is not merely red versus far-red. Sequential treatments ask whether a second wavelength can reverse the consequence of the first. A red treatment followed immediately by far-red reduces the promotion associated with red; adding another red treatment can restore it in responsive lettuce seeds. The effect therefore depends strongly on the final effective illumination when pulses are sufficiently strong and close together.

The word effective matters. A pulse that contains very few photons may fail to change the receptor population substantially. A long delay between treatments can permit an irreversible physiological commitment, so later far-red cannot necessarily undo germination. Different seed varieties, physiological states and experimental temperatures need not have identical dark controls or response thresholds. The historical result establishes reversible photoregulation under appropriate conditions rather than a universal rule that every seed responds to the last photon.

The observation distinguishes several hypotheses. A simple cumulative-energy hypothesis predicts that red plus far-red should provide at least as much incident energy as red alone, but does not naturally predict reversal. A damaging far-red treatment might also reduce germination, but recovery after a subsequent red pulse would be difficult to reconcile with permanent damage. A reversible molecular switch provides a coherent explanation: the wavelength of a subsequent pulse changes the distribution of the receptor between alternative states. The 1952 experiments were physiological evidence for this kind of switch; they were not by themselves a direct protein structure determination.

A good experiment includes untreated controls, identical hydration and temperature, equal incubation time, randomized positions and enough independent seed batches to assess variability. A germination endpoint must be defined, for example emergence of the radicle by a predetermined time. Scoring immediately after irradiation would be inappropriate because receptor conversion is much faster than the visible developmental output. Repeating alternating treatments at varied doses would test whether the final-pulse rule holds under the chosen conditions. The ability to distinguish the time of stimulus perception from the time of observable response is a central lesson of the experiment.

3. Architecture of the plant phytochrome molecule

The receptor has two chemically distinct parts. An apoprotein is the polypeptide before association with its light-absorbing cofactor. The chromophore is the chemical group whose electronic structure permits absorption of particular wavelengths. Their assembled functional complex is a holoprotein. In seed plants the natural chromophore is phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole rather than a closed chlorophyll ring. It is produced from heme through a pathway involving heme oxygenase and phytochromobilin synthase; the resulting bilin joins the receptor protein through a covalent linkage involving a conserved cysteine in its GAF domain. Consequently, producing the protein alone does not guarantee light sensitivity.

The N-terminal portion contains a photosensory module with PAS, GAF and PHY domains. These are folded regions of the protein that cooperate in binding the chromophore and transmitting the conformational consequence of excitation. A covalent bond prevents the chromophore from simply diffusing away after photon absorption. Its shape and the surrounding amino-acid environment determine which photons are absorbed efficiently. The C-terminal region participates in receptor association and signaling. Plant phytochromes are usually discussed as dimers, each subunit contributing a photosensory module. A dimer contains two polypeptide chains, not two different chemical states by definition: individual chromophores can occupy different states.

One can think of the N-terminal region as the sensor and the remaining portion as participating in communication of the signal, but the analogy has limits. The receptor is not assembled from an electrical sensor and an independent wire. Conformational coupling between protein regions, dimer interfaces and binding partners all contribute to activity. A mutation that abolishes chromophore attachment prevents normal photochemistry, whereas a mutation outside the binding pocket could leave absorption intact but interfere with signaling. These possibilities explain why pigment spectroscopy and developmental assays are complementary, not interchangeable.

In a biochemical reconstitution experiment, purified apoprotein may be compared with protein supplied with the appropriate bilin. Absorption spectra can then be measured before and after controlled red and far-red irradiation. A change in the spectrum after the addition of chromophore supports assembly of a photoreversible complex. If a chromophore-binding cysteine is replaced and the photoreversible difference spectrum disappears, the mutation supports a role for that residue in receptor function. It remains necessary to check protein abundance and folding, because absent activity could also reflect an unstable mutant protein. A separate in vivo complementation experiment would ask whether the molecularly functional receptor restores a physiological phenotype.

4. Pr, Pfr and bidirectional photoconversion

The letters Pr identify a form that preferentially absorbs red radiation; Pfr identifies a form that preferentially absorbs far-red radiation. They name the input each form absorbs best, not the color that creates it. This distinction prevents the common reversal of the pathway. Red photons favor conversion of Pr into Pfr. Far-red photons favor the reverse conversion. In many seed-plant responses, Pfr is the signaling-competent form. The chromophore changes its configuration following excitation and changes the structure of the surrounding protein. This provides a molecular route from wavelength discrimination to altered interactions with other molecules.

A newly synthesized receptor population in a dark-grown seedling is predominantly Pr. Red illumination increases the Pfr fraction, but it does not generally convert every receptor molecule. Both states have overlapping spectra, so even an apparently pure red light source can support some reverse photochemistry. Continued illumination leads toward a dynamic balance in which forward and reverse conversions continue while the average proportion of the two forms remains approximately constant. This is the photostationary state. Far-red irradiation usually produces a smaller Pfr fraction, not necessarily zero.

Darkness differs from far-red irradiation. After removal of light the photochemical reactions driven by incoming photons cease, but Pfr can return to Pr by thermal reversion. Protein degradation also changes the pool of receptors, particularly for phyA under activating conditions. Neither process requires a far-red photon. The rates of thermal reversion vary among phytochromes and can depend on temperature. Thus a plant may retain molecular information about a light pulse after the pulse ends, but that information decays.

The two forms must not be equated with different genes or separate pigments. A single functional photoreceptor can occupy either configuration, and multiple members of the phytochrome family each show the fundamental photochemistry. Similarly, a measurement of Pfr fraction is not automatically a measurement of total Pfr amount. Two seedlings can have the same fraction but different receptor abundance. Both the concentration of signaling-competent protein and downstream sensitivity contribute to output.

5. Quantitative reasoning about the active fraction

A simplified two-state model treats the total receptor pool as constant during the measurement. Let f be the fraction of the pool in Pfr, so 1 minus f is the Pr fraction. Let a and b be the effective forward and reverse first-order photoconversion rate constants under a specified light environment, and let k be the thermal reversion rate constant. The forward contribution is a times the Pr fraction. The reverse contribution is the sum of b and k times the Pfr fraction. Each rate constant has units of inverse time. Under constant illumination, the net rate of change is df/dt = a(1 − f) − (b + k)f.

At the photostationary state the net rate is zero. Setting the forward and reverse terms equal gives a − af = (b + k)f. Collecting terms yields f = a/(a + b + k). The fraction lies between zero and one for nonnegative rates and a positive denominator. Increasing a relative to b or k increases the stationary fraction. This expression is a teaching model, not a universal formula for determining plant development from a measured red to far-red ratio alone. In real samples, the effective rates depend on spectral photon flux, state-specific absorption and conversion efficiency, while synthesis, degradation and multiple receptor types can invalidate the fixed-pool assumption.

Consider a hypothetical red-enriched environment with a = 0.18 per minute, b = 0.02 per minute and negligible thermal reversion. The predicted fraction is 0.18/0.20 = 0.90. In a far-red-enriched environment with a = 0.02 and b = 0.18 per minute the same calculation yields 0.10. If the total receptor concentration were 100 arbitrary units in each condition, the predicted Pfr amounts would be 90 and 10 units. These numbers are invented for instruction, not measured phytochrome photostationary fractions.

After transfer to darkness, set a and b to zero and assume k remains constant. The equation reduces to df/dt = −kf. Integration gives f(t) = f(0)e^(−kt). For an initial fraction of 0.80 and a hypothetical half-life of 20 minutes, the fractions after 20 and 40 minutes are 0.40 and 0.20. This is exponential rather than linear decay. If the measured signal instead declines faster than the predicted fraction, possible explanations include receptor degradation, more than one thermal reversion process or changes in a downstream reporter. Data alone do not identify which possibility is true. Separate measurements of receptor abundance, photochemical state and biological output are needed.

A light source with equal red to far-red ratio to another source may have a different total irradiance. If a and b are multiplied by the same factor, the stationary fraction is unchanged in the ideal model without thermal reversion, but the approach to that fraction becomes faster. With appreciable thermal reversion, increasing both photochemical rates can also alter the stationary fraction because k is not multiplied. Therefore equal spectral ratios are not enough to conclude equal responses. A very brief exposure can terminate before the system reaches its predicted stationary state.

6. Receptor diversity and the phyA–phyB distinction

Arabidopsis has five phytochrome genes, PHYA through PHYE. The proteins encoded by these genes share red/far-red photoreversibility but differ in expression patterns, stability and contribution to particular responses. PhyA is the principal representative of type I phytochrome and accumulates strongly in etiolated seedlings. Its active pool is relatively susceptible to light-induced degradation. PhyB is an important type II receptor that remains influential under red illumination and in detection of changes in the canopy spectrum. The remaining family members can modify these responses, so comparisons of phyA and phyB describe dominant roles in standard model conditions rather than a complete list of functions.

A conventional low-fluence response can be induced by a red pulse and reversed with an appropriate subsequent far-red pulse. PhyB often dominates such classical responses. A very-low-fluence response can be triggered by a tiny conversion to Pfr, which may occur even under far-red illumination because the absorption spectra overlap. An additional far-red treatment may not reduce Pfr below the low threshold, so the response need not show the standard reversal pattern. PhyA is particularly important for such high-sensitivity behavior. A far-red high-irradiance response instead depends on sustained illumination and cannot be reduced to a simple final-pulse test. PhyA contributes prominently to this response.

These response classes illustrate why the statement that far-red light switches phytochrome off is inadequate. Far-red generally reduces the Pfr fraction compared with red, but a low fraction maintained by continuous photoconversion of a large receptor pool can still support significant phyA signaling. The temporal dynamics of receptor transport and interaction with other proteins also matter. A seedling grown under continuous far-red can therefore show de-etiolation instead of behaving as though it had never encountered light.

Loss-of-function mutants allow these roles to be separated experimentally. Compare wild type, phyA-deficient plants and phyB-deficient plants in darkness, continuous red and continuous far-red at matched developmental stages. In a representative Arabidopsis seedling assay, loss of phyA particularly weakens the response to continuous far-red; loss of phyB particularly weakens red-light-dependent inhibition of hypocotyl elongation. Residual responses may reflect overlapping activity of other family members or contributions of other photoreceptors. A double mutant and gene-specific complementation help test those explanations. An assay in only one lighting condition cannot fully characterize the receptor family.

7. From photochemistry to regulation of gene expression

Light changes the availability of signaling-competent phytochrome within the cell. Following activation, phytochromes accumulate in the nucleus, where transcription regulators and protein turnover machinery can respond to receptor activity. Nuclear accumulation can depend on different transport mechanisms for different family members. The exam-relevant point is that the receptor's state regulates the ability of the photoreceptor to affect downstream nuclear targets. The photon is absorbed by phytochrome, not by a transcription factor waiting on DNA.

Phytochrome-interacting factors, or PIFs, are a family of basic helix-loop-helix transcription regulators. In the dark, several PIFs support the developmental program characteristic of etiolated seedlings. Activated phytochromes can bind particular PIFs, promoting their phosphorylation, inactivation or removal through the ubiquitin–proteasome system depending on the PIF and context. A simplified causal sequence is red light, increased active phytochrome, altered PIF activity, changed transcription, and finally changed development. The distinction between PIF protein level and PIF messenger RNA is important because protein degradation can occur without a matching decrease in transcript.

A western blot measures the abundance of a particular protein after extraction, separation and detection using an antibody. Suppose a PIF band falls within minutes of red illumination, while a loading-control band remains constant. If a phyB mutant retains more of that PIF under the same treatment, this is consistent with phyB-dependent loss of PIF. If a proteasome inhibitor prevents the decline, a protein-turnover explanation becomes more plausible. Nevertheless, inhibitor specificity, tissue composition and receptor redundancy remain possible confounding factors. A blot alone cannot establish direct physical binding between the receptor and transcription factor; interaction assays address that separate question.

Another regulatory route involves the COP1–SPA complex and the transcription factor HY5. In dark conditions, COP1–SPA contributes to limiting the accumulation of positive regulators of photomorphogenesis, including HY5. Light-activated photoreceptors interfere with this repressive network, allowing HY5 to accumulate and participate in light-responsive transcription. PIF inhibition and stabilization of HY5 are complementary regulatory changes, not two names for the same molecular event. The balance of positive and negative regulators helps explain why changes in seedling shape require coordinated control of many genes.

8. Developmental output: germination, de-etiolation and shade

Phytochrome-mediated germination responses connect an environmental signal with the hormonal state of a seed. In Arabidopsis, PIF1 is an important regulator of this connection. In the dark, its activity can favor a hormonal balance that restrains germination; light-induced phytochrome signaling reduces that restraint through changes in the relevant regulatory network. The result depends on dormancy status and the interactions of gibberellins and abscisic acid. Phytochrome is not itself gibberellin, and neither hormone is the chromophore. The receptor changes how the seed regulates growth-promoting and growth-inhibiting processes.

During seedling de-etiolation, increasing phytochrome activity can inhibit excessive hypocotyl extension, promote opening of cotyledons and support the transition to photosynthetic competence. Chloroplast maturation requires many proteins and metabolic processes, so a phytochrome signal should not be confused with the light reactions that later produce ATP and reducing power. If a seedling has normal red-light absorption by phytochrome but defective chloroplast biogenesis, it may show some photomorphogenic responses while remaining pale. Such separation of outputs is experimentally informative.

Shade avoidance concerns a plant that already receives light and detects competition. Neighboring foliage modifies light quality, often lowering the red to far-red ratio. A reduction in active phyB can release constraints on certain PIFs, including regulators of auxin-related growth responses. Increased elongation of stems or petioles may improve access to light, but it has costs: resources devoted to extension cannot simultaneously be allocated to all other growth functions. The response is therefore not universally beneficial, and shade-tolerant plants can adopt different strategies.

It is useful to compare two treatments that produce similar reductions in total photon flux. A neutral-density filter reduces light broadly without necessarily producing the same spectral shift as foliage. A filter that specifically increases the relative abundance of far-red changes spectral information. If the latter causes stronger elongation even when total photosynthetically useful photon flux is comparable, a spectral-sensing mechanism is supported. The experiment must account for blue-light signals, temperature and differences in photosynthetic carbon supply. A plant senses multiple environmental signals at once, and a single receptor cannot explain every canopy response.

9. Interpreting spectral measurements and receptor action

The optical vocabulary used in photobiology describes several different physical measurements. Irradiance is radiant power delivered per unit area. It may be reported in watts per square metre. Photon flux density instead counts the photons arriving per unit area per unit time and is frequently expressed in micromoles of photons per square metre per second. A micromole specifies a number of photons, not a fixed amount of energy independent of wavelength. If equal radiant powers are delivered at two different wavelengths, the longer-wavelength beam contains more photons because each photon carries less energy. The conversion follows E = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light and λ is wavelength in metres. The relation is relevant whenever the light treatment is specified in energy units rather than photon units.

For example, compare a monochromatic 660 nm source with a 730 nm source of equal irradiance. The ratio of the number of photons arriving from the far-red source to the number from the red source is 730/660, approximately 1.106. This does not imply that the far-red beam creates 10.6 percent more of any particular receptor state. The absorption and photoconversion efficiency of the receptor are distinct from the photon number. Equal photon exposures likewise need not produce equal photochemical responses, because the probability of a useful interaction between an incident photon and the receptor differs with wavelength. A fair comparison of wavelengths begins by deciding which physical quantity is held constant and then considering the appropriate biological response.

An absorption spectrum is obtained by measuring how much incident radiation a sample absorbs as the wavelength changes. An action spectrum describes the relative effectiveness of different wavelengths at producing a specified biological effect. The two kinds of spectrum may resemble one another, particularly when a single receptor dominates a response, but they are not necessarily identical. The action spectrum is influenced by the receptor's abundance, the efficiency of conversion after absorption, tissue penetration, other absorbing pigments and the nonlinear relationship between receptor activation and the chosen output. For example, germination is often scored as a yes-or-no outcome for each seed. A small receptor conversion can produce a large change in the proportion of seeds reaching a germination threshold. Such an output cannot be interpreted as a direct spectroscopic determination of receptor concentration.

A student should also distinguish an absorption maximum from the position of a physiological maximum. If a graph shows a red-light peak for promotion of lettuce seed germination and a far-red peak for reversal, it supports the idea of spectral discrimination. It does not establish exact peak positions for a purified phytochrome of every species. Purified photoreceptor preparations allow a different test. When a population initially enriched in Pr is measured and then converted to a population enriched in Pfr, changes in absorption at selected wavelengths can be used to estimate the difference between the two spectra. The difference spectrum may have both positive and negative values because it represents the subtraction of one absorbance measurement from another. A negative difference is not negative physical absorption; it means that the post-treatment sample absorbs less at that wavelength than the reference sample.

The shape of an action spectrum may be altered by a dose effect. If every tested wavelength is provided at a very high dose, multiple wavelengths that are unequally effective at low doses may all produce the maximum response. The resulting curve would flatten and obscure spectral discrimination. If the dose is too small, none of the treatments may produce a detectable response. One experimental solution is to generate a dose-response curve for each wavelength and compare the photon fluences needed to reach a defined response level. This approach measures sensitivity more directly than a single response at an arbitrary light dose. It is also important to state the response endpoint, because an action spectrum for hypocotyl elongation need not match an action spectrum for the expression of a particular gene.

Light sources also have bandwidth. A lamp labelled red may emit photons across a broad band, while a filter may transmit small amounts of light outside its intended region. In a very-low-fluence phyA response, leakage that seems trivial from a human visual perspective can be biologically significant. An experimental test of dark-grown seedlings should therefore include checks for unintended illumination during handling. Measuring the actual spectral distribution at the position of the sample is preferable to relying on the colour name of a lamp. Distances between samples and lamps should also be controlled to avoid creating a photon-flux gradient across a supposedly uniform treatment.

Canopy measurements raise a related question. The red to far-red ratio is commonly estimated using defined wavelength windows, but researchers may choose windows of different widths. Two reported ratios cannot automatically be compared without checking those definitions. The spectrum can contain much more information than one ratio. For instance, a green canopy changes blue light and photosynthetically usable light as well as the red-to-far-red balance. A simple ratio remains useful when the objective is to investigate one major ecological cue, provided that its meaning and limitations are kept explicit. A ratio is not a measurement of phyB activity itself; converting environmental radiation into an estimate of receptor state requires a photochemical model or direct measurement.

These measurement distinctions help in experimental interpretation. Imagine that a mutant has a normal purified-receptor absorption spectrum but its action spectrum for hypocotyl inhibition is markedly altered. The result directs attention toward signal transmission, receptor localization, abundance or response sensitivity, rather than proving a defect in the chromophore. Conversely, a mutant that lacks the characteristic photoreversible difference spectrum may have a defect in chromophore attachment or photochemistry. It would still be necessary to demonstrate that the apoprotein is present and stable. Combining spectral data with molecular and physiological measurements is therefore more informative than relying on any one curve.

10. Chromophore assembly and separation of biochemical defects

The precursor relationship between heme and the phytochrome chromophore is a useful connection between pigment metabolism and developmental signaling. Heme contains a tetrapyrrole ring coordinated to iron. Heme oxygenase catalyses cleavage of that ring to produce biliverdin, and phytochromobilin synthase reduces biliverdin to the plant bilin chromophore. In Arabidopsis the HY1 and HY2 gene products are central components of this pathway. The chloroplast is a major site of the chromophore biosynthetic steps, whereas the phytochrome apoprotein is synthesized outside the plastid. The chromophore becomes available for assembly with the protein. It is the assembled receptor, rather than free heme or biliverdin, that normally senses red and far-red radiation in this pathway.

This relationship offers a genetic explanation for why a pigment-biosynthetic mutation may disrupt several phytochrome-mediated responses at once. A loss of one particular phytochrome gene removes or reduces the function of one family member. A defect that limits the common chromophore, however, may compromise the activities of several family members even if their apoproteins are still produced. This difference in breadth is useful in screening mutants but cannot be used as an absolute diagnostic rule. Other physiological consequences of disturbed tetrapyrrole metabolism may contribute to the phenotype. Genetic complementation and direct measurements of assembled photoreceptor are needed to identify the primary cause.

A rescue experiment is designed around a proposed defect. Suppose a mutant has a normal amount of phyB apoprotein but produces little chromophore. If a functional copy of the defective biosynthetic gene restores photoreversible absorption and the relevant red-light response, the combined evidence links the biosynthetic step to receptor function. In a biochemical assay, the addition of the appropriate chromophore to a purified apoprotein preparation can address whether the protein itself is capable of assembly. Adding a compound to an intact seedling is a different experiment, because uptake, transport, conversion and stability of the compound may limit rescue. Failure of an externally supplied precursor to restore a phenotype does not by itself disprove the proposed metabolic defect.

The phytochromobilin molecule includes four pyrrole-derived rings connected in an open chain. Its conjugated electronic structure allows it to absorb visible light. The protein binding pocket alters the chemical environment of the chromophore and consequently its spectroscopic and photochemical properties. The transition between the two photochemical forms involves isomerization about a bond in the chromophore and changes in the surrounding protein. At NET level, the important reasoning is that a local change in chromophore geometry can propagate through a much larger macromolecule. The photon does not need to break the protein apart, create a new receptor gene or cause the chromophore to dissociate each time the receptor changes state.

The domains of the photosensory module are not three interchangeable independent light sensors. The PAS, GAF and PHY regions participate in the integrated structure. The chromophore-binding site is associated with the GAF domain, and structural elements from the surrounding regions contribute to coupling photochemistry to conformation. A signaling defect can therefore result from a mutation that leaves the amino acid involved in the covalent attachment unchanged. A structural mutant may still absorb light but exhibit altered reversibility or a reduced ability to communicate the change to downstream regions. A mutation that compromises folding may instead lower the amount of recoverable receptor. These distinctions explain why describing a residue as necessary for a response does not establish its precise molecular role.

A useful comparison is between wild-type holoprotein, unassembled apoprotein and a hypothetical mutant holoprotein. Wild-type protein assembled with the natural bilin shows normal spectral changes. The unassembled protein lacks the cofactor-dependent photochemistry. The mutant holoprotein may show an altered ratio of forward to reverse conversion or may convert normally but fail to bind a signaling partner. Experimental measurements of absorbance, total receptor abundance and partner interaction can separate these scenarios. Measuring only seedling height would leave all three explanations plausible. Thus the phenotype is a starting observation, not the final mechanistic interpretation.

Phytochromes are often considered dimers because the associated polypeptide chains constitute an important functional organization of the receptor. It is easy to make the erroneous assumption that a dimer must contain one Pr and one Pfr subunit. The designation dimer specifies the number of protein chains, whereas Pr and Pfr specify photochemical configurations. A population may contain molecules with different combinations of chromophore states. The simple two-state fraction used earlier counts photochemical states without resolving the dimer combinations. If the probability of downstream interaction depends on whether one or both chains are converted, the relationship between total Pfr fraction and output may be nonlinear. Detailed dimer-specific kinetics are beyond this chapter, but awareness of the distinction prevents a misleading interpretation of the simplified model.

11. Time, dose and physiological response thresholds

Light treatments can be described using both their duration and their photon fluence. A constant beam delivering 10 micromoles of photons per square metre per second for 60 seconds supplies a total fluence of 600 micromoles per square metre. A beam delivering 60 micromoles per square metre per second for 10 seconds provides the same integrated fluence. If a simple photochemical response depends only on photon dose and negligible competing time-dependent reactions, the two treatments may have similar effects. This is a restricted reciprocity relationship, not a general property of every plant light response.

A sustained far-red response illustrates why reciprocity cannot always be assumed. During a lengthy illumination period, receptor transport, protein turnover and gene expression occur while photoconversion continues. A short intense pulse cannot necessarily reproduce those time-dependent processes. A comparison of treatments at identical fluence but different fluence rates can therefore distinguish a response controlled mainly by cumulative dose from one requiring sustained irradiation. To make the comparison meaningful, temperature and the time between the end of the treatment and physiological scoring must be considered. A stronger light source may warm the tissue or alter additional signaling pathways. These variables could otherwise be mistaken for a direct effect of photon flux.

The two-state model introduced earlier can also predict the approach to the photostationary state. Let f∞ denote the stationary fraction under a new constant illumination. Since df/dt = a − (a+b+k)f, subtracting the constant f∞ from f gives d(f−f∞)/dt = −(a+b+k)(f−f∞). The solution is f(t) = f∞ + [f(0)−f∞]e^(−(a+b+k)t). The sum of the rate constants controls the rate of approach to the new state, while their relative values determine the final fraction. This separates two parameters of the system that are often confused: the speed of change and the final distribution.

For a hypothetical treatment, let a=0.15 min⁻¹, b=0.04 min⁻¹ and k=0.01 min⁻¹. The predicted stationary fraction is 0.75, and the characteristic time is 1/0.20=5 minutes. Starting at f(0)=0.15, after five minutes the predicted fraction is 0.75−0.60e⁻¹, approximately 0.529. The receptor has covered about 63.2 percent of the distance between its initial fraction and the stationary value. It has not reached the stationary value at exactly one characteristic time. After another five minutes the remaining difference becomes about 0.081, giving a fraction near 0.669. These examples teach how to read time-course data and use consistent units; the constants are illustrative rather than literature measurements.

The kinetics of a receptor and the kinetics of a response often differ. Suppose receptor conversion occurs within seconds, a transcriptional response is detected after minutes and a difference in hypocotyl length becomes measurable after hours. These three time scales do not contradict one another. Molecular events downstream of the receptor require time: transcription changes must affect relevant protein activities, cells must alter wall properties or growth rates, and many small changes must accumulate before organ dimensions change. A measurement of length therefore integrates growth over time. It cannot show exactly when receptor activation first occurred. Conversely, receptor conversion can be measured rapidly without knowing whether it will lead to a visible phenotype.

A simple physiological threshold model can clarify the difference. Imagine two types of seeds with identical photochemistry but different sensitivity of germination-related signaling. One begins committing to germination above a hypothetical Pfr fraction of 0.10; the other requires 0.50. If a weak treatment creates a fraction of 0.20, the first type may respond while the second does not. This difference arises from the response threshold rather than from absorption by the receptor. Real germination responses are not governed by one universal sharp threshold; seed populations vary in dormancy and hormonal state. The model explains why the same photochemical state can produce different physiological outputs, and why comparing germination percentages alone may misidentify the cause.

A dose-response curve may also plateau. At high fluence, receptor conversion approaches the state distribution supported by the light spectrum, and downstream signaling may reach a maximum. Increasing the number of photons beyond this region may have little effect on the chosen response even though photons continue to be absorbed. A plateau in germination does not prove that every molecule is in Pfr. All viable responsive seeds may already have passed the relevant threshold. To distinguish receptor saturation from output saturation, compare optical measurements with the biological response over a range of doses. The difference is especially important in interpreting mutant experiments in which a response appears normal under strong light but is deficient under weak light.

For dark reversion, the half-life is related to the rate constant by t½=ln(2)/k in the simple first-order model. A hypothetical half-life of 30 minutes corresponds to k≈0.0231 min⁻¹. After 90 minutes, three half-lives have passed, so an initial fraction of 0.80 would become 0.10 if the receptor pool were conserved and no new activation occurred. If the experiment shows a change in total receptor abundance, interpreting the same calculation as a prediction of absolute Pfr concentration becomes inappropriate. One must separate the fraction of receptor in a photochemical form from the total number of receptor molecules.

Temperature offers an additional dimension. If two seedlings receive identical light treatments and are then transferred to darkness at different temperatures, differences in the decay of signaling competence may reflect thermal reversion. In vivo, however, temperature also changes growth rates, enzyme activities and hormonal signaling. A temperature treatment that increases hypocotyl length does not prove that phyB thermal reversion is its sole cause. A receptor-state measurement, a suitable mutant comparison and controls for ordinary temperature effects strengthen the inference. The examination lesson is to follow each step in the proposed causal chain instead of inferring molecular events directly from organ growth.

12. Genetic and cell-biological tests of phytochrome signaling

A loss-of-function mutation is a change that diminishes the normal activity of a gene product. In a photoreceptor experiment, a mutant with a longer hypocotyl under red light than wild type may have impaired perception of red light, impaired downstream signaling, or an unrelated growth alteration. It is therefore valuable to measure its hypocotyl length in darkness as well. If the mutant and wild type elongate similarly in darkness but differ after red treatment, the difference is more specifically associated with the light response. If the mutant is longer in every condition, a general growth effect must be considered. A single phenotype is rarely sufficient to identify the molecular defect.

Complementation is a way of testing whether a genetic change accounts for a phenotype. Introducing a functional copy of the candidate gene into the corresponding mutant should restore some or all of its function if that gene is causal and the transgene is appropriately expressed. The expression level matters: excessive receptor expression could produce abnormal sensitivity even if the protein sequence is normal. Ideally, complementation is compared against a suitable wild-type line and an appropriate control carrying no functional copy. Independent transformed lines reduce the risk of drawing conclusions from an unusual insertion site. These are standard principles of experimental reasoning rather than a requirement to know every technical step of plant transformation.

Receptor localization provides another independent line of evidence. A fluorescently tagged phytochrome can allow investigators to follow receptor distribution before and after illumination. If nuclear fluorescence rises after red light, that observation is consistent with light-dependent accumulation in the nucleus. It does not by itself prove that the fluorescent receptor retains normal activity or that nuclear accumulation is sufficient to produce a physiological response. A functional complementation test for the tagged protein, together with a nuclear marker and comparable imaging conditions, helps establish the reliability of the result.

For phyA, the proteins FHY1 and FHL contribute to light-dependent nuclear import in the Arabidopsis model. This illustrates how receptor photochemistry and nuclear transport can be experimentally separated. A defect in the transport machinery may leave the primary capacity for light absorption intact yet reduce downstream nuclear signaling. The prediction is that an appropriate transport-defective mutant should show reduced nuclear accumulation after irradiation while retaining some evidence of photochemical competence. The details of transport differ among family members, so this specific example should not be generalized to claim that every plant phytochrome requires precisely the same accessory proteins.

Once in the nucleus, phytochromes can accumulate in distinct subnuclear structures frequently called photobodies. Their observation is useful evidence that receptor state is associated with changes in intracellular organization. The presence of a photobody, however, is not equivalent to proof of a specific biochemical reaction occurring there. A fluorescent focus can be a location of concentrated protein without identifying which interactions are essential for the response. The examination-relevant distinction is between localization evidence and mechanistic evidence. If a question provides a time series in which nuclear foci increase while a PIF protein declines, the data establish an association in time. Further perturbations are needed to establish that one causes the other.

Genetic interaction experiments can help identify the ordering of components in a pathway. Consider a hypothetical system in which active phyB restrains elongation mainly by decreasing the activity of a growth-promoting PIF. A phyB-null line shows increased elongation in red light because the restraint is lost. A mutation that removes the relevant PIF may reduce elongation. If the double mutant resembles the PIF-deficient line rather than the phyB-deficient line, the result is consistent with the PIF acting downstream of phyB in the simplified pathway. This reasoning is called epistasis analysis. It is most informative when the mutations are well characterized and when the output is not limited by unrelated processes.

The same reasoning requires caution in a network containing several PIFs, several phytochromes and parallel hormonal outputs. A double mutant may have an intermediate phenotype because both proteins contribute through multiple branches. An incomplete suppression of the phyB phenotype does not necessarily disprove a role for the PIF. Other PIFs or additional regulators could retain activity. Conversely, complete phenotypic suppression does not prove direct physical binding. An indirect pathway may generate the same genetic pattern. Combining genetic interaction data with receptor-partner binding assays and protein stability measurements makes the mechanism more convincing.

An interaction assay also needs careful interpretation. If phytochrome and a PIF can be recovered in the same biochemical complex after red irradiation, this supports an association under the tested conditions. It does not necessarily show that the two proteins contact one another directly; a third protein could bridge them. If interaction is demonstrated with purified components under appropriate conditions, the case for direct binding is strengthened. If the interaction is state-dependent, photochemical control provides a plausible mechanism for light-dependent signaling. Measurements of transcriptional response and physiological output then address whether the interaction matters to the intact plant.

These approaches answer different questions. Spectroscopy asks whether the receptor converts between forms. Imaging asks where it accumulates. Biochemical association asks which molecular partners interact. Protein abundance assays ask whether stability changes. Gene-expression assays ask whether transcriptional output changes. Genetic experiments ask whether particular components are necessary for the observed response. A strong mechanistic explanation is built by connecting the answers rather than treating any single method as universal proof.

13. Interpreting seed and seedling phenotypes in physiological context

A seed begins with stored reserves, an embryo and protective structures that influence the conditions experienced by the embryo. Germination involves rehydration, metabolic reactivation and growth sufficient for radicle protrusion. Dormancy refers to a state in which a viable seed fails to germinate despite otherwise favorable conditions. Environmental light is one of several signals capable of modifying dormancy or germination in responsive species. It is important not to confuse the absence of germination with the death of a seed. A seed that fails to germinate in darkness may remain viable and respond later to a favorable treatment. Germination assays should therefore consider seed viability and physiological history.

In a light-sensitive seed, the receptor state can affect the balance between germination-promoting and germination-restraining regulatory processes. Gibberellins generally promote aspects of germination, whereas abscisic acid contributes to maintenance of dormancy and restraint of germination. The actual outcome involves changes in hormone biosynthesis, metabolism and sensitivity, as well as effects of other regulators. PIF1 participates in the dark-associated restraint of germination in the Arabidopsis model. When appropriate light signaling reduces PIF1 activity, the downstream hormonal and transcriptional balance may shift toward germination. This is a causal framework rather than an assertion that one hormone concentration alone fully determines whether a seed germinates.

Suppose that a hypothetical seed lot fails to germinate after red light because an essential downstream growth-promoting process is defective. If an externally supplied gibberellin restores germination, the result supports the possibility that the defect lies upstream of a gibberellin-responsive process or reduces endogenous gibberellin activity. It does not establish that the mutant has normal phytochrome, because the external hormone could bypass a receptor defect. Conversely, lack of hormone rescue may reflect a defect in hormone sensitivity, an independent block in embryo growth or inadequate delivery. The logic of rescue experiments is to identify what has been bypassed, not simply to assign every rescued phenotype to one pathway.

An etiolated Arabidopsis seedling has a long hypocotyl, relatively unexpanded cotyledons and a developmental program adapted to darkness. In darkness the shoot must emerge using existing reserves, and immediate investment in mature photosynthetic structures would not provide energy from illumination. When light is detected, photomorphogenesis reorganizes growth. Hypocotyl extension decreases, cotyledons open, and chloroplast development becomes coordinated with the change in environment. These traits can be separately measured. A mutation may impair one component of the transition more strongly than others, so a normal cotyledon-opening phenotype does not establish that every phytochrome-regulated response is intact.

For length measurements, the time of the measurement is critical. Suppose two seedlings are both 8 mm long at the start of illumination. After six hours the control is 9 mm long and the mutant is 12 mm long. The average elongation rates over that interval are 1/6 mm per hour and 4/6 mm per hour, respectively. The mutant elongates four times as much during the interval, but its final length is only 12/9, or 1.33 times the control. Which comparison is appropriate depends on the question. If the experiment concerns inhibition of growth after a light treatment, the change in length during the treatment may be more informative than final length alone. These are illustrative values, not measurements from a published experiment.

Cotyledon opening and hypocotyl extension are distinct morphological variables. A cotyledon angle can be measured from a standardized view of the plant, while hypocotyl length can be determined from the appropriate stem segment. Greening may be assessed using chlorophyll-related measurements, though colour alone is affected by illumination and camera conditions. If an experiment claims that a mutant is generally defective in de-etiolation, multiple complementary observations provide stronger evidence than one photograph. In a multiple-statement examination question, one should not accept the claim that longer hypocotyls automatically prove complete failure of photomorphogenesis.

Shade avoidance also involves a growth-rate change, but its ecological interpretation differs from the dark-to-light transition. A plant in open light generally maintains active phyB signaling that restrains inappropriate elongation. When neighbors alter the red-to-far-red spectrum, some of this restraint can be released and elongation may increase. An increase in growth is not the same as an increase in carbon fixation; elongation may depend partly on redistribution of resources and on changes in cell expansion. Long, thin structures may allow access to light but can be costly in crowded or otherwise stressful environments. Whether elongation improves eventual fitness depends on the ecological situation.

A controlled shade experiment may compare plants exposed to a high or low red-to-far-red ratio with similar usable light for photosynthesis. Investigators should record initial size, developmental stage, temperature and treatment duration. If wild-type plants show increased elongation under low ratio but phyB-deficient plants are already elongated under the high ratio and respond less strongly to lowering the ratio, the result is compatible with phyB contributing to the restraint of elongation in open light. A reduced difference between treatments can arise because the mutant starts nearer the upper limit of its growth response. It should not be described simply as an inability to grow.

PIF-dependent changes in auxin production and response contribute to several shade-avoidance outputs. The hormone mechanism should be interpreted as a link between perception and growth rather than a replacement for the receptor mechanism. The red-to-far-red ratio is not auxin, and PIFs do not function as auxin molecules. In the signaling chain, spectral change alters receptor state, receptor state changes regulatory protein activity, regulatory changes influence hormone-related processes, and tissues respond through growth. A perturbation can intervene at any of those steps. That layered explanation makes it possible to predict different mutant outcomes without memorizing separate lists of every phenotype.

Practice and reasoning

Part B

  1. Which form preferentially absorbs red light?
    AnswerPr; red illumination favors conversion to Pfr, not accumulation of Pr.
  2. Which natural cofactor is associated with higher-plant phytochrome?
    AnswerPhytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole, not chlorophyll or FAD.
  3. Which Arabidopsis phytochrome is particularly important for continuous far-red responses?
    AnswerPhyA; phyB is prominent in red and classical low-fluence responses.
  4. What does thermal reversion require?
    AnswerNo incident photon; Pfr can thermally return to Pr.
  5. What is HY5?
    AnswerA transcription regulator associated with photomorphogenesis, not a red-light chromophore.

Part C

  1. Under a hypothetical fixed-pool model, a=0.12 min⁻¹, b=0.03 min⁻¹ and k=0.05 min⁻¹. Calculate the stationary Pfr fraction.
    Answer0.12/(0.12+0.03+0.05)=0.60. The denominator includes both reverse processes.
  2. Red-induced PIF protein loss is absent in a phyB mutant but PIF transcript remains constant in both genotypes. What is supported and what remains uncertain?
    AnswerThe protein change is consistent with phyB-dependent post-transcriptional regulation; direct binding or proteasome involvement requires separate tests.
  3. Two red/far-red sources have the same spectral ratio but tenfold different photon flux. Under negligible thermal reversion, predict how the time to approach equilibrium changes.
    AnswerWith proportional effective forward and reverse rates, the stationary fraction stays the same while the higher-flux source drives faster equilibration.
  4. Why can an immediately applied far-red pulse reverse promotion of lettuce seed germination, while one applied much later may fail?
    AnswerEarly reversal changes photoreceptor state before commitment. A later treatment may arrive after downstream developmental steps have become irreversible.
  5. How would a chromophore-binding mutant and a signaling-domain mutant be distinguished?
    AnswerCompare photoreversible absorption spectra and physiological complementation at matched protein abundance. The first may lack normal photochemistry, whereas the second can retain photochemistry yet fail to restore development.
