1. A plant that anticipates dawn

A leaf experiences an extraordinary range of conditions during a single rotation of Earth. During daylight, chloroplasts can use incident radiation to support carbon fixation, while an open stomatal pore permits carbon dioxide to diffuse into the leaf. At night, that opportunity largely disappears in a conventional C3 leaf, but mitochondrial respiration, protein turnover, transport and growth continue. The plant therefore faces a problem of allocation over time: it must distribute its carbon reserves, prepare for changing opportunities and avoid activating expensive processes when they are unlikely to be useful. A purely reactive plant could wait until sunlight appears and then begin preparing its machinery. A plant with a biological clock can initiate preparatory changes before the environmental transition. The distinction between response and anticipation is central to the study of sensory photobiology.

Consider a bean leaf that changes its angle over a day. Its movement may be caused partly by immediate changes in illumination and partly by processes whose timing was established earlier. Leaf position alone does not establish which of these mechanisms operates. A closed stomatal pore at night is equally ambiguous: the absence of photosynthetic carbon demand, direct light signaling, water status and the internal clock may all contribute. An investigation of a biological clock must therefore separate the changing environment from the organism's ability to generate temporal structure. In the previous chapters on photoreceptors, light provided information about wavelength, direction and intensity. Here the problem is how that information helps establish the phase of an internal timekeeping system.

A circadian clock is an endogenous biological oscillator that can generate recurring temporal states with an approximately daily period under suitable constant environmental conditions. The term endogenous does not mean independent of metabolism, or unaffected by environmental cues. It means that a new external light transition is not required to initiate every successive oscillation. A clock is more than a single rhythm observed in an organ. A rhythmic output can be produced by the clock, by a periodically changing external stimulus, or by both. Conversely a functioning oscillator might control many outputs without producing a measurable oscillation in every physiological variable. Distinguishing the oscillator, its inputs and its outputs gives us a powerful framework for understanding observations throughout this chapter.

Plants do not have a brain or a single anatomical pacemaker equivalent to the mammalian suprachiasmatic nucleus. In Arabidopsis, oscillatory machinery is present in multiple cell types and tissues. These cells can exchange information, and their rhythms can become coordinated without all cells exhibiting identical phases or periods. Timekeeping thus belongs to the organization of a living system rather than to a mechanical device placed inside a particular organ. We will begin with how an endogenous rhythm is recognized, then examine how environmental signals synchronize that rhythm, how molecular feedback maintains temporal organization and how the resulting temporal information shapes daily physiology.

2. Recognizing endogenous timekeeping

The historical study of plant timekeeping began with observations of periodic leaf movements. The classic observation associated with Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan in 1729 involved continued leaf movement in a plant kept in darkness. Its importance lay in the distinction between a movement triggered afresh by light and a movement maintained when the obvious stimulus was absent. The observation was not a complete modern demonstration of a circadian mechanism: the constancy of every environmental variable and the physiological condition of the plant were not established with contemporary instrumentation. It nevertheless posed a question that could be tested with increasingly rigorous experiments. If an external change were responsible for each oscillation, eliminating the change should eliminate the periodic response after any transient effects have subsided.

A modern experiment begins with plants of comparable developmental stage exposed to a repeating light and dark schedule, often twelve hours of light followed by twelve hours of darkness. After several cycles the plants are placed in continuous light at a controlled temperature, or in continuous darkness when the chosen material remains physiologically suitable for the assay. Recording leaf angle, promoter activity or another output over several days reveals whether rhythmic behavior persists. A directly light-induced output might increase when lights are turned on, remain elevated or adapt, and decrease after lights are turned off. Once the environmental signal is held constant, that mechanism alone has no recurring transition to drive a new daily peak. A persistent near-daily sequence suggests an internal oscillator, especially if the spacing between peaks differs slightly from twenty-four hours.

Constant conditions are experimental approximations. Temperature, light intensity, humidity and measurement procedures should be monitored. Opening a growth chamber every morning to sample plants can introduce a recurring temperature or light disturbance; automatic recording or balanced sampling reduces that possibility. Continuous light can also alter the clock's period through photoreceptor signaling, while prolonged darkness may change carbon availability and the viability of the material. Therefore, a free-running period is always reported with its assay conditions. It is not a universally fixed number assigned to a genotype regardless of environment.

Another way to test endogenous regulation is to entrain two genetically similar groups to opposite light–dark schedules. If the plants are transferred at the same laboratory time into one constant environment, their subsequent peaks may remain separated according to their prior internal phases. Since the external conditions are now shared, the phase difference cannot be explained solely by current illumination. It reflects a persistent difference in temporal state established during entrainment. A suitable control is an identically handled group with the same entrainment history. If the two experimental groups instead immediately converge to an identical steady signal, the observation does not provide the same evidence for autonomous timing.

No single readout is infallible. A rhythm in leaf movement can change if a mutation alters motor tissue or cell expansion, even if the molecular oscillator is unaffected. A reporter rhythm can change if a mutation alters the reporter's promoter specifically, even when other clock outputs remain normal. When several independent outputs show corresponding changes in free-running period, a defect in shared timekeeping becomes a more plausible explanation. This experimental logic is illustrated by the isolation of the Arabidopsis toc1 mutant using luciferase imaging and confirmation of the phenotype in leaf movement.

3. Period, phase, amplitude and free-running time

The period of a rhythm is the time required to complete one cycle. If successive maxima occur at 10, 34 and 58 hours, the period is twenty-four hours. The Greek symbol tau commonly represents the period measured in constant conditions. The environmental cycle length, represented by T, is conceptually distinct. A plant with a free-running period of twenty-three hours may display a twenty-four-hour rhythm in a twenty-four-hour light–dark schedule because entrainment adjusts the oscillator. Its underlying free-running period becomes apparent only after the repeated synchronizing signals are removed. Do not infer tau from the interval between two imposed dawns.

The phase is the position of a rhythm within its cycle relative to a defined reference. For a gene-expression peak at hour four after lights-on, phase may be reported as ZT4 when the plants are under an entraining light schedule. ZT means zeitgeber time, with ZT0 conventionally assigned to the beginning of the light interval in many plant experiments. Under constant conditions, circadian time refers to internal timing, usually normalized to the oscillator period. A twenty-three-hour oscillator takes twenty-three real hours to progress through twenty-four circadian hours when this convention is used. The expressions subjective day and subjective night refer to the internal intervals that correspond to previous day and night, even though illumination no longer alternates. They describe temporal state, not the physical presence or absence of photons.

Amplitude describes how far the rhythm departs from its central level. For a symmetric sinusoidal trace with maximum 80 and minimum 20 arbitrary units, the amplitude is (80 − 20)/2 = 30 units and the mean level is 50 units. Some publications report peak-to-trough range instead, which would be 60 units in this example. Always identify the definition before comparing numbers. The biological rhythm may not be sinusoidal, and its amplitude may be estimated from a fitted curve, so the distinction matters. A lower amplitude is not the same as a longer period: the height and horizontal spacing of successive oscillations describe different properties.

A simple descriptive equation is y(t) = M + A cos[2π(t − φ)/τ], where y is the measured signal, M is its mean, A is amplitude, t is time, φ is the time of the peak and τ is the period. Both t and φ must use the same units as τ, so the argument of the cosine is dimensionless. If τ is twenty-four hours and φ is six hours, the maximum occurs at t = 6 h and again at 30 h. At t = 12 h, the cosine argument is π/2 and the signal equals M. At t = 18 h, the cosine equals −1 and the signal equals M − A. This model helps interpret a graph, but it is not a molecular explanation of the plant clock. Real expression curves can be asymmetrical, have drifting baselines or exhibit several regulatory peaks.

To estimate a period from multiple cycles, subtract the first observed peak time from the last and divide by the number of intervening cycles, not the number of recorded peaks. For example, maxima at 5, 29, 53 and 77 h indicate three intervals totaling 72 h, hence τ = 24 h. Peaks at 5, 27, 49 and 71 h instead give τ = 22 h. A single maximum reveals phase but not period. Noise can obscure peak positions, so estimates from many cycles and biological replicates are preferable. When a peak straddles a day boundary, phase must be treated cyclically. A peak moving from ZT23 to ZT1 has moved two hours later, not twenty-two hours earlier, if the relevant comparison is across the same adjacent midnight boundary.

An oscillator population can display declining collective amplitude even if individual cells continue to oscillate strongly. Imagine two cells of equal amplitude whose peaks occur twelve circadian hours apart. Their deviations from the mean cancel when averaged. Slight differences in individual periods can produce gradual loss of alignment under constant conditions. A bulk luminescence trace may therefore damp because individual clocks weaken, because phases disperse, because the reporter signal declines, or because the tissue becomes unhealthy. Single-cell or tissue-resolved observations and independent viability measurements help distinguish these alternatives.

4. Entrainment, phase resetting and masking

An oscillator is useful in nature because its temporal states can align with recurring changes in the environment. Entrainment is stable synchronization of an internal oscillator to a periodic external cue. A zeitgeber is such a timing cue: dawn, dusk and daily temperature cycles are major examples in plants. The internal phase usually bears a reproducible relationship to the external cycle, but the internal peak need not coincide with the cue itself. A plant can be fully entrained while a gene peaks four hours before dawn, provided that relationship is maintained across successive cycles. The stable relationship is called the phase angle of entrainment.

Suppose a plant has τ = 23 h in constant light but is exposed to a 24 h environmental cycle. Without any adjustment, its oscillator would complete its cycle one hour earlier on each day. In an entrained steady state, the net effect of daily environmental signals must compensate for that mismatch. A simplified phase description predicts a net delay of about one hour per environmental cycle. Conversely an oscillator with τ = 25 h would fall behind by one hour each day and requires a net advance of approximately one hour. These statements assume stable one-to-one entrainment and describe the net timing effect, not the biochemical response to a single specific photon pulse.

A phase response curve, or PRC, plots the phase shift caused by a standardized stimulus against the internal phase at which that stimulus is delivered. For a brief light pulse, one set of plants might show an advance when the pulse occurs late in subjective night and a delay when the pulse occurs earlier. During another interval the same pulse may produce little persistent change. This phase dependence means that light is not simply a reset switch that always sets the oscillator to zero. The molecular state of the clock changes the sensitivity to the same environmental signal. The detailed curve depends on genotype, wavelength, intensity, background conditions and pulse duration.

To obtain a PRC, entrain replicate groups, then transfer them to a constant background. Deliver identical pulses to separate groups at different internal phases. Include no-pulse controls for each comparison, record several subsequent cycles and fit the new rhythm after the immediate transient. Define positive shifts as advances and negative shifts as delays, or explicitly state another convention. If control peaks occur at 48 and 72 h while treated peaks occur at 45 and 69 h, the treated rhythm has advanced by three hours and its observed period remains twenty-four hours. If treated peaks occur at 45 and 67 h, both phase and period may have changed; further cycles help separate the two effects.

An immediate change in a measured output is not sufficient evidence of resetting. Light can open stomata through phototropin-dependent signaling or change expression of a light-responsive gene without producing a persistent shift of the oscillator. This direct effect on an output is often called masking when it modifies the expression of an underlying rhythm. To distinguish resetting from masking, continue recording after the stimulus ends and examine subsequent peaks of a clock-associated reporter or another independent rhythm. A large transient fluorescence or luminescence change followed by unchanged peak timing suggests direct output modulation rather than a lasting phase adjustment.

Entrainment has limits. A daily cue that is too weak may fail to correct the difference between environmental and internal periods. A very unusual environmental cycle may also fall outside the range to which an oscillator can lock. The result can be progressive drift, intermittent coordination or a more complicated timing pattern. In nature, plants receive several cues simultaneously, including temperature and light. If dawn is experimentally shifted while temperature continues to cycle at its old phase, different pathways may compete or combine to set the oscillator. Comparing light-only, temperature-only and conflicting-cue treatments is a useful approach to separating their contributions.

5. Temperature compensation and the thermal zeitgeber

A central property of circadian timekeeping is relative temperature compensation. Within a physiologically suitable range, the free-running period changes much less with temperature than the rates of many individual biochemical reactions. This is surprising because the oscillator is built from temperature-dependent processes such as transcription, translation, binding, phosphorylation and protein degradation. Temperature compensation arises at the level of the coupled system; it does not require every constituent reaction to proceed at a temperature-independent rate. The word relative is important: modest changes in period are possible, and compensation can break down outside a suitable range.

The period should not be confused with rate. If an oscillator completes one cycle in twenty-four hours, its frequency is 1/24 cycles per hour. For illustrative measurements of τ = 25 h at 20 °C and τ = 24 h at 30 °C, the frequency-based Q10 is (1/24)/(1/25) = 25/24 = 1.042. A hypothetical enzyme reaction whose rate doubles over that temperature interval has Q10 = 2. The clock frequency is comparatively buffered in the example. If the temperature difference is not exactly ten degrees, use Q10 = (r2/r1)^(10/(T2 − T1)), with temperatures in degrees Celsius and rates expressed in the same units. A Q10 calculated directly from periods would be the inverse of the frequency-based value and must be labeled accordingly.

Temperature compensation and temperature entrainment are not contradictory. Compensation describes how the stable free-running period responds to a sustained change of mean temperature. Entrainment concerns how an appropriately timed change can shift the phase. A clock can react to a warm-to-cool transition while still maintaining a near-daily period under either constant temperature. To test compensation, entrain matched groups and record their rhythms in constant light at different constant temperatures after allowing for initial transients. To test thermal entrainment, expose groups to recurring warm and cool intervals under otherwise constant conditions, then observe the phase after return to one common temperature. The two experimental designs answer different questions.

In Arabidopsis, PRR7 and PRR9, the evening complex and other oscillator regulators participate in responses to temperature. ELF3 is particularly relevant to the integration of thermal and light information with evening regulation. At the syllabus level, it is important to understand how an environmental signal alters the balance of clock regulators rather than assume that a distinct temperature sensor determines every thermal response. Temperature also changes physiology independently of the clock, so a temperature-dependent change in elongation or metabolism is not in itself evidence of altered oscillator phase. Clock-gene measurements and appropriately timed controls distinguish regulation of timing from changes in the rate of a downstream reaction.

6. How delayed feedback generates temporal order

The molecular oscillator of Arabidopsis is a regulatory network rather than a single molecule with an intrinsic twenty-four-hour lifetime. Transcription factors influence the expression of genes whose products in turn alter transcription or protein activity. When a regulator changes the production of a component that later influences the first regulator, a feedback loop is formed. Negative feedback can stabilize a system at a constant value, but when repression is delayed and sufficiently strong, it can also contribute to oscillation. Positive influences, multiple interconnected loops and timed protein turnover shape the stability and phase relationships of the network.

To see why delay matters, consider a conceptual regulator whose gene is transcribed when its protein concentration is low. Messenger RNA is produced and translated, and the protein accumulates. If protein subsequently represses its own gene, production of new RNA declines. The existing RNA and protein take time to disappear. As the protein concentration eventually falls, repression weakens and transcription can resume. The duration of these processes creates a delay between the initial transcriptional change and its eventual consequence. This description illustrates a feedback principle, not a claim that one isolated Arabidopsis gene is sufficient to sustain a circadian oscillator. A simple negative feedback loop without appropriate kinetics can instead approach equilibrium.

The Arabidopsis clock contains temporally organized transcriptional repressors and activators. Around subjective dawn, the MYB-like transcription factors CCA1 and LHY are important regulators. During the day, several members of the pseudo-response regulator family accumulate in a characteristic order. TOC1, also called PRR1, has evening-associated expression and represses several clock targets. The evening complex, containing ELF3, ELF4 and LUX, contributes to repression in the evening and early night. Other transcription factors, including REVEILLE proteins, help activate afternoon and evening gene expression. The order refers to the broad timing of expression and activity, not to a universal series of sharply separated intervals in which each protein becomes completely absent before the next appears.

Transcription, translation and protein stability provide separate layers of timing. If a transcript peaks at midday, its protein may peak later because synthesis and degradation have their own kinetics. If protein activity depends on phosphorylation or complex formation, activity may have a phase different from the measured total protein concentration. A promoter reporter measures one aspect of gene expression; it does not establish the abundance, location or DNA-binding capacity of the encoded protein. Time courses of RNA, protein and functional output are therefore complementary. Mutant phenotypes can arise through changes in any of these regulatory layers.

Clock diagrams often represent an arrow as a positive influence and a bar-ended line as repression. A line between two nodes is not always a physical interaction between the two proteins. One protein can repress transcription of the other's gene by binding a promoter, or indirectly by changing another regulator. Conversely proteins within the evening complex physically associate. This difference is important when interpreting biochemical experiments. Protein co-immunoprecipitation can support physical association; DNA occupancy can support promoter binding; an expression change after perturbation supports regulatory influence but does not by itself prove direct binding. A useful clock model specifies what the edges represent and which are simplified.

7. Morning MYB regulators: CCA1 and LHY

CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY) encode closely related MYB-like transcription factors in Arabidopsis. Their expression typically rises toward dawn and their proteins participate in establishing morning-associated transcriptional states. Among their important functions is repression of a range of genes whose normal expression is later in the day, including TOC1 and several other oscillator components. Certain evening-phased promoters contain a sequence known as the evening element, recognized by clock-associated MYB factors. The ability of different regulators to interact with related promoter sequences is one means by which timing information is translated into transcription.

Consider the TOC1 promoter at dawn. When CCA1 and LHY activity is high, repression contributes to keeping TOC1 transcription relatively low. Later, as morning regulator activity declines, that repression weakens. Release from repression permits transcription but is not equivalent to active stimulation; afternoon activators and the state of other regulatory pathways also matter. This distinction helps reconcile the presence of both negative and positive influences in a network. A promoter can require an activator even when its repressor has disappeared. If we remove CCA1 and LHY, an earlier expression phase of some targets may be expected, but the full phenotype depends on changes in the rest of the oscillator.

Partial functional redundancy means that two proteins carry overlapping biological functions. A mutation eliminating CCA1 may leave LHY-dependent activity, and a mutation eliminating LHY may leave CCA1-dependent activity. The double perturbation can produce a more severe disturbance than either individual perturbation. Clock mutant phenotypes should be described in relation to allele, genetic background and assay conditions. Shortened periods and weakened rhythms have been observed in perturbations of these morning regulators. Constitutive overexpression can be more disruptive than a simple loss of one gene because inappropriate expression throughout the cycle can prevent the usual temporal release of repression.

An experiment examining the regulatory relationship between CCA1 and TOC1 could compare TOC1 promoter reporter activity after inducing CCA1 at a defined phase. A reduction in promoter activity would support a negative regulatory effect. To determine whether that influence is direct, evidence of promoter occupancy and sequence-dependent binding is valuable. Altering the relevant binding sequence, while preserving other promoter functions as far as possible, provides an additional test. Measuring RNA promptly after induction and assessing other regulators helps reduce the likelihood that a long sequence of indirect changes explains the observation. None of these assays alone establishes that the tested interaction generates the entire oscillator.

8. Daytime PRRs and evening TOC1

The PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR family is named for a domain related to receiver domains in response-regulator proteins. Do not infer from that name that the plant circadian oscillator operates as a conventional bacterial histidine-to-aspartate two-component phosphorelay. Arabidopsis PRR9, PRR7, PRR5 and TOC1 (PRR1) contribute to temporal regulation through their effects on gene expression and their interactions with other clock components. PRR proteins accumulate in a broadly sequential manner during the day and toward evening, with overlapping expression intervals. In simplified terms, PRR9 is associated with an earlier portion of the day than PRR7 and PRR5, while TOC1 is associated with evening.

An important shared role of these PRRs is repression of morning-associated genes including CCA1 and LHY. As the morning regulators decline, sequential PRR activity contributes to preventing their premature return. When later repressors disappear or their activity diminishes during the night, repression of the morning state can be relieved. This chain of repression is one explanation of how timing is distributed over the whole cycle rather than concentrated in a single switch. Removing one PRR may change only part of this temporal interval; removing several may create a much larger disturbance. The actual free-running period cannot be deduced from the signs of the edges alone, because the remaining network can compensate or reorganize.

TOC1 became an important molecular entry point into circadian genetics following the identification of a mutant with altered rhythmic timing. Early models represented TOC1 as a positive regulator of morning gene expression. Later experimental work revised this view, identifying TOC1 as a DNA-binding transcriptional repressor. In contemporary introductory models, TOC1 and CCA1/LHY can participate in reciprocal repression embedded within a larger network. The historical correction is a useful lesson in scientific modeling: if changing the abundance of a regulator indirectly increases expression of a second gene, that does not establish a direct positive transcriptional effect. The regulator might repress another repressor, or its action might change the phase at which the second gene is measured.

The classic toc1 allele was identified through a shorter circadian period rather than merely a reduced expression level. Its effect on both a CAB-associated reporter and leaf movement supported a role in shared timing. In an examination, if the period of two independent outputs shortens by similar amounts while the amplitude of one output also changes, the consistent period phenotype is stronger evidence for involvement in common temporal regulation than an isolated amplitude change. Different alleles of a clock gene can have different effects, and a transgene expressed constitutively does not necessarily mimic a loss-of-function allele. Specify the perturbation before predicting the response.

PRR activity itself is timed by regulation at several levels. The morning and evening components affect expression of PRR genes, while protein degradation, interaction partners and intracellular localization influence their activity. Thus the observed sequence is produced by coupled regulation. A study in which PRR protein abundance changes without a corresponding change in its RNA abundance would invite investigation of translation, stability or localization. If a degradation-resistant protein persists beyond its normal interval, repression of its targets may persist as well, changing the timing of the next transcriptional state. This is one reason protein turnover is part of the oscillator mechanism rather than merely a disposal process.

9. The evening complex and the transition to night

The evening complex is a regulatory assembly containing EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), EARLY FLOWERING 4 (ELF4) and LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX). LUX is a DNA-binding transcription factor that helps target the complex to particular regulatory sequences. ELF3 and ELF4 contribute to assembly and regulatory function. This organization illustrates how protein partners that do not all recognize DNA independently can nevertheless influence transcription as part of a complex. The abundance of individual components, their association and their timing all matter. A measurement of ELF3 RNA by itself is therefore not a complete measure of evening-complex activity.

Among important evening-complex targets are PRR genes such as PRR9. Their repression during the evening and night helps restrict daytime-associated expression to the correct interval. As the system progresses into the next morning, changes in the abundance and activity of repressors allow the dawn-associated transcriptional program to return. This description is deliberately not a simple linear succession of seven switches: several regulators act simultaneously, and both mutual repression and activation contribute to the oscillator. The evening complex also regulates output genes, including PIF4 and PIF5 in the context of hypocotyl growth. The same complex can thus participate in the internal timing mechanism and link that timing to a developmental process.

Experiments that identified the complex combined genetic and biochemical evidence. Association of proteins supports a physical assembly, while promoter-binding experiments can establish association with particular gene regulatory regions. Expression and growth phenotypes after loss of a component help test its functional role. An important inference is that a mutation affecting the DNA-binding component may disrupt recruitment of the rest of the complex to a target promoter even if the other proteins are still present. A mutation affecting a scaffold or interaction surface can likewise alter regulation without abolishing the DNA-binding domain of LUX. These are distinct molecular failures with potentially similar organism-level outcomes.

Under relevant experimental conditions, mutations in ELF3, ELF4 or LUX can strongly impair free-running rhythms, while seedlings may also show altered elongation and flowering. The presence of a long hypocotyl by itself does not identify which molecular function is altered: the phenotype could arise from defective light perception, altered hormone response or mistimed repression of growth genes. To link growth to the clock, combine observations of growth rate at different times of day with clock reporter data and genetic perturbations. An evening complex mutation that alters both rhythmic expression and the timing of growth provides stronger evidence for temporal control than a final hypocotyl length measured once.

10. Activating influences and timed protein degradation

Although many core clock regulators act as transcriptional repressors, positive transcriptional influences are also essential to a useful description of the network. The REVEILLE family includes MYB-like factors such as RVE4, RVE6 and RVE8, which can promote expression of a number of afternoon- and evening-associated genes. RVE8 can act with LNK coactivators to promote transcription at targets including PRR5 and TOC1. This creates a useful contrast with CCA1 and LHY: factors with related DNA-binding properties may exert different effects on transcription depending on their regulatory domains and partners. The phase of transcription depends on the balance of these influences, rather than on the presence or absence of a single DNA motif.

Protein stability is also regulated. ZEITLUPE (ZTL) is an F-box-containing protein involved in timing the degradation of clock proteins, including TOC1 and PRR5. An F-box protein can contribute substrate recognition in an SCF-type ubiquitin ligase complex. Ubiquitin-dependent degradation by the proteasome helps remove particular proteins at appropriate times. ZTL also contains a light-responsive LOV domain, and its relationship with GIGANTEA (GI) contributes to regulation of ZTL stability. These links illustrate why the boundary between input and oscillator regulation is functional rather than absolute: light can influence the timing and abundance of proteins that help maintain the molecular clock.

If a protein is produced during the day and normally degraded during the night, failure to degrade it can prolong its activity beyond the usual interval. A loss of ZTL function has been associated with longer free-running periods under relevant conditions. This illustrates the relationship between molecular lifetime and system-level timing, but it does not mean that doubling the lifetime of a single protein must double the circadian period. The organism contains multiple interacting regulators, and both production and decay rates influence how long a transcriptional state persists. A mechanistic explanation therefore connects a changed lifetime with changed regulatory activity and then with the measured rhythm.

A useful comparison involves a promoter reporter for a clock gene and a protein fusion reporter for its encoded regulator. If promoter activity rises and falls normally but the protein remains high for longer than in wild type, the defect may concern protein stability. One could examine protein abundance after inhibiting new synthesis under carefully controlled conditions or compare a protein with a modified degradation-recognition region to its normal counterpart. Such interventions may also affect overall cellular physiology, so conclusions require appropriate controls and more than one observation. A single time point cannot distinguish increased synthesis from reduced degradation.

11. Light input and the distinction between sensor and clock

Phytochromes and cryptochromes supply important timing information to the plant clock. Phytochromes respond to red and far-red light and cryptochromes to blue and UV-A wavelengths, as discussed in the earlier nodes. Their actions influence transcription and protein regulation and thereby can change oscillator phase or period. A photoreceptor, however, is not identical to an oscillator. A receptor can convey information about dawn even if the oscillator itself continues to generate rhythmic states in a constant environment. Likewise a defect in one receptor may change how the oscillator responds to a particular wavelength without necessarily eliminating all rhythmic behavior.

One classic approach is to compare free-running reporter rhythms under different intensities or wavelengths of constant light in wild type and photoreceptor mutants. If the period changes with light intensity in wild type but the effect is reduced in a mutant, the affected gene is implicated in that light-dependent influence. Because phytochromes and blue-light-responsive pathways can overlap and act in different contexts, residual entrainment does not prove that the receptor has no role. The experiment addresses the tested light conditions and genetic background, not all possible environmental signals.

Light can affect the clock through indirect metabolic consequences as well. Photosynthesis generates carbohydrates that can influence timing signals and metabolic status. A light treatment may therefore change both photoreceptor activation and carbon availability. To separate these effects, an experiment might vary spectral quality and photosynthetic conditions while monitoring a clock reporter and controlling nutrition. Interpretation requires care because inhibitors of photosynthesis can cause stress, and added sugar can alter osmotic conditions as well as metabolism. The purpose of these comparisons is to distinguish competing hypotheses, not to assume that every light response has a single photoreceptor-only explanation.

Phototropin-mediated stomatal opening provides a clear example of the contrast between direct light signaling and temporal modulation. Blue light can activate guard-cell signaling and promote opening, while the clock can alter the sensitivity or baseline conductance at different times of day. Both processes can occur in the same plant, but the immediate guard-cell response is not a complete model of oscillator entrainment. The relevant examination question is often whether a stimulus has modified the oscillator or merely changed one measured output. Tracking several post-stimulus cycles makes that distinction more reliable.

12. Cellular and tissue-specific clocks

An intact plant includes tissues with very different roles and access to environmental cues. Mesophyll cells participate directly in photosynthesis, vascular cells transport metabolites and signals, root cells experience a largely different light environment, and guard cells regulate pore aperture. These cells may express overlapping sets of clock genes yet exhibit distinct rhythmic phases or amplitudes. A plant clock should therefore be understood as a collection of spatially organized oscillators whose states are influenced by local and systemic information, rather than a single uniform transcriptional waveform imposed on every cell.

A bulk RNA or luminescence measurement from a whole seedling sums signals from several cell populations. If the leaf signal peaks at one phase and the root signal at another, the average may peak at an intermediate time or show reduced amplitude. Changing the proportion of tissues can shift the apparent phase even without altering the phase in any individual cell. Tissue-specific reporters, separated organs and spatial imaging are useful because they can reveal which biological compartment contributes to an observed rhythm. Comparisons of isolated and intact tissues must consider changes in nutrition, wound response and environmental exposure caused by the isolation.

Clock coordination can involve communication between tissues. Shoots receive strong direct light cues and roots may receive systemic metabolic or regulatory information. A root rhythmic response is not automatically evidence that the root lacks an autonomous oscillator. Conversely the presence of clock-gene expression in a root does not establish independence from the shoot. A possible test compares the timing of a root reporter when shoot and root receive differently phased temperature or light cues, using conditions that prevent unintended exposure. Reciprocal grafts or tissue-specific genetic manipulation can further test how one organ influences another. These are conceptual tools for reasoning about causality, not a requirement to master specialist laboratory techniques.

Because clocks may differ across tissues, a mutation can affect one physiological output more strongly than another. A guard-cell clock perturbation might change the time course of stomatal conductance while leaving the dominant mesophyll signal comparatively intact. To establish a tissue-specific effect, compare the targeted tissue with an appropriate control tissue, verify specificity of the genetic change and check whether measured effects are consistent with local or systemic regulation. The biological lesson is that a daily temporal program coordinates specialized processes without requiring them all to occur simultaneously.

13. Daily physiological outputs and the value of anticipation

Photosynthesis depends immediately on light, but many components of photosynthetic capacity are regulated over longer time scales. Clock-controlled transcription can prepare parts of the photosynthetic machinery for the expected day, and rhythmic regulation can coordinate carbon fixation with downstream metabolism. Under continuous light, some aspects of photosynthetic physiology may retain rhythmic variation. However, the absence of a rhythm in net carbon assimilation in a particular experiment does not prove that chloroplast-related genes are not clock controlled. Measured gas exchange integrates many processes and environmental limitations.

The coordination of starch turnover provides an intuitive example of resource allocation. Carbon fixed during the day can be stored as starch and mobilized during darkness to supply metabolism and growth. If the plant consumed its reserve too early, it might experience carbon shortage before dawn. If it mobilized starch too slowly, useful reserves would remain inaccessible during much of the night. Daily timing therefore helps balance current demand and expected time until the next period of photosynthetic supply. The regulation is not simply a clock-controlled constant reaction rate; substrate availability, metabolic feedback and developmental demand also influence the observed rate. An illustrative calculation clarifies the problem: a reserve of 120 arbitrary carbon units distributed evenly across a twelve-hour night corresponds to ten units per hour. If the expected remaining night becomes fifteen hours and the reserve remains 120 units, an average rate of eight units per hour would conserve the reserve until dawn. These numbers express a resource-budgeting principle, not measurements from a published experiment.

Stomatal conductance reflects the ability of gases to move through the leaf's pores. It can be modulated by the clock in conjunction with light, carbon dioxide, humidity, ABA and water status. Anticipatory changes may prepare a leaf for gas exchange, but a drought-stressed plant can close stomata despite a clock phase favorable for opening. This distinction is important: clock regulation changes the probability, sensitivity or timing of a response rather than requiring that a fixed output occur independently of environmental constraints. CAM plants provide a useful reminder that nocturnal stomatal opening can be physiologically appropriate in a different photosynthetic strategy.

Growth is similarly timed. In Arabidopsis seedlings under particular light–dark schedules, clock-dependent regulation of growth-associated transcription factors interacts with light-dependent regulation of their proteins to restrict rapid hypocotyl elongation to favorable intervals. The evening complex helps repress PIF4 and PIF5 transcription during evening phases, while light signaling can regulate PIF protein abundance or activity. A plant with high transcript abundance does not necessarily grow rapidly if the corresponding protein is inhibited by light. The intersection of transcriptional timing and protein regulation explains why an output can be gated to a narrower interval than either input alone. This reasoning also helps distinguish a mutation that changes clock phase from a mutation that changes the response to light.

Flowering introduces a seasonal dimension to daily timekeeping. The oscillator can define intervals of competence during the day, and the environment determines whether those intervals coincide with light. In the Arabidopsis long-day flowering model, the clock influences the timing of CONSTANS expression while light-associated pathways influence CONSTANS protein stability and action. The overlap can favor expression of FLOWERING LOCUS T in leaf vascular tissue. The detailed day-length mechanism belongs to node 6.E.07; the essential prerequisite is that the oscillator supplies a phase-dependent state rather than directly measuring every photon received across the day.

The functional value of a clock can be tested by comparing organisms whose internal periods match or mismatch the environmental cycle. Dodd and colleagues compared Arabidopsis wild type and clock-period mutants under differing environmental cycle lengths and evaluated physiological performance and fitness-related traits. The results supported the advantage of appropriate temporal alignment in the tested conditions. This is stronger than merely observing a correlation between gene expression and daytime. It addresses whether the relationship between internal timing and external timing affects plant performance. It does not imply that the same period is universally optimal for every species or environment.

14. Luciferase reporters, mutants and experimental inference

A promoter reporter connects a regulatory DNA sequence to a measurable output. In a luciferase reporter construct, a promoter of interest drives expression of a luciferase coding sequence. Firefly luciferase produces light through an enzyme-catalyzed reaction involving luciferin, ATP and oxygen. Sensitive imaging allows repeated measurements in living plants. A CAB or CCA1 promoter reporter can reveal rhythmic promoter-associated activity over several days without destroying each specimen at every time point. Nevertheless emitted light is not a direct measurement of the endogenous clock protein concentration. Reporter stability, luciferin availability, ATP status, oxygen and imaging settings can influence the recorded signal.

In the landmark 1995 genetic screen by Millar and colleagues, rhythms of a luciferase reporter were used to identify Arabidopsis mutants with altered timing. The toc1 mutant exhibited a shorter period in both CAB-associated expression and primary leaf movement. The use of distinct readouts was important because the investigators could distinguish a shared timing phenotype from an isolated reporter-specific abnormality. The result identified a genetic entry point into the oscillator, but did not by itself establish the molecular sign of every TOC1 interaction. Later molecular work clarified its repressor function. Thus discovery of a gene by phenotype and definition of its biochemical mechanism are related but separate stages of evidence.

A well-controlled comparison uses wild type and mutant plants grown under equivalent conditions, matching age, light exposure and temperature. Several biological replicates should be recorded, preferably over multiple cycles. If mutants and wild type are compared in different growth chambers, chamber-specific temperature differences can confound genotype effects. Randomization or rotation of samples, alongside environmental monitoring, helps reduce bias. An appropriate complementation experiment introduces a functional copy of the candidate gene into the mutant background and tests whether rhythmic behavior approaches the wild-type pattern. Rescue supports attribution of the phenotype to the candidate gene, although expression level and transgene insertion effects must also be considered.

Consider an illustrative dataset in which wild-type reporter peaks occur at 8, 32, 56 and 80 h, and a mutant peaks at 8, 30, 52 and 74 h. Wild-type period is 24 h and mutant period is 22 h. Both begin at the same measured phase, but the mutant gradually advances relative to wild type because its period is shorter. By the fourth peak the mutant is six hours earlier. If leaf movement also exhibits a shorter period, the case for a timing defect is strengthened. If a second clock reporter retains a twenty-four-hour period but only the first reporter changes, promoter-specific regulation becomes a plausible alternative. It is then premature to assign the candidate gene as an essential core oscillator component.

One can further distinguish a stable period change from a phase shift by inspecting the spacing between successive peaks after a stimulus. An advance of four hours that remains constant relative to the control with unchanged spacing indicates a shifted phase. An advance that increases by two additional hours every cycle indicates a difference in period as well. A trace that loses visible peaks cannot be assigned a reliable period simply by counting noise maxima. A time series may need detrending, background correction or appropriate rhythmicity analysis before comparing parameters. Recording duration, sampling intervals and reproducibility are as important as the visual appearance of the graph.

Genetic interaction experiments can clarify the organization of the network. For example, if loss of an evening-complex component increases growth-associated transcription and eliminating the corresponding PIF genes suppresses excessive elongation, the result supports an output pathway in which PIF activity contributes to the growth phenotype. It does not automatically establish that removing PIFs restores the core oscillator. Separate measurements of growth and free-running clock reporters distinguish suppression of an output phenotype from rescue of molecular timekeeping. This is a recurring principle in Part C questions: the same mutation can affect more than one causal branch, and rescuing one branch need not rescue the others.
