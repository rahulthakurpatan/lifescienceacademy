1. The reproductive problem and the two plant generations

A flowering plant must make two very different kinds of reproductive structures. Its pollen carries the male gametophyte, whereas each ovule normally shelters a developing female gametophyte. The egg cell is only one constituent of this female structure. A second fertilization target, the central cell, must also be established. Other cells provide guidance and support. These requirements explain why development of the female side cannot be understood simply as a plant version of animal oogenesis. A reduction division is followed by a series of growth, nuclear division and differentiation events that produce an organized reproductive system inside a maternal organ. Understanding where one generation ends and another begins is the key to interpreting these events.

The visible flowering plant belongs to the sporophyte generation. Its somatic cells ordinarily contain two homologous chromosome sets in a diploid species. The sporophyte generates spores by meiosis. A spore is not a gamete: it can give rise to a new developmental phase without first fusing with another reproductive cell. The descendants of a haploid spore belong to the gametophyte generation. Gametophytes make gametes by differentiation within a haploid lineage; they need not undergo another reduction division. This alternation explains why a diploid megaspore mother cell gives rise to a haploid megaspore, and why mitotic descendants of that megaspore become the egg and the other female gametophytic cells. If a question asks which cell undergoes meiosis, distinguish the sporophyte's spore-producing cell from the actual egg.

The egg and the central cell have different reproductive destinations. In the usual sexual pathway, one sperm nucleus contributes to the zygote with the egg nucleus. Another sperm nucleus contributes to the primary endosperm nucleus with the central cell's maternal nuclear material. The central cell therefore functions as a second female fertilization target despite having a different nuclear constitution from the egg. Its role is not to generate a second embryo in the ordinary Polygonum-type arrangement. The future embryo and endosperm will develop in association with tissues of the ovule derived from the parent sporophyte. Thus, by the time a seed begins development, tissues with three distinct developmental origins may occupy a single small physical structure. The female gametophyte precedes these new fertilization products.

The terminology is easier to understand if we organize it around the processes. Megasporogenesis is the production of megaspores by the megaspore mother cell, including the meiotic reduction. Megagametogenesis is the development of the functional megaspore into the female gametophyte. The term embryo sac refers to the angiosperm female gametophyte, although a developing sac contains no embryo before egg fertilization. The prefix mega refers historically to the distinction between the two spore types rather than a fixed numerical size threshold. The male and female branches of the life cycle are linked by fertilization but each has its own cellular sequence. In the male branch a microspore becomes a pollen grain; in the female branch a megaspore remains enclosed within the maternal ovule and normally becomes an embryo sac.

A useful thought experiment is to imagine a diploid plant whose gametophytes were accidentally made by a purely mitotic route. In the absence of a compensating mechanism, the resulting gametes could carry unreduced chromosome complements. Their fusion with ordinary reduced gametes would then produce descendants with increased ploidy. This is a different situation from a normal central cell acquiring two haploid nuclei during development. The first concerns failure to reduce the chromosome complement in the germline, whereas the second concerns combination of nuclei after reduction has already occurred. Correct chromosome bookkeeping therefore depends on the sequence of developmental events rather than on the appearance of an isolated cell. Natural variation and apomictic pathways provide exceptions to the ordinary sexual route, but should not be used to confuse the standard mechanism.

2. Ovule anatomy and the maternal developmental environment

The female gametophyte develops inside the ovule, a structure produced by the placenta of the ovary. An ovule is not the same as the ovary: the ovary is a larger floral structure that may contain one or several ovules. The funiculus attaches an ovule to the placenta. The nucellus is the central sporophytic tissue within which the female reproductive lineage develops. One or two integuments usually grow around the nucellus, leaving an opening called the micropyle. The region where the integuments and nucellus meet near the base is termed the chalaza. The micropyle and chalaza establish a useful reference axis for describing the developing gametophyte, even when the ovule has curved or inverted during its growth.

In an anatropous ovule, a common arrangement among angiosperms, growth turns the body of the ovule so that the micropyle lies close to the funiculus. This geometry helps explain why the micropylar pole is not necessarily at the top of a published diagram. A textbook may draw an ovule with the micropyle above the nucellus to simplify embryo-sac organization, whereas a whole flower section may show it below or beside the funiculus. The correct identification uses the opening between integuments rather than the page orientation. The chalazal region is the opposite developmental reference for the gametophyte. The egg apparatus will generally develop on the micropylar side in the standard pattern; the antipodal region is on the chalazal side.

The maternal nucellus can provide the physical and metabolic environment required for growth of the enclosed gametophyte. Integuments contribute protection and later participate in seed coat formation. Their chromosomes belong to the maternal sporophyte; they do not become haploid just because a haploid megaspore develops nearby. This spatial juxtaposition is important when interpreting tissue samples. For example, extracting DNA from an entire immature ovule would include both somatic and reproductive cells. A strong diploid DNA signal from the sample is compatible with the presence of a haploid embryo sac. To establish the ploidy of the gametophytic cells, one must identify the relevant nuclei rather than infer their constitution from bulk ovule tissue.

A reproductive precursor differentiates within the young nucellus, commonly in a subepidermal position. In many species this precursor enlarges, acquires distinctive cytological features and develops as the megaspore mother cell. Developmental routes differ among species: a precursor can function directly as the mother cell or undergo a division giving a sporogenous cell and other tissue derivatives. It is useful to reserve the term megaspore mother cell for the cell entering the meiotic program. A large nucleus, dense cytoplasm or prominent nucleolus can help identify the presumptive cell histologically, but morphology alone does not prove meiosis. Chromosome organization, appropriate molecular markers and the appearance of meiotic products provide stronger evidence.

Maternal and gametophytic genetic functions must be distinguished. A mutation in a diploid ovule tissue may disrupt signals or resources needed by a genetically normal female gametophyte. Conversely, a mutation expressed within a haploid gametophyte can interfere with its own development even when the maternal plant appears healthy. When abnormal ovules are found in a heterozygous plant, a useful question is whether the phenotype is associated with the genotype of the gametophyte or with all maternal ovules sharing the same diploid genotype. This distinction provides a link to the developmental genetics of Unit 5.A. The female gametophyte is small, but it is not an isolated cell culture: its development depends on interactions with the surrounding organ.

An ovule is organized before the gametophyte becomes mature. Its integuments and nucellus are growing while the reproductive lineage undergoes its own changes. Consequently, size or shape of the entire ovule is only an approximate marker of the reproductive stage. Ovules within an inflorescence may not develop synchronously, and environmental conditions or mutations can alter timing. A series of microscopic specimens is normally arranged by recognizable cellular states rather than assumed to represent a clock that is identical in every flower. This principle becomes particularly important when interpreting experimental evidence for failure of embryo-sac development.

3. Megasporogenesis: meiosis and chromosome accounting

The transition from the sporophyte generation to the gametophyte begins when the megaspore mother cell enters meiosis. Before meiotic prophase, DNA replication creates a sister chromatid for each chromosome. Homologous chromosomes then pair, and recombination may exchange corresponding segments between nonsister chromatids. At the first meiotic division homologues separate, reducing the number of chromosome sets in each daughter nucleus. At the second division sister chromatids separate. These two segregation events follow one premeiotic DNA replication phase; no ordinary S phase separates meiosis I and meiosis II. The common monosporic pathway finishes with four haploid megaspores. Cell walls or cellular partitions establish four distinct products in the standard tetrad.

The symbols n and C describe different quantities. The first represents the number of chromosomes in one reduced set for the diploid system under consideration, while C denotes the DNA content of one unreplicated haploid genome. A diploid mother cell in G1 is 2n and 2C. After replication it remains 2n but contains 4C of nuclear DNA. Following meiosis I, each daughter nucleus ordinarily has n chromosomes and 2C of DNA because its chromosomes still consist of two sister chromatids. After meiosis II each product contains n chromosomes and C DNA. A single chromosome with two attached sister chromatids is still counted as one chromosome until the sister centromeres separate. A question describing a replicated diploid nucleus as tetraploid is therefore incorrect.

Suppose a species has 2n equal to 18. The G1 megaspore mother cell has eighteen chromosomes. After replication the nucleus has eighteen replicated chromosomes containing thirty-six chromatids. At the end of meiosis I each daughter nucleus has nine replicated chromosomes, or eighteen chromatids. At completion of meiosis II each megaspore has nine chromosomes consisting of nine chromatids. If DNA content is measured rather than chromosomes counted, the relative amounts are 2C, 4C, 2C and C respectively. The first daughter stage can have the same amount of DNA as the original G1 nucleus without having the same ploidy. This is not a contradiction: one nucleus contains two homologous sets of unreplicated chromosomes, and the other contains a single set of replicated chromosomes.

Tetrad arrangement is not an additional genetic division. In a linear tetrad the products lie along an axis, often corresponding broadly to the micropylar–chalazal direction. Other geometries occur, depending on spindle orientation and cytokinesis. The exact configuration is relevant because later selection of a functional megaspore often corresponds to a particular position. Nuclear number, cellular number and arrangement should be observed separately. For example, a cell with two meiotic nuclei but without a complete partition is not yet a conventional two-celled dyad. A cell containing four meiotic nuclei is not automatically a four-celled tetrad, a distinction that becomes essential when comparing monosporic, bisporic and tetrasporic development.

Recombination provides a source of genetic differences among the four products even though they derive from a single mother cell. In a heterozygous diploid with alleles A and a, conventional meiosis without segregation distortion gives two products carrying A and two carrying a at that locus. If just one product becomes functional, the other products do not contribute nuclear genomes to the usual monosporic embryo sac. Across many meioses, equal transmission of the two alleles is expected under assumptions of fair segregation, equivalent viability and no genotype-dependent selection. Within one tetrad, however, the functional product has only one of the two alleles. It is not a miniature diploid parent containing both. This provides the genetic basis for detecting transmission defects in a haploid female lineage.

Errors during meiotic chromosome segregation can yield products with unbalanced chromosome complements. A defect causing nondisjunction of a homologous pair during meiosis I differs from a failure in one of the second-division spindles. The first can produce two classes of aneuploid products, with the affected homologues segregating together. The latter can leave products from the unaffected first-division nucleus normal. Actual survival of abnormal megaspores will depend on the particular chromosome imbalance and the developmental context. This reasoning is valid even without memorizing a particular mutant name; it follows from the sequence of segregation and the fact that only selected meiotic products contribute to subsequent development.

4. Selection of the functional megaspore

In the widespread Polygonum-type pathway, one of the four meiotic products persists and the other three cease development and degenerate. The surviving cell is commonly the chalazal megaspore. Its position must not be confused with the future position of the egg, which is at the opposite pole of the mature gametophyte. The word functional indicates that this megaspore continues into megagametogenesis. It does not refer to an additional chromosome reduction, to a fertilized cell or to a spore that was originally diploid. All four normal meiotic products are haploid; they differ in their developmental outcome rather than the normal number of chromosome sets.

The mechanism of selection should not be reduced to a universal claim that the cell closest to the nutrient source simply outcompetes the others. Local tissue relationships, communication, differences in developmental state and genetic regulation can influence the survival of a meiotic product, and the details vary among species. The ordinary correlation between the chalazal position and functional fate in many monosporic ovules is an observation, not sufficient proof of any single causal signal. To establish a specific mechanism one would need to alter the proposed signal while preserving the initial meiotic arrangement, and then determine whether the survival pattern changes. This separates descriptive developmental botany from causal experimental biology.

Selection has two consequences. First, the chromosome complement of the embryo sac ordinarily derives from a single meiotic product. Second, the surviving cell can expand into a larger gametophytic domain while the discarded products do not compete for the same developmental space. In a bisporic or tetrasporic pathway, more than one meiotic nucleus contributes to the gametophyte instead. Thus degeneration of three products is a feature of the common monosporic route, not an obligatory defining event for every female gametophyte. Examination statements that begin with the phrase all angiosperms require particular care when dealing with tetrad fate.

Callose deposition can mark temporary reproductive cell boundaries during megasporogenesis in many plants. Callose is a glucan and its distribution may change as the surviving megaspore develops. Fluorescent staining of callose can therefore be useful in recognizing cell partitions and stages. However, staining establishes where the polymer is present, not which causal function it performs in a particular ovule. A loss of staining might reflect a change in synthesis, removal, developmental timing or sample preparation. If an investigator proposes that a difference in callose controls selection, complementary genetic and developmental evidence is needed. This is an example of how cell wall chemistry and developmental observations can inform each other without assuming causality from a single image.

When comparing a normal and mutant plant, it is helpful to record the earliest stage at which the populations differ. If most mutant ovules reach an ordinary tetrad but no megaspore enlarges, the defect is consistent with loss of functional megaspore survival or entry into megagametogenesis. If the mutant never produces four meiotic nuclei, the problem arises earlier and could prevent functional megaspore formation secondarily. If a large single megaspore appears and remains alive but does not undergo its first mitosis, the evidence instead points toward a later transition. These are provisional interpretations. A longitudinal observation or a series of stage-specific markers can distinguish a delayed transition from an irreversible arrest.

Developmental selection is also a useful point at which to discuss the difference between lineage and potency. The functional megaspore is the ancestor of every gametophytic cell in a standard monosporic sac. This fact alone does not establish that each of the other megaspores has lost its capacity to divide under all possible conditions. Fate is the outcome normally observed; developmental potency is what the cell is capable of doing in an appropriate experimental environment. The two should not be interchanged. Likewise, a finding that more than one megaspore survives in a mutant does not immediately establish that both complete gametogenesis, differentiate correctly or can support fertilization.

5. Megagametogenesis: three mitoses and spatial organization

The functional megaspore does not become the egg directly. In the usual Polygonum-type sequence, it enlarges, replicates its DNA and undergoes three rounds of mitotic nuclear division. Initially, these divisions are not accompanied by complete cytokinesis between all daughter nuclei. This arrangement is called a coenocytic or syncytial stage in the developmental literature. In this context the important feature is a shared cytoplasmic compartment containing more than one nucleus. Nuclear multiplication without full cellularization permits nuclei to change position within the same expanding cell, setting up domains in which different cell types will later form.

After the first mitosis, two nuclei are present within the gametophyte. They become separated toward opposite ends as a large vacuole develops between them. The vacuole is a membrane-bound compartment, not an empty cavity. It can occupy much of the volume, leaving cytoplasm around the periphery and influencing the spaces in which nuclei and cytoskeletal structures are located. Vacuole expansion depends on water and solute relations and on the ability of the cellular boundary to accommodate growth. These physical processes help make the polarity of the embryo sac visible. They do not by themselves explain all molecular decisions about cell identity.

At the next mitosis, both nuclei divide, yielding four. In the typical arrangement there are two nuclei at the micropylar pole and two at the chalazal pole. A third mitosis yields eight, four near each pole. These counts describe the usual end points of division rounds. In a real specimen, individual nuclei can be in different stages of the cell cycle, and a transitional specimen need not show an exact power of two at the instant of observation. The idealized sequence is a teaching model of completed rounds, not a guarantee of perfect synchrony in every ovule.

If every nucleus divides once per round, the number of nuclei after k completed rounds is N equal to 2 raised to the power k, beginning with one functional megaspore. The sequence therefore becomes one, two, four and eight. The total number of nuclear division events is not three; it is one plus two plus four, or seven. This is a standard branching-tree result: each division replaces one nucleus with two and increases the count by one. Seven increases are needed to turn one nucleus into eight. The distinction between rounds of divisions and total individual mitoses is a frequent source of erroneous examination answers.

Mitosis preserves the number of chromosome sets per daughter nucleus. If the functional megaspore is haploid, each ordinary daughter nucleus is haploid at the end of its mitotic cycle. DNA replication preceding each mitosis transiently doubles the amount of DNA without doubling ploidy. Adding up the total DNA in an eight-nucleate sac is not equivalent to measuring the genome content of a single nucleus. If all eight nuclei contain an unreplicated haploid genome, the sac contains eight C equivalents of nuclear DNA distributed among eight nuclei. It is not an octoploid cell in the sense of possessing an eight-set nucleus. The distinction is particularly important before cellularization because there is only one large cellular compartment containing all those nuclei.

Cytoskeletal organization is necessary for reliable division and movement. Microtubules contribute to the mitotic spindle that segregates chromatids. Cytoplasmic organization and cytoskeletal rearrangements help determine where daughter nuclei lie following division. A defect that causes an abnormal spindle might alter the number or genetic constitution of nuclei, while one that primarily alters nuclear movement can yield the expected number in unexpected positions. Both phenotypes can interfere with cell patterning, but their immediate mechanisms differ. Nuclear stains alone can reveal abnormal positioning, whereas additional observations of chromosome segregation and cytoskeletal structure are needed to establish the precise cause.

A mature developmental pattern requires more than numerical doubling. The initial four nuclei near each pole are not equivalent in their ultimate locations. One nucleus from each side will participate in the central cell, while the remaining three at each pole acquire distinct cellular associations. The migration of selected nuclei and formation of cellular boundaries must therefore be coordinated with the position of the vacuole and the organization of the surrounding cytoplasm. An eight-nucleate sac with misplaced nuclei is not automatically a normal mature sac. Counting is a necessary first test, but spatial arrangement and differentiated identity complete the developmental interpretation.

6. From eight nuclei to seven cells

At the eight-nucleate stage of standard Polygonum development, one nucleus from each pole becomes associated with the central domain. These are termed the polar nuclei. The three nuclei remaining near the micropyle are enclosed in three differentiated cellular territories, the egg cell and two synergids. The three at the chalazal pole become antipodal cells. The central cell occupies much of the remaining gametophyte volume and contains the two polar nuclei, which may fuse before fertilization depending on the species and developmental stage. The result is conventionally described as a seven-celled, eight-nucleate embryo sac before polar fusion. If the polar nuclei have fused, seven nuclei can be counted in the seven cells, while the central nucleus contains the contributions of two haploid nuclei.

The words cellularization and nuclear fusion refer to different processes. Cellularization partitions shared cytoplasm into territories bounded by membranes and, where formed, cell walls. Polar-nuclear fusion combines two nuclear compartments into one. Neither process should be counted as an ordinary mitosis. A diagram showing two central nuclei must not be interpreted as showing two central cells, because the two nuclei share the same cell boundary. Conversely, a diagram showing a single central nucleus need not represent a different embryo-sac type. The image may show the standard pattern after polar fusion.

Cell plate construction in plant cells requires regulated transport and fusion of membrane vesicles, and the phragmoplast organizes the developing division plane during typical cytokinesis. In the embryo sac, boundary formation follows a specialized pattern rather than partitioning each product immediately after each preceding nuclear division. The developmental significance is that final cell boundaries can reflect the positions reached by the nuclei, not only their lineage at the preceding division. A defect in cellularization can therefore leave several correctly divided nuclei within an abnormally shared compartment. A defect in nuclear migration can produce incorrect cell arrangement even if wall formation itself is functional. Distinguishing these phenotypes requires staining membranes or walls in addition to nuclei.

The central cell is a good illustration of why chromosome counts must refer to a defined object. In an unfused central cell, each polar nucleus has n chromosomes, and the two nuclei together carry two reduced maternal complements. After karyogamy, the secondary nucleus ordinarily has 2n chromosomes. Neither event involves paternal chromosomes. The paternal contribution enters later when a sperm fuses with the central cell. A diploid secondary nucleus is therefore not a zygote, despite having the same number of chromosome sets as the future zygote. Its maternal-only origin and developmental context distinguish it.

A schematic seven-cell embryo sac can be reconstructed from the lineage rule. Start by drawing a long compartment with a micropylar end and a chalazal end. Place two synergids adjacent to the micropylar region and the egg cell in the egg apparatus. At the opposite pole place three small antipodal cells. Assign the large interior domain to one central cell, and place two polar nuclei within it before fusion. Counting boundaries gives three plus three plus one, or seven. Counting nuclei gives three plus three plus two, or eight. This accounting works independently of whether the illustrator draws the central domain as rectangular, elongated or pear-shaped. Actual cells have more complex shapes and their walls can be incomplete or specialized at particular surfaces.

The mature pattern is a functional organization rather than merely a fixed arrangement of objects. The micropylar cells are associated with the region through which the pollen tube usually enters. The egg is positioned for sperm delivery and subsequent zygote formation, and the central cell is adjacent to the reproductive apparatus so it can receive the other sperm. The antipodal region lies toward the opposite maternal interface. The spatial arrangement helps coordinate the reproductive events without requiring every cell type to perform the same role.

7. The egg cell: specialization for the future embryo

The egg cell represents the female gamete that will contribute directly to the embryo. Its identity is acquired during megagametogenesis rather than immediately at the end of meiosis. In the standard sac, all egg apparatus nuclei are mitotic descendants of a single haploid megaspore. Yet an egg and a synergid differ markedly in their functions. This is a developmental example of genomic equivalence: nuclei carrying essentially the same inherited chromosome complement can support different patterns of gene expression, cell structure and behavior. The differences arise from regulation of the shared genetic information and from cellular context, not from the routine loss of all genes that are unnecessary for a particular cell.

The position of the egg within the apparatus places it near the future sperm-delivery region. The egg plasma membrane must retain the capacity for a specific fusion event while ordinary adjacent gametophytic cells remain distinct. Its cellular state must also permit the transition from an unfertilized haploid cell to a developing zygote. Egg identity includes these functional properties as well as visible features. A microscope image can show that a cell lies between the synergids, but a complete demonstration of egg identity would also consider molecular markers and the ability to participate in fertilization. A cell with the shape of an egg that fails to express appropriate differentiation genes may not be a fully functional gamete.

The egg is generally polarized. In Arabidopsis, its large vacuolar domain and its nuclear position differ from those of the adjacent synergids. In standard representations the egg nucleus lies toward the chalazal part of the egg while the vacuole is nearer the micropylar end. Synergid nuclei tend toward the micropylar side and their vacuolar regions toward the chalazal side. The precise appearance varies with stage and species, so the position of a nucleus is best used together with cell location, cell boundaries and structural specializations. A polarity diagram teaches the relative positions, not an invariant geometric shape.

Polarity has a cellular meaning. Different regions of a cell contain different amounts or distributions of organelles, proteins, vesicles and cytoplasm. These differences allow certain activities to occur preferentially near one surface. A polarized egg is not merely a cell whose nucleus happens to lie at one end. Its organization may be associated with preparation for fertilization and later developmental transitions. A student should distinguish polarity established during gametophyte development from the subsequent axis of the embryo; the latter is not simply a completed miniature body plan already present in the unfertilized egg.

The existence of an egg in the expected position does not guarantee seed production. The cell could be defective in its ability to fuse, in its transition into zygotic development or in its communication with other cells. If the egg is missing while the synergids and central cell persist, a pollen tube might still approach the embryo sac. The outcome would depend on the remaining functional interactions. Even if central-cell fertilization occurred, the ordinary embryo would not form without egg fertilization. This reasoning separates defects in female gametophyte differentiation from later failures of fertilization and embryo growth.

This contrasts with the simplest model of animal oogenesis. In many animals the primary oocyte uses asymmetric meiotic cytokinesis to retain cytoplasmic resources in one large reproductive cell while producing small polar bodies. The flowering plant megaspore mother cell instead makes meiotic products, one of which usually proliferates mitotically to create the female gametophyte. The synergids and antipodals in the mature plant sac are therefore not equivalents of the animal polar bodies. They arise after meiosis through the functional megaspore's mitotic lineage and undergo specialized differentiation. Similarity in the presence of a female gamete does not imply identity of developmental mechanisms.

8. Synergids and the filiform apparatus

A nonmotile flowering-plant sperm depends on delivery by a pollen tube. The ovule must therefore do more than produce an egg: it must establish a route by which the male gametophyte can find the correct destination. The two synergids lie alongside the egg and typically occupy the micropylar region of the embryo sac. Their name reflects their cooperative role within the egg apparatus. They are haploid gametophytic accessory cells rather than additional eggs. The arrangement couples a local guidance function to the physical location of the two female fertilization targets.

The micropylar surface of a synergid frequently develops a highly specialized wall region called the filiform apparatus. In microscopy this region appears as elaborate wall ingrowths projecting into the cell. Cell wall polymers and associated materials contribute to this organization. The geometry increases the interface between the cell surface and its extracellular environment, and is associated with exchange, secretion and interactions with approaching pollen tubes. The apparatus is not a cilium, flagellum or a separate cell. Its structure is a differentiation feature of the synergid itself. Therefore an image showing strong wall ingrowths near the micropyle supports a synergid identification, especially when the other components of the egg apparatus are also visible.

A secretory cell must synthesize, process and deliver its signaling molecules to the appropriate extracellular region. Proteins synthesized through the secretory pathway enter the endoplasmic reticulum and can pass through the Golgi apparatus before reaching the cell surface. The specialized micropylar region provides a local interface where secreted signals can influence the pollen tube. This does not mean that all proteins found in the apparatus are chemoattractants. Some may form structural components, regulate extracellular conditions or participate in later communication. A protein's localization is evidence for where it might act, not a sufficient demonstration of its biological function.

Work on Torenia has provided direct evidence that synergids contribute short-range attraction signals. In this experimental system, the exposed portion of the embryo sac makes it possible to observe interactions between pollen tubes and individual female gametophytic cells. Selective removal of synergids disrupts attraction, while retention of a functional synergid can allow attraction to persist under suitable conditions. This is a stronger test than simply observing that pollen tubes often grow toward synergids: targeted perturbation identifies which cells are needed for the response. The experimental context matters, because tube competence and signals from the surrounding female tissues are also relevant before the final approach.

Small secreted peptides including LURE attractants have been identified in representative flowering plants. Such peptides can be produced in synergids and influence the behavior of pollen tubes that have acquired the necessary competence. It is important not to claim that every flowering plant uses the same peptide or receptor. Conservation of the overall guidance problem does not require that the molecules solving it remain identical. The next chapter will address the complete pollen-tube journey and reception; the focus here is the developmental production of cells with the necessary structure and secretory capacity.

The Arabidopsis MYB98 transcription factor provides a useful link between cell differentiation and biological function. Mutant synergids can retain several features of synergid identity while exhibiting an abnormal filiform apparatus and defective pollen-tube guidance. This separates a cell's broad identity from one of its specialized functions. A transcription factor can regulate downstream genes involved in particular structural and secretory properties without being the only determinant of the cell's original fate. If the mutation caused all synergid cells to be replaced by eggs, the interpretation would be different. A specific differentiation defect is consistent with recognizable synergid cells that are poor guides.

The distinction also helps interpret rescue experiments. Suppose a mutant with abnormal guidance expresses a normal gene copy only in the synergids. Restoration of attraction would support the conclusion that expression in those cells contributes to the phenotype. It would not automatically demonstrate that the protein itself is the secreted attractant. If the gene encodes a transcription factor, restoration could occur through recovery of a set of target genes that control wall structure or secretion. A localized expression rescue and a biochemical ligand test answer different questions. Good examination reasoning avoids assigning the molecular role solely from recovery of a visible behavior.

Synergid function also has a temporal dimension. Before sperm delivery, the cell must remain competent to communicate with pollen tubes. During normal reception one synergid commonly becomes the receptive cell and degenerates in association with tube discharge. The other may retain function for a period, and in Arabidopsis it can participate in recovery when the initial reproductive event fails. It is therefore misleading to describe both synergids as dead from the start or to regard their disappearance at a late stage as evidence that they were never important. Cell death can be part of a successful developmental sequence. The relevant questions are which cell dies, when this occurs and whether the expected reproductive function has already been performed.

9. The central cell and the significance of the polar nuclei

The central cell occupies the large internal domain of the typical mature female gametophyte. Its unusual nuclear composition is a direct consequence of the earlier pattern of mitoses and nuclear migration. One nucleus from each pole participates in its development. In the monosporic Polygonum type the two nuclei derive from the same functional megaspore, so they normally contain the same inherited haploid chromosome complement at a given locus. They are not a pair of independently inherited paternal and maternal genomes, as would be present in a typical sexually formed diploid sporophyte. Both are maternal relative to the future offspring.

A fused secondary nucleus containing two reduced maternal sets can be described as diploid in a conventional diploid species. If a megaspore carries allele A at a locus, the fused secondary nucleus ordinarily carries two copies of that allele. It would not become heterozygous simply because two nuclei fused. This provides a simple link between cell lineage and later parent-of-origin analysis. In a normal cross, a sperm may contribute a different allele to the primary endosperm nucleus. The resulting three copies then have two maternal contributions and one paternal contribution, whereas the zygote contains one of each. The origin of the nuclear genomes is more informative than the total ploidy alone.

The central cell has a distinct differentiation program. It is not a large undifferentiated storage chamber or a second version of the egg. Its transcriptional state, cellular organization and response to fertilization permit the formation of endosperm rather than an ordinary embryo. Endosperm is a separate developmental product that can support embryo growth and nutrient transfer and persists to different degrees in mature seeds of different species. Those later developmental dynamics belong to a subsequent chapter. Here it is important to recognize that the mature female gametophyte establishes an endosperm precursor before any sperm has arrived.

Genetic regulation prevents premature activation of some aspects of seed development. In Arabidopsis, fertilization-independent seed class genes illustrate how chromatin-associated regulation in the female reproductive system restricts inappropriate development before fertilization. Some mutants can initiate endosperm-like proliferation without the normal sperm contribution. This does not mean that every such mutant produces a complete viable seed by asexual reproduction. Initiation of proliferation, normal differentiation of endosperm, embryo production and final seed viability are separate outcomes. The example is useful because it demonstrates that the central cell is not merely passively waiting for an added chromosome set; its developmental competence is actively regulated.

Nuclear fusion can be examined using optical sections and nuclear-envelope markers. Two adjacent fluorescent nuclear domains may indicate polar nuclei approaching each other, but proximity is not identical to completed karyogamy. In a single image overlapping fluorescence may also arise because nuclei lie above one another in the viewing direction. A z-series through the central cell can help distinguish these alternatives. Evidence for a single continuous nuclear compartment is stronger than an apparent overlap in a projection. This kind of observation explains why authors may describe a sac as binucleate or fused depending on the developmental stage sampled and the method used.

Suppose an ovule contains a normal egg and two normal synergids but the central cell has only one haploid nucleus because the other polar nucleus failed to reach it. A normal sperm contributes n chromosomes at fertilization. If fertilization of that abnormal central cell succeeds and no other genome-doubling event intervenes, the initial fusion product would contain 2n rather than the usual 3n. Its maternal-to-paternal genomic ratio would also differ from the standard 2:1. The egg-derived zygote could still be 2n. These outcomes are conditional predictions, not a guarantee of embryo or endosperm viability. A missing nucleus could impair additional central-cell functions and prevent fertilization altogether.

10. Antipodal cells and chalazal organization

Three cells normally differentiate at the chalazal end in the standard seven-celled scheme. They are called antipodals because they lie opposite the egg apparatus. Like the egg and synergids, their nuclei are descendants of the functional megaspore and normally begin with a haploid complement in a diploid species. They are not residual megaspores that remained alive after the initial tetrad. The three discarded megaspores were products of meiosis, whereas antipodal nuclei are products of later gametophytic mitoses. These two groups can share a numerical count of three without sharing an origin.

The position of antipodal cells next to chalazal maternal tissues suggests a potential role in physiological interaction between the developing gametophyte and the ovule. Their persistence and degree of elaboration differ considerably among species. In some grasses, antipodals divide further, forming a cluster of cells at the chalazal region. Such proliferation is consistent with an important interface between tissues, although the precise functions and physiological contributions need to be established for individual species. In Arabidopsis, antipodal cells are relatively short-lived around the mature stage. Thus the seven-celled diagram is an excellent description of the initial standard organization, but not an absolute count of living cells at every later time.

One should not infer that a transient cell has no developmental function. A cell may produce a signal, transfer material or establish spatial organization and subsequently degenerate. To test its contribution, removing it at different developmental times would be more informative than simply comparing the number of cells in a mature ovule. An ablation before a function has been completed can have a different consequence from an ablation afterward. In a species with extensive antipodal proliferation, loss of the cluster may have a more pronounced effect on maternal interactions than in a species where the cells are transient.

An experimental analysis of antipodal proliferation must distinguish mitosis from endoreduplication. Mitosis followed by partitioning can increase cell number, whereas endoreduplication increases the DNA content of a nucleus without the corresponding production of daughter cells. Large nuclei do not prove that more cells have formed. In a chalazal domain with many small separated cells, cell walls and nuclei can be counted together. In a domain with a few enlarged nuclei, a DNA-content measurement would be needed before claiming increased ploidy or replication. These distinctions connect embryo-sac development with the general cell-cycle principles taught in Unit 2.

The chalazal and micropylar ends are not equivalent developmental territories in the standard embryo sac. A reversal or displacement of nuclear positions could alter which cells form near which surrounding tissues. Conversely, a normal-looking spatial arrangement could conceal a failure of transcriptional differentiation. A useful experimental strategy is to combine location markers with cell-type-specific gene-expression reporters. If a nucleus occupies the chalazal position but expresses markers characteristic of the egg, the result is evidence that cell identity and location have become uncoupled. A misplaced nucleus does not automatically imply an ectopic egg, because cell identity must be tested independently.

11. Monosporic, bisporic and tetrasporic development

The Polygonum pattern is widespread, but the underlying diversity of flowering plants is a major source of analytical questions. Embryologists classify female gametophyte development partly by how many nuclei produced by one meiosis participate in forming the embryo sac. In the monosporic category one of the meiotic products contributes. In the bisporic category two meiotic nuclei contribute. In the tetrasporic category all four contribute. The classification is not defined by the number of mitotic rounds, the number of mature eggs or the final number of nuclei alone. It concerns meiotic lineage contributions.

The Polygonum type is monosporic and typically reaches an eight-nucleate condition by three mitotic rounds of the functional megaspore nucleus. Another monosporic arrangement, the Oenothera type, is classically described as forming a four-nucleate sac. Two rounds of nuclear division yield four rather than eight nuclei. The resulting organization has an egg apparatus of three cells and a central cell with one polar nucleus, with no typical three-cell antipodal group. If the central nucleus is haploid and fuses with a normal haploid sperm, the initial endosperm nuclear product is diploid. This comparison shows why it is unsafe to assert that endosperm must be triploid in every angiosperm.

In the Allium-type bisporic pathway, the first meiotic division is accompanied by partitioning to produce two dyad cells, and the second meiotic division can produce two nuclei within a functional dyad compartment without dividing it into two separate megaspores. Both of those haploid nuclei contribute to subsequent development. Two rounds of nuclear multiplication can then produce eight nuclei. The final count can resemble the Polygonum type, but its ancestry differs. The relevant question is not whether the final sac has eight nuclei; it is whether those nuclei descend from one or two of the four meiotic products.

The tetrasporic Fritillaria type illustrates a further possibility. In the classical description all four meiotic nuclei remain in a common cytoplasmic domain. Three chalazal nuclei combine their chromosome complements while the remaining micropylar nucleus stays haploid. Subsequent divisions produce nuclear populations of different ploidy. In the resulting typical arrangement, the egg apparatus has haploid nuclei, the antipodal nuclei carry three sets each, and the polar nuclear contributions to the central cell are one haploid and one triploid. If those contributions fuse, the resulting central nucleus contains four sets. With a normal haploid sperm, the primary endosperm nucleus would contain five sets. This is a conditional textbook accounting for the classical Fritillaria pattern; it is not a description of all tetrasporic sacs.

Tetrasporic development does not require all four meiotic nuclei to fuse into a single tetraploid nucleus. It simply requires participation of all four in the lineage. Particular types differ in nuclear migrations, fusions and rounds of division. Likewise, bisporic development does not necessarily mean two eggs or two separate mature sacs. It is possible for different developmental routes to converge on a similar arrangement of cells while retaining differences in ancestry and ploidy. This is why embryological classification requires developmental observations across a sequence rather than a single terminal specimen.

Evolutionary reasoning benefits from recognizing variation in this reproductive structure. A reduced female gametophyte is retained within the sporophyte, where its development is coordinated with the ovule. Lineages have evolved different timings of partitioning, patterns of nuclear multiplication and levels of persistence of accessory cells. Describing one pattern as common does not establish that every rare pattern is defective or evolutionarily inferior. In an examination, the appropriate comparison is between the defined reproductive consequences of each pattern, not between imagined rankings of complexity.

Apomixis provides another boundary to the conventional sexual sequence. In some plants a female gametophyte can arise from an unreduced reproductive lineage through altered meiosis or from a somatic ovule cell that takes on gametophytic development. The terms diplospory and apospory distinguish these two general routes. In an apomictic pathway embryo initiation may proceed without fertilization of the egg, while endosperm formation can be autonomous or may still require fertilization, depending on the system. These are distinct features: unreduced embryo-sac formation alone does not demonstrate parthenogenesis, and parthenogenesis alone does not establish autonomous endosperm. This narrow comparison clarifies the limits of the normal sexual pathway without replacing it with a full treatment of plant breeding.

12. How developmental evidence is obtained and interpreted

A mature ovule is small and enclosed in maternal tissues, so observing the female lineage requires methods that distinguish its structures from surrounding cells. Classical embryological studies used serial sections and staining to reconstruct stages across many ovules. If one section contains a large central cell, another adjacent section may reveal the egg apparatus or antipodals. Serial reconstruction avoids treating each profile as a separate cell. Modern optical sectioning similarly permits investigators to examine three-dimensional organization without relying exclusively on physically cut sections. These approaches provide evidence for the existence of a developmental sequence; time-resolved observation is needed to follow an individual cell directly.

An important principle is to separate the measurable variable from the biological property it represents. A DNA stain identifies nuclei and may reveal chromosome configurations. A membrane reporter can reveal cellular boundaries. A gene-expression reporter can show activity of a promoter in a particular region or cell. These readouts are complementary but not interchangeable. For instance, eight stained nuclear profiles do not establish eight cells, and a reporter expressed near the micropyle does not prove that the labeled cell is a functional egg. Combining nuclear markers, membrane boundaries and differentiated cell markers makes the inference substantially stronger.

Cell-type-specific expression is often examined using a promoter linked to a fluorescent protein. The location of fluorescence can reveal where the tested promoter is active under the conditions studied. Such a construct has limitations: reporter maturation may be delayed, the protein may persist after transcription has ceased, and the construct may not include all regulatory sequences of the native gene. A promoter reporter is thus a useful indicator of expression rather than an exact real-time measurement of every molecule of native protein. A functional fusion, direct RNA detection or another independent marker can strengthen the result.

The earliest abnormal stage is particularly informative when assessing developmental mutants. Consider a mutant plant with a high proportion of empty-looking ovules at flowering. Examining only the mature stage cannot distinguish failure to produce a megaspore mother cell, meiotic arrest, degeneration of the functional megaspore, failed mitosis or late loss of differentiated cells. A comparison of stage distributions across developing buds can locate the first divergence. If the wild type and mutant have comparable mother-cell and tetrad stages but differ strongly at the two-nucleate stage, the transition from megaspore survival to the first mitotic division becomes a focus. This remains a hypothesis until timing and cell viability are examined.

A mutation expressed in the haploid gametophyte can be studied through reciprocal genetic crosses. A heterozygous plant can be used as the female parent in one cross and as the pollen donor in another. Reduced transmission of a mutant allele specifically through the female side suggests a female reproductive defect. However, seed or embryo lethality can also create a distorted progeny ratio. To distinguish a gametophytic defect from later zygotic selection, investigators may examine unfertilized embryo sacs and early reproductive events in addition to scoring surviving offspring. The reciprocal cross identifies a direction of transmission failure; developmental observations locate the affected process.

A mutant associated with an abnormal filiform apparatus illustrates causal separation. The investigator can ask whether synergid identity has been lost, whether only its specialized wall structure is altered, whether secretion has changed, or whether otherwise normal pollen tubes cannot respond. Tests with normal pollen on mutant female tissues, and mutant pollen on normal female tissues where appropriate, distinguish defects on the two reproductive sides. Observing a mutant synergid that still expresses independent markers of synergid identity but cannot guide a pollen tube supports a differentiation defect rather than a wholesale fate transformation. This logic is more useful for examination problems than memorizing an unqualified statement that a particular gene controls fertility.

Cell ablation provides a complementary approach. In a system that permits access to individual embryo-sac cells, investigators can selectively disrupt one cell type and observe how pollen tubes behave. The inference requires suitable controls: intact sacs should attract tubes under the same conditions, and disruption of an unrelated cell should not simply damage the whole sac. If removal of both synergids specifically abolishes attraction while one retained synergid is sufficient to support it, the result supports a causal role for these cells. It does not rule out indirect signals from the egg, central cell or surrounding maternal tissue that influence synergid activity. The question tested is local guidance under the defined assay conditions.

Molecular evidence can also help distinguish the production of a signal from the response to that signal. A secreted peptide expressed in synergids is a candidate for a local attractant. If an isolated peptide elicits directional growth of competent pollen tubes in a suitable assay, that provides evidence of signal activity. If removing the peptide impairs guidance, the necessity of that molecule can be evaluated. Multiple related peptides can have overlapping roles, so a weak phenotype after removal of one gene does not necessarily show that the whole signaling system is unimportant. These are standard principles of loss-of-function, redundancy and sufficiency applied to the female gametophyte.

Quantitative microscopy must account for biological sampling. A plant with many ovules supplies many measurements, but ovules from the same plant share genotype and environment. Treating every ovule as a completely independent biological replicate may overstate certainty. Comparisons across multiple independent plants strengthen conclusions. In a NET-style dataset, proportions can be calculated directly, but a student should not claim statistical significance solely because two percentages differ. Sample size, variance, experimental controls and replication determine how strong the inference can be.

13. How the female reproductive lineage became an experimental subject

The discovery of the angiosperm reproductive cycle required botanists to connect structures that were not easy to observe together. Pollen grains are conspicuous and can often be collected from anthers. By comparison, the small reproductive structures inside the ovule are obscured by maternal tissues. Early microscopic studies of plant reproduction therefore faced a problem of continuity: a single section of a flower could show a cell that looked like an egg, but it could not by itself reveal that cell's developmental origin. Investigators assembled a sequence by comparing ovules from buds at different stages and using the changing patterns of nuclei and cellular boundaries as landmarks. The distinction between the megaspore mother cell, meiotic products and the differentiated embryo sac depended on such reconstruction.

The recognition of double fertilization at the end of the nineteenth century transformed the interpretation of mature embryo-sac anatomy. The work associated with Sergei Nawaschin in 1898 and subsequent independent observations by Léon Guignard showed that the second sperm nucleus participates in the reproductive process rather than being an unused product of male gametophyte development. The large central cell consequently had to be interpreted alongside the egg as a fertilization target. A classical diagram of the embryo sac became explanatory when its two future fusion events were understood. Detailed historical priority and taxonomic details are less important for this chapter than the biological consequence of the discovery: the female gametophyte prepares for two different developmental products.

Comparative plant embryologists next distinguished repeated developmental patterns among flowering plants. The existence of sacs with the same mature number of nuclei but different meiotic origins showed why staging matters. The names of the developmental types are conventional labels associated with representative genera, not a list of taxa in which a pattern is exclusively found. The standard Polygonum scheme became particularly useful for teaching because it occurs widely and exposes the relationships among meiosis, mitotic multiplication, nuclear positioning and cell differentiation. A careful reading of embryological literature therefore recognizes both the value and the limits of a standard schematic.

The development of genetic analysis in model plants introduced a different approach. Instead of only comparing the normal organization of ovules, researchers could examine lines in which particular developmental steps failed. If a mutant formed an ordinary megaspore mother cell but did not complete the female reproductive sequence, a gene required after reproductive initiation became a candidate. If it developed recognizable cells that nevertheless failed to guide pollen tubes, the analysis could distinguish structural cell differentiation from function. Genetic tools did not replace classical microscopy; they made its observations experimentally interpretable. The combination is central to modern developmental biology because neither a gene sequence nor a static structure can explain a complete developmental process on its own.

The study of Torenia and Arabidopsis illustrates the complementary strengths of biological models. The naturally accessible female reproductive cells of Torenia made a selective functional perturbation possible. Arabidopsis offered a system in which genetic transmission, cell-specific gene expression and developmental mutants could be compared systematically. Maize, on the other hand, provides a valuable example of female gametophyte organization in a grass, including the proliferation of antipodals. A conclusion from one model should be transferred to another only when there is a defensible biological basis. Model systems are selected because a question can be studied effectively in them, not because each represents every feature of the angiosperms.

14. Regulating the transition to a reproductive lineage

An important early decision precedes the first meiotic division. Most cells in an ovule primordium continue to develop as ordinary maternal tissue, while a limited number become competent to initiate the reproductive lineage. This selection is not explained by chromosome complement: the future megaspore mother cell and its neighboring cells are initially diploid. Instead, their developmental histories, regulatory states and local signals differ. A cell that becomes a mother cell must acquire the machinery needed for meiosis while maintaining appropriate communication with the surrounding tissue. A neighboring cell that remains somatic must follow a different developmental program, even though its genome is largely the same.

The restriction of reproductive competence is especially interesting in plants because germline precursors are generally specified during the development of reproductive organs rather than being set aside permanently during very early embryogenesis as in some animal models. This general distinction should not be overstated, because animal germline specification also varies among species. For the flowering plant under discussion, the emergence of a megaspore mother cell within a growing nucellus illustrates the ability of an organ to establish a new reproductive lineage through regulated developmental decisions. The ovule is therefore not simply a protective shell that is constructed around a pre-existing female gamete.

In Arabidopsis, work on small-RNA-associated regulation has linked the ARGONAUTE9 pathway to the restriction of reproductive cell fate in ovule tissues. Some mutant ovules show additional cells with reproductive or gametophytic characteristics. This is an example of a non-cell-autonomous influence: regulatory activity in surrounding tissues can affect the developmental potential of nearby cells. The interpretation must remain precise. The presence of multiple cells with some germline-associated markers is not by itself proof that all enter normal meiosis or ultimately produce viable embryo sacs. In some situations, an ectopic gametophytic program can begin without following the conventional mother-cell meiotic sequence. Distinguishing these possibilities requires observation of lineage history.

This example connects small RNA pathways studied in molecular biology with a developmental question. A regulatory RNA pathway can influence gene-expression states in a tissue and thereby restrict where a specialized developmental program occurs. It is not necessary to memorize every small RNA or chromatin component to understand the principle. A mutation that broadens the domain of reproductive competence is mechanistically different from one that arrests meiosis in a correctly specified mother cell. The first changes the number or spatial distribution of cells adopting reproductive characteristics; the second affects the execution of the reproductive program after specification.

One can design a conceptual test for this distinction using stage-specific expression. Imagine a reporter for early reproductive specification and a second marker for meiotic chromosome behavior. If a mutant displays three neighboring cells with the early reporter, but only one of those cells shows meiotic chromosomes, then specification-associated expression has expanded without evidence that all three undergo meiosis. If three cells show authentic meiotic figures, the conclusion would be stronger and different. Even then, a later observation would be needed to establish whether multiple normal functional megaspores or mature sacs form. This kind of stepwise inference avoids turning a marker phenotype into an exaggerated statement about reproduction.

Developmental competence is also distinct from determination. A cell may be able to adopt a reproductive identity when given an appropriate environment, without having irreversibly committed to that identity under all possible conditions. To demonstrate determination, one would need to examine whether the cell retains its developmental fate when placed in a different environment or exposed to an appropriate perturbation. In many plant developmental systems such a test is experimentally difficult, so a precise description of observed behavior is preferable to an unsupported declaration of irreversible commitment. The concepts of specification, competence and differentiation from Unit 5.A gain concrete meaning in this context.

Specification must be coordinated with the developmental condition of the surrounding ovule. If integument growth is severely abnormal, the reproductive lineage may lack a suitable physical or physiological environment. The resulting sterility could be a secondary consequence rather than evidence that the affected gene acts directly in meiotic chromosome segregation. Conversely, a mutation causing a female meiotic defect might leave integument organization largely intact. An investigator should compare the timing of somatic abnormalities with reproductive abnormalities and use tissue-specific rescue where possible. Restoration of the normal gene in the appropriate tissue can help distinguish where the primary requirement lies.

15. How nuclear position becomes a differentiated cell pattern

After the functional megaspore has formed, a new kind of developmental problem arises. The descendants of that single haploid nucleus must adopt several identities. If nuclear division alone determined cell fate, an early asymmetric division might be expected to produce separate precommitted branches that always form the same cell types. The shared-cytoplasm phase permits a different possibility: a nucleus can move to a region where local signals and cytoplasmic conditions influence the developmental program of the cell that will ultimately surround it. Lineage and position are both relevant, but the mature arrangement cannot simply be deduced from the chronological order of divisions.

Experimental analyses of Arabidopsis support a significant role for final nuclear position in egg-apparatus patterning. Altering nuclear location can alter the identities acquired by cells. A simple interpretation is that cells receive positional information, although the source and molecular nature of that information must be established rather than presumed. The movement of nuclei during development is not just a way of fitting eight objects into a compartment. It changes their exposure to distinct intracellular and tissue environments, which can affect gene regulation. The resulting differentiated pattern is a useful plant counterpart to the positional-information concepts introduced in the general development chapter.

The distinction between an intracellular gradient and a tissue-derived positional signal is particularly important. An early model proposed that auxin could form a distribution within the embryo sac that helps specify different cell types. Later research has examined the contributions of surrounding sporophytic tissues, localized signaling and nuclear positions. The evidence should not be reduced to a universal quantitative rule saying that a particular auxin concentration invariably produces a particular cell type in every plant. A reporter for hormone response is not necessarily a direct map of hormone concentration, because sensitivity and signaling competence influence its output. To distinguish a hormone concentration gradient from differential response, one needs measurements or perturbations that separate those variables.

The Arabidopsis histidine kinase CKI1 provides a more direct example of spatial regulation linked to female gamete distinction. CKI1 can activate a plant two-component signaling pathway. During female gametophyte development its spatial restriction is associated with specification of the central-cell lineage and restriction of inappropriate egg identity. The 2016 study on CKI1 demonstrated a connection between its localization, nuclear migration and the establishment of distinct female gamete fates. The key examination insight is that a localized signaling component can produce different transcriptional and differentiation outcomes in a common developmental field. The molecular architecture of the complete phosphorelay belongs to the signaling unit.

A gene's name does not by itself reveal the stimulus that activates its pathway. CKI1 is named cytokinin-independent because its signaling activity is not equivalent to the ordinary requirement of a cytokinin receptor for a particular ligand. A statement that CKI1 function necessarily demonstrates direct cytokinin binding would go beyond what is justified by the name or developmental phenotype. The relevant principle is activation of a signaling pathway, not an assumption about ligand recognition. A loss-of-function phenotype affecting central-cell identity can be used to test the role of the pathway in gamete dimorphism, but it does not establish that the pathway is the only determinant of cell fate.

Transcription factors can then help maintain or elaborate differentiated cell identities. In Arabidopsis, regulators associated with the egg, synergid and central cell have been identified. MYB98 is especially useful because its mutant phenotype distinguishes specialized synergid differentiation from complete absence of the cell. Central-cell regulators such as AGL61 and AGL80 are associated with its differentiated development. The broader idea is that a cell-type-specific transcriptional program regulates groups of target genes, allowing a coordinated change in wall architecture, secretory capacity or reproductive competence. A transcription factor can be necessary for a subset of these characteristics without being sufficient to produce a fully functional cell in every context.

Local cell-to-cell interactions can reinforce distinctions after the initial pattern emerges. If a developing egg emits a signal that restricts acquisition of egg identity by adjacent cells, removal of that signal could permit an accessory cell to express egg-associated markers. Such a phenotype would differ from creating more egg-like nuclei by performing an extra mitotic division. In the first case cell identity changes without necessarily increasing the total cell count. In the second case there are additional nuclei that may later adopt identities based on available positions. A strong experimental analysis records both the number of cells and the types of markers expressed rather than assuming that an increase in one necessarily implies an increase in the other.

Suppose a developing sac has four micropylar nuclei instead of the normal three destined for the egg apparatus. One possible outcome is that the additional nucleus enters a territory with an existing differentiation signal and acquires the identity associated with that territory. Another is that the abnormal geometry prevents proper cellularization or causes the whole structure to arrest. These alternative predictions make the developmental system testable. The number of nuclei alone does not tell which fate will be acquired, and a positional model should be distinguished from an assertion that every nuclear displacement will be tolerated. The mature female gametophyte is a coordinated structure, and disruptions can affect several aspects of its organization at once.

16. Cell-cycle control and developmental arrest

Nuclear multiplication in the growing embryo sac is subject to regulation. The normal developmental sequence requires that nuclei enter S phase, replicate their DNA, perform mitosis and then stop proliferating at an appropriate point as differentiated cells form. A mechanism that permits mitosis at an early stage may need to be attenuated later. Without this transition, the developing gametophyte could contain extra nuclei that disrupt its normal cellular geometry. Conversely, an overly restrictive cell-cycle program could prevent the first or subsequent mitotic rounds. Fertility depends on achieving the proper sequence, not on maximizing proliferation.

The retinoblastoma-related protein in plants is associated with the regulation of cell-cycle progression. Arabidopsis female gametophytes with defects in this regulatory system can exhibit excess nuclear divisions. Maize indeterminate gametophyte1 provides another example in which control over nuclear proliferation and developmental pattern is disturbed. These examples do not mean that the same regulatory gene is mutated in both systems. They illustrate a shared developmental requirement: nuclear multiplication must be limited and coordinated with cellular differentiation. A question about these mutants is best approached by examining whether the change in proliferation precedes or follows the changes in cell identity.

An extra mitotic round before cellularization could, in the simplified case of synchronous division of all eight nuclei, yield sixteen nuclei. A defect that affects only one nucleus would instead yield nine at the time the other nuclei remain unchanged. Therefore a count of more than eight does not uniquely establish that the entire embryo sac completed an additional synchronous round. In experimental images, counts may be nonuniform because different nuclei behave differently. A numerical phenotype should be described accurately before assigning a particular cell-cycle mechanism.

The same care is needed for an apparently reduced nuclear number. If a nucleus fails to divide but survives, nuclear count will be lower than expected. If normal divisions occur and one nucleus subsequently degenerates, the terminal count can be equally low. A cytological stain showing six nuclei at a late stage cannot distinguish those histories on its own. Time series or lineage tracking is needed. Cell-cycle markers can provide evidence of entry into particular phases, but a marker of DNA replication does not demonstrate successful completion of mitosis. Distinct observations are needed for DNA synthesis, chromosome segregation and survival.

Coordination between nuclear division and cytokinesis is another independent dimension. In maize, genetic studies of cellularization have identified the contribution of microtubule-associated factors to organizing the female gametophyte. A mutant with misplaced boundaries might have the correct number of nuclei but an altered pattern of cells. To distinguish a spindle defect from a boundary-placement defect, investigators can examine chromosomes and microtubules during division and then compare membrane or wall organization. A microtubule-associated protein may participate in more than one structure; it is not sufficient to infer that every mutant phenotype arises from the spindle simply because the protein binds microtubules.

Energy is required to execute these processes. DNA replication involves nucleotide incorporation and associated enzymatic reactions, chromosome movement is supported by cytoskeletal dynamics, and vesicle trafficking during cellularization uses regulated molecular motors and membrane fusion. The developing vacuole also requires maintenance of solute and water relations across membranes. A severe metabolic defect could therefore produce a general developmental arrest without identifying a specific developmental regulator. In an examination, the mechanistic interpretation of an inhibitor should consider its specificity and potential secondary effects. An inhibitor that broadly suppresses ATP production cannot be used on its own to demonstrate that one particular signaling pathway controls female gametophyte identity.

The timing of a perturbation matters. A reversible block of mitosis before the first division should leave a different immediate phenotype from the same treatment after all eight nuclei have formed. A disruption of wall formation applied during cellularization can be tested independently of its effects on earlier nuclear division. In a stage-controlled experiment, the starting developmental state should be recorded and the earliest changes observed. If treatment is applied throughout ovule development, an abnormal mature embryo sac may reflect an indirect effect on precursor tissues rather than the intended late-stage process. This principle links experimental design with developmental chronology.

17. Physical constraints of growth and spatial organization

A functional megaspore grows considerably as the female gametophyte develops. Growth involves acquisition of water, solutes and biosynthetic resources as well as changes in nuclear number. The expansion of a vacuole is a useful way to increase cellular volume without requiring the entire new volume to be filled with densely packed cytoplasm. However, water uptake requires a suitable difference in water potential and must be coordinated with the properties of the membrane and surrounding cellular structures. The general plant physiology of water potential provides a useful prerequisite for interpreting this growth.

For a simplified cellular system in which gravitational effects can be neglected, water potential can be described as the sum of solute potential and pressure potential. Solute accumulation generally lowers the solute component, while positive turgor pressure contributes positively. Water tends to move in the direction of lower water potential when a permeable route exists. These are general principles, not a measured quantitative model for the particular gametophyte. Applying them requires caution because the developing embryo sac is surrounded by living tissues and its vacuolar and cytoplasmic compartments are separated by membranes. A difference in solute content alone does not establish the actual direction or rate of water movement.

As a hypothetical example, suppose a simplified intracellular compartment initially has solute potential of minus 0.6 megapascals and pressure potential of plus 0.2 megapascals. Its calculated water potential is minus 0.4 megapascals. If the surrounding compartment has water potential of minus 0.2 megapascals and water can pass between the two, the initial tendency is for water to enter the lower-potential intracellular compartment. As water enters, solute concentration and pressure may change, reducing the driving difference. The calculation predicts direction under the stated assumptions, not a specific growth rate. Neither value is presented as an experimentally measured embryo-sac water potential.

The location of the vacuole influences nuclear positioning in a geometric sense because nuclei generally occupy the cytoplasmic domains rather than the vacuole lumen. A large vacuolar compartment can separate nuclear groups and shape the available routes of cytoplasmic movement. Cytoskeletal connections and active cellular regulation are still necessary to produce reproducible spatial arrangements. It would be an error to imagine that the nuclei are freely suspended particles inside vacuolar sap or that simple passive diffusion alone accounts for the highly ordered final pattern.

A geometric model can show why cell size and nuclear number are separate. If a developing compartment were approximately spherical and its radius increased from ten to twenty micrometres, its volume would increase by a factor of eight, whereas its surface area would increase by a factor of four. These results follow from the radius-cubed and radius-squared relationships, respectively. The actual embryo sac is not a sphere and often grows asymmetrically, so the calculation illustrates a physical principle rather than providing a morphological measurement. A larger compartment may have altered demands for exchange and internal organization even if the number of nuclei has not changed.

Specialized wall architecture provides another example of structure and function. A flat surface can be modified by ingrowths to increase the area of membrane associated with a given projected region. In a synergid, the filiform apparatus creates such an elaborate interface. Increasing area can increase potential sites for exchange and secretion, but actual flux also depends on the density and activity of transport or secretion machinery, local concentrations and available energy. A mutant with reduced wall ingrowths may therefore exhibit a functional defect for several reasons. The altered shape alone cannot quantify its secretory output.

These physical constraints help explain why the female gametophyte is organized as a differentiated set of cells rather than eight identical nuclei with no stable boundaries. The mature pattern requires specific interfaces with other cells and with the future pollen tube. Localized activity is possible when secretory and reproductive functions are organized in appropriate cellular domains. Geometry, physiology and gene regulation are complementary explanatory levels. None should be mistaken for a complete alternative to the others.

18. Genetic segregation, transmission and parental genome origin

The haploid nature of the standard female gametophyte makes it especially informative for genetic analysis. In an ordinary diploid tissue, a functional allele can sometimes compensate for a recessive defective allele. In a haploid nucleus, a recessive defect may be expressed because there is no second homologous allele in that nucleus. A plant that is heterozygous and healthy as a sporophyte can therefore produce a mixture of genetically normal and defective female gametophytes. This provides a distinctive route by which a mutation can impair fertility without causing an obvious vegetative phenotype.

Consider a heterozygous female plant carrying a normal allele A and a mutant allele a, crossed to a normal AA pollen donor. If female gametophytes of both genotypes form and function equally, the expected offspring genotypes are AA and Aa in a one-to-one ratio. If the mutant gametophyte has only half the probability of successful transmission compared with a normal gametophyte, the successful contributions are proportional to one half times one and one half times one half. The ratio becomes two AA to one Aa. Among successful offspring, one third are predicted to carry the mutant allele. This is a conditional ratio calculated from differential transmission and is not identical to the proportion of all ovules that contain a mutant gametophyte.

If the same heterozygous plant is used as a pollen donor, a female-specific defect should not necessarily distort male transmission. A reciprocal cross with normal female tissue can help reveal the difference. However, progeny genotype alone may be insufficient: a mutation can influence seed development after fertilization, and this too can produce a transmission distortion. Counting mature sacs before pollination, following early reproductive events and scoring aborted ovules or seeds can locate the failure more accurately. These additional observations distinguish the process being tested from the endpoint that is easiest to measure.

The maternal origin of the polar nuclei can be followed using the same allele model. In a Polygonum-type embryo sac derived from an A-bearing megaspore, the egg ordinarily carries A and the two polar nuclei each carry A. A sperm carrying a contributes to the egg product and another genetically comparable sperm contributes to the central-cell product, under the usual male gametophyte lineage. The zygote is then Aa and the initial endosperm genotype is AAa. The maternal sporophyte surrounding the developing seed may itself be Aa if it was the original heterozygous plant. Thus three neighboring tissues can differ in both genetic dosage and origin.

A student may encounter a question asking whether the egg and central cell of a monosporic embryo sac are genetically identical. The accurate response depends on what identical means. At a particular nuclear locus, their haploid ancestral genomes ordinarily have the same allele because they come from one meiotic product. But the central cell has two maternal nuclear contributions rather than one, and its overall nuclear dosage differs from the egg. The two cells also have different patterns of gene expression, and differences in organelle distribution or epigenetic state may exist. It is therefore more precise to say that their reduced nuclear genome copies share a common lineage than to call the differentiated cells identical.

In bisporic development the genetic interpretation requires greater care because two products of the same meiosis contribute to the sac. Depending on the chromosome segregation and recombination history, the contributing nuclei may differ at particular loci. A blanket assumption that every maternal genome copy in every type of embryo sac carries the same allele is therefore not justified. Tetrasporic development can incorporate an even broader representation of meiotic products. The correct procedure is to identify which meiotic nuclei contribute to each final nuclear compartment and then count the alleles accordingly. Final ploidy alone cannot answer the ancestry question.

This reasoning also illuminates the distinction between genomic dosage and gene expression. In the ordinary endosperm precursor, two maternal genome copies may carry the same allele. After fertilization, the paternal copy introduces an additional genome contribution. The presence of three copies does not mean that each will be expressed at an equal rate, since regulation and genomic imprinting can affect their activity. Conversely, observing biased expression does not establish a change in DNA copy number. A DNA-based assay and an RNA-based assay measure different properties. Detailed parent-of-origin regulation belongs to the earlier epigenetic and later endosperm chapters, but this foundational separation is needed here to interpret genetic data correctly.

19. Interpreting developmental datasets and functional assays

Developmental experiments often report the proportion of ovules at different stages. To interpret these data, one must define the denominator and the sampling time. A population of ovules sampled at one flower stage may contain individuals that have not all completed the same number of divisions. If the mutant develops more slowly than the wild type, it may have a larger proportion at earlier stages without suffering a permanent developmental arrest. Repeating the observations later can distinguish delay from failure to complete the sequence. The stage distribution is a snapshot of a dynamic process, not a direct measurement of transition rates unless additional assumptions are justified.

For a hypothetical experiment, imagine that at a defined early sampling point one hundred wild-type ovules include sixty four-nucleate sacs and thirty-eight eight-nucleate sacs, with the remaining two at other stages. One hundred mutant ovules sampled at the same point include eighty-five four-nucleate sacs and ten eight-nucleate sacs, with five at other stages. The excess of four-nucleate mutant sacs suggests a delay or impairment near the third round of nuclear multiplication. If later observations show that most mutant sacs eventually become eight-nucleate and differentiate, the earlier result is better described as a developmental delay. If they remain four-nucleate and subsequently degenerate, evidence for a persistent defect is stronger.

To estimate a transition probability from such observations, it is preferable to follow a defined set of initial four-nucleate sacs. Suppose eighty initially four-nucleate sacs are observed over a chosen interval and sixty complete the next division round. The observed proportion completing the transition is sixty divided by eighty, or 0.75. If the corresponding mutant value is twenty out of eighty, its observed transition proportion is 0.25. The ratio of these observed proportions is one third. These numbers describe the defined interval and sample. They do not establish that the intrinsic molecular reaction rate is one third of normal, because developmental transitions can depend on cell-cycle timing, heterogeneity and viability.

Now consider a functional guidance assay comparing intact sacs with sacs retaining one synergid and those in which both synergids have been removed. Hypothetical values of ninety, seventy and five successful attractions per hundred tested sacs would support a strong contribution of synergids to local attraction. But the low residual value could reflect background tube approaches, incomplete cell removal or another weak source of guidance. A useful control would test whether removal of an unrelated cell causes a similar decrease by damaging the ovule. The distinction between residual biological function and experimental background is important when interpreting near-zero responses.

A rescue experiment can be expressed in similar terms. If a mutant attracts tubes in twenty of one hundred trials, and a cell-specific rescue line attracts them in eighty of one hundred trials, the difference is sixty percentage points. The rescue line has four times the observed attraction frequency of the mutant. These statements describe different mathematical quantities and should not be interchanged. The increase relative to the mutant baseline is three hundred percent, but it would be misleading to claim that guidance has become three hundred percent of normal without knowing the wild-type frequency. Quantitative language should specify which reference value is used.

For examining fertility, seed set is often a downstream aggregate measure. Suppose a plant forms a hundred ovules, eighty of which contain recognizable mature female gametophytes. Seventy of those eighty receive a pollen tube and sixty of those seventy ultimately yield a seed. The overall seed set is sixty percent of all ovules. The observed fraction of mature sacs receiving a tube is seventy divided by eighty, or 87.5 percent. The fraction of tube-receiving sacs yielding a seed is sixty divided by seventy, or approximately 85.7 percent. These conditional proportions identify different potential limitations. Multiplying them by the initial mature-sac fraction recovers the overall outcome, provided the categories are nested as stated.

If an investigator instead reports that sixty seeds were produced from a hundred flowers, without reporting ovules per flower, it is impossible to infer that sixty percent of ovules formed seeds. The denominator has changed from ovules to flowers. Likewise, if a genotype alters the number of ovules produced per ovary, reporting only seeds per fruit may conceal an important maternal developmental defect. The choice of denominator is therefore a biological question as well as a mathematical one. Accurate examination reasoning requires reading exactly what was counted before performing an arithmetic operation.

Together, these examples provide a general approach to unfamiliar datasets. Identify the biological stage, state whether the numbers represent nuclei, cells, ovules, flowers or successful reproductive events, and specify what the denominator is. Determine whether a result is an observed proportion, a conditional probability, a difference or a ratio. Then ask whether the design distinguishes the proposed mechanism from alternative explanations. This approach permits careful quantitative reasoning within the developmental scope of the chapter without introducing artificial kinetic equations or assuming that all cellular events can be described by a single rate constant.

20. Integrating the female pathway with reproduction and development

The standard female gametophyte illustrates the coordination of several processes studied separately in earlier units. Meiosis changes chromosome-set number and produces genetically distinct products. Developmental selection determines which product contributes to the lineage. Mitosis increases nuclear number without altering the number of sets in ordinary daughter nuclei. Vacuolation, cytoskeletal rearrangement and nuclear migration generate spatial organization. Cellularization partitions a shared cytoplasm into distinct cells. Differential gene expression gives those cells specialized identities and reproductive functions. The outcome is a miniature developmental system that cannot be understood from any one of these processes alone.

An effective way to solve an unfamiliar problem is to place the observed defect on this causal sequence. If there is no mother cell, later defects may be secondary to failed reproductive specification. If meiosis produces abnormal products, the functional lineage may begin with the wrong chromosome complement. If the functional megaspore is present but nuclear number remains one, a mitotic or developmental transition may be impaired. If eight nuclei are present without proper boundaries, cellularization becomes a candidate. If the correct cells are present but do not guide pollen tubes, investigate differentiation and signaling. If the guidance is normal but no seed forms, evaluate fertilization and later developmental events rather than concluding that megasporogenesis necessarily failed.

One of the principal biological constraints is that the gametophyte must develop in coordination with surrounding maternal tissues. Protection by integuments would be of little reproductive value if no access route or communication mechanism were established. Conversely, an opening in the ovule would not be enough if the cells inside were developmentally incompetent. The reproductive system solves both problems through spatially organized tissue growth and the specialization of the micropylar region. The female gametophyte's physical size is reduced relative to free-living gametophytes of many non-seed plants, yet its differentiated roles remain essential.

The crop significance of these events follows from reproductive efficiency. A plant can produce a large number of flowers and still form few seeds if female gametophytes fail to mature. A breeding experiment involving reciprocal crosses can help determine whether poor seed formation reflects problems primarily associated with pollen or with the female reproductive system. Inspection of mature embryo sacs and stage distributions can further localize the failure. This is relevant to evaluating fertility and understanding the developmental effects of mutations or environmental stress. It would be unjustified, however, to attribute all reduced seed set to the embryo sac; pollination, tube growth, fertilization, resource limitation and embryo or endosperm development can independently reduce final seed number.

The central principle of the next chapter is that two male gametes must reach two different female targets. That process is possible because the male pathway creates sperm-bearing pollen and the female pathway creates an egg apparatus and central cell within an appropriately organized ovule. The distinction between gamete formation and gamete fusion should remain clear. A mature unfertilized sac may have a fused maternal secondary nucleus, but it does not yet contain a zygote or fertilization-derived endosperm. Recognition, delivery and the two fusion events are the next causal stage, not part of megaspore production itself.

Worked numerical examples

Example 1. Chromosome number versus DNA content

A plant has 2n=16. At G1 the mother cell has 16 chromosomes and 2C DNA. After replication it has 16 chromosomes, 32 chromatids and 4C DNA. After meiosis I each nucleus has 8 chromosomes, 16 chromatids, 2C DNA. After meiosis II each megaspore has 8 chromosomes and C DNA. The egg has 8 chromosomes, and the fused central nucleus has 16. These counts assume normal segregation and no extra replication.

Example 2. Nuclear division counting

Let N₀=1 and Nₖ=2Nₖ₋₁. Three rounds give 2, 4 and 8 nuclei. The number of individual divisions is (2³−1)=7. Each division adds one nucleus. This model assumes all nuclei divide exactly once per round and none die.

Example 3. Differential female transmission

An Aa female produces A and a megaspores with equal probability. Let relative success be 1 for A and 0.4 for a. Successful contributions are 0.5 and 0.2. Mutant transmission among offspring is 0.2/(0.5+0.2)=2/7≈28.6%. This assumes no postfertilization genotype-dependent survival.

Example 4. Conditional seed formation

Of 120 ovules, 90 mature, 72 of these receive a tube, and 54 of those form seed. The stage fractions are 90/120=0.75, 72/90=0.80 and 54/72=0.75. Their product is 0.45, equal to 54/120. These fractions describe a nested hypothetical sample, not independent physiological rate constants.

Example 5. Central cell contributions across types

For n=9: Polygonum polar contributions 9+9=18; after sperm fusion 18+9=27. Classical Fritillaria central contributions 9+27=36; after normal sperm fusion 36+9=45. Oenothera contributes 9 to the central nucleus and produces 18 after fusion. These are initial chromosome counts, not a promise of later seed viability.

Example 6. Water potential model

Let Ψw=Ψs+Ψp, where all terms are measured in MPa. For Ψs=−0.8 and Ψp=+0.3, Ψw=−0.5 MPa. If the surrounding tissue is −0.25 MPa, water tends inward across a permeable boundary. This simplified calculation neglects other potential components and does not predict actual flow rate.

  2. All ovules in a heterozygous mother show the defect, and maternal-tissue rescue restores development
  3. Normal pollen cannot enter any flower of another species
  4. Nuclear DNA doubles before mitosis
Answer and explanation

B. All ovules in a heterozygous mother show the defect, and maternal-tissue rescue restores development

A shared maternal environment and tissue-specific rescue support sporophytic control. Selective haploid failure supports a gametophytic requirement; remaining options are not informative.

Question 42

A central nucleus is scored as fused because two fluorescent profiles overlap in one projection. Which observation best strengthens the inference?

  1. A count of all pollen grains
  2. An increase in petal size
  3. Optical sections and nuclear-envelope evidence of one continuous compartment
  4. A single projection at lower resolution
Answer and explanation

C. Optical sections and nuclear-envelope evidence of one continuous compartment

Optical sections resolve overlap along the viewing axis, and envelope continuity addresses actual fusion. Other observations do not resolve the ambiguity.

Question 43

A mutant attraction rate rises from 20% to 80% after synergid-specific rescue. The increase in percentage points and fold change are:

  1. 60 points and fourfold
  2. 60 points and twofold
  3. 300 points and fourfold
  4. 80 points and threefold
Answer and explanation

A. 60 points and fourfold

80−20=60 points; 80/20=4. Relative increase is 300%, not 300 percentage points. The remaining values misuse baseline or arithmetic.

Question 44

An ovule contains a compartment at −0.4 MPa water potential and surrounding tissue at −0.2 MPa. With a permeable route, initial net water tendency is:

  1. Out of the compartment
  2. None because both values are negative
  3. Indeterminate solely because the inside is haploid
  4. Into the compartment
Answer and explanation

D. Into the compartment

Water tends toward lower water potential, −0.4 MPa. The sign alone does not prohibit movement, and ploidy does not determine its direction.

Integrated practice: assertion–reason, matching and reasoning

Original practice questions. Each explanation follows its question.

Question 45

Assertion: The central cell of the standard Polygonum-type embryo sac is one cell before polar nuclear fusion. Reason: Its two polar nuclei occupy a common cytoplasmic compartment.

  1. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both true, but the reason is unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion

The shared central cytoplasm defines one cell containing two nuclei. This directly explains the assertion.

Question 46

Assertion: All eight nuclei in a standard pre-cellularization Polygonum embryo sac are haploid at mitotic completion. Reason: Each nucleus is generated by a fresh meiotic reduction.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion true, reason false

Mitosis preserves the haploid complement established by the original meiosis. No new meiotic reductions occur in megagametogenesis.

Question 47

Consider statements: I. Synergids originate from mitotic descendants of the functional megaspore. II. Antipodals are the three discarded meiotic spores. III. The nucellus is maternal sporophytic tissue. Which are correct?

  1. I and II only
  2. II and III only
  3. I and III only
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

C. I and III only

I and III correctly trace lineage. II confuses the chalazal accessory cells with degenerating meiotic products.

Question 48

Match: P Polygonum; Q Oenothera; R Allium; S Fritillaria. 1 monosporic four-nucleate; 2 classical tetrasporic mixed ploidy; 3 monosporic eight-nucleate; 4 bisporic eight-nucleate.

  1. P–1, Q–3, R–2, S–4
  2. P–3, Q–1, R–4, S–2
  3. P–4, Q–2, R–3, S–1
  4. P–3, Q–4, R–1, S–2
Answer and explanation

B. P–3, Q–1, R–4, S–2

The correct match uses both lineage and terminal nuclear count. The alternatives swap the distinct developmental patterns.

Question 49

A diagram shows an opening at the lower end of an inverted ovule and three accessory cells at the opposite end. Where is the chalazal group?

  1. Always at the bottom of the page
  2. Within the opening itself
  3. Opposite the opening, irrespective of page orientation
  4. In the funiculus only
Answer and explanation

C. Opposite the opening, irrespective of page orientation

The opening identifies the micropyle; antipodals are opposite it. Page orientation does not establish biological polarity.

Question 50

A heterozygous plant shows mutant-allele transmission of 10% through ovules and 50% through pollen. Which next test most directly localizes the female defect?

  1. Examine staged unfertilized ovules and early fertilization products
  2. Assume the allele is lethal in all embryos
  3. Count only total leaf cells
  4. Ignore the reciprocal result
Answer and explanation

A. Examine staged unfertilized ovules and early fertilization products

Stage-specific analysis distinguishes female gametophyte failure from maternal or postfertilization effects; the ratios alone do not prove a universal lethal mechanism.

Question 51

An A-bearing functional megaspore produces an egg and a standard fused secondary nucleus. Both are fertilized by a-bearing sperm. Which genotypes follow?

  1. Zygote AA; endosperm aaa
  2. Zygote Aa; primary endosperm AAa
  3. Zygote Aa; endosperm Aa
  4. Zygote AAa; endosperm Aa
Answer and explanation

B. Zygote Aa; primary endosperm AAa

Egg contributes one A, central cell contributes two A, and each sperm contributes a. The alternatives misassign parental dosage.

Question 52

A simplified spherical compartment grows from radius 10 μm to 20 μm. Its volume and surface-area factors are:

  1. 2 and 2
  2. 4 and 8
  3. 8 and 8
  4. 8 and 4
Answer and explanation

D. 8 and 4

Volume scales as r cubed and area as r squared. Doubling gives 8 and 4; the idealization is not an actual ovule shape.

Question 53

A meiosis-I nondisjunction affects one homologous pair. Assuming otherwise normal division, the four products are:

  1. Two normal and one n+1 and one n−1
  2. Two n+1 and two n−1
  3. Four normal
  4. Four n+1
Answer and explanation

B. Two n+1 and two n−1

Both homologues segregate together at meiosis I; subsequent normal chromatid separation gives two excess and two deficient products. The second option is typical of one meiosis-II nondisjunction.

Question 54

A mutant has normal nuclear counts, normal mature cell markers and low seed set. Which claim is not justified by these observations alone?

  1. Later pollen-tube reception could be defective
  2. Gamete fusion could be defective
  3. Megasporogenesis is necessarily the primary failed process
  4. Postfertilization growth could be defective
Answer and explanation

C. Megasporogenesis is necessarily the primary failed process

Normal early morphology does not prove all functions, but it also does not demonstrate a primary meiotic failure. The remaining choices are plausible later hypotheses.

Examination traps and corrections

Ovule equals embryo sac
The ovule encloses the gametophyte and includes diploid maternal tissues. Whole-ovule measurements do not reveal the isolated gametophyte's ploidy.
Egg directly results from meiosis
Meiosis makes megaspores; the egg differentiates later through mitoses. Distinguish plant and animal pathways.
A replicated mother cell is tetraploid
DNA doubles during S phase but homologous sets remain two. Read n and C independently.
All four megaspores contribute to Polygonum
One normally contributes. Tetrasporic development is a different class.
Three mitoses means only three divisions total
Three rounds include seven individual divisions when all nuclei double synchronously.
Eight nuclei means eight cells
Before cellularization eight nuclei share a compartment; the standard mature sac has seven cells.
The two polar nuclei form two central cells
The nuclei occupy one central domain. A question may show them separately without showing two walls.
A fused central nucleus is a zygote
It has two maternal contributions, not a sperm contribution. Identical ploidy does not imply identical origin.
Antipodals are leftover megaspores
They derive through gametophytic mitoses after functional megaspore selection.
The micropyle must be at the top of the figure
It is identified by the integument opening, regardless of diagram rotation.
A synergid is an extra egg
It is an accessory cell with distinct differentiation and guidance functions.
The filiform apparatus is a flagellum
It is a specialized wall region, not an organelle for cell locomotion.
All angiosperm endosperms start triploid
Oenothera and Fritillaria provide contrasting classical counts. Determine central nuclear contribution first.
All antipodals disappear immediately
Persistence and proliferation differ among species; grass antipodal clusters are a relevant exception.
A normal count proves normal fertility
Cell identity, function and fertilization competence must also be assessed.
A promoter reporter proves causality
Expression shows an association; perturbation and rescue can test functional roles.
A lower stage proportion proves permanent arrest
A single time point may capture delay. Follow later stages and viability.
A haploid gametophyte has no maternal environment
It is embedded in sporophytic tissues that can influence its development.

Revision and mastery

Megasporogenesis reduces chromosome-set number in a diploid precursor. Megagametogenesis organizes a haploid-derived lineage into distinct reproductive cell types. In the standard monosporic pathway the functional megaspore undergoes three rounds of nuclear division, followed by nuclear migration and cellularization. The initial seven-cell pattern comprises an egg apparatus, a central cell and a chalazal antipodal group. Before fusion there are two polar nuclei inside the central cell. Compare alternative patterns by meiotic contributions and the ploidy of the nuclei that actually participate, not by mature cell counts alone.
