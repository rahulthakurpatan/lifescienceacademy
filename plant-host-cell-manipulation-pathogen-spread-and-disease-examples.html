From local infection to plant disease

The leaf of a healthy plant is an organized living tissue rather than an inert surface on which microbes grow. Epidermal cells help regulate exchange with the environment, mesophyll cells use light to fix carbon, and vascular tissues distribute water, minerals and assimilates. These functions depend on cooperation between cells. A pathogen that alters one of these functions can disturb the behaviour of cells that it never physically enters. This is why plant disease must be studied as a change in a biological system rather than simply as the presence of a foreign organism. The central question is how an agent at one location gains access to host resources, changes cellular processes and produces effects at increasing distances from the original infection site.

The distinction between infection and disease is particularly important here. Infection means that a pathogen has established a biologically meaningful association with a host. Disease means that normal host function is impaired. A plant can contain a small population of a compatible microorganism before any visible abnormality develops. Conversely, a plant may wilt or develop yellow leaves without infection, for example when its roots cannot supply water or a mineral nutrient is deficient. The visible phenotype is therefore a consequence to explain, not an identification of the underlying cause. A useful account of a disease establishes which cells or tissues are affected, what their normal function is and how an infection changes that function.

Consider a bacterium that is present in an intercellular space of a leaf. The extracellular continuum outside the plasma membranes of plant cells is called the apoplast. It includes cell walls, intercellular spaces and the lumina of mature conducting xylem elements. It is topologically distinct from the cytoplasm of an adjacent living cell. A bacterial cell in the apoplast can absorb dissolved nutrients and multiply, but many of the enzymes, nucleic acids and signaling proteins that determine the behaviour of its host are on the other side of a plasma membrane. To manipulate those targets, the bacterium must deliver a molecule to them or cause a signal that propagates into the host cell. The bacterium itself does not have to enter the cytoplasm.

The symplast, in contrast, consists of the cytoplasm of living cells connected by plasmodesmata. These channels are bounded by plasma membrane and contain a central strand of endoplasmic reticulum, termed the desmotubule. The cytoplasmic sleeve around that strand provides a route for movement of certain solutes and macromolecules. Movement through plasmodesmata is regulated rather than unrestricted. The distinction matters because an extracellular bacterium, an intercellular fungal hypha and an intracellular plant virus occupy different biological compartments. If an examination asks which pathogen has direct access to the host ribosomes, the location of the infectious agent is more informative than its ability to cause severe disease.

A leaf is also a dynamic nutritional environment. Photosynthesis in illuminated tissues generates assimilates, but the carbon fixed by photosynthetic cells does not simply fill the intercellular spaces. Membrane transporters, metabolism and vascular loading control its distribution. The extracellular concentration of a nutrient reflects a balance among release, uptake, diffusion and consumption. A pathogen may benefit if this balance changes in its favour. Alteration of nutrient availability need not involve wholesale destruction of cells; changes in the activity of a host transporter may be sufficient. Conversely, death of all living cells around an obligate biotroph could destroy the physiological conditions on which it depends.

Plant pathogens differ in their relationships with host cell survival. A biotroph obtains resources from living host tissue, whereas a necrotroph derives resources from tissue that has been killed. Hemibiotrophic pathogens exhibit a living-host-associated phase followed by a destructive phase. These terms describe biological strategies and are not simply synonyms for bacterial or fungal taxonomy. Their value lies in prediction. A host response that limits a pathogen by rapidly sacrificing a group of cells may be disadvantageous against an organism that thrives on dead tissue. The same observable event, cell death, can have different consequences depending on the pathogen and stage of infection.

The word effector refers to a pathogen-derived molecule that alters the interaction with the host. Many effectors are proteins that change a host process after delivery to a particular cellular compartment. An effector may promote infection by modifying metabolism, transport, signaling or defense. Some effectors can also be recognized by a particular host genotype, producing a response that limits infection. Therefore an effector is not synonymous with a toxin, and its presence is not a guarantee of greater disease on every host. The biological outcome depends on the molecular target, recognition capacity and physiological setting. The broader plant immune signaling network will be studied separately; here recognition is relevant because it changes the consequences of pathogen manipulation.

Disease spread occurs at several spatial scales. Within a cell, pathogen components must reach appropriate sites for their activity. Between cells, a pathogen or its infectious material must traverse tissue boundaries. Between organs, it may exploit a transport system or grow along a continuous extracellular route. Finally, spread between separate plants is transmission, not systemic movement within the same individual. These distinctions prevent a common error: finding a pathogen in an inoculated leaf and assuming that it has reached the whole plant. Local replication, local movement, vascular access, transport and establishment in distant tissues are separate biological processes.

Time introduces another distinction. A pathogen can change signaling within a short interval after contact, while visible symptoms may develop only after cumulative physiological dysfunction. A later sample might contain extensive injury but few viable pathogens because susceptible tissue has already been destroyed. A single observation cannot reliably reconstruct the order of events. Mechanistic reasoning instead compares early and late observations, the location of the pathogen and measurements of the cellular process being altered. Such a comparison makes it possible to distinguish an initiating event from a secondary consequence.

A productive way to organize this chapter is to follow the information crossing each biological boundary. In a common bacterial strategy the agent that crosses the host plasma membrane is a protein. In crown gall disease it is a defined segment of bacterial DNA. A fungal feeding structure creates a specialized host-pathogen interface through which nutrients and signals are exchanged. In a plant viral infection the transferable infectious material contains a viral genome. These differences explain why perturbing a protein secretion apparatus, DNA delivery system, fungal interface or viral movement protein gives different phenotypes, even when all the affected plants eventually exhibit reduced growth.

Bacterial protein delivery and altered host physiology

Many Gram-negative bacterial pathogens of plants multiply outside the host cytoplasm. Their cell envelope consists of an inner membrane, a periplasm containing peptidoglycan and an outer membrane. A protein synthesized on bacterial ribosomes begins inside the bacterial cytoplasm. Before it can influence a plant cytosolic enzyme directly, it must cross the bacterial envelope, the extracellular region and the plant plasma membrane. A conventional extracellular secreted protein may act on cell wall components or extracellular receptors, but it cannot automatically pass through the lipid bilayer. A dedicated translocation system solves a different physical problem from secretion into the surroundings.

The Type III secretion system is a multiprotein apparatus used by many plant-associated bacterial pathogens to transfer protein substrates. Its conserved export machinery spans the bacterial envelope, and extracellular components help connect the bacterium to the host surface. Plant-pathogenic systems commonly have an elongated pilus suited to the presence of a cell wall. Components associated with the host membrane enable transfer of selected substrates into a host compartment. The complete apparatus should not be imagined as a bacterium-sized tunnel. It provides a route for molecular transport, while the much larger bacterial cell remains outside the host protoplast. This architectural distinction is central to interpreting microscopy of an infected leaf.

Substrates are synthesized by the bacterium and recognized by the secretion machinery, sometimes with assistance from chaperones that help maintain appropriate substrate properties. The transfer process is not the passive diffusion of a folded enzyme through two freely permeable membranes. Membrane-associated export machinery uses energy, including contributions from proton motive force and an associated ATPase, to support substrate handling and export. The transferred proteins may attain their functional conformations and interact with host targets after delivery. The exact organization varies among systems, but the exam-relevant principle is that a secretion apparatus physically links pathogen protein production to a change in host-cell biochemistry.

It is useful to separate secretion and translocation experimentally. Detection of a bacterial protein in a cell-free extracellular fraction is evidence that the protein has left bacterial cells under the tested conditions, provided contamination from lysed cells is excluded. It does not establish that it reaches plant cytosol. A host-localized signal or an activity that depends on the cytoplasmic location of the protein supplies different evidence. The distinction is especially important when comparing an exported enzyme that acts on a wall polymer with an effector whose substrate is a host intracellular kinase. The same label, secreted protein, does not reveal the cellular side of the membrane on which the relevant reaction takes place.

A transferred effector can alter the rate of a host biochemical process. Imagine a host regulatory protein that must be phosphorylated to stimulate an extracellular defense response. If an effector blocks the kinase responsible for that modification, the quantity of active regulatory protein may decrease despite unchanged transcription of the corresponding gene. If another effector accelerates degradation of the protein, the total quantity may decrease as well. These possibilities have different signatures on a protein abundance measurement and a measurement of phosphorylation. They illustrate the relationship between host signaling and pathogen virulence without requiring an inventory of every known effector.

A host defense response frequently depends on the transport of molecules to specific locations. Vesicles deliver proteins and wall-associated materials to the cell surface, and cytoskeletal organization contributes to vesicle traffic. A perturbation of transport may therefore reduce the accumulation of a defensive product even if the enzymes that synthesize it remain active. This provides a mechanistic reason why effectors that alter host trafficking can influence infection. Measuring gene expression alone would miss such a defect: the transcript and protein could be present while the final product fails to reach the relevant extracellular location.

In a compatible interaction, suppression of host responses can permit bacterial growth in the apoplast. However, reduced defense and increased nutrient availability are not logically equivalent. A bacterium may face both restrictions at once, and relieving one may still leave the other limiting. Water availability provides a third constraint. A normal leaf apoplast often contains gas-filled spaces, whereas some bacterial infections are associated with an abnormally aqueous extracellular environment. A water-rich niche can facilitate movement and access to dissolved substances, but the appearance of water soaking is not an assay for a single effector. Different bacterial factors and host physiological responses can contribute to this phenotype.

Pseudomonas syringae is a familiar model for studying these interactions. Different pathovars show distinct associations with particular host plants, and the well-studied tomato pathovar illustrates the relationship between an extracellular bacterial population and intracellular host targets. Type III effectors contribute to the ability of compatible bacteria to colonize leaves and alter host physiology. The disease phenotype can include spots, chlorotic margins and necrotic regions, but these observations reflect integrated tissue responses. A leaf with a small lesion is not necessarily resistant because of a particular molecular pathway; bacterial abundance and cellular response must be examined to interpret the observation.

Pseudomonas syringae also illustrates why a small secreted molecule and a translocated protein should not be conflated. Some strains produce coronatine, a molecule that mimics aspects of jasmonate signaling and can influence stomatal behaviour and defense-related physiology. Its action is a distinct form of molecular manipulation from delivery of a protein by Type III secretion. The distinction becomes relevant when an experiment bypasses a surface-entry barrier. A factor whose principal contribution is entry through stomata may have a smaller effect when bacteria are already inside the leaf, whereas factors needed for maintaining the internal niche can remain important. This does not imply that coronatine has only one function.

A gene encoding a component of the protein delivery apparatus differs conceptually from a gene encoding one transported effector. Loss of an essential apparatus component can disrupt the action of multiple effector proteins simultaneously. Loss of a single effector might have a small phenotype if other effectors act on overlapping host processes. It may have a large phenotype when that effector supplies a distinct function in a particular host. Thus a weak phenotype after removal of one gene is not proof that the protein is irrelevant to disease. The experimental interpretation must account for functional overlap and environmental context, as well as the possibility that the gene has more than one role.

An apparatus-defective bacterial strain may retain the ability to multiply in a nutrient-rich artificial environment while showing poor accumulation in living host tissue. Those observations would be compatible with a defect in host interaction rather than an absolute failure of cellular growth. The reverse inference is not automatic: a low bacterial population in a leaf can arise for several reasons. If a perturbation also compromises bacterial membrane integrity, the in-host phenotype cannot be attributed solely to altered effector delivery. Appropriate comparisons of baseline viability and the measured host process help distinguish these interpretations. This is an example of reasoning from experimental evidence rather than treating a gene name as a complete explanation.

The historical development of this subject established that pathogen-host specificity and host responses can depend on bacterial genes whose products act inside plant cells. Genetic studies of hypersensitive response and pathogenicity, often associated with the abbreviation hrp, helped identify bacterial functions required for these interactions. Later work on the relationship between these bacterial genes and conserved secretion systems provided a molecular interpretation of the delivery process. The history is informative because the same machinery could participate in disease on a compatible plant and contribute to recognition on an incompatible plant. A result that looks paradoxical when disease promotion and defense activation are regarded as mutually exclusive becomes understandable when both depend on whether a pathogen-derived protein reaches its target compartment.

The difference between a protein that changes signaling and one that changes transcription can be understood through a concrete textbook example. In a compatible plant interaction, the Pseudomonas syringae effector AvrPto can interfere with receptor-associated kinase signaling. A plant receptor that recognizes an extracellular microbial cue normally initiates intracellular changes in kinase activity, and these changes can lead to downstream responses. Interference at the receptor-associated signaling step can uncouple recognition of an external cue from the expected internal response. The relevant outcome is not necessarily loss of the receptor molecule itself. A receptor can remain present at the membrane while its ability to transmit a signal is impaired. This is a reason why a measurement of receptor abundance is not interchangeable with a measurement of receptor activity. The example is context-dependent: recognition of an effector by a particular plant genotype can alter the outcome of the interaction. It should not be generalized into a claim that every effector has the same target or that defense is abolished in every plant.

Transcriptional reprogramming and bacterial blight

A host cell regulates its nutrient distribution in part through transcription. Changing the abundance of a membrane transporter may therefore change the local supply of a nutrient to neighbouring cells or extracellular organisms. Xanthomonas provides a particularly useful example of this principle. Some species produce transcription activator-like effectors, commonly abbreviated TALEs. These are proteins delivered to the host cell through the Type III secretion system, after which they can influence host gene expression. Their biological effect is consequently different from that of a toxin whose primary target is an enzyme at the cell surface.

A transcription activator-like effector contains a central series of repeated protein units that participate in DNA sequence recognition, together with regions needed for nuclear targeting and transcriptional activation. The amino acid differences in the repeats contribute to recognition of particular nucleotides in a target DNA sequence. Once the protein reaches the nucleus of a suitable host cell, binding at a compatible regulatory region can change transcription of a nearby gene. The effector does not need to permanently insert its own gene into the host genome for this activity to occur. It functions as a regulator of an existing host DNA sequence, unlike the T-DNA transfer mechanism discussed in the next section.

This form of regulation makes a host susceptibility gene a meaningful concept. A susceptibility gene is a host gene whose normal function or pathogen-induced activity can facilitate disease in a particular interaction. It does not necessarily exist for the benefit of the pathogen; it may have an essential role in healthy development or metabolism. For example, members of the SWEET family of sugar transporters normally participate in sugar distribution. In some rice bacterial blight interactions, particular Xanthomonas oryzae pathovar oryzae TALEs activate particular SWEET genes. The changed expression can favour the bacterial interaction by altering sugar availability. The relevant relationship is between a particular effector, a compatible host promoter and an appropriate physiological target.

Transcription of a transporter gene is only one link in the causal chain. The mRNA must be translated, the resulting protein must be correctly localized, and the transporter must operate in a cellular environment that supports flux. SWEET proteins generally mediate facilitated sugar transport; the net movement depends on substrate gradients and other physiological conditions rather than on a universal outward pumping mechanism. In a context where increased host transporter activity favours sugar release, the pathogen can benefit from the resulting extracellular nutrient availability. This example connects regulation of transcription, membrane transport and host-associated microbial nutrition in a single causal explanation.

A useful conceptual comparison involves two plants whose transporter proteins have identical amino acid sequences but different regulatory DNA. If the effector recognizes the promoter of one plant but not the other, induced transcription may differ despite the identical transporter coding sequences. This is not a mutation in the catalytic properties of the transporter. It is a difference in pathogen-responsive regulation. A further possibility is that the bacterium possesses a different effector recognizing a distinct host promoter. Therefore failure of one recognition event does not demonstrate universal resistance to the bacterial species. The outcome depends on the particular combination of pathogen and host genotypes.

Bacterial blight of rice involves infection and colonization of vascular-associated tissues, notably the xylem, by Xanthomonas oryzae pathovar oryzae. Normal xylem conduction depends on a continuous hydraulic pathway from roots toward transpiring leaves. Local tissue injury and disturbances of vascular function can contribute to the progressive development of diseased leaf regions. The characteristic appearance of blight is therefore a tissue-scale outcome, whereas activation of a susceptibility gene is a cellular-scale event. An explanation of pathogenesis should connect these levels without treating any individual transporter as the sole cause of every feature of the disease.

The term blight describes extensive and often rapidly developing injury of plant tissues, especially foliage. It is not the name of a universal pathogen class. Bacteria, fungi and oomycetes can all be associated with diseases described as blights. Similarly, a transcription activator-like effector is not a diagnostic marker of the visible phenotype. The interpretation of a particular case requires identification of the infectious agent and its effect on host function. In an examination, the strongest answer is the one that links the specified molecular perturbation to the specified phenotype rather than relying on the word blight alone.

The Xanthomonas example also distinguishes an altered host phenotype from the stable transformation discussed in crown gall disease. A plant cell whose transcription is changed by a delivered bacterial protein may regain its previous state after the protein disappears, assuming that secondary changes do not persist. A cell containing integrated bacterial DNA has a different basis for persistence of altered gene expression. Both represent pathogen-mediated manipulation of host transcription, but only the latter necessarily involves acquisition of new genetic material by the transformed lineage. The comparison prepares the way for understanding how Agrobacterium changes cell growth and differentiation.

Crown gall: a genetic change in a plant cell

An overgrowth of plant tissue poses a different physiological problem from a necrotic leaf spot. In healthy growth, cells divide and expand in relation to signals from their surroundings, developmental history and available resources. A mass of proliferating cells can form when that regulation is persistently altered. Crown gall disease, associated with pathogenic Agrobacterium strains, illustrates a natural interaction in which bacteria induce altered development by supplying genetic information to plant cells. The resulting gall is composed of plant cells. It should not be imagined as a colony of bacterial cells large enough to form a lump on the plant.

The early interpretation of crown gall developed through several kinds of observation. Work by Erwin F. Smith and C. O. Townsend in 1907 associated the disease with a bacterial agent. Subsequent observations showed that gall-derived plant tissue could display unusual persistence of growth in the absence of the initiating bacteria. These observations raised a question that simple continual stimulation by bacterial secretions could not satisfactorily answer: how could the altered cellular behaviour be maintained after the bacterium was absent? The landmark 1977 work of Chilton and colleagues identifying a defined portion of bacterial plasmid DNA in transformed plant cells provided a genetic explanation. It was the combination of cellular behaviour and molecular evidence, rather than the appearance of a tumor alone, that established the nature of the process.

The bacterial genetic information responsible for this characteristic interaction is associated with a tumor-inducing plasmid, often called the Ti plasmid. A plasmid is a DNA replicon distinct from the main bacterial chromosome. The Ti plasmid contains regions that perform different functions. One region, the transferred DNA or T-DNA, is the source of genetic information that can become established in the plant. Other plasmid regions encode bacterial virulence proteins responsible for processing and transferring that information. These functions should be kept conceptually separate. A bacterial gene can be essential for delivery without being part of the DNA that is transferred into the plant.

Wounded plant tissue is an important ecological setting for crown gall disease. Wounding exposes living cells and changes the local chemical environment. Some released plant compounds act as signals to the bacterium, linking the presence of a suitable host environment to the expression of bacterial virulence functions. This is a biological response to information from the surroundings, not an example of the plant directing its own normal cell division program. The bacterial signal-sensing system controls the deployment of its transfer machinery. The plant cell will subsequently interpret transferred genetic information using its own molecular machinery.

In the classical molecular model, the transferable DNA is derived from the region bounded by short border sequences on the Ti plasmid. The bacterial VirD proteins participate in processing this region, with VirD2 remaining associated with the end of the transferred single DNA strand. A Type IV secretion system is involved in the transfer of DNA-associated material and certain bacterial proteins to the plant. Type IV and Type III systems are not interchangeable terms. Earlier in this chapter the transferable cargo was an effector protein that acted on an existing plant substrate. Here the defining cargo includes DNA that can supply a new template for gene expression.

Once transferred, the DNA and associated bacterial proteins interact with plant cellular processes. Nuclear localization is significant because stable acquisition of the information by the host genome and expression of its genes depend on access to the nuclear compartment. Virulence proteins can assist events involving the transferred DNA, including its protection and nuclear targeting. The exact contribution of individual host factors and the sequence of molecular intermediates can vary. For NET-level reasoning, the important distinction is between DNA transfer into a plant cell, arrival in the nucleus, transient expression and stable incorporation into a plant chromosome. These are related but separate outcomes.

Stable integration does not mean that the entire Ti plasmid becomes a plant chromosome. The usual defining event is establishment of T-DNA-derived sequences within plant nuclear DNA. Integration makes it possible for those sequences to be copied when the plant chromosome is replicated and inherited by daughter cells. A cell might express a transferred gene before a stable integration event has occurred; transient expression is therefore not by itself sufficient evidence of stable transformation. Conversely, detecting integrated DNA does not prove that every gene within it is expressed at the same level in every transformed cell. Chromosomal environment and transcriptional regulation also matter.

The distinction between bacterial and plant transcription explains how a bacterial gene product can change host physiology after DNA transfer. The transferred region in a naturally tumorigenic Ti plasmid includes genes whose expression in plant cells alters phytohormone metabolism. Their effects depend on transcription and translation in the plant cell. The bacterium does not need to remain in the cytoplasm continually supplying the corresponding enzymes. The new genetic information persists in the transformed lineage, changing the cell's capacity to produce particular substances. This is a concrete example of horizontal gene transfer between very different organisms and of the ability of a cellular environment to express acquired genetic information.

Plant hormones normally coordinate development at very low concentrations. Auxin contributes to many processes, including cell expansion, developmental patterning and context-dependent division. Cytokinins participate in regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation. A tissue does not respond simply to a total quantity of all hormones added together; it responds to particular signals and their interactions. Naturally oncogenic T-DNA can increase synthesis of auxin and cytokinins, disrupting the balance that ordinarily regulates growth. A transformed cell may therefore continue growing under conditions in which an otherwise comparable cell requires external growth-regulating signals.

The result is an altered developmental programme in the host. A gall contains many cells, and the appearance of the whole structure depends not only on division but also on expansion, differentiation, vascular connections and resource supply. This explains why a mass of transformed cells can have a complex organization rather than being a uniform sphere of identical cells. The comparison to animal tumors is useful at the level of altered growth control, but plant cell walls and the organization of plant development impose different constraints. Crown gall must not be described as metastatic animal cancer. The genetic cause, tissue context and routes by which abnormal growth occurs are different.

T-DNA can also carry genes directing the production of opines. Opines are unusual metabolites synthesized by transformed plant tissue that certain Agrobacterium strains can use as nutrients. The corresponding capacity to utilize particular opines resides in the bacterium. This creates a biological division of labour: host cells perform a metabolic reaction under the influence of transferred genes, and associated bacteria may gain access to the products. The phenomenon makes the gall more than an accidental disturbance of growth. It demonstrates that pathogen-driven changes in development and metabolism can together create a favorable ecological niche.

It is tempting to explain every crown gall phenotype solely through the amount of auxin, but cytokinin production and the response properties of the host also contribute. A cell that produces a hormone but cannot respond appropriately to it does not necessarily exhibit the same growth response as a responsive cell. Conversely, a change in sensitivity can alter development even when hormone concentrations are unchanged. A careful experiment would distinguish measurement of the signaling molecule from measurement of downstream cellular behaviour. Hormone synthesis, hormone perception and the ultimate response are distinct mechanistic levels.

A persistent gall and a persistent bacterial population are also distinct. Once a plant lineage has acquired and expressed relevant T-DNA, removal of the initiating bacterial population need not immediately reverse its altered growth. If a study finds no viable bacteria in an established tissue sample but observes continued hormone-independent proliferation and evidence of transferred sequences, a genetic explanation becomes more plausible than ongoing secretion alone. However, failure to detect bacteria is not proof of complete absence; detection sensitivity and contamination controls remain important. The strength of the conclusion increases when independent observations converge.

The scientific evidence for transfer can be organized according to the question each measurement answers. Detection of a bacterial DNA sequence in material containing both bacteria and plants is insufficient by itself because bacterial DNA may contaminate the sample. Evidence connecting the sequence to plant chromosomal DNA is more specific for integration. Evidence of an RNA or protein produced from the transferred genes addresses expression. Continued inheritance of the sequence and phenotype through plant cell division addresses persistence. These measurements complement one another because each excludes a different class of alternative explanation. It is this logic of evidence, rather than the mere recognition of Ti plasmid terminology, that is especially valuable in analytical examination questions.

A conceptual comparison with the TALE example clarifies two distinct routes to transcriptional manipulation. A delivered TALE changes expression by binding a regulatory sequence that is already present in the host. A transferred T-DNA segment can provide new expressed genes that were absent from the original plant lineage. Either process can change the abundance of a host-relevant protein, but the molecular basis differs. In the first example the bacterial protein may need continual replenishment; in the second example a stable plant chromosome can preserve the capacity for expression. The difference explains why temporary biochemical modulation and stable genetic transformation should not be treated as synonyms.

Agrobacterium is also an important conceptual foundation for plant biotechnology. Understanding that transfer functions and the transferred genetic material are separable explains why this naturally occurring system became useful for introducing genetic information into plants. A biotechnology application is not the same phenomenon as crown gall pathology, however: the presence of foreign DNA does not necessarily cause a gall. The outcome depends on what genes are transferred and what their expression does. Detailed vector construction and laboratory transformation methods belong to later biotechnology units. Here the relevance is that the discovery of a natural host manipulation mechanism led to a general insight about the transfer and expression of genetic information.

Fungal invasion, feeding structures and host-cell interfaces

A filamentous fungus has a physical form very different from that of an individual bacterial cell or a virus particle. Its vegetative growth often consists of hyphae, which are elongated cellular filaments with a wall and a plasma membrane. Growth at the tips and branching allow the mycelium to explore a tissue. The fungus is a eukaryote with its own ribosomes and metabolic machinery, so it does not depend on the plant ribosome to translate fungal genes in the way an intracellular virus depends on host translation. Nevertheless, a pathogenic fungus needs access to a source of nutrients and a suitable environment in the living or damaged tissue.

Invasion by a fungal filament does not have one inevitable relationship with a plant plasma membrane. A hypha can grow in extracellular spaces and remain separated from the plant cytoplasm. Some fungi breach walls and grow within damaged tissue. Some biotrophic fungi form highly differentiated structures that are situated within the outline of a living plant cell but separated from its cytoplasm by a host-derived membrane. This last case is particularly instructive because the word intracellular can refer either to geometrical position within the host cell boundary or to direct exposure to host cytosol. The two meanings must not be confused.

A haustorium is a specialized feeding and interaction structure formed by certain biotrophic fungal and oomycete pathogens. In a classical rust fungus, a differentiated fungal structure contacts the host cell wall, and the haustorial portion extends inward while the host plasma membrane forms a surrounding interface. The fungal cell wall and plasma membrane remain distinct from the host-derived extrahaustorial membrane. Between the fungal surface and host-derived membrane lies the extrahaustorial matrix. Nutrient molecules leaving the plant cytoplasm must traverse the relevant interface before they can enter the fungal cytoplasm. The fungus is not simply floating freely within plant cytosol.

The extrahaustorial membrane is especially important for interpreting a diagram. Even though it is derived from the host membrane system, its molecular composition and organization need not be identical to the rest of the plant plasma membrane. An interface can become specialized because of altered recruitment, retention or exclusion of membrane proteins and because of membrane remodeling. The extrahaustorial matrix is also not equivalent in every respect to the unrestricted intercellular apoplast. In many interfaces, local structural features help distinguish this compartment from the surrounding extracellular continuum. Thus the haustorium represents a differentiated communication and exchange surface.

The large interface between host and fungus is beneficial only if the necessary molecules can cross it. A nutrient that is abundant in the host cytoplasm may be unavailable to the fungus if host membrane transport restricts its release. Similarly, sugar present within the interface does not enter the fungus merely because a concentration gradient exists; the fungal membrane must have an appropriate transport pathway. The architecture suggests that the specialization of transporter expression will be relevant to nutritional exchange. A measurement of total fungal biomass cannot reveal which interface transporters support it.

A classic investigation by Voegele and colleagues, published in 2001, examined nutrient uptake in the broad bean rust fungus Uromyces fabae. The study identified a hexose transporter, HXT1, associated with the haustorial membrane and characterized its transport properties. This provided molecular support for the idea that the haustorium is an important sugar acquisition interface. The conclusion was stronger than the observation that a fungal structure was located next to a plant cell: it connected localization of a particular transporter with its ability to transport sugars. It did not establish that every fungal nutrient is acquired exclusively through that one protein or that every haustorium uses identical transporters.

The physical chemistry of uptake is worth considering. A neutral sugar cannot efficiently cross a lipid bilayer unaided, but a transporter can permit its passage. If transport is coupled to movement of protons down their electrochemical gradient, the favorable proton movement can support sugar uptake under conditions in which sugar accumulation alone would be energetically unfavorable. A fungal proton pump contributes to maintaining the gradient using metabolic energy. The coupling is not a creation of energy; it converts energy stored in an electrochemical gradient into a change in the distribution of another molecule. This links a specialized pathogen feeding structure to the general principles of membrane bioenergetics.

The dependence on a proton gradient also gives a way to interpret transport measurements. Reduced sugar accumulation after loss of a proton gradient is consistent with proton-coupled uptake, but it does not by itself identify a particular transporter. The perturbation may affect other aspects of cell physiology. Independent information about the transport protein, its localization and the dependence of uptake on substrate and electrochemical conditions makes the interpretation more specific. When exam questions provide data from a heterologous expression system, the purpose is often to distinguish transport activity of a protein from the much broader physiological phenotype of the native fungal cell.

Many fungal pathogens secrete proteins that act on host molecules. Some extracellular effectors act in the apoplast, for example by interfering with enzymes or recognition processes in that compartment. Others act on plant intracellular targets after reaching the relevant host compartment. Secretion from the fungus and delivery into the plant cytoplasm are again separate events. There is no universal fungal counterpart that should automatically be called the bacterial Type III apparatus. Fungal and oomycete effector delivery mechanisms are diverse, and the existence of an intracellular target must not be taken as proof of a particular translocation route.

Biotrophic fungi have a strong physiological reason to preserve suitable host cells during nutrient acquisition. A living mesophyll cell carries out carbon metabolism, maintains ion gradients and sustains membrane transport. Killing the cell could destroy the controlled interface and alter the available resources. Effectors that moderate a host response may therefore support the feeding relationship. This does not imply that the infected plant remains physiologically normal or that the fungus is harmless. The costs of supporting the pathogen, changing resource distribution and inducing host responses may reduce photosynthesis, growth and reproductive success without requiring immediate death of every infected cell.

A rust infection provides a tissue-scale context for these ideas. Rust fungi can develop intercellular hyphae, feeding interfaces and spore-producing structures during infection of susceptible hosts. Pustules visible on plant surfaces reflect fungal reproduction and associated disruption of the surface. The spores are a pathogen sign, meaning a directly observed feature of the infectious agent. Nearby chlorosis is a symptom, meaning an alteration of the host. Confusing the two obscures the distinction between the organism and the host response. The distinction also matters in an examination that asks whether a visible structure belongs to the fungus or is a change in plant tissue.

Necrotrophic fungi present a contrasting relationship. Their use of killed tissue can be associated with secretion of extracellular enzymes or other molecules that disrupt tissue integrity. Degradation of wall components can loosen the adhesion between neighbouring plant cells, while damage to membranes disturbs compartmentation. Nutrients that were previously enclosed within cells may then become accessible. The biological effect of cell death differs from the outcome for an obligate biotroph. Nevertheless, necrotrophic disease should not be reduced to a single universal toxin; the causal contributions of different secreted molecules and host responses vary with the particular interaction.

The middle lamella, rich in pectic substances, contributes to adhesion between adjacent plant cells. Loss of its integrity can produce a soft or disorganized tissue even before every component of the wall has been degraded. This is a useful structural explanation for why changing a particular wall constituent can affect an organ more dramatically than expected from the quantity of polymer removed. The mechanical property of a tissue emerges from the way cells are joined and from the pressure and stiffness of individual cells. A pathogen-associated enzyme that disrupts the connections can therefore cause a macroscopic symptom without needing to degrade the entire cell wall.

Fungal strategies may also change as infection progresses. A hemibiotroph can initially maintain living host cells and later enter a destructive phase. Evidence of cell death at a late time is not sufficient to establish that the organism began as a necrotroph. To classify the strategy, the student should consider the sequence of pathogen growth, host viability and tissue changes. A snapshot is especially misleading if only the visible center of a lesion is examined, because cells at its advancing margin may be at a different stage of the interaction. Spatial and temporal variation must both be considered.

Oomycetes can produce filamentous structures and feeding interfaces resembling those of fungi, but they are not true fungi. Similar functional solutions can evolve independently in distantly related organisms that encounter comparable biological problems. Their cell walls, evolutionary relationships and some molecular components differ. The relevant comparison in this chapter is functional, not a complete taxonomic classification: a pathogen that maintains an interface with living cells faces constraints of nutrient exchange, membrane separation and host signaling irrespective of its lineage. Morphological resemblance alone cannot establish shared ancestry.

A final contrast concerns the movement of fungal cells and the movement of fungal effectors. Hyphal growth can extend through connected extracellular or invaded tissue, whereas a secreted protein can act at a location distinct from the fungal filament. An area of host response is therefore not necessarily a map of the current distribution of the fungus. This is one reason that tissue imaging, pathogen-specific molecular measurements and physiological observations are complementary. The question is not merely whether infection has occurred but which entity has crossed which boundary and what cellular consequence followed.

Vascular invasion and the physiology of wilt

Plants maintain the pressure of water inside cells through continual exchange with their environment. Roots absorb water, xylem supplies aerial tissues and transpiration removes water from leaves. A living cell with a sufficiently hydrated vacuole exerts turgor against its wall. Turgor contributes to expansion and to the mechanical support of many nonwoody tissues. If water loss exceeds the supply for long enough, turgor can fall and the leaf or stem may become limp. Wilt is therefore a hydraulic and physiological symptom. A pathogen causing wilt does not necessarily have to infect every wilting cell.

Fusarium oxysporum includes strains associated with vascular wilt diseases in different host plants. In a representative pathogenic interaction, infection begins in roots and is followed by access to xylem vessels. Hyphal growth and fungal propagules can occupy vascular pathways. Mature conducting vessel elements are dead at functional maturity, unlike the living cells surrounding them. The pathogen can therefore colonize an extracellular conducting space without crossing the plasma membrane of each adjacent living cell. This anatomical fact connects vascular invasion with the apoplast concept introduced at the beginning of the chapter.

The hydraulic disturbance of a diseased plant may reflect several interacting factors. Fungal structures can occupy parts of a conducting pathway; host responses can deposit materials or produce vascular occlusions; and changes in water movement or tissue damage can reduce effective hydraulic conductivity. It is too simplistic to attribute every wilt exclusively to the physical mass of a fungus filling a pipe. The vascular system is branched, has pits and conduits of different dimensions, and exhibits dynamic responses. The relative contribution of the pathogen and the host depends on the disease and its stage.

A plant can initially compensate for reduced water supply by decreasing stomatal conductance. Closing stomata reduces transpiration but also restricts diffusion of carbon dioxide into photosynthetic tissues. Thus a hydraulic disturbance can cause a reduction in carbon assimilation before complete loss of turgor is evident. As the disturbance increases, leaves may display wilting, chlorosis or necrosis. These symptoms can be spatially separated from the original root infection. Their distribution depends partly on the architecture of the vascular connections and the ability of the plant to redistribute water through alternative pathways.

This relationship offers a straightforward quantitative model. Over a restricted range of conditions, water flow through a tissue can be represented as the product of its effective hydraulic conductance and the difference in water potential across it. Conductance has units of flow per pressure difference, and water potential is expressed in pressure units such as megapascals. At a given driving force, a reduction in conductance reduces flow. If the plant can increase the driving force or reduce transpiration, the effect on hydration may be partly compensated. Therefore an observation of decreased conductance predicts a constraint on water supply but does not fix the final leaf water potential without further information.

One of the strongest diagnostic lessons from vascular wilt is that the same symptom can have unrelated causes. Dry soil can reduce water availability at the root surface; salt can change the water potential of the external solution; root damage can decrease absorbing capacity; and vascular disease can restrict internal conduction. Each can lead to low turgor. Evidence of a pathogen within xylem, impaired conductance and a suitable temporal relationship between infection and wilting strengthens a disease explanation. A photograph of the wilt alone cannot distinguish among these mechanisms.

The contrast with crown gall is useful for reasoning at the organ scale. In gall disease the initiating manipulation changes the developmental behavior of transformed cells and alters resource allocation. In vascular wilt the initiating disturbance may primarily constrain the movement of water. Both can reduce growth of organs distant from the original site, but their immediate physiological variables differ. An examination question describing a large local proliferating mass and one describing an otherwise structurally intact leaf with reduced water supply should therefore lead to different causal models even though both plants appear unhealthy.

Viral multiplication is necessary but not sufficient for spread

Unlike a bacterium or fungus, a virus has no self-sufficient cellular machinery with which to produce its proteins. A plant virus that reaches the extracellular spaces of a leaf cannot multiply simply by absorbing dissolved sugars. Its genetic material must gain access to a living cell and exploit appropriate host functions. Plant cell walls make this access a distinctive problem, and the entry routes covered in the preceding node provide the context for understanding what follows. Once viral genetic material reaches a susceptible cell, a new problem arises: progeny must reach additional cells if infection is to extend beyond the original site.

The words replication and infection are sometimes used as if they were interchangeable. Replication of a viral genome is a molecular process that increases the number of copies of its genetic material. Establishment of infection also requires expression of the functions that support multiplication, persistence or movement in the host. A virus can replicate in an individual cell but be unable to establish a spreading infection in an intact leaf. A virus may also be detectable in a particular tissue shortly after delivery without any evidence that its genome has replicated. The distinction is important when comparing a measurement of viral RNA with a measurement of the number of infected cells.

Tobacco mosaic virus, usually abbreviated TMV, is a classic example. Its mature particle has a rod-shaped architecture formed by many copies of a coat protein arranged around a single-stranded RNA genome. The RNA is of positive polarity, meaning that suitable portions can serve directly as messenger RNA for translation after becoming accessible to the host machinery. An intact coat protects the genome in the extracellular environment, but it also restricts access to the RNA by ribosomes. The requirements of protection outside a cell and expression inside a cell are therefore different. Uncoating, or exposure of the genome, makes the genetic information available for the processes that initiate intracellular multiplication.

The long history of TMV research helped distinguish infectious agents from ordinary cellular microbes. Early experiments with sap from diseased tobacco demonstrated that the agent could remain infectious after passage through filters that retained ordinary bacteria. Later work established that the agent could be purified and that its RNA is central to infectivity. These findings made it clear that an infectious agent need not possess the structural organization of a cell. They also provided a useful system for studying the separate roles of a viral genome and structural proteins. A particle can carry the information needed to initiate an infection without carrying all the enzymes or ribosomes that will participate in its multiplication.

Within an infected cell, the viral RNA must support synthesis of viral proteins needed for production of additional RNA. An RNA-dependent RNA polymerase is required to copy RNA from an RNA template; ordinary plant nuclear transcription does not perform the same viral replication function. In the case of TMV, intracellular viral replication is associated with host membranes and viral proteins. Replication-associated structures help organize the relevant molecular components within the host cell. It is important not to equate association with a membrane with the presence of an enveloped mature virus particle. A nonenveloped RNA virus may exploit host intracellular membranes without acquiring a lipid envelope as a defining part of its virion.

Translation and replication have different physical requirements. A ribosome reads a messenger RNA to synthesize a protein, whereas an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase reads a template to generate a complementary RNA. The positive-strand viral genome supports production of viral replication proteins and then participates in a cycle of RNA copying through complementary intermediates. Newly produced positive-strand molecules can subsequently serve several functions: they can be used in additional rounds of expression or replication, be associated with coat proteins or participate in movement to another cell. The fate of an individual RNA molecule depends on its interactions with proteins and the stage of the infection.

Movement proteins and coat proteins solve different problems. The coat protein contributes to packaging and protection of viral RNA and can participate in movement in some contexts. A movement protein contributes to transport of infectious material between cells by interacting with the host structures through which that material must pass. A virus with a functional replication apparatus and defective movement protein may produce substantial RNA in a cell but fail to expand the area of infection. The converse, a viral genome that cannot replicate, cannot sustain a spreading infection simply because a movement function remains available. One function provides infectious material and the other makes that material available to new cells.

These distinctions become particularly clear when experiments compare isolated plant cells with organized plant tissue. A protoplast is a plant cell whose wall has been removed; it can provide a useful context for examining intracellular phenomena without the normal constraints of wall-bounded cell-to-cell passage. If a viral genotype produces similar amounts of newly synthesized RNA in isolated cells but causes markedly smaller infection foci in an intact leaf, a defect in intercellular spread is a plausible explanation. However, a protoplast is not equivalent to a cell in a normal leaf. Wall removal, altered physiology and loss of tissue interactions can change gene expression or other cellular properties. Results from the two contexts must be interpreted with those limitations in mind.

A focus of infection is a spatially defined collection of infected cells descended, in terms of the spread of infection, from an initial infection site. The cells need not be descendants of the same dividing cell. Infection of an adjacent cell can enlarge a focus without cell division. Measuring the dimensions or number of infected cells within a focus therefore provides a different type of information from measuring the size of a plant cell or the growth of the leaf. It can help distinguish a viral movement phenotype from altered host growth, provided the measurement identifies infected cells rather than only tissue injury.

The initial infected cell is also not guaranteed to survive indefinitely. Viral replication can alter the allocation of host resources, interfere with host regulatory systems and induce defense-associated responses. A severely injured cell may cease to support new viral production even though viral nucleic acids remain detectable. Therefore the total RNA concentration in a homogenized sample reflects several factors: how many cells are infected, how much RNA each contains, the amount of residual RNA in damaged cells and the contribution of uninfected tissue to the sample mass. It is rarely a direct measure of any one of these quantities in isolation.

Viruses can differ substantially in which of these events limits infection. A plant genotype might permit entry and initial translation but restrict replication. Another might allow considerable intracellular multiplication but limit movement across cell boundaries. Yet another might permit local spread in a leaf while restricting access to systemic tissues. A common examination error is to infer the exact step of resistance from the mere absence of symptoms in a distant leaf. Such an observation is compatible with several distinct barriers. Independent measurements at different stages are needed to localize the restriction.

TMV is especially useful for the following discussion because local spread can be linked to the activity of a well-studied movement protein. The core biological principle extends beyond TMV: the structural organization of a multicellular plant makes intercellular passage a regulated process. The exact viral components and transport forms differ among viral groups, however. One should use TMV as an experimentally grounded model rather than as a universal description of every plant virus.

Plasmodesmata and the physical problem of intercellular transport

A growing plant tissue contains cells separated by walls that support mechanical integrity. If every cell were completely isolated from the cytoplasm of its neighbors, coordination of development and metabolism would require all communication to occur through extracellular routes and membrane transport. Plasmodesmata provide an additional route. They form narrow channels that cross the wall between adjacent living cells and permit regulated symplastic communication. Small solutes and some larger molecules can pass between connected cells, depending on channel structure, physiological state and interactions involving the cargo.

The plasma membrane at a plasmodesma is continuous with the membranes of the neighboring cells. A central endoplasmic reticulum-derived desmotubule is typically present, and a cytoplasmic sleeve lies between it and the surrounding plasma membrane. This geometry explains why a molecule passing through the cytoplasmic route does not necessarily have to cross two separate lipid bilayers. The molecule still faces a physical restriction: the channel is narrow and contains structural and regulatory components. The continuity of cytoplasm therefore does not establish free movement of all molecules at their bulk cytoplasmic diffusion rates.

The size exclusion limit of a plasmodesma describes its ability, under specified conditions, to permit passage of molecular probes of different effective sizes. It is a functional property inferred from transport behavior, not a permanently fixed diameter that is identical in all plant cells. Molecular shape, interactions with the channel, developmental state and the time scale of observation influence which molecules pass. A relatively extended nucleic acid associated with proteins is not equivalent to a compact globular protein of the same molecular mass. Thus an increase in apparent size exclusion limit indicates altered intercellular transport properties but does not by itself identify the form in which a particular viral genome moves.

Callose, a glucan that can accumulate around the neck region of plasmodesmata, is an important regulator of channel permeability. Deposition of additional callose can constrict the available passage and reduce transport, while changes in its turnover can alter permeability in the opposite direction. Callose metabolism is integrated with other cellular processes, including development and responses to stress. A high level of callose near a channel is therefore not an unambiguous signature of viral infection. Its mechanistic importance lies in the effect of deposition and removal on intercellular connectivity.

The geometry of the channel helps explain why a modest structural change might have a substantial effect on transport. In a simplified picture, molecules whose effective dimensions are far smaller than the available opening may pass relatively readily. Molecules approaching the size limit are much more sensitive to restrictions. If a channel becomes slightly narrower, passage of large probes may decline even while small solutes continue to move. This is consistent with selective changes in functional connectivity rather than a complete separation of the two cells. Real plasmodesmata are more complex than uniform cylindrical holes, so one should not use the geometry of a simple pipe as an exact molecular transport equation.

Virus-encoded movement proteins can modify the relationship between a viral genome and this intercellular barrier. The TMV movement protein, often referred to as the 30-kDa movement protein, associates with viral RNA and with host structures relevant to intracellular and intercellular transport. Its ability to influence plasmodesmatal transport has been demonstrated experimentally. It is not itself a motor that can be assumed to pull a rigid virus rod through a wall. Its function involves interactions with the infectious material and with the cellular environment in which that material must move. The molecular form transported can depend on the virus and the stage of infection.

The intracellular location of a viral RNA matters before it reaches a plasmodesma. A genome that is synthesized in association with intracellular membranes must reach a position from which it can enter an intercellular channel. Host endomembrane organization and cytoskeletal dynamics can influence the distribution of viral components. Observing association of a viral protein with microtubules or another cellular structure does not prove that the structure transports every infectious genome. A protein may accumulate at a site because it is being stored, degraded or redistributed rather than because the site is the essential transport route. Experimental evidence must distinguish association from a causal requirement.

At the boundary between two cells, successful movement requires more than the disappearance of an extracellular obstacle. An infectious genome must remain in a state that can initiate viral expression in the recipient cell. A transported RNA-protein complex may protect the RNA or assist its delivery, but those associations must also be compatible with subsequent translation or replication. Transport of an isolated viral protein that lacks the genome does not establish a new complete infection. This distinction is analogous to the difference between delivery of an effector and delivery of the genetic information that encodes it in the bacterial examples, although the biological machinery involved is different.

A useful prediction follows from distinguishing a movement defect from a replication defect. If a genome has a defective movement function but retains the ability to multiply within the initially infected cell, an independently supplied compatible movement protein may restore local spread in an appropriate experimental context. This is called complementation of a function. The restored phenotype does not mean that the viral genome itself has necessarily been genetically repaired. The missing protein may be provided from another source in the same biological environment. In contrast, an RNA sequence whose own replication depends on an essential cis-acting element cannot automatically be rescued by adding an unrelated mobile protein.

Historical work by Deom, Oliver and Beachy in 1987 demonstrated the importance of the TMV movement protein through studies in plants expressing the protein, including the ability to support spread of a movement-defective viral system. Subsequent investigations, including the 1989 study by Wolf and colleagues, used molecular tracers to examine changes in plasmodesmatal transport. These approaches supplied different kinds of evidence: functional complementation linked the protein to the spread phenotype, while tracer movement linked the protein to changes in intercellular permeability. Taken together, they supported a mechanism in which the virus exploits existing plant communication channels. A change in movement of a tracer alone would not have been sufficient to establish that the virus requires the protein to spread.

A student should also distinguish cell-to-cell movement from cell-to-cell fusion. In passage through a plasmodesma, two adjacent cells remain organized as separate walled units with regulated cytoplasmic continuity through a channel. Their nuclei do not have to enter a common large cytoplasm produced by merging whole cells. The next node deals with normal and abnormal fusion of entire cells; that process involves a different scale and organization of membrane remodeling. Viral movement through plasmodesmata is not a plant equivalent of the formation of an animal syncytium.

Different plant viruses have evolved different arrangements for exploiting cell communication. Some use a single movement protein, whereas others require several viral proteins, and some transport infectious particles or other nucleoprotein structures through modified channels. In certain systems, movement-associated structures can remodel the plasmodesmatal architecture extensively. The important generalization is that the viral movement strategy must be compatible with the host wall and membrane boundaries. The statement that all plant viruses travel as naked RNA, or that all travel as intact virions, is incorrect. The most accurate answer depends on which virus and which stage of transport the question specifies.

Vascular access and long-distance viral movement

Expansion of an infection focus in a leaf is usually not sufficient to explain the eventual appearance of symptoms in leaves far from the original site. Cell-to-cell spread operates across local boundaries and can progressively involve groups of nearby cells. Long-distance movement requires access to pathways that connect different plant regions. The vascular system provides such connectivity, but its two principal conducting tissues have different functions. Xylem primarily distributes water and dissolved mineral nutrients, while phloem transports assimilates and other mobile substances between source and sink tissues.

A source is a region that exports a particular assimilate, often a mature photosynthesizing leaf. A sink is a region that imports assimilates for metabolism, storage or growth, such as a developing leaf, root tip or growing fruit. These roles can change during development. A young leaf that initially imports carbon can later become a net exporter. Movement in the phloem therefore reflects the physiological arrangement of sources and sinks rather than a universal rule that all substances move upward. The connectivity between organs and the direction of relevant mass flow can influence where a virus accumulates after gaining access to the transport pathway.

The phloem contains conducting sieve elements closely associated with companion cells in flowering plants. Mature sieve tube elements lack a nucleus and have reduced cellular machinery, but they remain living conducting cells whose function depends on neighboring cells. Sieve elements are connected by sieve plates containing pores that facilitate movement of phloem sap. Their physical arrangement is different from the narrow plasmodesmata that join many ordinary living cells. A virus that reaches conducting phloem can exploit a continuous long-distance route without independently invading every cell between the original leaf and a distant organ.

The crucial phrase is reaches conducting phloem. Viral material moving locally through a leaf must encounter the appropriate tissues, cross barriers involved in vascular loading and remain compatible with transport. At a distant site it must leave the transport pathway and establish infection in susceptible cells. Loading, transport, unloading and local establishment are distinct processes. A viral genotype might be detected in tissue close to a vein yet fail to accumulate within conducting elements. Another might reach the vascular stream but fail to establish an infection after transport. Such defects would produce different distributions even though the final symptom of a distant leaf might be absent in both cases.

For many plant viruses, including TMV, the coat protein has important roles beyond its contribution to the mature particle. Efficient long-distance infection can require viral functions that are not necessary for all local cell-to-cell movement. This helps explain how a viral mutation can produce a normal or nearly normal local focus and yet a severe reduction of systemic infection. The requirement for coat protein and the nature of the transported form should be treated as properties of the specified virus and host rather than generalized to all plant viruses. Some viruses employ different combinations of structural and movement functions during these stages.

An examination dataset might show similar RNA accumulation in an inoculated leaf for two viral genotypes, with viral RNA detected in an upper leaf only for the first genotype. The evidence indicates a difference in some process required for systemic establishment, but it does not by itself prove a defect specifically in phloem loading. The second genotype might have failed during transport, unloading or replication in the upper leaf. Additional localization measurements can narrow the possibilities. If the infectious material is present inside a conducting element but absent in nearby recipient cells, failure of access to the conducting element is no longer the simplest explanation.

The distinction between movement of a pathogen and movement of a host-derived signal becomes especially important over long distances. Infection in a source leaf can induce physiological responses in uninfected tissues through hormones, signaling molecules or changes in assimilate distribution. A distant leaf may therefore exhibit altered gene expression even before infectious material is detected there. The absence of a pathogen-specific signal is not necessarily proof that the tissue is uninfected, because detection has a limit, but the presence of a host response alone does not establish systemic infection. The two observations measure different entities.

Disease symptoms can also lag behind the arrival of a virus. A cell may contain viral genetic material while retaining normal appearance until sufficient physiological disruption has accumulated. The spatial pattern of a mosaic symptom consequently need not coincide precisely with the distribution of viral RNA or protein at one moment. Conversely, recovery of normal pigmentation in part of a leaf does not automatically show that all viral material has been eliminated. Host response, development and the timing of infection contribute to the visible pattern.

Systemic spread is influenced by plant architecture as well as viral molecular functions. Two leaves at similar physical distances from an inoculated region may be connected differently through the vascular network. A developing leaf with strong sink activity may receive substances through a pathway that differs from the pathway supplying a mature source leaf. Therefore a straight-line distance measured on the plant is not an adequate predictor of transport time or the probability of infection. A route based on physiological connectivity is a better explanatory model.

A final distinction concerns the spread of disease from one plant to another. Within-plant movement through phloem does not establish the mode of transmission between plants. Some viruses depend on biological vectors for transmission, whereas TMV is well known for mechanical transmission and persistence of infectious particles in suitable contexts. The requirements of an infectious particle outside one plant can differ from the requirements of a genome moving locally between cells of a single plant. A mutation that changes the stability of the mature particle might therefore affect transmission differently from its effect on intracellular replication. Identifying the spatial scale of the question is essential before selecting a mechanism.

How changes in cells become visible disease symptoms

The colors of a healthy leaf arise from pigments whose relative abundance and cellular distribution depend on tissue development and metabolism. Chlorophyll is concentrated in the photosynthetic apparatus of chloroplasts. A chlorotic region has lost some of its normal green coloration, commonly because chlorophyll content, chloroplast development or pigment maintenance has been altered. Chlorosis does not necessarily imply that all cells within the affected region are dead. A living mesophyll cell with fewer functional photosynthetic complexes may still carry out respiration and other metabolic functions. This distinction makes it possible to interpret a pale leaf as evidence of reduced physiological function without immediately treating it as a region of necrosis.

Viral infection can influence chloroplast function through changes in host gene expression, redistribution of resources and disruption of cellular organization. The exact molecular cause of chlorosis varies among plant-virus interactions. Loss of green pigmentation may result from reduced synthesis of chlorophyll, accelerated breakdown of pigment or altered chloroplast development. Reduced photosynthetic efficiency can also occur without a proportional decrease in chlorophyll abundance. A chlorophyll measurement and a measurement of carbon assimilation therefore answer related but different questions. The first addresses the amount of a pigment, while the second addresses the physiological performance of the photosynthetic tissue.

A mosaic is a spatial pattern in which differently colored regions occur next to one another. TMV infection is historically associated with mosaic symptoms in susceptible tobacco and other compatible plants. The appearance emerges from differences in the physiological state of groups of cells and from the progression of infection and leaf development. It is tempting to interpret each pale area as containing virus and each green area as entirely virus-free, but such a one-to-one correspondence cannot be assumed. Viral accumulation, host responses and tissue development can vary independently in adjacent regions. A direct measurement of viral RNA or antigen in separately sampled regions would provide evidence about pathogen distribution that the visible color pattern alone cannot supply.

Mosaic can arise in leaves that develop while a systemic infection is progressing. During leaf expansion, cells differ in age, developmental program and connectivity to neighboring tissues. The physiological consequences of infection at different developmental stages can differ as well. A local disturbance of chloroplast differentiation in a growing leaf may become more visible as that leaf expands. The final spatial pattern therefore records both cellular events and the history of tissue growth. This is a useful reminder that a mature disease symptom is often the accumulated outcome of past processes, not an instantaneous image of the current infection.

Necrosis refers to death of host tissue. A necrotic spot represents a more severe loss of cellular integrity than chlorosis alone. Membrane damage, failure of ion homeostasis and cessation of metabolism can accompany cell death. The lesion may result directly from pathogen-associated damage, indirectly from a host response or from a combination of the two. The relative contribution cannot be determined merely by observing a brown spot. If a host response causes localized death that restricts a pathogen dependent on living tissue, the lesion may represent effective containment rather than unrestricted pathogen growth.

The margin of a necrotic lesion is particularly informative because it often separates cells in different physiological states. Cells toward the center may be dead, cells at the margin may be stressed or infected, and apparently healthy surrounding cells may be responding to signals from the diseased region. The composition of a tissue sample depends on how these regions are combined. A sample containing mostly dead central tissue could show lower levels of a transiently expressed host response than a sample from the living margin. That difference does not necessarily indicate that the response was unimportant in initiating the lesion.

Water soaking is a distinct visual phenotype. Healthy leaves frequently contain intercellular spaces occupied in part by gas. Accumulation of water in these regions can alter the way light passes through the tissue, producing a darker or more translucent appearance. The phenotype can arise in the context of bacterial manipulation of the apoplastic environment, but its presence does not identify a unique molecular pathway. A water-soaked region need not have undergone complete cell death. Conversely, necrotic tissue may subsequently lose water and become dry. The order of these appearances can therefore provide clues about changes in tissue physiology if interpreted alongside other evidence.

Blight describes extensive damage that often develops rapidly across foliage or other plant organs. A disease may spread through multiple adjacent sites or along an anatomical route, causing lesions to enlarge and coalesce. Loss of viable photosynthetic surface can then reduce the energy available for growth elsewhere in the plant. In a rice bacterial blight example, colonization associated with vascular pathways is part of the mechanism; in a different blight, direct damage to leaf tissues may play a more prominent role. Thus the term describes the scale and appearance of injury rather than one conserved molecular mechanism.

A gall is an abnormal local outgrowth rather than simply a discolored or dead region. In crown gall the central mechanism is altered growth control following transfer and expression of bacterial genetic information. Other plant galls can have different causes, including other organisms and developmental stimuli. A student must distinguish the observed morphology from the specific cause in the case described. A gall may affect the whole plant if it disrupts resource allocation or transport, but its presence does not prove that all plant cells are genetically transformed. A locally altered group of cells can impose systemic physiological costs.

Wilt reflects insufficient turgor, and it can be accompanied by chlorosis and necrosis as physiological dysfunction progresses. These symptoms therefore need not represent three independent diseases. A vascular infection that reduces water supply may cause stomatal closure, impaired carbon fixation and eventually cell damage. The sequence connects hydraulic transport with metabolism and survival. In contrast, a virus associated with mosaic can alter chloroplast function without a primary failure of xylem conduction. Comparison of water status, vascular function and pathogen localization helps determine whether similar changes in leaf appearance arose through distinct mechanisms.

Stunting is another integrated outcome of infection. Growth depends on cell division, cell expansion, assimilate availability and the coordinated transport of water and nutrients. A plant may become smaller because infection diverts resources, restricts photosynthesis, alters hormonal regulation or damages conducting tissues. A reduction in total height alone cannot distinguish among these causes. If an examination describes a reduction in cell expansion with normal cell number, the immediate defect is different from a reduction in cell number with normal cell dimensions. Both can reduce organ size, but they implicate different aspects of developmental physiology.

The distinction between a disease sign and a symptom offers a practical way to evaluate observations. A fungal pustule containing spores is evidence of a pathogen structure, whereas yellowing of surrounding plant tissue is evidence of a host change. A gall is a symptom even when bacterial DNA is demonstrably present within transformed cells. A visible symptom can be useful for recognizing a disease pattern, but it is not by itself a complete causal diagnosis. At examination level, the important skill is to infer a mechanism from a combination of observations: the affected compartment, the agent, the molecular perturbation and the resulting change in host function.

Quantitative reasoning about population, movement and physiology

Different measurements of pathogen success are not interchangeable because they describe different variables. The abundance of bacterial cells may be expressed as a count relative to tissue mass, viral RNA as a molecular quantity, the number of infected cells as a spatial count, and disease severity as a fraction of affected tissue. A larger value of one does not guarantee a larger value of all the others. For example, an agent that strongly alters host physiology per infected cell may cause extensive symptoms despite a modest pathogen population. A comparison between two experimental conditions must identify the measured variable before interpreting the size of the difference.

Bacterial counts are frequently compared on a logarithmic scale because populations can vary over several orders of magnitude. For two positive counts measured in the same units, the ratio of the larger to the smaller is a fold difference. The difference between their base-ten logarithms is the logarithm of that ratio. Consequently, a difference of two log units corresponds to a hundredfold difference, not a doubling. A change from ten thousand to one million measurable units is a factor of one hundred. If the counts represent viable colony-forming units per gram of tissue, the interpretation is about recoverable viable units relative to tissue mass rather than a direct count of every bacterium present.

A population change measured between two times is a net outcome. Cell division increases the population, while death and removal can reduce it. In living plant tissues the availability of water, nutrients, space and the activity of host responses can all change over the same interval. The exponential growth model, in which abundance equals initial abundance multiplied by an exponential function of time, is appropriate only when the net per capita growth rate is approximately constant. Taking natural logarithms gives a linear relationship between log abundance and time under that assumption. Deviations from linearity can reflect changing net growth conditions, but the shape of a growth curve alone does not identify the responsible host factor.

A useful analytical comparison considers two bacterial groups with identical initial recoverable populations and different final populations. If the group associated with a particular host interaction has lower final abundance, several explanations are possible: slower division, greater death, loss from the sampled location or altered recovery in the measurement. A claim that the host specifically killed the bacteria requires evidence beyond the difference in final abundance. A carefully chosen time series may help discriminate among the alternatives, but even a time series of viable counts cannot always separate cell division from death without an independent measure of one of those processes.

Quantification of viral movement introduces a geometric issue. An approximately circular infection focus has area equal to pi multiplied by the square of its radius. If the radius doubles, the area becomes four times as large. Under the additional assumption of uniform cell density and a filled circular focus, the number of infected cells would approximately quadruple. This result is a geometric consequence of the expanding boundary and does not establish that viral replication per cell has increased fourfold. Real leaves contain veins, irregular cell arrangements and direction-dependent barriers, so infection foci need not be circular. The area model is useful as a simplifying approximation, not as a universal law of viral spread.

Measurements of total viral RNA can be interpreted through a simple accounting relationship. If the infected cells are approximately comparable and the contribution of extracellular or residual RNA is negligible, total viral RNA equals the number of infected cells multiplied by the mean RNA quantity per infected cell. A difference in total RNA may then arise from a difference in either factor. If one sample contains twice as much viral RNA and twice as many infected cells as another, the mean RNA per infected cell may be unchanged. Conversely, equal total RNA in two tissues may conceal different combinations of intracellular accumulation and spatial spread. This relationship explains why a population-level assay cannot by itself isolate a movement defect.

Hydraulic measurements require careful attention to signs and units. Water tends to flow from a region of higher water potential toward a region of lower water potential when a conducting pathway is available. In a simplified steady-state model the magnitude of flow is the effective conductance multiplied by the positive difference between upstream and downstream water potential. Water potential may be expressed in megapascals, so a conductance in milliliters per hour per megapascal produces a flow in milliliters per hour. Reducing conductance by half at a fixed water-potential difference reduces the predicted flow by half. In a real plant, however, the water-potential difference can change in response to the disturbance, and stomatal regulation can alter water loss. The calculated value is a conditional prediction.

Disease severity is often represented as a percentage of tissue that displays a specified symptom. If the affected area is divided by the total examined area and multiplied by one hundred, the result is a percentage. Comparison is meaningful only if the same symptom definition and sampling procedure are used. A leaf with two large lesions may have a larger affected fraction than a leaf with many small lesions. Lesion number, lesion area and severity are therefore distinct measurements. Likewise, disease incidence, the fraction of examined plants classified as diseased, differs from disease severity within any given plant.

A mechanistic study benefits from combining these measurements. If a host alteration reduces bacterial abundance and lesion area while leaving the physiological variable of interest unchanged, one explanation might be reduced pathogen establishment through a different process. If abundance is similar but lesion area is smaller, a difference in host sensitivity or tissue injury becomes plausible. If viral RNA per initially infected cell is comparable but focus area is much smaller, a difference in cell-to-cell spread is a more direct hypothesis. The combined evidence narrows the possible causes without forcing a single measurement to answer a question for which it was not designed.

Reasoning from evidence across pathogen classes

Comparative reasoning begins with the common problem of acquiring a resource from another organism without necessarily occupying the same compartment. A bacterial cell can maintain its own cytoplasm outside a plant cell while delivering proteins across the host boundary. A fungal hypha can establish a persistent interface with a living cell, and a virus can require access to the host cytoplasm for the production of viral components. The repeated use of host transport, membrane organization and signaling illustrates convergent functional solutions, not evidence that these pathogens possess a common cellular apparatus. A bacterial injection apparatus and a viral movement protein solve different transport problems, even though both act across boundaries imposed by the host.

An evolutionary trade-off follows from the dependence of a pathogen on its host. A pathogen that obtains nutrients from a living interface can lose its source of nutrients if host death occurs too early. A pathogen that exploits damaged tissue may benefit from cell death instead. Nevertheless, the labels biotroph and necrotroph summarize a pattern of resource acquisition rather than predict every molecular interaction. Host species, developmental stage and environmental conditions can modify the costs and benefits of maintaining an interface. A disease phenotype should therefore be interpreted alongside evidence of the infection stage, the pathogen's position and the condition of the invaded cells.

The same principle connects the chapter to membrane transport in earlier units. For a neutral sugar moved from one compartment to another, the chemical contribution to the molar free-energy difference is RT ln(Cdestination/Csource), when concentrations reasonably approximate activities. R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and the concentration ratio is dimensionless. At equal concentrations the chemical term is zero; it is positive for accumulation against the concentration gradient. For a charged proton the electrical contribution also matters. Proton-coupled uptake can be favorable overall even when sugar accumulation alone is unfavorable. This is why an altered proton gradient may change nutrient uptake without changing the amount of transporter protein. It also explains why a change in transporter abundance is insufficient to determine flux without knowledge of the concentrations and the transport mechanism.

At the tissue scale, spatial measurements introduce another distinction. A visible lesion may contain living infected cells, dead host cells, extracellular pathogen material and surrounding cells that are responding to infection without containing the pathogen. A measurement averaged over the lesion mixes these compartments. A reduction in the fraction of living tissue can decrease a host transcript measured per lesion even if the transcript remains unchanged per surviving cell. The appropriate denominator depends on the question. Measurements normalized to tissue mass, host cell number, viable pathogen count or infected-cell number do not necessarily answer the same question. This limitation is particularly important when comparing an early biotrophic interaction with a later destructive stage.

Scientific explanations are most convincing when several independent observations support the same causal model. In host-pathogen biology, the mere association of a protein with an infected tissue does not show that it caused the disease. A protein may become abundant as a result of tissue injury or as part of an unrelated physiological response. A more informative comparison asks whether a specific host process changes when the relevant interaction is absent, whether the change precedes the disease outcome and whether independent evidence places the responsible molecule at the required site of action.

Negative controls establish the baseline against which an observation can be interpreted. A mock-treated host can reveal changes caused by handling or tissue injury rather than infection. An appropriate uninfected host sample can reveal the normal abundance of a measured molecule. A measurement of baseline pathogen viability can distinguish general cellular defects from altered performance specifically in the host. Controls need to match the question being asked; a control suitable for measuring transcript abundance is not necessarily sufficient for establishing the location of a protein.

A fluorescent signal associated with a host nucleus, for example, can help localize a molecule, but localization alone does not establish transcriptional activity. A nuclear protein may bind DNA, interact with another protein or have no relevant activity under the tested conditions. If a host transcript increases at the same time, the combination supports a hypothesis of regulation but does not necessarily establish direct recognition of the corresponding promoter. Evidence for promoter association and dependence of the transcript response on the relevant regulatory sequence would be more specific. This is why the transcriptional reprogramming example is useful for practicing distinctions among localization, association and causation.

The same logic applies to a fungal feeding interface. Detection of a transporter at a haustorial membrane establishes its location when the detection method is specific. Transport activity characterized in another cellular system supplies evidence that the protein can move its substrate under defined conditions. Neither observation alone quantitatively establishes the total flux of the substrate from host to fungus in a living leaf. The native membrane environment, substrate supply and energetic conditions also matter. The biological interpretation is strongest when structural, biochemical and physiological observations are consistent.

Historical studies of crown gall offer a further example of evidence at different levels. Continued abnormal proliferation after removal of detectable bacteria supports persistence of a host change. Detection of transferred DNA in association with plant chromosomal material addresses a genetic basis for that persistence. Expression of the transferred genes and altered phytohormone levels provide a connection between the DNA and the growth phenotype. The evidence is more informative when the measurements are considered in their causal order rather than as a list of associated observations.

For plant viruses, complementation of a movement defect illustrates the separation of a protein function from the genome that normally encodes it. A restored spreading phenotype in a context where the missing protein is supplied independently supports a role for the protein in movement. The conclusion is strongest when intracellular viral multiplication is assessed separately. Without that comparison, a difference in focus size could also result from a difference in the amount of infectious material produced in each cell. This is why local spread experiments benefit from both spatial and molecular readouts.

These cases reveal a recurring pattern. A pathogen or its molecular product first reaches a particular compartment; it then changes a host process; and the altered process influences the ability of the pathogen or the host tissue to function. The location of the molecular product constrains the possible mechanisms, the nature of the host process constrains the expected physiological phenotype, and the timing helps identify cause and consequence. This framework allows unfamiliar questions to be solved by biological reasoning even when the exact pathogen species has not been memorized.

The next chapter will examine an interaction with a different topological result: two separate cells becoming part of a shared cellular structure through fusion. Plant pathogen movement through plasmodesmata, the surrounding membrane of a fungal haustorium and the delivery of a bacterial effector all involve communication across cellular boundaries, but none is in itself equivalent to fusion of whole cells. Understanding these distinctions provides the foundation for comparing normal developmental fusion with pathogen-associated abnormal fusion in animal tissues.

Worked quantitative examples

All values below are hypothetical teaching data, not reported experimental observations.

Viable-count ratio

Hypothetical populations are 2 × 10⁶ and 5 × 10⁴ CFU g⁻¹. Divide: (2 × 10⁶)/(5 × 10⁴) = 40. The difference is log₁₀(40) ≈ 1.602 log units. This describes recoverable viable units, not the contribution of growth versus death.

Net growth rate

Suppose an abundance rises fourfold over 8 h and the net rate is constant. From N = N₀eʳᵗ, r = ln(4)/8 = 0.1733 h⁻¹. The corresponding doubling time is ln(2)/r = 4 h. A time-varying rate would invalidate a single constant-rate interpretation.

Focus geometry

Two ideal circular foci have radii 0.5 mm and 1.5 mm. Areas are π(0.5)² ≈ 0.785 mm² and π(1.5)² ≈ 7.069 mm², a ninefold difference. Uniform cell density and completely filled foci are assumed.

RNA accounting

Sample A contains 600 RNA units in 30 infected cells; sample B contains 900 units in 45 infected cells. Both average 20 units per infected cell. Total RNA differs because the number of infected cells differs in this simplified example.

Hydraulic flow

Conductance is 2.0 mL h⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ and upstream and downstream water potentials are −0.2 and −0.8 MPa. The positive difference is 0.6 MPa. Flow = 2.0 × 0.6 = 1.2 mL h⁻¹. At conductance 0.5, the same gradient predicts 0.3 mL h⁻¹, a 75% reduction. These are steady-state estimates.

Severity and incidence

If symptomatic area is 12 cm² out of 80 cm², severity is 100 × 12/80 = 15%. If 6 of 20 plants are symptomatic, incidence is 30%. The measures have different denominators and are not interchangeable.

Integrated revision and examination traps

Plant disease connects pathogen location, molecular activity, host physiology and tissue-level outcomes. Bacterial effectors, transferred T-DNA, fungal interfaces and viral movement proteins alter different cellular boundaries. Compare these mechanisms before interpreting symptoms.

Essential quantitative relationships

Fold change = N₂/N₁; log difference = log₁₀(N₂/N₁); net growth r = ln(N₂/N₁)/Δt; circular focus area = πr² (radius r); mean RNA per infected cell = total RNA / infected-cell count; hydraulic flow = conductance × positive water-potential difference; severity = affected area / total area × 100%; incidence = affected individuals / examined individuals × 100%. Each relation assumes the measurement definitions and conditions explained in the theory.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Apoplast is the same as cytoplasm

The apoplast is outside living plasma membranes. An intercellular bacterium is not necessarily intracellular. Common source of confusion: The word intercellular is often mistaken for intracellular. Examination distinction: Locate the bacterial body and its effector separately.

2. A protein in the extracellular fraction proves translocation

Extracellular release and delivery into the host cytosol are distinct. Common source of confusion: Protein detection is easily conflated with proof of its destination. Examination distinction: Choose the appropriate location-specific measurement.

3. Every effector promotes disease in every genotype

Recognition and context can reverse the outcome. Common source of confusion: The term virulence factor suggests a universal rather than genotype-dependent outcome. Examination distinction: Distinguish compatible and incompatible host interactions.

4. All effector genes become part of the host genome

Protein delivery is not DNA integration. Common source of confusion: Protein-mediated transcriptional change resembles genetic transformation in its effects. Examination distinction: Compare a TALE with T-DNA.

5. A SWEET transporter always pumps sugar outward

Facilitated transport depends on gradients; expression alone does not fix flux. Common source of confusion: Increased expression is mistaken for an ATP-driven pump. Examination distinction: Predict flux only with information about localization and gradients.

6. All Ti plasmid genes transfer to plants

The transferred region is distinct from many bacterial virulence functions. Common source of confusion: The names Ti plasmid and T-DNA are often used as if interchangeable. Examination distinction: Distinguish cargo from machinery.

7. Type III and Type IV mean the same apparatus

They are distinct secretion classes with different cargo roles in these examples. Common source of confusion: Both systems are called secretion apparatuses. Examination distinction: Match the pathway to the entity being delivered.

8. Transient foreign-gene expression proves stable integration

Persistence and chromosome association require separate evidence. Common source of confusion: Expression of an incoming DNA molecule is mistaken for its incorporation into a chromosome. Examination distinction: Interpret RNA detection without a chromosome junction.

9. A crown gall is a mass of bacteria

It consists of altered plant tissue. Common source of confusion: The visible mass is mistaken for a mass of the initiating organism. Examination distinction: Distinguish the initiating agent from the growth response.

10. Opines are phytohormones

Opines are specialized metabolites; auxin and cytokinins regulate growth. Common source of confusion: Both opines and hormones are synthesized in transformed tissue, but their functions differ. Examination distinction: Match metabolic and developmental outcomes separately.

11. A haustorium lies freely in host cytosol

The host-derived extrahaustorial membrane separates the host cytoplasm from the interface. Common source of confusion: An inward projection looks as though it has crossed the host membrane. Examination distinction: Trace the membrane boundaries in Figure 5.

12. Every fungus requires dead host cells

Biotrophs depend on living cells; necrotrophs exploit dead tissue. Common source of confusion: Visible tissue death can obscure different nutrient-acquisition strategies. Examination distinction: Infer consequences of host-cell death in the specified strategy.

13. Wilt always proves fungal blockage

Turgor can fall through abiotic water limitation or diverse vascular changes. Common source of confusion: A characteristic disease name is mistaken for a unique cause of a general water-deficit symptom. Examination distinction: Interpret hydraulic and localization measurements together.

14. Viral multiplication proves movement

Intracellular replication and intercellular transport are separate functions. Common source of confusion: Replication within a cell and transport between cells are conflated. Examination distinction: Compare isolated-cell RNA with focus size.

15. All plant viruses cross walls as intact virions

Transport forms and movement functions vary. Common source of confusion: The familiar image of a viral particle is mistaken for its only possible transport form. Examination distinction: Restrict the conclusion to the specified virus and stage.

16. Plasmodesmata are always fully open

Permeability is regulated, including by callose. Common source of confusion: Static diagrams of channels can conceal their regulated permeability. Examination distinction: Interpret tracer size and transport differences.

17. Local spread proves systemic infection

Vascular access, transport and unloading are additional requirements. Common source of confusion: The successful crossing of one cellular boundary is mistaken for access to vascular transport. Examination distinction: Use local and distant tissue measurements independently.

18. Mosaic is a perfect map of viral distribution

Pigment changes and viral accumulation need not coincide exactly. Common source of confusion: The visible phenotype is mistaken for direct localization of viral components. Examination distinction: Compare a visible symptom with a pathogen-specific assay.

19. Disease incidence equals severity

Incidence concerns affected individuals, severity concerns extent of affected tissue. Common source of confusion: Two percentages with different denominators are treated as equivalent. Examination distinction: Check denominators before interpreting percentages.
