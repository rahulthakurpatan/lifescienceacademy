1. The problem of recognizing a microbe

A leaf provides a moist, nutrient-containing environment to microorganisms that succeed in entering its internal air spaces. Yet a healthy leaf is not sterile. Bacteria, yeasts and other microorganisms live on surfaces, and some establish relationships that cause little injury or even benefit their host. The physiological problem is therefore not simply to eliminate every foreign organism. A living plant must detect signs of microbial presence or cellular damage, evaluate those signals in their local context and restrict potentially damaging invasion without continually sacrificing photosynthesis and growth. Preformed barriers such as the cuticle reduce the probability of contact with living cells, but they cannot explain why the same plant rapidly changes its gene expression upon exposure to a small, purified microbial molecule.

Plant innate immunity comprises genetically encoded mechanisms by which cells recognize and respond to threats. Innate in this context means that the receptor repertoire does not arise through the somatic immunoglobulin gene rearrangements and clonal selection characteristic of vertebrate adaptive immunity. Plants have highly diverse and heritable immune receptors, and their response to a second challenge may be altered by prior exposure. Such priming is not equivalent to producing antigen-specific antibodies or expanding a clone of B lymphocytes. Most living plant cells can participate in defense, rather than relying on a circulating population of specialized immune cells.

A useful distinction is between pre-existing resistance and induced resistance. A wax layer can impede entry even before microbial contact; a receptor-triggered change in guard-cell aperture is an active physiological response. A mutation in a cuticle biosynthetic enzyme can increase access to the apoplast while leaving perception of a purified elicitor normal. Conversely, a mutation in a surface immune receptor may preserve the physical cuticle but compromise the ability to detect a particular microbial signal after contact. Equal disease susceptibility in these two mutants would not establish that both genes function in the same molecular pathway.

Pattern-triggered immunity, abbreviated PTI, is the set of immune responses initiated when cell-surface pattern recognition receptors perceive accessible microbial or host-derived danger-associated patterns. A pattern is not a geometrical shape in the ordinary sense. It is a molecular feature that can be recognized by a receptor, often because the feature occurs in a class of microorganisms or is released during injury. The pattern can be a defined peptide, a carbohydrate fragment or another molecular structure. Recognition initiates biochemical events, which may include ion fluxes, protein phosphorylation, reactive oxygen production, transcriptional changes and reinforcement of tissue barriers.

The word resistance describes a reduction in pathogen entry, growth or spread, whereas tolerance describes the capacity to maintain performance despite a given burden. An apparently healthy plant may have high pathogen populations because it is tolerant, and a plant with a strong visible local response may nevertheless restrict pathogen growth. The endpoints are not interchangeable. An immune study that measures only a rapid oxidative response has established a signaling phenotype, not necessarily a resistance phenotype. Experiments need to link the early molecular observation to an appropriate later outcome.

PTI is often introduced as a first layer of inducible defense because the relevant receptors can perceive material outside the plasma membrane, before or during microbial entry. The designation is a useful organizational device, not an assertion that each pathogen must pass through a rigid sequence of discrete immune stages. Intracellular effector recognition can strengthen components of surface-receptor signaling. The two modes of recognition differ in their inputs and receptor organization, but many outputs overlap. This chapter develops the surface-perception mechanism; intracellular immune receptors and systemic defense are developed in the subsequent nodes.

Recognition at the plant–environment interface

The spatial organization of a leaf helps explain why a defense mechanism based on surface receptors is useful. The epidermis is the outer cellular layer, and the cuticle covers much of its exposed wall. Beneath the epidermis lies mesophyll tissue, with intercellular air spaces that facilitate gas diffusion. The apoplast includes cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membranes of living cells. It is not equivalent to the cell cytosol. An invading extracellular bacterium can multiply in suitable apoplastic environments without entering the cytoplasm of each plant cell. A receptor exposed to that environment can detect a molecular feature of the bacterium while the plasma membrane still separates bacterial products from the cytosol.

The distinction is equally important in fungal interactions. A fungal hypha may grow on the epidermis or between plant cells, and particular fungi develop specialized structures that interact closely with living host cells. The wall of a hypha and products of wall remodeling can expose recognizable carbohydrates. Host enzymes can contribute to the availability of those fragments. The location of the fungal organism, the exposed chemical structures and the location of plant receptors all influence whether recognition occurs. A receptor expressed in the root is not automatically able to detect a fungal molecule confined to a distant leaf. Whole-organism defense requires both local responsiveness and coordination across tissues.

The plant cell wall provides another challenge for recognition. It must remain sufficiently permeable to allow exchange of water, dissolved nutrients and signals, but it can also retard the movement of macromolecules and microorganisms. A microbial molecule detected at the plasma membrane has therefore encountered a physical environment that affects its transport and accessibility. A soluble peptide applied to a cut leaf disc may reach receptor-bearing cells differently from a bacterial flagellar filament on an intact waxy leaf. This is why purified elicitors are excellent for studying receptor signaling but must be interpreted alongside intact-plant experiments when evaluating the consequences of recognition during infection.

The history of plant immunity illustrates the transition from describing disease outcomes to examining their molecular causes. Early studies of cultivar-specific resistance established that plant genotype and pathogen genotype jointly determine compatibility. Subsequent observations that preparations of microbial material induced defense in the absence of living microbes demonstrated that discrete chemical signals could be sufficient. Genetic analysis of insensitive plants then allowed investigators to identify host components required for recognition. A single disease phenotype can arise through many mechanisms; measuring responsiveness to an isolated stimulus narrows the question to a more experimentally defined component of immunity.

This progression is reflected in the difference between a susceptible mutant and an elicitor-insensitive mutant. A susceptible mutant is identified by a disease phenotype under a specified challenge, while an elicitor-insensitive mutant is identified by failure of a particular response to a defined ligand. A mutant with defective cell walls, stomatal regulation, signal transduction or antimicrobial metabolism could belong to the first category. A mutant with loss of ligand perception belongs to the second but may not be susceptible to every pathogen. A genetic screen using an elicitor response is consequently useful for identifying components of a defined pathway without requiring a complete understanding of the complex infection process.

The experimental phenotype used for screening also matters. Seedling growth inhibition after prolonged exposure to flg22 is a useful immune-associated readout because the consequences of perception can be measured without continuously recording rapid biochemical changes. However, growth depends on many developmental processes. A seedling that fails to inhibit growth in response to flg22 may have a defect in perception, signal transmission or the coupling between immune signaling and growth. Testing ROS production, transcription and receptor function after the initial screen can separate these possibilities. The growth phenotype identifies candidates, not the complete molecular interpretation.

One cannot define PTI by a fixed list of mandatory outputs. Different plant organs express different sets of channels, receptors, transcription factors and metabolic enzymes. A guard cell can change the aperture of a pore, but a mesophyll cell cannot perform that same anatomical action. Both can possess surface immune receptors and activate biochemical defense mechanisms. An experimental result obtained in seedlings, isolated guard cells or mature leaves therefore has a specific physiological context. The common organizing principle is recognition through surface immune receptor systems, while individual outputs depend on the capacity of the responding cell.

2. Microbial patterns and endogenous danger signals

A microbial-associated molecular pattern, or MAMP, is a molecular feature characteristic of microorganisms that can stimulate host pattern recognition. The term pathogen-associated molecular pattern, or PAMP, is used historically for many of the same ligands. MAMP is often more precise because flagellin occurs in bacteria that are not pathogens of the plant. A plant receptor cannot directly determine the ecological intention of a bacterium from its flagellin. Its response is influenced by ligand accessibility, concentration, other signals, tissue context and mechanisms that regulate receptor activity.

Flagellin is a structural protein of the bacterial flagellar filament. In Arabidopsis, a conserved region within flagellin can be represented experimentally by a synthetic 22-residue peptide called flg22. The peptide is a useful elicitor because the plant can recognize it without exposure to live bacteria, bacterial multiplication or additional secreted compounds. It is not the complete flagellum and does not rotate. Recognition of flg22 demonstrates that a limited region of the microbial protein is sufficient to trigger selected responses. A purified peptide experiment therefore separates the molecular input from other aspects of infection, but it cannot reproduce every aspect of interaction with intact bacteria.

Elongation factor Tu is a bacterial protein involved in translation. The amino-terminal peptide elf18 can trigger immune responses in plants that possess an appropriate receptor, such as Arabidopsis EFR. The protein has an essential intracellular microbial function, but plant recognition requires an accessible form of the relevant molecular feature. The evolutionary constraint on a microbial protein does not mean every residue in its recognized region is immutable. Sequence variation, release, masking and modification can all affect detectability. An organism may retain protein function while becoming less readily recognized.

Chitin is a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine residues linked by beta-1,4 glycosidic bonds. It is a structural component of the walls of many fungi. Chitin-derived oligomers can be released or exposed during interactions with plants, including through the activity of plant chitinases. The oligomer is a carbohydrate rather than a peptide, so it is chemically distinct from flg22 and elf18. Binding depends on oligomer length and other structural properties, illustrating why pattern recognition is molecularly specific despite its broad defensive role.

A damage-associated molecular pattern, or DAMP, originates from the host rather than from the microorganism. Oligogalacturonides are fragments of pectic polysaccharide that can arise during cell-wall degradation. Extracellular ATP can signal damage when a normally intracellular metabolite becomes available in an extracellular compartment. Endogenous plant elicitor peptides, such as members of the Pep family, can stimulate signaling through PEPR receptors. Damage signals may reinforce a response initially triggered by microbial patterns; they also explain why an immune-like response can occur after sterile injury. DAMP signaling is not proof that a living pathogen is present.

The concept of an elicitor refers to the ability of a substance to induce a measured response. An elicitor preparation may contain several molecular species, whereas a defined synthetic peptide permits more precise interpretation. A sterile solution of peptide and a suspension of viable bacteria are different treatments even if both provoke an oxidative response. To test whether the peptide sequence itself matters, an investigator can compare an active peptide with a sequence-altered peptide prepared and delivered under matching conditions. A solvent-only treatment controls for handling and vehicle effects. A response to the active ligand alone supports sequence-sensitive perception, although receptor involvement requires additional evidence.

The biological importance of pattern recognition extends beyond discrimination between fungal and bacterial molecules. A single microbial encounter can supply several elicitors, some of which converge on shared downstream signals. A mutation that removes one receptor may therefore abolish the response to its purified ligand while leaving substantial defense against a complex infection. Multiple receptors can compensate partly at the level of tissue resistance without being interchangeable at the level of ligand binding. This distinction is essential when reading receptor-mutant experiments.

Pattern availability, specificity and combinations of signals

Pattern recognition is often described as recognition of conserved microbial structures. Conservation should be interpreted as a tendency for certain molecular regions to retain features required for biological function over evolutionary time. A conserved structure may be an attractive target for a host receptor because a microorganism may pay a fitness cost if it loses the relevant function. However, the cost depends on the environment. A bacterium that does not need motility in a particular niche may reduce expression of flagellar components, and a functionally tolerated sequence substitution may change recognition without eliminating the protein's normal activity. Thus conserved patterns are relatively reliable signals, not immutable molecular signatures.

An additional distinction is between the concentration of a pattern in a preparation and the amount available to a receptor. A bacterial suspension can contain flagellin incorporated into assembled filaments, released protein or soluble degradation products. The region recognized by FLS2 must be accessible in an appropriate molecular context. Equal numbers of two bacterial strains may yield different accessible ligand concentrations even if their flagellin proteins contain an identical recognized sequence. The host might then respond differently because the stimulus is presented differently rather than because its receptor has altered specificity.

This distinction suggests a simple experimental strategy. Prepare a defined peptide corresponding to each strain's recognized sequence and compare dose-response curves under identical plant conditions. If the purified peptides produce similar responses but intact bacteria produce different responses, differential presentation or other microbial factors become plausible explanations. If one peptide has substantially lower elicitor activity even when applied at the same free concentration, sequence-dependent recognition or receptor-complex assembly may differ. The result still needs controls for peptide integrity and availability; it cannot establish which individual amino acid mediates binding without additional substitutions or structural evidence.

The bacterial EF-Tu example emphasizes the value of independent perception routes. EF-Tu participates in delivering aminoacyl-tRNAs to the translating ribosome in bacteria. Recognition of its amino-terminal region by EFR does not imply that the host receptor participates in translation. The host detects a molecular feature derived from a microbial protein with another physiological function. The plant does not need to observe the protein operating inside a bacterial ribosome to recognize an accessible fragment of it. Similarly, flagellin recognition does not require the plant to measure the rotation rate of a flagellum. The molecular structure is the immediate stimulus.

Some plant species respond to particular epitopes that other species detect weakly or not at all. Differences can arise from presence or absence of the relevant receptor, receptor sequence, receptor expression, associated components and physiological context. A lack of responsiveness to elf18 in one species does not mean that species lacks surface innate immunity. It may have a different repertoire of recognizable microbial features. The breadth of a plant's immune perception reflects the ensemble of receptors rather than the behavior of one receptor in a model organism. A comparison across species should specify the ligand tested and the output measured.

Damage signals provide information that is complementary to microbial structures. A fragment of pectin is chemically related to a normal component of the wall, so its significance depends on altered structure or availability. Extracellular ATP illustrates a location-dependent signal: a metabolite necessary for intracellular energy transfer becomes informative when detected outside the cell. Neither signal has to be intrinsically toxic to trigger a response. A damage receptor provides a route for the cell to respond to a disturbance in the normal organization of its own molecules.

Plant elicitor peptides illustrate the possibility of amplification through endogenous signaling. A cell exposed to a microbial pattern can alter the production or processing of host-derived signals, which can influence nearby receptor-bearing cells. The signal generated by the host is not a second microbial ligand and does not need to bind the same receptor that initially sensed the microorganism. PEPR1 and PEPR2 in Arabidopsis provide a representative receptor system for endogenous Pep signals. These receptors use a distinct ligand-recognition route that can engage parts of the same downstream machinery as other surface immune receptors.

A combined exposure to a microbial elicitor and a host damage elicitor is therefore biologically plausible. If the combined response is larger than either response alone, this does not by itself prove synergistic interaction. For example, if untreated tissue produces 2 units of a marker, the microbial elicitor produces 8 and the damage elicitor produces 6, the absolute-response additive prediction is 8 + 6 − 2 = 12. A combined response of 12 would exceed both individual responses but fit the additive model. A combined response of 8 might reflect shared pathway saturation or negative interaction. A combined response of 18 would exceed the additive prediction, but additional experiments would be needed to identify the mechanism. The choice of response scale must be stated.

DAMP and MAMP treatments can also create experimental confounding. Cutting leaf discs can generate wound-associated signals before a microbial elicitor is applied. The untreated disc is therefore not identical to an untouched leaf. Resting discs under matched conditions and including a mock treatment help account for the shared effects of preparation. A plant genotype with altered wound signaling might show a different baseline even before addition of the microbial pattern. Comparing only the final signal would risk confusing baseline differences with changes in ligand responsiveness. The relevant contrast is often the change relative to each genotype's appropriately matched baseline.

3. Receptor architecture and molecular specificity

Pattern recognition receptors at the plant cell surface are membrane-associated proteins whose extracellular regions perceive ligands or participate in perception complexes. A receptor-like kinase, or RLK, generally contains an extracellular domain, a membrane-spanning segment and an intracellular protein kinase domain. The extracellular domain is exposed toward the apoplast and the catalytic domain is positioned toward the cytoplasm, where ATP and cytoplasmic substrates are available. This topology allows an extracellular stimulus to modify intracellular phosphorylation without requiring the ligand to cross the membrane.

The extracellular domain of FLS2 contains leucine-rich repeats, abbreviated LRRs. Repetition of a structural motif creates an extended surface suitable for molecular interactions. The function of an LRR is not determined simply by the presence of leucine. The arrangement of residues within the folded domain determines the interaction surface. Different LRR receptors recognize different ligands. FLS2 is not interchangeable with the brassinosteroid receptor BRI1 merely because both possess extracellular LRRs and intracellular kinase domains.

A receptor-like protein, or RLP, resembles a cell-surface receptor but lacks a conventional intracellular kinase domain. Such a receptor can still initiate signaling by associating with kinase-containing partners. In several immune LRR-RLP complexes, SOBIR1 supplies a constitutively associated kinase and a SERK-family co-receptor such as BAK1 participates after elicitor perception. The exact architecture depends on the receptor family. A short cytoplasmic tail therefore does not mean that a receptor is incapable of communicating with intracellular enzymes. It means that signal transduction must be achieved using an appropriate interaction partner.

A receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase, or RLCK, is a kinase related to RLKs but lacks the extracellular ligand-binding receptor organization. BIK1 is a well-studied example associated with immune receptor complexes at the inner face of the plasma membrane. Its function differs from that of FLS2: BIK1 helps transfer and distribute the signal inside the cell rather than recognizing extracellular flg22 as the principal binding receptor. It can be involved downstream of multiple inputs, which explains why its mutation may alter more than one elicitor response.

A co-receptor participates in productive signaling alongside a ligand-selective receptor. It need not possess the same ligand specificity. BAK1, also known as SERK3, contributes to several surface receptor systems, including development and immunity. Shared use of BAK1 does not imply that brassinosteroid and flg22 are interchangeable signals. The composition, location and phosphorylation state of the activated complex influence downstream outputs. A strong phenotype of a co-receptor mutant may reflect several disrupted pathways, including changes in development that complicate interpretation of immune assays.

A receptor's ligand-binding capacity and its signaling capacity can be distinguished experimentally. A protein may retain a correctly folded extracellular region while losing catalytic activity because of a mutation in its kinase domain. Such a receptor could still bind a ligand but fail to transmit the signal normally. Conversely, a receptor with a defective extracellular interaction surface may retain kinase activity in a biochemical assay while failing to respond to ligand in intact cells. The interpretation requires a measurement relevant to each function: receptor abundance and localization, ligand binding, complex formation, phosphorylation and physiological output.

A simple equilibrium model provides intuition about ligand binding. For one class of noninteracting binding sites with ligand in excess, fractional occupancy is theta = L/(Kd + L), where L is free ligand concentration and Kd is the equilibrium dissociation constant in the same concentration units. If Kd equals 20 nM and L equals 5 nM, occupancy is 5/25 = 0.20. At 80 nM, occupancy is 80/100 = 0.80. These are illustrative values, not measured FLS2 constants. The concentration producing half-maximal physiological response need not equal Kd, because receptor reserve, amplification, feedback, peptide degradation and co-receptor recruitment can alter the relationship between binding and output. Occupancy is a molecular probability, whereas response amplitude measures a downstream event.

Binding, activation and receptor-complex regulation

Receptor specificity arises because a ligand must form energetically favorable contacts with a suitable molecular surface. A peptide contains a sequence of amino acids whose side chains differ in charge, size, polarity and hydrophobicity. A carbohydrate oligomer displays hydroxyl, acetyl and other groups in a defined spatial arrangement. Receptor proteins recognize these features through combinations of noncovalent interactions. No single chemical force explains all specificity. A substitution that removes one favorable interaction can reduce binding or alter the arrangement needed for co-receptor recruitment without necessarily preventing every ligand association.

A ligand-binding experiment asks a question different from a physiological dose-response experiment. In a binding assay, one seeks evidence that a particular molecular species associates with a receptor under specified conditions. In a physiological assay, one measures a downstream outcome such as growth inhibition, calcium elevation or gene expression. A receptor can bind a ligand yet generate a weak response if the ligand does not support productive signaling. Conversely, a strong response can occur at relatively low occupancy if downstream amplification is efficient. Consequently, an EC50 for a biological response is not automatically a measurement of binding affinity.

To understand the distinction quantitatively, consider the reversible association R + L ⇌ RL. At equilibrium, Kd equals the product of the free receptor and free ligand concentrations divided by the concentration of receptor–ligand complex. The total receptor concentration is the sum of free receptor and complex, assuming no other receptor states. Rearranging the equilibrium expression produces the single-site occupancy relationship introduced above. The derivation assumes equilibrium and a defined stoichiometry. In a membrane receptor system with multiple complexes, a changing ligand pool and subsequent trafficking, the equation serves as a simplified starting model rather than an exhaustive kinetic description.

Binding kinetics add another dimension. For a simple one-step interaction, the association rate depends on free receptor, free ligand and an association rate constant with units of inverse concentration per time. The dissociation rate depends on complex concentration and a dissociation rate constant with units of inverse time. Their ratio is the equilibrium Kd under the simple model. Two receptor–ligand pairs can have the same equilibrium affinity but different association and dissociation rates. During a brief ligand exposure, the faster association may produce a different transient response. After ligand removal, a longer complex lifetime may prolong occupancy, although signaling can still terminate through other mechanisms. This explains why equilibrium and time-course assays answer complementary questions.

The number of surface receptors can also influence response. A plant expressing half as many receptor molecules may still achieve near-normal output at saturating ligand concentration if receptor abundance exceeds what is needed to activate the downstream machinery. At lower ligand concentration, the difference may become clearer. Alternatively, the response may be highly sensitive to receptor number if receptor complexes are limiting. A single saturating dose is therefore not always the most discriminating treatment when comparing receptor variants. Dose-response measurements and surface-localization data provide additional information.

The structural term kinase domain refers to a folded region that binds ATP and catalyzes transfer of a phosphoryl group to protein substrates. A kinase can phosphorylate itself or another protein in a receptor complex. Autophosphorylation need not always mean that a molecule modifies its own residue within the same polypeptide; intermolecular phosphorylation between two molecules of the same kinase is also possible. Transphosphorylation between different receptor complex members provides a mechanism by which the state of one protein changes the activity of another. Exam questions often distinguish these processes by manipulating catalytic activity in a mixed complex.

If an inactive kinase retains its ability to associate with a receptor partner, association and catalytic function can be experimentally separated. A result showing complex assembly in the presence of an inactive kinase demonstrates that catalysis is not necessary for that measured association under the assay conditions. It does not prove that the resulting complex can signal normally. Similarly, a failure to detect phosphorylation after association might reflect missing catalytic activity, incorrect substrate positioning or phosphatase action. The most informative experiments measure association and catalytic output independently.

Receptor regulation protects the plant from inappropriate activation. The receptor population available at the surface is influenced by synthesis, delivery to the plasma membrane, internalization and turnover. Negative regulatory proteins can affect the phosphorylation state or stability of receptor complexes and associated kinases. A constitutively active immune signaling component may elevate basal defense output in the absence of an added elicitor, but it may also alter growth and development. A mutant with an elevated baseline and a smaller additional response is not necessarily immune-deficient. The absolute response, fold induction and physiological condition must all be considered.

Shared signaling components create the possibility of interactions between growth and defense. BAK1's involvement in brassinosteroid and immune receptor systems is an example of a component with more than one physiological role. The simple statement that pathways compete for a shared component may be plausible under particular conditions, but competition is not established just because the component is shared. Distinct pools, differing affinities, additional co-receptors and tissue-specific expression can affect the interaction. Demonstration of competition requires evidence that availability of the shared component limits one pathway when the other is activated.

4. Flagellin perception by FLS2 and BAK1

The flagellin response provides an experimentally tractable example of how plants discriminate extracellular information. The Arabidopsis gene FLS2 was identified through genetic analysis of flagellin sensitivity; the gene encodes an LRR receptor-like kinase. A plant lacking functional FLS2 responds poorly to the elicitor flg22 in characteristic assays, whereas responses mediated by other receptors need not disappear. This observation establishes that a particular plant gene is needed for the normal response to the peptide, rather than demonstrating that all microbial recognition depends on a single receptor.

The ligand-binding surface of FLS2 recognizes flg22 in the extracellular space. Ligand perception promotes assembly of a signaling complex containing BAK1. An informative feature of the receptor complex is that the peptide participates in forming a productive interface between receptor and co-receptor. Thus ligand occupancy and receptor association are mechanistically connected. The plasma membrane constrains the location of the receptor components and positions the cytoplasmic kinase domains for signaling. The extracellular peptide itself does not catalyze phosphorylation.

The receptor-complex discovery was supported by biochemical experiments showing rapid association of FLS2 and BAK1 after flagellin perception, as reported by Chinchilla and colleagues in 2007. To understand why this was significant, consider two models. In one, FLS2 acts entirely independently and BAK1 affects immunity only through a distant developmental pathway. In the other, BAK1 physically participates in the activated FLS2 complex. A ligand-dependent increase in recovery of BAK1 with FLS2 in a co-immunoprecipitation assay supports the second model. Comparing untreated and elicitor-treated samples is crucial. Input controls showing that both proteins are present rule out the trivial explanation that an absent immunoprecipitated signal simply reflects lack of protein expression.

Co-immunoprecipitation identifies proteins that are associated under the assay conditions, not necessarily a direct contact between every pair of recovered molecules. A bridging protein can produce the same result. Structural and biochemical evidence complements genetic and association experiments in establishing the receptor architecture. An exam question asking whether co-immunoprecipitation alone proves direct ligand binding should therefore be answered negatively, even when the association is biologically meaningful.

On the cytoplasmic side, the active receptor complex undergoes rapid phosphorylation. A protein kinase transfers the terminal phosphate group of ATP onto suitable residues of a substrate. Phosphorylation can change the catalytic activity, conformation, localization or interaction partners of a signaling protein. The reaction is reversible through phosphatases. Signal initiation therefore depends on a change in the balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation rather than on the mere presence of kinase protein.

BIK1 and related cytoplasmic kinases are associated with surface immune receptor systems. Upon activation, phosphorylation and rearrangement of these complexes allow intracellular substrates to be regulated. BIK1 can phosphorylate regulatory sites on RBOHD, a membrane NADPH oxidase. This connection explains one rapid route from flagellin perception to extracellular reactive oxygen production. It does not imply that the entire immune network is a single linear FLS2–BAK1–BIK1–RBOHD chain. The receptor complex also connects to calcium entry, other cytoplasmic kinases and MAP kinase pathways.

Imagine a fls2 mutant in which introduction of a functional FLS2 transgene restores flg22-dependent oxidative response. This complementation strengthens the conclusion that the original defect was caused by loss of FLS2 function. Restoration of response to one ligand need not alter responsiveness to chitin if the chitin perception pathway was intact. Expression of a kinase-inactive FLS2 version would provide a further test, provided its expression, membrane localization and stability are verified. A failure of that version to rescue signaling could otherwise reflect misfolding or absence of receptor rather than loss of catalysis.

Response termination is as important as initiation. Receptor phosphorylation states can be reversed, associated components can be modified or degraded, and receptor trafficking can alter surface availability. Ligand can be degraded or removed from the local environment. The decline of a measured output can occur despite ongoing receptor occupancy because downstream pathways adapt. A second elicitor pulse may produce a different response depending on which negative regulatory mechanisms are active. It is not appropriate to infer the exact half-life of receptor complexes from the duration of an oxidative burst alone.

Genetic and biochemical evidence for the flagellin pathway

The early Arabidopsis flagellin studies used natural variation and mutants to identify a heritable determinant of sensitivity. A defined peptide response made it possible to compare plant genotypes without variations in pathogen multiplication. In a simplified genetic model, suppose a functional receptor allele F confers sensitivity and an insensitive allele f is recessive. Crossing FF with ff produces sensitive Ff offspring. Self-fertilization of Ff gives expected genotypes FF, Ff and ff in proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter. If one functional allele is sufficient and the assay reliably distinguishes phenotypes, the expected sensitivity ratio is three to one. The ratio indicates a genetic contribution consistent with one segregating locus; it does not itself identify the receptor protein.

For an illustrative sample of 160 segregating seedlings, this model predicts 120 sensitive and 40 insensitive plants. If the observed numbers are 116 and 44, the chi-square statistic is (116−120)²/120 + (44−40)²/40 = 0.133 + 0.400 = 0.533, approximately. With one degree of freedom under the stated two-class model and adequate expected counts, that small departure is compatible with sampling variation. A large departure could reflect incorrect genetic assumptions, viability effects, imperfect phenotype classification or additional loci. These numbers are a worked teaching example rather than a reproduction of the historical experiment. The genetic inference remains distinct from biochemical proof of receptor function.

Gómez-Gómez and Boller reported the FLS2 receptor-like kinase in 2000. Identification of an RLK associated with flagellin sensitivity provided a molecular explanation for the genetic phenotype. Subsequent work examined flg22 association and the capacity of FLS2 to determine ligand recognition specificity. A useful logical progression is from a segregating phenotype to a candidate gene, then from a gene to receptor protein function. Each stage eliminates a different set of alternative explanations. Mapping and complementation establish genetic involvement, whereas binding and transfer of responsiveness provide evidence about the recognition step.

Consider receptor transfer into a recipient cell that does not normally respond to a particular peptide. If expression of the candidate receptor creates a ligand-dependent response and the appropriate control remains insensitive, the candidate can confer responsiveness in the tested cellular background. This result is stronger than a simple correlation between receptor abundance and responsiveness. However, it does not mean the transferred receptor functions entirely alone. The recipient already contains ATP, membranes, kinases, ion channels and other components that may support the newly introduced receptor. Sufficiency is therefore conditional on the background used.

An experiment comparing ligand association in membrane preparations from wild type, receptor-deficient plants and complemented plants can strengthen the ligand-recognition model. Reduced specific association in the mutant and restoration in complemented tissue are consistent with receptor dependence. To distinguish specific association from nonspecific adsorption to membrane material, excess unlabeled ligand can be used as a competitor in a binding assay. A structurally unrelated peptide provides a further comparison. The principle is that specific sites have limited capacity and recognize particular molecular features, whereas nonspecific association may not show the same competition behavior.

The FLS2–BAK1 model can be examined by considering four conditions: presence or absence of ligand and presence or absence of a functional co-receptor. A ligand-dependent increase in receptor association in wild type supports activation-dependent assembly. A mutant with impaired co-receptor function might retain some primary ligand binding but have reduced receptor-complex signaling. Because related SERK proteins can have overlapping functions, a single mutant may produce a partial phenotype rather than complete absence of every output. A partial response is not proof that the proposed co-receptor is irrelevant. The possibility of redundancy needs to be assessed genetically and biochemically.

Lu and colleagues reported in 2010 that BIK1 associates with the flagellin receptor complex and participates in downstream signaling. The experimental distinction between receptor association and phosphorylation is valuable. An associated protein may be present before ligand application but change its phosphorylation state after stimulation. Detection of an altered phosphorylation-dependent mobility or other phosphorylation-sensitive signal provides evidence that the associated component changes state. A kinase-inactive allele, a receptor mutant or biochemical phosphorylation assay can help determine which catalytic activity contributes to the change.

A receptor-associated kinase may have different effects on different biological outputs. The fact that BIK1 contributes to flg22-induced signaling does not imply that a bik1 mutant is uniformly susceptible to every pathogen. The original BIK1 studies and later work illustrate that altered hormone and defense networks can influence disease phenotypes in ways that are not predicted from one elicitor assay. This is not a contradiction of the receptor-associated role. Rather, it illustrates that signaling specificity and disease outcome operate at different levels. An examination question should not ask students to infer universal susceptibility from a reduction in one rapid output.

Another informative experiment compares receptor-dependent responses with a downstream perturbation. Suppose a receptor mutant cannot respond to flg22, but a treatment that activates a downstream component by a separate route still induces defense gene expression. This would indicate that at least part of the output machinery remains functional. Such a bypass experiment is useful when the bypass is well characterized. However, a broadly acting treatment might activate several parallel pathways; rescue of the final output would not prove an exact linear ordering of every intermediate. A rigorous causal model combines the bypass result with specific interaction and biochemical evidence.

5. LysM receptors and fungal chitin perception

A fungus and a bacterium differ substantially in cell architecture, so the host needs more than a flagellin receptor to detect microbial structures. LysM, or lysin motif, is a small carbohydrate-binding domain present in a number of plant receptor proteins. Several LysM-containing surface proteins contribute to recognition of chitin-derived oligomers. Their use illustrates how a different extracellular binding architecture can be connected to downstream immune responses that overlap with those induced by peptide ligands.

In Arabidopsis, LYK5 contributes strongly to chitin perception, acting with CERK1, a LysM receptor-like kinase with an active intracellular kinase domain. LYK4 can also contribute to recognition. CERK1 has roles in productive receptor assembly and intracellular signal transmission. In rice, the chitin-binding receptor-like protein CEBiP acts with OsCERK1. The two examples share a division of labor between carbohydrate recognition and kinase-dependent signaling, but the individual proteins and receptor arrangements are not identical. A student should therefore specify the organism when describing the receptor complex rather than assigning rice receptor names directly to Arabidopsis.

Chitin recognition depends on the molecular properties of oligomers. Short and long fragments are not necessarily equivalent at a fixed molar concentration. A long fragment may provide interaction surfaces favorable for assembly of a receptor complex, whereas a short fragment may bind differently or fail to promote productive assembly. The total mass of carbohydrate supplied also differs if equal molar amounts of different lengths are used. When analyzing an experiment, determine whether concentration refers to oligomer molecules, monomer equivalents or mass. Without that information, a quantitative comparison of elicitor potency can be ambiguous.

CERK1-associated cytoplasmic kinases transmit signals to downstream components. PBL27 provides an experimentally supported example of a route connecting the chitin perception complex to MAP kinase activation in Arabidopsis. The broader principle is that surface immune complexes recruit particular intracellular kinases, some shared and others preferentially associated with specific inputs. This arrangement permits convergence in certain responses and divergence in others. A CERK1 mutant may lose a major chitin response yet preserve substantial flg22 responsiveness because FLS2 and its associated partners still operate.

Chitin also participates in an instructive comparison with microbial signals during beneficial plant interactions. LysM-containing receptors recognize various N-acetylglucosamine-containing molecules, including signaling compounds involved in symbiosis. The presence of a LysM domain does not identify a receptor as an immune receptor without further evidence. Ligand modification, receptor composition and host context can route chemically related inputs toward different developmental or defensive outcomes. The detailed symbiotic signaling pathway belongs with nitrogen fixation, but the distinction prevents an incorrect generalization about carbohydrate recognition.

To distinguish the receptor families, a factorial experiment can expose wild-type, fls2 and cerk1 plants to both flg22 and a defined chitin oligomer. If fls2 loses flg22 response while retaining chitin response and cerk1 shows the complementary pattern, the experiment supports distinct perception routes. If a fourth genotype loses both, it may disrupt a shared downstream component, a co-receptor used by multiple routes or general cellular competence. Measuring tissue viability, baseline signals and a response outside these pathways helps separate the alternatives. A cross-elicitor assay is considerably more informative than a single measurement from one mutant after one stimulus.

Evidence for carbohydrate recognition and overlapping receptor functions

The early chitin-receptor investigations provide a useful example of how two model species can solve a similar biological problem with different molecular arrangements. Rice CEBiP was identified as a membrane-associated chitin-binding protein lacking a conventional intracellular kinase domain. Its architecture immediately raises a mechanistic question: how can binding outside the cell alter intracellular phosphorylation? The association with OsCERK1 provides a solution in which the receptor-like protein and kinase-containing partner cooperate. Evidence from reducing expression of one component and from ligand-dependent complex analysis supports the involvement of both proteins.

Arabidopsis genetic studies identified CERK1 as a crucial component of chitin signaling. Subsequent analysis established important roles for LYK5 and the overlapping function of LYK4. The sequence of discoveries teaches why scientific models may become more complete without earlier findings necessarily being false. An early study can establish that a kinase is required for a response, while later work identifies a major ligand-binding partner. Necessity for signal transmission is not equivalent to being the only molecule involved in perception. Both types of evidence are needed to describe a multiprotein receptor system.

Cao and colleagues reported that Arabidopsis LYK5 forms a chitin-induced complex with CERK1 and that LYK4 has overlapping roles. In a hypothetical comparison, a lyk5 single mutant might retain a reduced response while a lyk4 lyk5 double mutant has a more severe defect. This pattern would be consistent with partially redundant receptor functions. Redundancy does not mean that the two proteins must have identical expression, binding properties or contributions in all tissues. It means that the loss of one can be compensated to some extent by the presence of the other for the tested output.

Double-mutant experiments can distinguish several genetic relationships. If a mutation in either gene alone completely abolishes a particular response, the double mutant may add little information about whether the genes act at different steps in the same required process. If each single mutant retains part of the response and the double mutant loses much more, the genes may have partially overlapping contributions or participate in parallel inputs. If the double mutant restores a phenotype lost in one single mutant, antagonistic regulation or compensatory effects become possible. The interpretation is not determined by the word double mutant; it depends on the actual pattern of quantitative phenotypes and additional mechanistic evidence.

Ligand length provides a physical basis for productive assembly. An oligomer must fit the binding surfaces and may help orient receptor components relative to each other. A very short fragment may contain the same monosaccharide type but lack the required spatial arrangement. To understand the difference between mass and molarity, assume a solution contains 80 micromolar monomer equivalents of an eight-residue oligomer. If every oligomer has exactly eight residues, the concentration of oligomer molecules is 10 micromolar. A preparation of four-residue oligomers with the same monomer equivalents contains 20 micromolar oligomer molecules. These preparations deliver equal residue amounts but different numbers and lengths of ligand molecules. Receptor activation cannot be predicted solely from total carbohydrate mass.

A useful conceptual comparison is between a fungal chitin oligomer and a bacterial peptidoglycan fragment. Both may contain N-acetylglucosamine-related structural features, but peptidoglycan also contains N-acetylmuramic acid and peptide cross-links in its characteristic architecture. The fact that receptor proteins possess carbohydrate-binding motifs does not justify treating these molecules as identical. Some plant LysM-related proteins participate in responses to bacterial peptidoglycan, and CERK1 can contribute to more than one ligand-perception system. The specific ligand and participating receptor proteins should be stated. The principal exam-level distinction is between the chemical identity of the pattern and the components necessary for its perception.

Fungal structures are not equally accessible throughout growth. Chitin may be buried beneath other wall components or altered by wall remodeling. Chitin-binding microbial proteins can influence availability of fragments to the plant. Consequently, the ability of an isolated chitin oligomer to trigger PTI does not prove that all fungi expose it to the same degree in a natural interaction. A purified elicitor bypasses part of the availability problem. A plant that responds normally to added chitin but poorly to a particular fungus might be encountering a pathogen that limits pattern exposure or suppresses downstream defense.

The PBL27 connection demonstrates that receptor systems can have preferential downstream partners. In Arabidopsis, studies of CERK1-associated kinase signaling link chitin perception to MAPK activation through PBL27. A mutation in a preferential partner may weaken one elicitor-induced output more strongly than another. A general schematic of immune signaling must therefore preserve both convergence and receptor-specific branches. Replacing all receptor-associated cytoplasmic kinases with a single generic node can be a useful first approximation, but it conceals the genetic predictions that distinguish receptor systems in advanced questions.

6. Calcium, phosphorylation and reactive oxygen

The earliest responses to surface perception often occur within seconds or minutes, before the cell can synthesize large quantities of new defense proteins. A central principle is the regulation of existing molecules. Ion channels can open or close, enzymes can be phosphorylated and local redox chemistry can change. These events generate signals that branch toward several later outputs. The rapid response is possible because the cell maintains ion gradients, ATP, reducing equivalents and signaling proteins before contact with a microbe.

Calcium entry changes the concentration of free cytosolic calcium. The resting concentration is maintained low compared with several extracellular and intracellular pools by transport and buffering. Opening a calcium-permeable channel therefore allows rapid movement down a favorable electrochemical gradient. The concentration change depends not only on entry but also on extrusion, sequestration and binding. A fluorescent calcium indicator reports a particular measurable property of the calcium signal, rather than the total amount of calcium in the tissue. Different elicitors and cell types can display different time courses.

Calcium-binding proteins convert the ion signal into biochemical action. Calcium-dependent protein kinases, or CPKs, combine a calcium-responsive regulatory region and a kinase domain, allowing calcium binding to influence phosphorylation of substrates. Calmodulin is a different type of calcium sensor that regulates other proteins through interactions rather than functioning as a kinase itself. The specificity of calcium action depends on the accessible targets, spatial proximity and characteristics of the calcium transient. The same ion can participate in immune signaling and drought responses without making the two pathways identical.

The NADPH oxidase RBOHD transfers electrons from cytosolic NADPH through the plasma membrane to molecular oxygen on the extracellular side. Oxygen gains an electron to form superoxide, which can undergo spontaneous or enzyme-assisted dismutation to hydrogen peroxide. Thus the site of NADPH consumption and the initial site of superoxide production lie on opposite sides of the membrane. This arrangement is especially suited to interactions in the apoplast, where microbes and cell-wall components are located.

The simplified reaction for dismutation is 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Two superoxide radicals are required per hydrogen peroxide molecule produced by this reaction. RBOHD does not directly convert two molecules of water to hydrogen peroxide, nor does hydrogen peroxide have the same chemical reactivity as a hydroxyl radical. The redox reactions and antioxidant systems introduced in the oxidative-stress chapter provide the chemical basis for interpreting immune-associated ROS.

RBOHD is regulated by several inputs. Its amino-terminal region contains calcium-binding EF-hand motifs, and regulatory phosphorylation by kinases can influence its activity. Kadota and colleagues reported direct phosphorylation of RBOHD by BIK1, connecting a receptor-associated kinase to the oxidative burst. Calcium-dependent phosphorylation can affect other sites. These mechanisms can cooperate: receptor signaling and calcium signals influence oxidase activity rather than necessarily representing successive indispensable links in one chain.

ROS can in turn affect calcium-permeable signaling pathways. Feedback can amplify or extend signaling, and removal of one component may alter a later phase without abolishing the first event. Consider an illustrative calcium trace with an early peak and a later smaller peak. If a mutant lacking RBOHD retains the first peak but loses the second, the result supports an upstream contribution to the first peak and a possible redox-dependent feedback route to the second. It does not demonstrate that RBOHD is a calcium channel. Other evidence is required to identify the actual channel and intermediate signals.

The oxidative burst describes a rapid increase in ROS production or measured ROS-associated signal. A luminol-based assay is commonly used to follow extracellular oxidative responses in leaf tissue. Light emission depends on the reaction chemistry, including detection reagents and peroxidase activity, and is not automatically an absolute molar concentration. If a mutant changes peroxidase activity, the light signal may differ even with similar hydrogen peroxide availability. Appropriate controls and an independent readout can strengthen the interpretation.

A simple mass balance clarifies the difference between production and measured abundance. Let H be an effective extracellular hydrogen peroxide concentration, P the production rate in micromolar per minute, and k a first-order removal constant in inverse minutes. Then dH/dt = P − kH. With constant P = 2 micromolar per minute and k = 0.5 per minute, the model predicts a steady concentration of 4 micromolar. Doubling both P and k leaves the steady concentration at 4 micromolar but doubles production and removal fluxes. The model ignores spatial diffusion, changing enzyme activity and nonlinear removal; it is a teaching model, not a fit to published data. Its implication is that a single concentration cannot independently establish the rate of ROS synthesis.

Interpreting rapid signaling as a dynamic network

An ion flux has both a direction and an electrical consequence. Calcium moving into a plant cell carries positive charge and can contribute to changes in membrane potential, while the cell also adjusts other ion conductances. The membrane voltage is determined by the combined currents of many transport proteins, not by calcium concentration alone. A measured depolarization after elicitor addition is therefore evidence of altered electrical behavior but not proof that a particular calcium channel is the sole cause. Ion-specific reporters, electrophysiological measurements and channel mutants can provide complementary evidence.

The thermodynamic driving force for calcium entry is often large because the cytosolic free concentration is low and the cell interior is electrically negative. For example, at 298 K a hypothetical external free calcium concentration of 1 mM and internal free concentration of 0.1 micromolar give a concentration ratio outside to inside of 10,000. The Nernst potential is (RT/2F) ln(10,000), approximately +0.118 V using R = 8.314 joules per mole per kelvin and F = 96,485 coulombs per mole. If the actual membrane potential is −0.100 V, opening a calcium-selective pathway favors inward calcium movement. The example assumes concentration approximates activity and does not describe the reversal potential of a channel permeable to several ions.

The large gradient enables a transient response with relatively small movement of calcium compared with total cellular calcium stores. Calcium pumps and exchangers then contribute to recovery. A prolonged elevation could arise from prolonged entry, reduced removal or altered buffering. Conversely, a small observed signal could occur despite substantial entry if sequestration increases. This is why a calcium reporter and an ion current measurement need not display identical time courses. Each measures a different property of the system.

RBOHD activity depends on access to oxygen and reducing equivalents as well as regulatory state. NADPH supplies electrons and the membrane-spanning oxidase transfers them to oxygen outside the cell. The process couples metabolism to immune signaling: a cell must provide substrate and maintain physiological conditions for enzyme function. Because NADPH participates in many cellular reactions, a global change in NADPH metabolism could affect the oxidative burst without directly changing receptor activation. If tissue oxygen availability differs between experimental conditions, the enzyme's substrate environment may differ as well. Matched conditions are necessary for comparisons.

The chemical products of the oxidative burst are related but not equivalent. Superoxide is charged and its mobility is constrained by the local chemical environment. Hydrogen peroxide is more stable than many radical species and can participate in signaling across cellular boundaries through appropriate routes. Reactive oxygen species can modify proteins, influence wall-associated processes and change the availability of other signaling intermediates. The biological consequence depends on location, concentration, duration and the capacity of antioxidant systems. A controlled burst in the apoplast differs from sustained, widespread oxidative injury.

One electron transferred from NADPH ultimately reduces one oxygen molecule to superoxide. NADPH is a two-electron donor, so in an idealized accounting one molecule of NADPH can provide two electrons for formation of two superoxide radicals. Dismutation of those radicals produces one hydrogen peroxide molecule and one oxygen molecule. This stoichiometric reasoning does not imply that every NADPH molecule consumed in living tissue generates exactly one measured extracellular hydrogen peroxide molecule, because competing redox reactions, diffusion and scavenging intervene. It identifies the maximum simple stoichiometric relation for the specified sequence of reactions.

A critical experiment on RBOHD regulation asks whether receptor-associated phosphorylation directly changes oxidase activity. Demonstration that BIK1 can phosphorylate regulatory residues in an appropriate biochemical assay supports a direct biochemical relationship. The physiological relevance can be tested using oxidase variants in which selected phosphorylation sites are altered, provided the protein is still expressed and localized normally. If such a variant supports weaker elicitor-induced ROS despite retaining other properties, the sites contribute to the physiological response. Substituting residues can also alter protein structure, so interpretation benefits from multiple site-specific and functional controls.

An important feature of the published BIK1–RBOHD work is that receptor-associated phosphorylation and calcium-dependent regulation can act at distinct regulatory residues. This is more informative than a model in which BIK1 affects the oxidase only by first increasing calcium. A calcium-independent phosphorylation event can occur while the enzyme's final physiological output still depends on calcium binding or calcium-dependent kinases. The distinction between independence of one biochemical reaction and independence of the complete pathway is often tested in analytical questions. An inhibitor that removes the calcium signal need not eliminate every receptor-associated phosphorylation event.

Feedback in this system explains how signals can differ in duration and amplitude. Suppose receptor activation generates a small calcium increase, which promotes RBOHD activity. ROS then increases the probability of further calcium signaling. The second step can reinforce the first, creating a positive feedback loop. Such a loop need not be indefinitely self-sustaining because substrate availability, phosphatases, antioxidant activity and ion removal oppose amplification. In a living cell, the balance of positive and negative influences can produce a transient peak rather than runaway activity. The timing of the measured signal reflects the whole network.

A simplified quantitative comparison can help distinguish rate from amplitude. Imagine that extracellular hydrogen peroxide excess is 6 micromolar immediately after production ceases and falls to 1.5 micromolar six minutes later. Under a first-order removal model, two halvings occur in six minutes and the half-life is three minutes. The effective rate constant is ln(2)/3, about 0.231 per minute. If a second genotype has a half-life of six minutes under the same assumptions, its removal constant is about 0.116 per minute. A larger response in that genotype could therefore result from slower removal rather than stronger production. These hypothetical values are not experimental measurements of immune ROS.

The choice of normalization affects comparisons of rapid responses. A larger leaf disc may produce a larger absolute luminescence signal simply because it contains more responsive tissue. Normalizing by area can help when comparing discs, but differences in thickness, cell number and viable tissue fraction can remain. Dividing a stimulated signal by a nearly zero baseline can produce a very large and unstable fold change. Reporting the baseline, absolute change and integrated response using consistent measurement conditions provides a fuller description. Statistical comparison should respect the independent biological unit rather than treating every time point as a separate plant.

7. MAP kinase cascades and transcription

A kinase cascade transfers a signal through a hierarchy of protein kinases. The conventional MAP kinase module includes a MAP kinase kinase kinase, a MAP kinase kinase and a MAP kinase. Each activated tier phosphorylates the next under appropriate conditions. In plants, immune inputs can activate modules containing MKK4 or MKK5 and MPK3 or MPK6, among other combinations. The exact upstream components and downstream substrates can differ with the receptor, stimulus and experimental context.

The repeated catalytic steps can amplify a signal. One activated kinase molecule can phosphorylate multiple substrate molecules over its active lifetime. Amplification is nevertheless limited by substrate abundance, ATP availability, phosphatases, localization and competing interactions. An arrow between two kinases represents a regulatory or catalytic relationship, not an increase of one fixed factor. A cascade is not automatically more active merely because more kinase protein is present. Phosphorylation status and catalytic competence are often more relevant to a short-term response.

A useful way to measure activation is to compare a phosphorylation-sensitive immunoblot with a measurement of total kinase. In an illustrative experiment, untreated tissue has a phospho-MPK band of 12 arbitrary units and a total MPK band of 60. Treated tissue has a phospho band of 36 and a total band of 90. If both assays are in a linear detection range, the normalized signal changes from 0.20 to 0.40, a twofold increase. The raw phospho band increased threefold, but that number does not account for the change in total MPK. The normalized ratio is an assay proxy, not an exact molecular fraction unless calibrated appropriately.

Activated kinases affect the activity of numerous substrates, including regulatory proteins involved in gene expression. WRKY transcription factors participate prominently in plant immune networks. A transcription factor is a protein that influences gene transcription by interacting with regulatory DNA and other proteins. WRKY proteins recognize characteristic DNA sequence motifs, commonly W-box elements, but not every family member acts as a positive regulator of every defense gene. Some restrict or reshape immune outputs. A change in the expression of a WRKY gene is distinct from a change in the activity of its encoded protein.

Transcriptional responses can begin within minutes of elicitor treatment and can alter the production of enzymes, transport proteins and regulators over longer periods. Commonly used marker genes include FRK1 in Arabidopsis, although no single marker establishes all aspects of PTI. A gene may respond to more than one stimulus or be affected by developmental state. The choice of marker should match the proposed hypothesis. Defense-associated gene induction does not by itself demonstrate direct DNA binding by a proposed regulator.

Relative transcript abundance can be assessed using quantitative PCR. Under the approximation of equal near-perfect doubling efficiencies, the relative abundance between treatment and control is 2 to the power minus delta-delta Ct. If control target Ct is 27 and reference Ct is 21, delta Ct is 6. If treated target Ct is 23 and reference Ct is 20, delta Ct is 3. Delta-delta Ct is minus 3, yielding an eightfold relative increase. Directly subtracting target Ct values would instead give an apparent sixteenfold change, ignoring the reference gene. The example assumes stable reference expression and appropriate amplification efficiencies. Transcript abundance is not equivalent to protein activity or resistance.

Branching makes pathway order an experimental question. If loss of RBOHD abolishes a particular ROS output but leaves substantial MAP kinase phosphorylation, the results are consistent with separable contributions from the surface receptor network. Conversely, changes in redox signaling can influence MAP kinase responses in particular contexts. It is unsafe to conclude either that ROS is always upstream of MAPKs or that ROS can never influence MAPKs. A causal conclusion should be tied to the measured output, genotype, stimulus and time interval.

From short-lived kinase activation to sustained defense output

A protein phosphorylation signal is a reversible molecular state. If K denotes a kinase substrate and Kp its phosphorylated form, a simplified description treats phosphorylation and dephosphorylation as opposing reactions. At steady state, the fraction phosphorylated reflects their relative effective rates. Suppose an effective first-order phosphorylation rate is 0.3 per minute and dephosphorylation rate is 0.1 per minute, with total substrate held constant. The simple two-state model predicts a phosphorylated fraction of 0.3/(0.3+0.1) = 0.75. If dephosphorylation increases to 0.3 per minute without a change in the phosphorylation rate, the fraction becomes 0.50. A decrease in phosphorylation does not automatically prove decreased kinase catalytic activity.

This model assumes a single effective phosphorylated state, constant rates and no changes in protein synthesis or degradation. MAP kinases typically require phosphorylation at specific residues in their activation loop, and an antibody may recognize a particular phosphorylation state. The two-state model simplifies this complexity. It is nevertheless useful for understanding how phosphatases can alter signal duration and amplitude. When interpreting an experiment, it is important to identify whether the measurement concerns protein quantity, modification state or actual catalytic activity.

The activation of a kinase cascade can change downstream transcription by modifying transcription factors, other regulators and components that influence hormone metabolism. Some transcription factors are already present before elicitation, so phosphorylation or changes in binding partners can rapidly modify their activity. Other regulatory proteins are synthesized in response to the first wave of gene expression and can control a later wave. The resulting transcriptional program is not expected to occur simultaneously for every gene. Early genes often include regulatory components, whereas later expression can include enzymes and structural defense proteins, although the timing varies with the stimulus and tissue.

A promoter reporter provides a way to investigate regulation at a specific gene. A promoter fragment can be linked to an easily measured reporter, such as a fluorescent or luminescent protein. If the reporter responds to flg22, the fragment contains sufficient regulatory information for responsiveness under the test conditions. Mutating a W-box and observing reduced induction supports a functional contribution of the sequence. However, it does not directly identify which WRKY protein binds the site in the intact plant, because many related regulators may recognize similar motifs. Binding assays and genetic perturbation of candidate regulators provide complementary evidence.

Reporter signal may also reflect events after transcription. If a fluorescent reporter is stable, accumulated fluorescence can persist after promoter activity declines. A transcript measurement at a given time may respond more rapidly than the abundance of a stable reporter protein. Conversely, protein modification may alter function without a corresponding immediate change in mRNA. Students interpreting time-course experiments should match the measurement to the molecular event. A late fluorescence maximum does not necessarily mean the promoter itself was activated late.

In quantitative PCR, a difference of one amplification cycle approximately corresponds to a twofold difference in initial template abundance when amplification efficiency is ideal. The assumption is not merely mathematical; it depends on the behavior of the amplification reaction. If treatment alters the abundance of the reference transcript, normalization can be misleading. A gene widely used as a reference in one experiment may be unsuitable under another stress or developmental condition. Checking reference stability and amplification behavior is part of drawing a reliable biological conclusion.

A comparison of two genes can illustrate why fold induction is not the same as absolute abundance. If gene A has a relative basal abundance of 1 unit and rises to 20 units, it shows twentyfold induction. If gene B starts at 100 units and rises to 200 units, it shows twofold induction but has a much higher final abundance on the illustrative common scale. The stronger relative change in gene A does not mean that its protein product is more abundant or more important. The importance of a regulated gene must be established using its function, localization and perturbation phenotype rather than fold change alone.

Transcriptional changes also help explain why pretreatment can alter later infection. A brief exposure to an elicitor may end before challenge with a microorganism, yet induced gene products or altered regulatory states may persist. Protection after pretreatment is therefore compatible with a lasting physiological effect of earlier receptor activation. It does not establish an animal-like acquired immune system. A comparison between elicitor-treated and mock-treated tissue, both challenged after the same interval, can distinguish an elicitor-associated protection effect from the effect of handling or time. The mechanism may involve multiple defense products and requires further tests.

8. Stomatal defense and cell-wall reinforcement

Stomata are pores bordered by two guard cells that regulate exchange of carbon dioxide and water vapor. They also provide potential access to the internal leaf environment. An open pore is useful for photosynthesis, but it may permit microbial entry. Guard cells therefore integrate physiological and immune information. Closing a pore during an appropriate microbial challenge reduces access through that route while potentially limiting carbon dioxide assimilation. The response represents a tradeoff rather than an unconditional improvement in fitness.

Melotto and colleagues reported that stomata participate actively in defense against bacterial invasion. Exposure to bacteria or defined elicitors could induce closure, and virulent strains of Pseudomonas syringae could counteract this response through coronatine. The significance of this finding was that stomata were no longer interpreted only as passive openings in the leaf surface. Recognition-dependent changes in guard-cell physiology contribute to an active defense barrier.

Guard-cell closure requires changes in solute content and turgor. Activation of anion efflux pathways can depolarize the plasma membrane, favor outward potassium movement under suitable conditions, and decrease the osmotic retention of water. Water moves out and guard-cell turgor falls, narrowing the pore. Immune-associated calcium and ROS signaling can contribute to this regulation, and the ABA pathway intersects with guard-cell responses. The complete molecular sequence depends on the stimulus and conditions; immune closure should not simply be identified with ABA-induced drought closure. Both converge on the mechanics of the same cell type.

The importance of stomatal defense can be evaluated by comparing surface inoculation with infiltration. Surface exposure requires bacteria to encounter the epidermal barrier before reaching the apoplast. Infiltration introduces bacteria into internal tissue, bypassing much of the entry step. If a mutant differs markedly from wild type after surface exposure but shows similar bacterial multiplication after infiltration, the evidence supports an entry-related contribution. The inference is stronger when inoculum, initial internal population, humidity, leaf age and the effects of the infiltration procedure are controlled. The result does not exclude every role for the mutated protein after entry.

Callose is a beta-1,3-glucan deposited at selected locations in plant cell walls. During defense, callose can accumulate near attempted sites of penetration and in other localized wall structures. It is distinct from cellulose, whose glucan chains are beta-1,4-linked, and from lignin, a complex phenolic polymer. These different materials affect wall properties through different chemical and structural mechanisms. Callose deposition can contribute to reinforcement or compartmentalization, but the amount of callose alone is not a universal measure of resistance.

Callose synthase PMR4/GSL5 is an important component of induced callose deposition in Arabidopsis. The significance of a callose-deficient mutant must be interpreted carefully because perturbing the wall can alter compensatory defense signaling. Reduced callose and enhanced resistance in a particular pathosystem would not establish that callose is generally harmful or that deposition never contributes to defense. Multiple outputs of the immune network can change simultaneously. A genetic rescue and measurement of additional defense pathways help distinguish direct and compensatory effects.

Callose is often visualized by fluorescence staining with aniline blue. Counting deposits per standardized area or quantifying stained area can compare treatments, but results depend on the tissue region, imaging settings and definition of a deposit. Measurements should sample comparable tissues and use biological replication. A larger count could reflect more sites of deposition, whereas a larger total area could reflect expansion of existing deposits. Neither measurement directly quantifies pathogen viability.

Defense transcription can increase production of antimicrobial proteins, enzymes and metabolites. Chitinases and glucanases can act on susceptible microbial wall components or release additional elicitors. Production of specialized defense metabolites depends on the plant species and the biosynthetic pathways available. These processes connect PTI to secondary metabolism and the barrier mechanisms covered in the previous node. They do not require every immune response to induce exactly the same suite of genes or chemicals.

Physiological evidence for entry control and local reinforcement

Stomatal aperture is determined by the mechanical properties of guard cells and their turgor relative to surrounding tissue. In many species, differential wall thickness and orientation of wall components help translate changes in turgor into changes in guard-cell shape. Opening depends on solute accumulation and water uptake; closure depends on changes that reduce guard-cell turgor. These mechanics are not immune-specific. Immune perception modifies the transport processes that control the pore, allowing a molecular stimulus at the surface to change the anatomical route available to microorganisms.

For a simplified guard-cell solute model, the solute potential is approximately −iCRT, where i is an effective number of particles produced per solute unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant in suitable units and T is absolute temperature. If an idealized intracellular solution loses enough osmotically active material to reduce effective solute concentration from 0.30 to 0.20 osmolar at 298 K, its solute potential becomes less negative by approximately 0.10 × 0.008314 × 298 = 0.248 MPa. This calculation isolates the solute component and assumes ideal behavior, constant temperature and the stated effective concentration change. The actual guard-cell water potential also includes pressure and other contributions, and cell volume changes during water movement. The example explains the direction of the osmotic effect rather than predicting a particular aperture.

Anion efflux and potassium transport are coupled through electrical and osmotic effects. Loss of negatively charged ions can depolarize the membrane, which influences voltage-dependent potassium channels. Net solute loss reduces the tendency to retain water, and hydraulic responses follow. Different stimuli and guard-cell regulatory states can recruit different channel combinations. The final pore response therefore cannot be assigned to one ion current alone without testing the other components. A normal ion channel protein abundance does not guarantee normal gating after elicitation.

The experiments of Melotto and colleagues helped distinguish a dynamic immune barrier from an anatomical opening. Observation of guard-cell closure after exposure to bacteria and defined elicitors showed that microbial perception can regulate aperture. The behavior of bacteria producing coronatine demonstrated a counter-defense capable of altering that response. Coronatine is a microbial virulence-associated compound with activity related to jasmonate signaling. Its capacity to counteract closure illustrates that disease depends not only on the initial plant response but also on the pathogen's ability to modify the host after perception.

A useful experimental comparison includes wild-type bacteria and a coronatine-deficient bacterial variant with a matched inoculum. If both initially cause closure but only the wild-type strain efficiently promotes reopening under the test conditions, the difference supports a contribution of coronatine to the later aperture response. A suitable restoration of the bacterial trait and host signaling analysis can strengthen causal interpretation. Differences in bacterial viability or growth before the aperture measurement would otherwise complicate the comparison. The expected sequence is not a universal clock for all plant–bacterium interactions; it is a model derived from defined systems.

Aperture measurements are usually collected from many individual stomata, but multiple stomata on one leaf share environmental conditions and developmental history. The plant or leaf, depending on the design, may be the independent biological unit. Treating a hundred pores from one plant as one hundred independent plants exaggerates the strength of evidence. Sampling across independent plants, using matched light and humidity, and recording the distribution of pore widths can reveal whether an apparent mean difference reflects a consistent change or a few extreme observations.

Callose deposition is another useful physiological output because it converts signaling into a localized structural change. Callose synthases use UDP-glucose as a donor substrate to form beta-1,3-linked glucan. The polymer can accumulate at sites of attempted penetration and in specialized wall or plasmodesmal regions. The substrate originates from carbohydrate metabolism, connecting the defense response to the plant's allocation of fixed carbon. A large deposition response can therefore represent a material and energetic commitment, not merely a change in a signaling molecule.

Localization matters because reinforcement is most useful at relevant sites. An increase in total callose distributed away from the site of invasion may not provide the same barrier function as a focused deposit at a penetration site. A microscope image showing localized fluorescence can provide information that a bulk biochemical measurement lacks. Conversely, an image of one striking deposit does not quantify the average response across tissue. Spatial and quantitative measurements are complementary.

Nishimura and colleagues reported an instructive exception in 2003: loss of a callose synthase was associated with salicylic acid-dependent resistance in a specific Arabidopsis disease system. Blocking salicylic acid signaling restored susceptibility in the callose-deficient genetic background. The double-mutant comparison supported an explanation involving compensatory defense signaling rather than a simple equation equating callose abundance with disease resistance. The finding demonstrates that a mutation can alter a physical barrier and regulatory networks simultaneously. It does not negate the capacity of callose deposition to contribute to resistance in other contexts.

Antimicrobial gene expression has similar interpretative limitations. A chitinase may hydrolyze susceptible chitin-containing structures and contribute to production of chitin fragments, but a particular enzyme's activity depends on substrate availability and localization. A beta-1,3-glucanase can act on susceptible glucans, and its biological effect depends on which structures it encounters. Expression of a gene encoding an antimicrobial protein is not the same as accumulation of an active protein at the site of infection. Measuring transcript, enzyme activity and pathogen phenotype addresses three different levels of the causal chain.

Together, stomatal control, wall reinforcement and antimicrobial outputs illustrate the spatial scale of PTI. A guard cell can restrict access, epidermal or mesophyll cells can respond to accessible patterns, and localized defense responses can affect the environment in which a microorganism attempts to grow. Not every pathogen encounters the same barriers, and a pathogen that enters through a wound may bypass stomatal control. The relevance of an output must be assessed against the lifestyle and infection route of the particular attacker.

9. PTI and ETI are cooperating immune layers

A microbial pattern and a pathogen effector differ in their biological role. A microbial pattern may arise from a structural or housekeeping molecule. An effector is a molecule delivered or released by an interacting organism that alters host biology and can promote colonization. Certain effectors suppress aspects of surface-receptor signaling, whereas intracellular plant immune receptors can detect effectors or their consequences. Effector-triggered immunity, ETI, is developed in the next chapter. Here the essential distinction is the receptor mode and the relationship between the two types of response.

The historical zig-zag model, associated with Jones and Dangl's 2006 synthesis, organized the interaction into recognition of microbial patterns, effector-mediated suppression, recognition of effectors and subsequent evolutionary changes in host and pathogen. It remains a useful framework for understanding selection and immune evasion. It should not be treated as a precise time-series diagram that describes every cell in every infection. Receptors, effectors and defense outputs can operate concurrently, and the strength and timing of their interactions vary across systems.

Work published in 2021 by Ngou and colleagues and by Yuan and colleagues provided evidence that surface and intracellular receptor-mediated immunity cooperate. Studies combining immune activation modes or examining mutants deficient in surface receptor systems showed that activation of intracellular receptors does not simply render surface receptor pathways irrelevant. Reinforcement of shared defense machinery can contribute substantially to a strong response. In particular, ROS generation and components of surface signaling are important in experimental examples of combined immunity.

One way to examine cooperation is a two-by-two factorial design with an independently controllable PTI elicitor and an independently controllable ETI trigger. Suppose illustrative normalized response values are 1 for neither input, 4 for PTI only, 6 for ETI only and 15 for both. Under an additive absolute-response model, the combined prediction is 4 + 6 − 1 = 9. The observed combined response is 6 units above this prediction. This demonstrates a positive interaction on that particular scale in the hypothetical dataset. It does not show that every immune output must be synergistic, and a different scale or nonlinear saturation model may change the analysis.

The distinction between an early immune marker and resistance is especially important when both modes of recognition are active. Rapid ROS, transcriptional induction, localized wall reinforcement and cell death can contribute in different proportions. A strong signal does not invariably predict a lower pathogen population. Localized cell death may be associated with certain intracellular immune responses but is not a defining or obligatory outcome of PTI. Disease limitation should be assessed directly rather than inferred from the visual intensity of a defense phenotype.

A plant allocates resources to growth, reproduction, maintenance and defense. Activation of immune responses changes metabolism and signaling, sometimes at a cost to growth in the absence of disease. Shared co-receptors, hormonal cross-talk, negative feedback and inducible expression help regulate these commitments. In crop plants, increasing receptor responsiveness may be useful in an appropriate pathogen context, but a phenotype measured in one controlled environment does not guarantee improved field yield across environments. The resistance benefit, physiological cost and breadth of protection need separate evaluation.

The main logic of this chapter is therefore a chain of experimentally distinguishable events: a pattern becomes accessible, a compatible surface receptor complex assembles or changes activity, intracellular networks transmit the signal, and cells alter physiological behavior and defense gene expression. Distinct receptor inputs can converge on shared machinery, and the effectiveness of the response depends on pathogen behavior and host context. This framework permits predictions about mutants, ligand combinations, time courses and tissue-specific responses without assuming that immunity is a single universal linear pathway.

Experimental meaning of immune cooperation

The early two-layer description of plant immunity was useful because it distinguished widespread surface recognition of microbial patterns from recognition of effectors by intracellular immune receptors. A challenge arises when this conceptual distinction is converted into an assumption of completely independent signaling pathways. If two receptor systems converge on the same downstream enzymes, an intervention in a shared enzyme may affect outputs from both systems. Conversely, one recognition mode may increase the abundance or activation competence of components used by the other. The distinction between inputs remains valid even when the output machinery is shared.

The studies by Ngou and colleagues and Yuan and colleagues in 2021 approached this problem using complementary experimental designs. One approach combined separately controllable activation of surface and intracellular receptors and measured the response produced by each alone and together. This permits an explicit comparison of interaction rather than inference from the response to a whole pathogen containing many simultaneous stimuli. A second approach examined immune responses in plants deficient in combinations of surface receptors or co-receptors after a challenge capable of activating intracellular immune receptors. Impairment of strong resistance or selected ETI-associated outputs in these backgrounds supports a functional role for surface signaling during combined immunity.

The conclusions are strongest when the design separates initiation of effector recognition from execution of a later response. If a surface receptor mutant does not produce normal ETI-associated ROS, this does not necessarily mean the intracellular immune receptor failed to recognize the effector. The missing surface components may contribute to the downstream machinery that produces the oxidative response. Conversely, a treatment that activates intracellular recognition may increase the responsiveness of later surface-receptor outputs. Measuring receptor activation, gene expression, ROS and pathogen population helps determine which stage is affected.

The distinction is particularly important for interpreting cell death. A localized hypersensitive response may occur after activation of some intracellular immune receptors, but the occurrence of cell death is not a direct measurement of pathogen restriction. In a biotrophic interaction, death of living host cells can impair the pathogen's access to resources. A necrotrophic pathogen may benefit from host death. Cell death can also arise through nonspecific chemical injury. A study evaluating immunity should distinguish the occurrence and location of cell death from the growth or spread of the pathogen. The detailed mechanisms of hypersensitive responses belong to the next chapter.

A factorial experiment can be analyzed by comparing conditional effects. With hypothetical response values of 1, 4, 6 and 15 for neither, PTI only, ETI only and both, respectively, the effect of adding PTI without ETI is 4−1 = 3 units. The effect of adding PTI in the ETI-active condition is 15−6 = 9 units. The difference of these conditional effects is 6 units, equivalent to the additive interaction calculation above. The computation shows why evaluating only the largest response is insufficient. The relevant question is whether the effect of one input changes depending on the presence of the other. Replication and an appropriate statistical model are required for an inference about biological interaction.

There are limits to simple additivity. If a readout has a maximum imposed by substrate availability or instrument saturation, a combined treatment may produce less than the arithmetic sum even when biological signaling is cooperative. If one stimulus raises the baseline before the other is applied, the choice of time interval and baseline subtraction can alter the apparent interaction. A time-resolved factorial design can reveal whether the combined response changes the early peak, sustained phase or recovery. Different phases need not show the same interaction.

Plants also use signal attenuation to control the physiological cost of defense. Constitutive activation of one output can reduce growth, but the relationship between growth and defense is context-dependent. A plant that grows more slowly in the absence of disease might retain more biomass under substantial disease pressure if its resistance is improved. Conversely, increased signaling that does not reduce pathogen burden might impose costs without corresponding protection. The relevant comparison involves both infected and uninfected plants under appropriate environments, not growth measurements under only one condition.

The distinction between immediate defense and priming prepares the transition to systemic resistance. A locally perceived pattern can initiate responses in the exposed cells; later responsiveness in the same or other tissues may be altered by communication and regulatory changes. A primed tissue may show little constitutive defense expression but respond more rapidly to a subsequent challenge. This differs from continuous maximal activation. The mechanisms of systemic acquired resistance and induced systemic resistance are considered later. Within PTI, the key point is that the consequences of surface recognition can outlast the original ligand exposure without requiring adaptive antibody production.

10. Experimental reasoning: from perception to resistance

A well-designed experiment begins by identifying the causal claim to be tested. If the claim is that FLS2 recognizes flg22, the most informative measurements concern ligand specificity and receptor-dependent response. If the claim is that RBOHD contributes to pathogen restriction, a ROS measurement alone is insufficient; pathogen entry or multiplication should also be assessed. If the claim concerns a relationship between PTI and ETI, both modes of activation should be varied rather than inferred from a single infection.

Consider a hypothetical mutant with reduced flg22-induced ROS. At least four explanations are plausible: ligand recognition is impaired, receptor-to-oxidase signaling is impaired, oxidase capacity itself is impaired, or the detection chemistry is altered. The experimenter can measure receptor abundance, test a different elicitor, examine calcium and MAPK outputs, and compare ROS detection methods. Restoration by a wild-type transgene strengthens a genetic explanation, but does not on its own distinguish all mechanistic possibilities. Converging evidence from different assays is more informative than collecting many replicates of only one assay.

Time courses reveal features that a single endpoint hides. A mutant may have a delayed rather than reduced response. If wild type reaches maximum ROS at ten minutes and the mutant reaches the same maximum at twenty minutes, comparing them only at ten minutes exaggerates the apparent defect in amplitude. The area under the response curve, peak time and decay rate provide different descriptors. Sampling intervals should be short enough to resolve rapid events and long enough to include recovery.

For a baseline-subtracted response sampled at 0, 5, 10 and 15 minutes with values 0, 8, 12 and 4 arbitrary units, the trapezoidal area is 5(0+8)/2 + 5(8+12)/2 + 5(12+4)/2 = 20 + 50 + 40 = 110 arbitrary-unit minutes. A second trace with the same peak but faster decline can have smaller integrated response. This calculation approximates the integral between measurements; unobserved sharp changes and unequal intervals affect accuracy. The area is not a concentration unless the signal is calibrated.

Bacterial growth is often represented as colony-forming units per unit tissue area or mass. A tenfold change corresponds to one log10 unit. If two genotypes have initial internal populations of 10³ colony-forming units per square centimeter, but after two days have 10⁶ and 10⁵ respectively, the difference at the final time is one log unit. Growth relative to initial population is 1000-fold and 100-fold respectively. If the initial populations were different, final counts alone would not isolate multiplication from entry. This example is hypothetical and illustrates why normalization to starting burden can be essential.

Biological replicates should represent independent plants or independent experimental units. Several images of a single leaf or multiple measurements from the same leaf disc can characterize within-sample variation, but they are not automatically independent biological replicates. Averaging technical readings within an independent unit may be appropriate before comparing groups. Randomization, matched conditions and reporting of variability help prevent misleading conclusions. A dramatic difference in one photographed leaf is not by itself convincing evidence of a general genotype effect.

When a mutant loses flg22 and chitin responses, a useful next step is to examine general cellular function and a stimulus that enters the network by a different route. Loss of all responses could reflect tissue death, altered development or a broad signaling defect rather than a shared immune receptor. A normal housekeeping process does not guarantee every aspect of physiology is intact, but viability measurements provide important context. Complementation, multiple independent mutant alleles and comparable genetic backgrounds further improve the causal interpretation.

The same reasoning applies to inhibitors. A compound that reduces ROS may inhibit the oxidase, quench ROS, interfere with the detection reagent or injure the tissue. A solvent control excludes only the vehicle as an explanation. Dose-response analysis, independent measurement, reversibility and a genetic test of the proposed target provide stronger evidence. The lesson for Part C is to distinguish an intervention from its interpretation: the reported measurement is an observation, while placement of a component within a pathway is a model supported by appropriately discriminating tests.

Building and testing a causal model from multiple assays

An experimental model of a perception pathway is strongest when observations constrain several distinct steps. Consider a hypothetical plant line A with normal morphology, normal chitin response, reduced flg22 binding and reduced flg22-dependent ROS and transcription. A lesion in the flg22 perception system is more plausible than a general defect in transcription or oxidase function because a separate elicitor can still activate outputs. Yet altered availability of the receptor at the surface might explain the same data as altered binding-site chemistry. Measuring total receptor abundance alone is insufficient; surface localization and direct association are important.

Now consider a line B with normal flg22 binding and normal ligand-dependent association of FLS2 and BAK1, but weak phosphorylation of receptor-associated cytoplasmic kinases. This pattern shifts attention toward signal transmission after assembly. It does not establish that BIK1 is necessarily the affected gene. A receptor catalytic defect, loss of a partner or increased dephosphorylation could produce similar observations. A candidate-gene rescue and biochemical phosphorylation assay would help distinguish them. The point of organizing the data by mechanistic step is to identify which alternatives remain possible.

In a third hypothetical line, C, flg22-dependent receptor phosphorylation and MAPK activation are retained, but extracellular ROS production is strongly impaired. This is consistent with a defect in the oxidative branch, such as oxidase regulation, substrate availability or ROS detection. If an independent assay confirms that ROS production is impaired rather than only luminescence detection, the interpretation is stronger. If the plant retains callose deposition or defense transcription, this supports the idea that the outputs are not obligatorily arranged in a single linear chain. Some outputs can still depend on redox signaling under other conditions.

A combined table of observations can serve as an experimental logic map. The rows are genotypes and the columns are defined readouts: receptor binding, complex formation, calcium transient, phospho-MAPK, ROS, callose, and pathogen growth. It is tempting to fill the table with a binary plus or minus for each response, but real phenotypes are often partial and time dependent. A genotype may show half the wild-type amplitude, delayed peak time or normal initial response with faster decline. Quantitative values and uncertainty intervals retain information that binary scoring loses. Binary patterns remain useful for initial conceptual reasoning provided their limitations are stated.

Complementation is especially valuable because mutant lines can contain unrelated genetic variation. Introducing a functional copy of a candidate gene and restoring the phenotype supports the conclusion that the gene contributes to the defect. For receptor proteins, the expression level and tissue distribution of the transgene matter. Excessive overexpression can create signaling behavior different from that of the endogenous gene, and expression from a nonnative promoter can alter the tissues involved. A rescue experiment is strongest when it restores relevant function without introducing unrelated physiological changes.

Epistasis examines the combined phenotypes of genetic perturbations. If a receptor mutant abolishes ligand-dependent ROS and an oxidase mutant abolishes the same output, a double mutant may also lack ROS. This is compatible with the receptor and oxidase contributing to the same response, but an absent output cannot become more absent. The floor effect limits inference about order. Measuring receptor activation in the oxidase mutant and oxidase regulation in the receptor mutant can provide directional information that the double-mutant endpoint alone cannot. In a branched network, conclusions from epistasis are always specific to the output examined.

A more informative quantitative example involves a partial mutant. Suppose wild type generates 100 normalized units of ROS, mutant A 60 units, mutant B 50 units and the double mutant 30 units. If the two effects act as independent proportional reductions, the expected combined response is 100 × 0.60 × 0.50 = 30 units. The observed result matches that illustrative multiplicative expectation. Under an additive absolute-loss model, the predicted response would be 100−40−50 = 10 units. Different null models therefore lead to different interpretations. Biology and the properties of the response scale determine which model is appropriate; neither should be applied mechanically.

Time-course analysis can reveal differences between initiation and termination. Suppose two genotypes begin responding at the same time and reach identical maxima, but one retains an elevated signal for longer. This suggests altered maintenance or termination rather than necessarily altered receptor sensitivity. If a new elicitor pulse is applied after recovery and the second response differs, the previous exposure may have changed pathway competence. A matched group receiving only the later pulse controls for developmental changes over the interval. Such experiments are useful for distinguishing transient desensitization from persistent changes in response capacity.

A statistical comparison should account for the design. Repeated observations from the same leaf over time are correlated, and independent leaves from the same plant may share effects of plant condition. Means and variability can summarize the data, but the number of independent biological units must be reported. The aim is not to demand sophisticated statistical modeling in every NET question. It is to recognize that the strength of causal inference depends on replication, controls and measurement validity. A numerical difference by itself does not establish that the difference is reproducible.

The final step is connecting molecular signaling to disease resistance. A purified elicitor can be applied before a defined challenge to compare protection in wild type and receptor-deficient plants. A receptor-dependent reduction in pathogen burden after pretreatment supports the contribution of that recognition pathway to induced resistance. It does not identify which downstream output is responsible. Testing mutants in candidate branches, measuring the relevant physiological outputs and comparing the actual pathogen populations can refine the explanation. The chapter's central experimental principle is that perception, signaling and resistance are linked but distinguishable biological levels.

Misconceptions and examination traps

PTI is antibody-mediated
Plants rely on inherited immune receptor systems rather than vertebrate antibody production; priming does not imply clonal selection.
MAMP means a pathogen is definitely present
Beneficial and neutral microorganisms can possess the same patterns. Recognition alone does not determine ecological outcome.
DAMPs originate in microbial cells
They arise from host molecules exposed, released or processed during damage.
flg22 is the whole flagellum
It is an experimental peptide representing a recognized region of flagellin.
All surface receptors possess their own kinase
RLPs can signal through associated kinase partners.
BAK1 determines every ligand specificity
BAK1 is a shared co-receptor; ligand-selective receptors and complex composition matter.
BIK1 binds extracellular flagellin directly
BIK1 is a cytoplasmic kinase, not the primary extracellular ligand receptor.
Chitin is cellulose
Chitin consists of N-acetylglucosamine, whereas cellulose contains glucose.
Rice and Arabidopsis chitin receptors have identical organization
CEBiP–OsCERK1 and LYK5–CERK1 are related but distinct systems.
RBOHD produces hydroxyl radicals directly
It transfers electrons to extracellular oxygen to generate superoxide; downstream reactions generate other ROS.
ROS is always damaging
Regulated production can contribute to signaling; excessive accumulation can cause damage.
Kinase protein abundance proves kinase activation
Activation depends on catalytic and regulatory state; measure phosphorylation or activity with proper controls.
Callose is lignin
Callose is a beta-1,3-glucan, whereas lignin is a phenolic polymer.
Stomata are only passive infection portals
Guard cells can actively close in response to immune signals.
PTI becomes irrelevant when ETI occurs
Evidence supports cooperation between surface and intracellular immune pathways; neither is universally dispensable.

Which ligand is a host-derived damage signal?

  2. elf18
  3. Oligogalacturonides
  4. Chitin
Answer and explanation

C. Oligogalacturonides. Oligogalacturonides arise from host pectin degradation. The other options represent microbial-derived patterns.

Which receptor is the canonical Arabidopsis flg22 sensor?

  1. FLS2
  2. EFR
  3. CERK1
  4. BRI1
Answer and explanation

A. FLS2. FLS2 perceives flg22. EFR perceives elf18, CERK1 participates in chitin perception and BRI1 responds to brassinosteroids.

What distinguishes an RLP from a typical RLK?

  1. Absence of every extracellular domain
  2. Absence of a conventional intracellular kinase domain
  3. Obligatory nuclear localization
  4. Binding only DNA
Answer and explanation

B. Absence of a conventional intracellular kinase domain. RLPs can participate in perception but recruit kinase partners; they are not necessarily signaling-inactive.

4. Part B

BAK1 is best described as:

  1. An antibody
  2. A chitin oligomer
  3. A bacterial effector
  4. A shared surface co-receptor kinase
Answer and explanation

D. A shared surface co-receptor kinase. BAK1 participates in several RLK complexes, including FLS2 and BRI1 systems. Its participation does not make ligand specificities identical.

Which protein is a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase?

  1. BIK1

  4. Callose

A. BIK1. BIK1 is an intracellular kinase associated with immune receptor complexes; the others are a membrane receptor or polysaccharides.

The major initial product of oxygen reduction by RBOHD is:

  1. ATP
  2. Hydroxyl radical
  3. Superoxide
  4. Molecular nitrogen

C. Superoxide. RBOHD transfers electrons to oxygen across the membrane, generating superoxide, which can subsequently dismutate.

B. LYK5–CERK1. LYK5 and CERK1 contribute to chitin recognition and signaling. The other pairs represent distinct regulatory systems or an incorrect combination.

  3. Phenolic polymer

D. Beta-1,3-glucan. Callose is predominantly beta-1,3-linked glucose. The first describes chitin and the third lignin.

  3. Leaf mass alone

B. Normalized activation-loop phosphorylation. MAPK activation is commonly assessed via phosphorylation or catalytic activity, not unrelated leaf traits.

The principal distinction between PTI and ETI concerns:

  1. Receptor-mediated recognition mode
  2. Presence versus absence of all ROS
  3. Plant versus animal species
  4. Whether all cells die
Answer and explanation

A. Receptor-mediated recognition mode. Surface pattern perception and predominantly intracellular effector recognition are different modes that share many outputs.

A mutant loses flg22-induced ROS but responds normally to chitin. Which hypothesis is favored?

  1. General loss of tissue viability
  2. Loss of all NADPH oxidase activity
  3. Defect in the flg22-specific perception route
  4. Loss of every immune receptor
Answer and explanation

C. Defect in the flg22-specific perception route. Preserved chitin response argues against a universal inability to produce ROS. A specific input-route defect is plausible but requires further genetic tests.

  3. Identity of all phosphorylation sites

Answer and explanation

A. Association under assay conditions. Protein complexes can contain bridging partners; association does not by itself prove direct molecular contact or downstream physiological outcome.

D. 0.75. 60/(60+20)=0.75. This is an equilibrium occupancy model, not necessarily the fractional physiological response.

  3. No change
  4. Half

  4. 8-fold

D. 8-fold. Delta-delta Ct is −3. Therefore 2³=8, assuming stable normalization and the stated efficiency.

  1. 25
  2. 50
  3. 100
  4. 10

  4. 6

Revision and mastery

Distinct surface receptor complexes recognize accessible microbial or host damage patterns. They activate interacting kinase, calcium and ROS networks, causing changes in guard-cell physiology, wall reinforcement and transcription. PTI and ETI can reinforce one another. Assess resistance using pathogen burden as well as signaling measurements.
