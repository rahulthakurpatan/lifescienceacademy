1. The problem of recognizing a microbe

A leaf provides a moist, nutrient-containing environment to microorganisms that succeed in entering its internal air spaces. Yet a healthy leaf is not sterile. Bacteria, yeasts and other microorganisms live on surfaces, and some establish relationships that cause little injury or even benefit their host. The physiological problem is therefore not simply to eliminate every foreign organism. A living plant must detect signs of microbial presence or cellular damage, evaluate those signals in their local context and restrict potentially damaging invasion without continually sacrificing photosynthesis and growth. Preformed barriers such as the cuticle reduce the probability of contact with living cells, but they cannot explain why the same plant rapidly changes its gene expression upon exposure to a small, purified microbial molecule.

Plant innate immunity comprises genetically encoded mechanisms by which cells recognize and respond to threats. Innate in this context means that the receptor repertoire does not arise through the somatic immunoglobulin gene rearrangements and clonal selection characteristic of vertebrate adaptive immunity. Plants have highly diverse and heritable immune receptors, and their response to a second challenge may be altered by prior exposure. Such priming is not equivalent to producing antigen-specific antibodies or expanding a clone of B lymphocytes. Most living plant cells can participate in defense, rather than relying on a circulating population of specialized immune cells.

A useful distinction is between pre-existing resistance and induced resistance. A wax layer can impede entry even before microbial contact; a receptor-triggered change in guard-cell aperture is an active physiological response. A mutation in a cuticle biosynthetic enzyme can increase access to the apoplast while leaving perception of a purified elicitor normal. Conversely, a mutation in a surface immune receptor may preserve the physical cuticle but compromise the ability to detect a particular microbial signal after contact. Equal disease susceptibility in these two mutants would not establish that both genes function in the same molecular pathway.

Pattern-triggered immunity, abbreviated PTI, is the set of immune responses initiated when cell-surface pattern recognition receptors perceive accessible microbial or host-derived danger-associated patterns. A pattern is not a geometrical shape in the ordinary sense. It is a molecular feature that can be recognized by a receptor, often because the feature occurs in a class of microorganisms or is released during injury. The pattern can be a defined peptide, a carbohydrate fragment or another molecular structure. Recognition initiates biochemical events, which may include ion fluxes, protein phosphorylation, reactive oxygen production, transcriptional changes and reinforcement of tissue barriers.

The word resistance describes a reduction in pathogen entry, growth or spread, whereas tolerance describes the capacity to maintain performance despite a given burden. An apparently healthy plant may have high pathogen populations because it is tolerant, and a plant with a strong visible local response may nevertheless restrict pathogen growth. The endpoints are not interchangeable. An immune study that measures only a rapid oxidative response has established a signaling phenotype, not necessarily a resistance phenotype. Experiments need to link the early molecular observation to an appropriate later outcome.

PTI is often introduced as a first layer of inducible defense because the relevant receptors can perceive material outside the plasma membrane, before or during microbial entry. The designation is a useful organizational device, not an assertion that each pathogen must pass through a rigid sequence of discrete immune stages. Intracellular effector recognition can strengthen components of surface-receptor signaling. The two modes of recognition differ in their inputs and receptor organization, but many outputs overlap. This chapter develops the surface-perception mechanism; intracellular immune receptors and systemic defense are developed in the subsequent nodes.

2. Microbial patterns and endogenous danger signals

A microbial-associated molecular pattern, or MAMP, is a molecular feature characteristic of microorganisms that can stimulate host pattern recognition. The term pathogen-associated molecular pattern, or PAMP, is used historically for many of the same ligands. MAMP is often more precise because flagellin occurs in bacteria that are not pathogens of the plant. A plant receptor cannot directly determine the ecological intention of a bacterium from its flagellin. Its response is influenced by ligand accessibility, concentration, other signals, tissue context and mechanisms that regulate receptor activity.

Flagellin is a structural protein of the bacterial flagellar filament. In Arabidopsis, a conserved region within flagellin can be represented experimentally by a synthetic 22-residue peptide called flg22. The peptide is a useful elicitor because the plant can recognize it without exposure to live bacteria, bacterial multiplication or additional secreted compounds. It is not the complete flagellum and does not rotate. Recognition of flg22 demonstrates that a limited region of the microbial protein is sufficient to trigger selected responses. A purified peptide experiment therefore separates the molecular input from other aspects of infection, but it cannot reproduce every aspect of interaction with intact bacteria.

Elongation factor Tu is a bacterial protein involved in translation. The amino-terminal peptide elf18 can trigger immune responses in plants that possess an appropriate receptor, such as Arabidopsis EFR. The protein has an essential intracellular microbial function, but plant recognition requires an accessible form of the relevant molecular feature. The evolutionary constraint on a microbial protein does not mean every residue in its recognized region is immutable. Sequence variation, release, masking and modification can all affect detectability. An organism may retain protein function while becoming less readily recognized.

Chitin is a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine residues linked by beta-1,4 glycosidic bonds. It is a structural component of the walls of many fungi. Chitin-derived oligomers can be released or exposed during interactions with plants, including through the activity of plant chitinases. The oligomer is a carbohydrate rather than a peptide, so it is chemically distinct from flg22 and elf18. Binding depends on oligomer length and other structural properties, illustrating why pattern recognition is molecularly specific despite its broad defensive role.

A damage-associated molecular pattern, or DAMP, originates from the host rather than from the microorganism. Oligogalacturonides are fragments of pectic polysaccharide that can arise during cell-wall degradation. Extracellular ATP can signal damage when a normally intracellular metabolite becomes available in an extracellular compartment. Endogenous plant elicitor peptides, such as members of the Pep family, can stimulate signaling through PEPR receptors. Damage signals may reinforce a response initially triggered by microbial patterns; they also explain why an immune-like response can occur after sterile injury. DAMP signaling is not proof that a living pathogen is present.

The concept of an elicitor refers to the ability of a substance to induce a measured response. An elicitor preparation may contain several molecular species, whereas a defined synthetic peptide permits more precise interpretation. A sterile solution of peptide and a suspension of viable bacteria are different treatments even if both provoke an oxidative response. To test whether the peptide sequence itself matters, an investigator can compare an active peptide with a sequence-altered peptide prepared and delivered under matching conditions. A solvent-only treatment controls for handling and vehicle effects. A response to the active ligand alone supports sequence-sensitive perception, although receptor involvement requires additional evidence.

The biological importance of pattern recognition extends beyond discrimination between fungal and bacterial molecules. A single microbial encounter can supply several elicitors, some of which converge on shared downstream signals. A mutation that removes one receptor may therefore abolish the response to its purified ligand while leaving substantial defense against a complex infection. Multiple receptors can compensate partly at the level of tissue resistance without being interchangeable at the level of ligand binding. This distinction is essential when reading receptor-mutant experiments.

3. Receptor architecture and molecular specificity

Pattern recognition receptors at the plant cell surface are membrane-associated proteins whose extracellular regions perceive ligands or participate in perception complexes. A receptor-like kinase, or RLK, generally contains an extracellular domain, a membrane-spanning segment and an intracellular protein kinase domain. The extracellular domain is exposed toward the apoplast and the catalytic domain is positioned toward the cytoplasm, where ATP and cytoplasmic substrates are available. This topology allows an extracellular stimulus to modify intracellular phosphorylation without requiring the ligand to cross the membrane.

The extracellular domain of FLS2 contains leucine-rich repeats, abbreviated LRRs. Repetition of a structural motif creates an extended surface suitable for molecular interactions. The function of an LRR is not determined simply by the presence of leucine. The arrangement of residues within the folded domain determines the interaction surface. Different LRR receptors recognize different ligands. FLS2 is not interchangeable with the brassinosteroid receptor BRI1 merely because both possess extracellular LRRs and intracellular kinase domains.

A receptor-like protein, or RLP, resembles a cell-surface receptor but lacks a conventional intracellular kinase domain. Such a receptor can still initiate signaling by associating with kinase-containing partners. In several immune LRR-RLP complexes, SOBIR1 supplies a constitutively associated kinase and a SERK-family co-receptor such as BAK1 participates after elicitor perception. The exact architecture depends on the receptor family. A short cytoplasmic tail therefore does not mean that a receptor is incapable of communicating with intracellular enzymes. It means that signal transduction must be achieved using an appropriate interaction partner.

A receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase, or RLCK, is a kinase related to RLKs but lacks the extracellular ligand-binding receptor organization. BIK1 is a well-studied example associated with immune receptor complexes at the inner face of the plasma membrane. Its function differs from that of FLS2: BIK1 helps transfer and distribute the signal inside the cell rather than recognizing extracellular flg22 as the principal binding receptor. It can be involved downstream of multiple inputs, which explains why its mutation may alter more than one elicitor response.

A co-receptor participates in productive signaling alongside a ligand-selective receptor. It need not possess the same ligand specificity. BAK1, also known as SERK3, contributes to several surface receptor systems, including development and immunity. Shared use of BAK1 does not imply that brassinosteroid and flg22 are interchangeable signals. The composition, location and phosphorylation state of the activated complex influence downstream outputs. A strong phenotype of a co-receptor mutant may reflect several disrupted pathways, including changes in development that complicate interpretation of immune assays.

A receptor's ligand-binding capacity and its signaling capacity can be distinguished experimentally. A protein may retain a correctly folded extracellular region while losing catalytic activity because of a mutation in its kinase domain. Such a receptor could still bind a ligand but fail to transmit the signal normally. Conversely, a receptor with a defective extracellular interaction surface may retain kinase activity in a biochemical assay while failing to respond to ligand in intact cells. The interpretation requires a measurement relevant to each function: receptor abundance and localization, ligand binding, complex formation, phosphorylation and physiological output.

A simple equilibrium model provides intuition about ligand binding. For one class of noninteracting binding sites with ligand in excess, fractional occupancy is theta = L/(Kd + L), where L is free ligand concentration and Kd is the equilibrium dissociation constant in the same concentration units. If Kd equals 20 nM and L equals 5 nM, occupancy is 5/25 = 0.20. At 80 nM, occupancy is 80/100 = 0.80. These are illustrative values, not measured FLS2 constants. The concentration producing half-maximal physiological response need not equal Kd, because receptor reserve, amplification, feedback, peptide degradation and co-receptor recruitment can alter the relationship between binding and output. Occupancy is a molecular probability, whereas response amplitude measures a downstream event.

4. Flagellin perception by FLS2 and BAK1

The flagellin response provides an experimentally tractable example of how plants discriminate extracellular information. The Arabidopsis gene FLS2 was identified through genetic analysis of flagellin sensitivity; the gene encodes an LRR receptor-like kinase. A plant lacking functional FLS2 responds poorly to the elicitor flg22 in characteristic assays, whereas responses mediated by other receptors need not disappear. This observation establishes that a particular plant gene is needed for the normal response to the peptide, rather than demonstrating that all microbial recognition depends on a single receptor.

The ligand-binding surface of FLS2 recognizes flg22 in the extracellular space. Ligand perception promotes assembly of a signaling complex containing BAK1. An informative feature of the receptor complex is that the peptide participates in forming a productive interface between receptor and co-receptor. Thus ligand occupancy and receptor association are mechanistically connected. The plasma membrane constrains the location of the receptor components and positions the cytoplasmic kinase domains for signaling. The extracellular peptide itself does not catalyze phosphorylation.

The receptor-complex discovery was supported by biochemical experiments showing rapid association of FLS2 and BAK1 after flagellin perception, as reported by Chinchilla and colleagues in 2007. To understand why this was significant, consider two models. In one, FLS2 acts entirely independently and BAK1 affects immunity only through a distant developmental pathway. In the other, BAK1 physically participates in the activated FLS2 complex. A ligand-dependent increase in recovery of BAK1 with FLS2 in a co-immunoprecipitation assay supports the second model. Comparing untreated and elicitor-treated samples is crucial. Input controls showing that both proteins are present rule out the trivial explanation that an absent immunoprecipitated signal simply reflects lack of protein expression.

Co-immunoprecipitation identifies proteins that are associated under the assay conditions, not necessarily a direct contact between every pair of recovered molecules. A bridging protein can produce the same result. Structural and biochemical evidence complements genetic and association experiments in establishing the receptor architecture. An exam question asking whether co-immunoprecipitation alone proves direct ligand binding should therefore be answered negatively, even when the association is biologically meaningful.

On the cytoplasmic side, the active receptor complex undergoes rapid phosphorylation. A protein kinase transfers the terminal phosphate group of ATP onto suitable residues of a substrate. Phosphorylation can change the catalytic activity, conformation, localization or interaction partners of a signaling protein. The reaction is reversible through phosphatases. Signal initiation therefore depends on a change in the balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation rather than on the mere presence of kinase protein.

BIK1 and related cytoplasmic kinases are associated with surface immune receptor systems. Upon activation, phosphorylation and rearrangement of these complexes allow intracellular substrates to be regulated. BIK1 can phosphorylate regulatory sites on RBOHD, a membrane NADPH oxidase. This connection explains one rapid route from flagellin perception to extracellular reactive oxygen production. It does not imply that the entire immune network is a single linear FLS2–BAK1–BIK1–RBOHD chain. The receptor complex also connects to calcium entry, other cytoplasmic kinases and MAP kinase pathways.

Imagine a fls2 mutant in which introduction of a functional FLS2 transgene restores flg22-dependent oxidative response. This complementation strengthens the conclusion that the original defect was caused by loss of FLS2 function. Restoration of response to one ligand need not alter responsiveness to chitin if the chitin perception pathway was intact. Expression of a kinase-inactive FLS2 version would provide a further test, provided its expression, membrane localization and stability are verified. A failure of that version to rescue signaling could otherwise reflect misfolding or absence of receptor rather than loss of catalysis.

Response termination is as important as initiation. Receptor phosphorylation states can be reversed, associated components can be modified or degraded, and receptor trafficking can alter surface availability. Ligand can be degraded or removed from the local environment. The decline of a measured output can occur despite ongoing receptor occupancy because downstream pathways adapt. A second elicitor pulse may produce a different response depending on which negative regulatory mechanisms are active. It is not appropriate to infer the exact half-life of receptor complexes from the duration of an oxidative burst alone.

5. LysM receptors and fungal chitin perception

A fungus and a bacterium differ substantially in cell architecture, so the host needs more than a flagellin receptor to detect microbial structures. LysM, or lysin motif, is a small carbohydrate-binding domain present in a number of plant receptor proteins. Several LysM-containing surface proteins contribute to recognition of chitin-derived oligomers. Their use illustrates how a different extracellular binding architecture can be connected to downstream immune responses that overlap with those induced by peptide ligands.

In Arabidopsis, LYK5 contributes strongly to chitin perception, acting with CERK1, a LysM receptor-like kinase with an active intracellular kinase domain. LYK4 can also contribute to recognition. CERK1 has roles in productive receptor assembly and intracellular signal transmission. In rice, the chitin-binding receptor-like protein CEBiP acts with OsCERK1. The two examples share a division of labor between carbohydrate recognition and kinase-dependent signaling, but the individual proteins and receptor arrangements are not identical. A student should therefore specify the organism when describing the receptor complex rather than assigning rice receptor names directly to Arabidopsis.

Chitin recognition depends on the molecular properties of oligomers. Short and long fragments are not necessarily equivalent at a fixed molar concentration. A long fragment may provide interaction surfaces favorable for assembly of a receptor complex, whereas a short fragment may bind differently or fail to promote productive assembly. The total mass of carbohydrate supplied also differs if equal molar amounts of different lengths are used. When analyzing an experiment, determine whether concentration refers to oligomer molecules, monomer equivalents or mass. Without that information, a quantitative comparison of elicitor potency can be ambiguous.

CERK1-associated cytoplasmic kinases transmit signals to downstream components. PBL27 provides an experimentally supported example of a route connecting the chitin perception complex to MAP kinase activation in Arabidopsis. The broader principle is that surface immune complexes recruit particular intracellular kinases, some shared and others preferentially associated with specific inputs. This arrangement permits convergence in certain responses and divergence in others. A CERK1 mutant may lose a major chitin response yet preserve substantial flg22 responsiveness because FLS2 and its associated partners still operate.

Chitin also participates in an instructive comparison with microbial signals during beneficial plant interactions. LysM-containing receptors recognize various N-acetylglucosamine-containing molecules, including signaling compounds involved in symbiosis. The presence of a LysM domain does not identify a receptor as an immune receptor without further evidence. Ligand modification, receptor composition and host context can route chemically related inputs toward different developmental or defensive outcomes. The detailed symbiotic signaling pathway belongs with nitrogen fixation, but the distinction prevents an incorrect generalization about carbohydrate recognition.

To distinguish the receptor families, a factorial experiment can expose wild-type, fls2 and cerk1 plants to both flg22 and a defined chitin oligomer. If fls2 loses flg22 response while retaining chitin response and cerk1 shows the complementary pattern, the experiment supports distinct perception routes. If a fourth genotype loses both, it may disrupt a shared downstream component, a co-receptor used by multiple routes or general cellular competence. Measuring tissue viability, baseline signals and a response outside these pathways helps separate the alternatives. A cross-elicitor assay is considerably more informative than a single measurement from one mutant after one stimulus.

6. Calcium, phosphorylation and reactive oxygen

The earliest responses to surface perception often occur within seconds or minutes, before the cell can synthesize large quantities of new defense proteins. A central principle is the regulation of existing molecules. Ion channels can open or close, enzymes can be phosphorylated and local redox chemistry can change. These events generate signals that branch toward several later outputs. The rapid response is possible because the cell maintains ion gradients, ATP, reducing equivalents and signaling proteins before contact with a microbe.

Calcium entry changes the concentration of free cytosolic calcium. The resting concentration is maintained low compared with several extracellular and intracellular pools by transport and buffering. Opening a calcium-permeable channel therefore allows rapid movement down a favorable electrochemical gradient. The concentration change depends not only on entry but also on extrusion, sequestration and binding. A fluorescent calcium indicator reports a particular measurable property of the calcium signal, rather than the total amount of calcium in the tissue. Different elicitors and cell types can display different time courses.

Calcium-binding proteins convert the ion signal into biochemical action. Calcium-dependent protein kinases, or CPKs, combine a calcium-responsive regulatory region and a kinase domain, allowing calcium binding to influence phosphorylation of substrates. Calmodulin is a different type of calcium sensor that regulates other proteins through interactions rather than functioning as a kinase itself. The specificity of calcium action depends on the accessible targets, spatial proximity and characteristics of the calcium transient. The same ion can participate in immune signaling and drought responses without making the two pathways identical.

The NADPH oxidase RBOHD transfers electrons from cytosolic NADPH through the plasma membrane to molecular oxygen on the extracellular side. Oxygen gains an electron to form superoxide, which can undergo spontaneous or enzyme-assisted dismutation to hydrogen peroxide. Thus the site of NADPH consumption and the initial site of superoxide production lie on opposite sides of the membrane. This arrangement is especially suited to interactions in the apoplast, where microbes and cell-wall components are located.

The simplified reaction for dismutation is 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Two superoxide radicals are required per hydrogen peroxide molecule produced by this reaction. RBOHD does not directly convert two molecules of water to hydrogen peroxide, nor does hydrogen peroxide have the same chemical reactivity as a hydroxyl radical. The redox reactions and antioxidant systems introduced in the oxidative-stress chapter provide the chemical basis for interpreting immune-associated ROS.

RBOHD is regulated by several inputs. Its amino-terminal region contains calcium-binding EF-hand motifs, and regulatory phosphorylation by kinases can influence its activity. Kadota and colleagues reported direct phosphorylation of RBOHD by BIK1, connecting a receptor-associated kinase to the oxidative burst. Calcium-dependent phosphorylation can affect other sites. These mechanisms can cooperate: receptor signaling and calcium signals influence oxidase activity rather than necessarily representing successive indispensable links in one chain.

ROS can in turn affect calcium-permeable signaling pathways. Feedback can amplify or extend signaling, and removal of one component may alter a later phase without abolishing the first event. Consider an illustrative calcium trace with an early peak and a later smaller peak. If a mutant lacking RBOHD retains the first peak but loses the second, the result supports an upstream contribution to the first peak and a possible redox-dependent feedback route to the second. It does not demonstrate that RBOHD is a calcium channel. Other evidence is required to identify the actual channel and intermediate signals.

The oxidative burst describes a rapid increase in ROS production or measured ROS-associated signal. A luminol-based assay is commonly used to follow extracellular oxidative responses in leaf tissue. Light emission depends on the reaction chemistry, including detection reagents and peroxidase activity, and is not automatically an absolute molar concentration. If a mutant changes peroxidase activity, the light signal may differ even with similar hydrogen peroxide availability. Appropriate controls and an independent readout can strengthen the interpretation.

A simple mass balance clarifies the difference between production and measured abundance. Let H be an effective extracellular hydrogen peroxide concentration, P the production rate in micromolar per minute, and k a first-order removal constant in inverse minutes. Then dH/dt = P − kH. With constant P = 2 micromolar per minute and k = 0.5 per minute, the model predicts a steady concentration of 4 micromolar. Doubling both P and k leaves the steady concentration at 4 micromolar but doubles production and removal fluxes. The model ignores spatial diffusion, changing enzyme activity and nonlinear removal; it is a teaching model, not a fit to published data. Its implication is that a single concentration cannot independently establish the rate of ROS synthesis.

7. MAP kinase cascades and transcription

A kinase cascade transfers a signal through a hierarchy of protein kinases. The conventional MAP kinase module includes a MAP kinase kinase kinase, a MAP kinase kinase and a MAP kinase. Each activated tier phosphorylates the next under appropriate conditions. In plants, immune inputs can activate modules containing MKK4 or MKK5 and MPK3 or MPK6, among other combinations. The exact upstream components and downstream substrates can differ with the receptor, stimulus and experimental context.

The repeated catalytic steps can amplify a signal. One activated kinase molecule can phosphorylate multiple substrate molecules over its active lifetime. Amplification is nevertheless limited by substrate abundance, ATP availability, phosphatases, localization and competing interactions. An arrow between two kinases represents a regulatory or catalytic relationship, not an increase of one fixed factor. A cascade is not automatically more active merely because more kinase protein is present. Phosphorylation status and catalytic competence are often more relevant to a short-term response.

A useful way to measure activation is to compare a phosphorylation-sensitive immunoblot with a measurement of total kinase. In an illustrative experiment, untreated tissue has a phospho-MPK band of 12 arbitrary units and a total MPK band of 60. Treated tissue has a phospho band of 36 and a total band of 90. If both assays are in a linear detection range, the normalized signal changes from 0.20 to 0.40, a twofold increase. The raw phospho band increased threefold, but that number does not account for the change in total MPK. The normalized ratio is an assay proxy, not an exact molecular fraction unless calibrated appropriately.

Activated kinases affect the activity of numerous substrates, including regulatory proteins involved in gene expression. WRKY transcription factors participate prominently in plant immune networks. A transcription factor is a protein that influences gene transcription by interacting with regulatory DNA and other proteins. WRKY proteins recognize characteristic DNA sequence motifs, commonly W-box elements, but not every family member acts as a positive regulator of every defense gene. Some restrict or reshape immune outputs. A change in the expression of a WRKY gene is distinct from a change in the activity of its encoded protein.

Transcriptional responses can begin within minutes of elicitor treatment and can alter the production of enzymes, transport proteins and regulators over longer periods. Commonly used marker genes include FRK1 in Arabidopsis, although no single marker establishes all aspects of PTI. A gene may respond to more than one stimulus or be affected by developmental state. The choice of marker should match the proposed hypothesis. Defense-associated gene induction does not by itself demonstrate direct DNA binding by a proposed regulator.

Relative transcript abundance can be assessed using quantitative PCR. Under the approximation of equal near-perfect doubling efficiencies, the relative abundance between treatment and control is 2 to the power minus delta-delta Ct. If control target Ct is 27 and reference Ct is 21, delta Ct is 6. If treated target Ct is 23 and reference Ct is 20, delta Ct is 3. Delta-delta Ct is minus 3, yielding an eightfold relative increase. Directly subtracting target Ct values would instead give an apparent sixteenfold change, ignoring the reference gene. The example assumes stable reference expression and appropriate amplification efficiencies. Transcript abundance is not equivalent to protein activity or resistance.

Branching makes pathway order an experimental question. If loss of RBOHD abolishes a particular ROS output but leaves substantial MAP kinase phosphorylation, the results are consistent with separable contributions from the surface receptor network. Conversely, changes in redox signaling can influence MAP kinase responses in particular contexts. It is unsafe to conclude either that ROS is always upstream of MAPKs or that ROS can never influence MAPKs. A causal conclusion should be tied to the measured output, genotype, stimulus and time interval.

8. Stomatal defense and cell-wall reinforcement

Stomata are pores bordered by two guard cells that regulate exchange of carbon dioxide and water vapor. They also provide potential access to the internal leaf environment. An open pore is useful for photosynthesis, but it may permit microbial entry. Guard cells therefore integrate physiological and immune information. Closing a pore during an appropriate microbial challenge reduces access through that route while potentially limiting carbon dioxide assimilation. The response represents a tradeoff rather than an unconditional improvement in fitness.

Melotto and colleagues reported that stomata participate actively in defense against bacterial invasion. Exposure to bacteria or defined elicitors could induce closure, and virulent strains of Pseudomonas syringae could counteract this response through coronatine. The significance of this finding was that stomata were no longer interpreted only as passive openings in the leaf surface. Recognition-dependent changes in guard-cell physiology contribute to an active defense barrier.

Guard-cell closure requires changes in solute content and turgor. Activation of anion efflux pathways can depolarize the plasma membrane, favor outward potassium movement under suitable conditions, and decrease the osmotic retention of water. Water moves out and guard-cell turgor falls, narrowing the pore. Immune-associated calcium and ROS signaling can contribute to this regulation, and the ABA pathway intersects with guard-cell responses. The complete molecular sequence depends on the stimulus and conditions; immune closure should not simply be identified with ABA-induced drought closure. Both converge on the mechanics of the same cell type.

The importance of stomatal defense can be evaluated by comparing surface inoculation with infiltration. Surface exposure requires bacteria to encounter the epidermal barrier before reaching the apoplast. Infiltration introduces bacteria into internal tissue, bypassing much of the entry step. If a mutant differs markedly from wild type after surface exposure but shows similar bacterial multiplication after infiltration, the evidence supports an entry-related contribution. The inference is stronger when inoculum, initial internal population, humidity, leaf age and the effects of the infiltration procedure are controlled. The result does not exclude every role for the mutated protein after entry.

Callose is a beta-1,3-glucan deposited at selected locations in plant cell walls. During defense, callose can accumulate near attempted sites of penetration and in other localized wall structures. It is distinct from cellulose, whose glucan chains are beta-1,4-linked, and from lignin, a complex phenolic polymer. These different materials affect wall properties through different chemical and structural mechanisms. Callose deposition can contribute to reinforcement or compartmentalization, but the amount of callose alone is not a universal measure of resistance.

Callose synthase PMR4/GSL5 is an important component of induced callose deposition in Arabidopsis. The significance of a callose-deficient mutant must be interpreted carefully because perturbing the wall can alter compensatory defense signaling. Reduced callose and enhanced resistance in a particular pathosystem would not establish that callose is generally harmful or that deposition never contributes to defense. Multiple outputs of the immune network can change simultaneously. A genetic rescue and measurement of additional defense pathways help distinguish direct and compensatory effects.

Callose is often visualized by fluorescence staining with aniline blue. Counting deposits per standardized area or quantifying stained area can compare treatments, but results depend on the tissue region, imaging settings and definition of a deposit. Measurements should sample comparable tissues and use biological replication. A larger count could reflect more sites of deposition, whereas a larger total area could reflect expansion of existing deposits. Neither measurement directly quantifies pathogen viability.

Defense transcription can increase production of antimicrobial proteins, enzymes and metabolites. Chitinases and glucanases can act on susceptible microbial wall components or release additional elicitors. Production of specialized defense metabolites depends on the plant species and the biosynthetic pathways available. These processes connect PTI to secondary metabolism and the barrier mechanisms covered in the previous node. They do not require every immune response to induce exactly the same suite of genes or chemicals.

9. PTI and ETI are cooperating immune layers

A microbial pattern and a pathogen effector differ in their biological role. A microbial pattern may arise from a structural or housekeeping molecule. An effector is a molecule delivered or released by an interacting organism that alters host biology and can promote colonization. Certain effectors suppress aspects of surface-receptor signaling, whereas intracellular plant immune receptors can detect effectors or their consequences. Effector-triggered immunity, ETI, is developed in the next chapter. Here the essential distinction is the receptor mode and the relationship between the two types of response.

The historical zig-zag model, associated with Jones and Dangl's 2006 synthesis, organized the interaction into recognition of microbial patterns, effector-mediated suppression, recognition of effectors and subsequent evolutionary changes in host and pathogen. It remains a useful framework for understanding selection and immune evasion. It should not be treated as a precise time-series diagram that describes every cell in every infection. Receptors, effectors and defense outputs can operate concurrently, and the strength and timing of their interactions vary across systems.

Work published in 2021 by Ngou and colleagues and by Yuan and colleagues provided evidence that surface and intracellular receptor-mediated immunity cooperate. Studies combining immune activation modes or examining mutants deficient in surface receptor systems showed that activation of intracellular receptors does not simply render surface receptor pathways irrelevant. Reinforcement of shared defense machinery can contribute substantially to a strong response. In particular, ROS generation and components of surface signaling are important in experimental examples of combined immunity.

One way to examine cooperation is a two-by-two factorial design with an independently controllable PTI elicitor and an independently controllable ETI trigger. Suppose illustrative normalized response values are 1 for neither input, 4 for PTI only, 6 for ETI only and 15 for both. Under an additive absolute-response model, the combined prediction is 4 + 6 − 1 = 9. The observed combined response is 6 units above this prediction. This demonstrates a positive interaction on that particular scale in the hypothetical dataset. It does not show that every immune output must be synergistic, and a different scale or nonlinear saturation model may change the analysis.

The distinction between an early immune marker and resistance is especially important when both modes of recognition are active. Rapid ROS, transcriptional induction, localized wall reinforcement and cell death can contribute in different proportions. A strong signal does not invariably predict a lower pathogen population. Localized cell death may be associated with certain intracellular immune responses but is not a defining or obligatory outcome of PTI. Disease limitation should be assessed directly rather than inferred from the visual intensity of a defense phenotype.

A plant allocates resources to growth, reproduction, maintenance and defense. Activation of immune responses changes metabolism and signaling, sometimes at a cost to growth in the absence of disease. Shared co-receptors, hormonal cross-talk, negative feedback and inducible expression help regulate these commitments. In crop plants, increasing receptor responsiveness may be useful in an appropriate pathogen context, but a phenotype measured in one controlled environment does not guarantee improved field yield across environments. The resistance benefit, physiological cost and breadth of protection need separate evaluation.

The main logic of this chapter is therefore a chain of experimentally distinguishable events: a pattern becomes accessible, a compatible surface receptor complex assembles or changes activity, intracellular networks transmit the signal, and cells alter physiological behavior and defense gene expression. Distinct receptor inputs can converge on shared machinery, and the effectiveness of the response depends on pathogen behavior and host context. This framework permits predictions about mutants, ligand combinations, time courses and tissue-specific responses without assuming that immunity is a single universal linear pathway.

10. Experimental reasoning: from perception to resistance

A well-designed experiment begins by identifying the causal claim to be tested. If the claim is that FLS2 recognizes flg22, the most informative measurements concern ligand specificity and receptor-dependent response. If the claim is that RBOHD contributes to pathogen restriction, a ROS measurement alone is insufficient; pathogen entry or multiplication should also be assessed. If the claim concerns a relationship between PTI and ETI, both modes of activation should be varied rather than inferred from a single infection.

Consider a hypothetical mutant with reduced flg22-induced ROS. At least four explanations are plausible: ligand recognition is impaired, receptor-to-oxidase signaling is impaired, oxidase capacity itself is impaired, or the detection chemistry is altered. The experimenter can measure receptor abundance, test a different elicitor, examine calcium and MAPK outputs, and compare ROS detection methods. Restoration by a wild-type transgene strengthens a genetic explanation, but does not on its own distinguish all mechanistic possibilities. Converging evidence from different assays is more informative than collecting many replicates of only one assay.

Time courses reveal features that a single endpoint hides. A mutant may have a delayed rather than reduced response. If wild type reaches maximum ROS at ten minutes and the mutant reaches the same maximum at twenty minutes, comparing them only at ten minutes exaggerates the apparent defect in amplitude. The area under the response curve, peak time and decay rate provide different descriptors. Sampling intervals should be short enough to resolve rapid events and long enough to include recovery.

For a baseline-subtracted response sampled at 0, 5, 10 and 15 minutes with values 0, 8, 12 and 4 arbitrary units, the trapezoidal area is 5(0+8)/2 + 5(8+12)/2 + 5(12+4)/2 = 20 + 50 + 40 = 110 arbitrary-unit minutes. A second trace with the same peak but faster decline can have smaller integrated response. This calculation approximates the integral between measurements; unobserved sharp changes and unequal intervals affect accuracy. The area is not a concentration unless the signal is calibrated.

Bacterial growth is often represented as colony-forming units per unit tissue area or mass. A tenfold change corresponds to one log10 unit. If two genotypes have initial internal populations of 10³ colony-forming units per square centimeter, but after two days have 10⁶ and 10⁵ respectively, the difference at the final time is one log unit. Growth relative to initial population is 1000-fold and 100-fold respectively. If the initial populations were different, final counts alone would not isolate multiplication from entry. This example is hypothetical and illustrates why normalization to starting burden can be essential.

Biological replicates should represent independent plants or independent experimental units. Several images of a single leaf or multiple measurements from the same leaf disc can characterize within-sample variation, but they are not automatically independent biological replicates. Averaging technical readings within an independent unit may be appropriate before comparing groups. Randomization, matched conditions and reporting of variability help prevent misleading conclusions. A dramatic difference in one photographed leaf is not by itself convincing evidence of a general genotype effect.

When a mutant loses flg22 and chitin responses, a useful next step is to examine general cellular function and a stimulus that enters the network by a different route. Loss of all responses could reflect tissue death, altered development or a broad signaling defect rather than a shared immune receptor. A normal housekeeping process does not guarantee every aspect of physiology is intact, but viability measurements provide important context. Complementation, multiple independent mutant alleles and comparable genetic backgrounds further improve the causal interpretation.

The same reasoning applies to inhibitors. A compound that reduces ROS may inhibit the oxidase, quench ROS, interfere with the detection reagent or injure the tissue. A solvent control excludes only the vehicle as an explanation. Dose-response analysis, independent measurement, reversibility and a genetic test of the proposed target provide stronger evidence. The lesson for Part C is to distinguish an intervention from its interpretation: the reported measurement is an observation, while placement of a component within a pathway is a model supported by appropriately discriminating tests.

Misconceptions and examination traps

PTI is antibody-mediated
Plants rely on inherited immune receptor systems rather than vertebrate antibody production; priming does not imply clonal selection.
MAMP means a pathogen is definitely present
Beneficial and neutral microorganisms can possess the same patterns. Recognition alone does not determine ecological outcome.
DAMPs originate in microbial cells
They arise from host molecules exposed, released or processed during damage.
flg22 is the whole flagellum
It is an experimental peptide representing a recognized region of flagellin.
All surface receptors possess their own kinase
RLPs can signal through associated kinase partners.
BAK1 determines every ligand specificity
BAK1 is a shared co-receptor; ligand-selective receptors and complex composition matter.
BIK1 binds extracellular flagellin directly
BIK1 is a cytoplasmic kinase, not the primary extracellular ligand receptor.
Chitin is cellulose
Chitin consists of N-acetylglucosamine, whereas cellulose contains glucose.
Rice and Arabidopsis chitin receptors have identical organization
CEBiP–OsCERK1 and LYK5–CERK1 are related but distinct systems.
RBOHD produces hydroxyl radicals directly
It transfers electrons to extracellular oxygen to generate superoxide; downstream reactions generate other ROS.
ROS is always damaging
Regulated production can contribute to signaling; excessive accumulation can cause damage.
Kinase protein abundance proves kinase activation
Activation depends on catalytic and regulatory state; measure phosphorylation or activity with proper controls.
Callose is lignin
Callose is a beta-1,3-glucan, whereas lignin is a phenolic polymer.
Stomata are only passive infection portals
Guard cells can actively close in response to immune signals.
PTI becomes irrelevant when ETI occurs
Evidence supports cooperation between surface and intracellular immune pathways; neither is universally dispensable.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

Which ligand is a host-derived damage signal?

  1. flg22
  2. elf18
  3. Oligogalacturonides
  4. Chitin
Answer and explanation

C. Oligogalacturonides. Oligogalacturonides arise from host pectin degradation. The other options represent microbial-derived patterns.

2. Part B

Which receptor is the canonical Arabidopsis flg22 sensor?

  1. FLS2
  2. EFR
  3. CERK1
  4. BRI1
Answer and explanation

A. FLS2. FLS2 perceives flg22. EFR perceives elf18, CERK1 participates in chitin perception and BRI1 responds to brassinosteroids.

3. Part B

What distinguishes an RLP from a typical RLK?

  1. Absence of every extracellular domain
  2. Absence of a conventional intracellular kinase domain
  3. Obligatory nuclear localization
  4. Binding only DNA
Answer and explanation

B. Absence of a conventional intracellular kinase domain. RLPs can participate in perception but recruit kinase partners; they are not necessarily signaling-inactive.

4. Part B

BAK1 is best described as:

  1. An antibody
  2. A chitin oligomer
  3. A bacterial effector
  4. A shared surface co-receptor kinase
Answer and explanation

D. A shared surface co-receptor kinase. BAK1 participates in several RLK complexes, including FLS2 and BRI1 systems. Its participation does not make ligand specificities identical.

5. Part B

Which protein is a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase?

  1. BIK1
  2. FLS2
  3. Chitin
  4. Callose
Answer and explanation

A. BIK1. BIK1 is an intracellular kinase associated with immune receptor complexes; the others are a membrane receptor or polysaccharides.

6. Part B

The major initial product of oxygen reduction by RBOHD is:

  1. ATP
  2. Hydroxyl radical
  3. Superoxide
  4. Molecular nitrogen
Answer and explanation

C. Superoxide. RBOHD transfers electrons to oxygen across the membrane, generating superoxide, which can subsequently dismutate.

7. Part B

Which is a representative Arabidopsis chitin perception complex?

  1. FLS2–BRI1
  2. LYK5–CERK1
  3. COI1–JAZ
  4. PYL–PP2C
Answer and explanation

B. LYK5–CERK1. LYK5 and CERK1 contribute to chitin recognition and signaling. The other pairs represent distinct regulatory systems or an incorrect combination.

8. Part B

Which polymer is callose?

  1. Beta-1,4-linked N-acetylglucosamine
  2. Alpha-1,4-glucan
  3. Phenolic polymer
  4. Beta-1,3-glucan
Answer and explanation

D. Beta-1,3-glucan. Callose is predominantly beta-1,3-linked glucose. The first describes chitin and the third lignin.

9. Part B

Which observation most directly assesses MAPK activation?

  1. Total chlorophyll
  2. Normalized activation-loop phosphorylation
  3. Leaf mass alone
  4. Stomatal density alone
Answer and explanation

B. Normalized activation-loop phosphorylation. MAPK activation is commonly assessed via phosphorylation or catalytic activity, not unrelated leaf traits.

10. Part B

The principal distinction between PTI and ETI concerns:

  1. Receptor-mediated recognition mode
  2. Presence versus absence of all ROS
  3. Plant versus animal species
  4. Whether all cells die
Answer and explanation

A. Receptor-mediated recognition mode. Surface pattern perception and predominantly intracellular effector recognition are different modes that share many outputs.

11. Part C

A mutant loses flg22-induced ROS but responds normally to chitin. Which hypothesis is favored?

  1. General loss of tissue viability
  2. Loss of all NADPH oxidase activity
  3. Defect in the flg22-specific perception route
  4. Loss of every immune receptor
Answer and explanation

C. Defect in the flg22-specific perception route. Preserved chitin response argues against a universal inability to produce ROS. A specific input-route defect is plausible but requires further genetic tests.

12. Part C

A ligand increases co-recovery of BAK1 with FLS2. What does co-immunoprecipitation alone establish?

  1. Association under assay conditions
  2. Direct binding of every recovered protein to peptide
  3. Identity of all phosphorylation sites
  4. Complete disease resistance
Answer and explanation

A. Association under assay conditions. Protein complexes can contain bridging partners; association does not by itself prove direct molecular contact or downstream physiological outcome.

13. Part C

At L = 60 nM and Kd = 20 nM, what is simple one-site occupancy?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.67
  4. 0.75
Answer and explanation

D. 0.75. 60/(60+20)=0.75. This is an equilibrium occupancy model, not necessarily the fractional physiological response.

14. Part C

Control phospho/total MPK values are 10/50 and treated values are 40/100. What is the ratio change?

  1. Fourfold
  2. Twofold
  3. No change
  4. Half
Answer and explanation

B. Twofold. The ratios are 0.2 and 0.4, so normalized phosphorylation rises twofold. The raw band changes fourfold.

15. Part C

A mutant lacks ROS but retains the earliest calcium peak. Which is the most cautious interpretation?

  1. RBOHD must be the calcium channel
  2. All calcium signaling depends on ROS
  3. The initial measured calcium response can occur without the missing ROS output
  4. ETI is absent
Answer and explanation

C. The initial measured calcium response can occur without the missing ROS output. The perturbation separates the measured early calcium event from the oxidative output; later feedback may still involve ROS.

16. Part C

Surface inoculation reveals a mutant defect, but infiltration gives similar growth in wild type and mutant. Which contribution is supported?

  1. A barrier or entry-related contribution
  2. Universal failure of internal growth control
  3. Failure of bacterial translation
  4. Absence of chitin
Answer and explanation

A. A barrier or entry-related contribution. Bypassing epidermal entry reduces the difference, implicating an entry-related process under the tested conditions.

17. Part C

Control delta Ct is 7 and treated delta Ct is 4. Under equal perfect efficiency, what is relative expression?

  1. 3-fold
  2. 4-fold
  3. 16-fold
  4. 8-fold
Answer and explanation

D. 8-fold. Delta-delta Ct is −3. Therefore 2³=8, assuming stable normalization and the stated efficiency.

18. Part C

A hypothetical ROS trace has baseline-subtracted values 0, 10 and 0 at 0, 5 and 10 min. What is trapezoidal area?

  1. 25
  2. 50
  3. 100
  4. 10
Answer and explanation

B. 50. Each five-minute interval contributes 25 arbitrary-unit minutes; the total is 50.

19. Part C

A combined response is 14, with baseline 2, PTI alone 5 and ETI alone 7. What is the excess over an additive absolute model?

  1. 0
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. 6
Answer and explanation

C. 4. The additive prediction is 5+7−2=10. Observed minus predicted equals 4 units; the interpretation depends on response scale.

20. Part C

A callose-deficient plant unexpectedly restricts a pathogen better. What should be tested next?

  1. Whether compensatory defense changes accompany loss of callose
  2. Assume callose never contributes to immunity
  3. Discard all mutant data
  4. Assume callose is chitin
Answer and explanation

A. Whether compensatory defense changes accompany loss of callose. A mutation can change multiple outputs, including compensatory defenses. Genetic rescue and additional pathway measurements can distinguish mechanisms.

Revision and mastery

Distinct surface receptor complexes recognize accessible microbial or host damage patterns. They activate interacting kinase, calcium and ROS networks, causing changes in guard-cell physiology, wall reinforcement and transcription. PTI and ETI can reinforce one another. Assess resistance using pathogen burden as well as signaling measurements.

Active recall

  1. Distinguish MAMP, PAMP and DAMP using one example each.
  2. Sketch an RLK and an RLP signaling complex.
  3. Explain why fls2 and cerk1 mutants can have complementary elicitor responses.
  4. Trace FLS2 activation through BAK1 and BIK1 to RBOHD.
  5. Explain the direction of electron transfer through RBOHD.
  6. Distinguish calcium regulation from direct kinase regulation of RBOHD.
  7. Explain why phospho/total MAPK is useful.
  8. Predict how ion efflux changes guard-cell turgor.
  9. Distinguish callose, cellulose and chitin.
  10. Explain how surface inoculation and infiltration test different stages.
  11. Describe evidence supporting PTI–ETI cooperation.
  12. Explain why ROS amplitude alone cannot establish disease resistance.

Mastery checklist

  • Identify receptor classes and distinguish their functions.
  • Draw and explain early signaling with correct membrane topology.
  • Interpret receptor mutants and properly normalized time courses.
  • Distinguish signaling markers from resistance endpoints.
