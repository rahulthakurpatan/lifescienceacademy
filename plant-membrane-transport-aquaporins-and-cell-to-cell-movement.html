1. From water potential to selective transport

In the preceding chapter, water potential explained why water moves between compartments. That principle alone cannot explain why a living root accumulates potassium against its concentration gradient, why a guard cell changes its turgor in minutes, or how sucrose reaches a neighboring cell without entering the extracellular space. These events depend on selective membranes and intercellular connections. A membrane is not merely a wall: it determines which solutes move, how fast they move, and whether their movement requires energy.

The plant plasma membrane separates the cytosol from the apoplast, whereas the tonoplast separates the cytosol from the vacuole. The cell wall is porous to many small molecules but is not equivalent to the selectively permeable plasma membrane. The vacuole can occupy most of a mature plant cell, making the tonoplast central to ion storage, osmotic regulation and cytosolic homeostasis. A root epidermal cell can therefore maintain three distinct environments: soil-facing apoplast, cytosol and vacuolar lumen.

Phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer whose hydrophobic interior resists passage of charged and most polar molecules. Small nonpolar gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross more readily, although membrane composition and proteins also affect gas permeability. Water can cross a lipid bilayer slowly and can move much faster through aquaporins. Ions such as K⁺, NO₃⁻ and Ca²⁺ require protein-mediated routes because shedding their hydration shell and entering the hydrophobic core is energetically unfavorable.

Three questions for any transport problem

Which membrane is involved? What is the direction of the electrochemical or water-potential gradient? Is the protein a channel, carrier or energy-coupled pump?

2. Electrochemical potential: predicting direction

Concentration differences alone predict movement only for an uncharged ideal solute. An ion also responds to electrical potential. Define membrane voltage Vm = ψin − ψout. For transfer of one mole from outside to inside, the free-energy change is:

ΔGin = RT ln(ain/aout) + zFVm

R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T = absolute temperature; z = ionic charge; F ≈ 96,485 C mol⁻¹. Activities a can be approximated by concentrations in dilute solutions.

A negative ΔG means inward movement is thermodynamically favorable through an available passive pathway. A positive value means inward movement requires coupling to a favorable process. It does not mean that the membrane is permeable: a closed channel or missing transporter can prevent even favorable movement. For outward movement reverse the sign of ΔG.

At equilibrium, ΔG = 0, giving the Nernst potential Eion = (RT/zF) ln(aout/ain). At 25°C, E ≈ (59.16 mV/z) log10(aout/ain). For K⁺ with 10 mM outside and 100 mM inside, EK ≈ −59 mV. At Vm = −120 mV, inward K⁺ movement is favorable despite the outward concentration gradient: ΔG ≈ 5.71 − 11.58 = −5.87 kJ mol⁻¹. At −40 mV, the direction reverses.

For an anion the voltage term has the opposite sign. Negative cytosolic voltage opposes inward anion movement. This explains why nitrate uptake often uses proton coupling rather than relying on a simple anion channel. For neutral sucrose, z = 0; only the activity ratio contributes to its direct transfer free energy.

The resting membrane potential is not identical to the Nernst potential of one ion. It reflects electrogenic pumps and several membrane conductances. Opening a selective channel tends to move Vm toward that ion's equilibrium potential, but the actual change depends on competing currents and regulation.

3. Channels, carriers and pumps

Transport proteins can be classified by mechanism rather than by the molecule they transport. Channels provide an aqueous pathway through the membrane and permit rapid passive movement when open. Selectivity filters distinguish ions according to size, coordination chemistry and hydration energetics. Gating responds to voltage, ligands, mechanical tension, phosphorylation or other conditions. A channel cannot maintain uphill net transport using the transported ion's own gradient.

Carriers bind substrates and expose binding sites alternately to opposite sides of the membrane. Alternating access prevents the carrier from forming a continuously open pore. Facilitated uniport moves a substrate downhill, whereas symport and antiport couple the movements of two or more species. Carriers have finite turnover, often show saturation with increasing substrate and may be inhibited by competing substrates. Channel currents may also saturate or display complex concentration dependence, so saturation alone is not absolute proof of a carrier.

Primary active pumps couple transport directly to an energy source such as ATP hydrolysis or pyrophosphate hydrolysis. Secondary active transport does not hydrolyze ATP at the cotransporter itself; it uses a gradient established elsewhere. This distinction is experimentally important: removal of ATP may inhibit both mechanisms, directly for a pump and indirectly for a cotransporter.

4. The plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase: the plant transport battery

Historical electrophysiological and acidification studies established that plant membranes actively export protons. Unlike animal cells, where the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is a major generator of ion gradients, plant plasma membranes mainly use P-type H⁺-ATPases. ATP hydrolysis drives H⁺ export from cytosol to apoplast, making the exterior more acidic and the cell interior electrically negative. A typical plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase cycle transfers a proton through ATP-dependent conformational changes involving a transient phosphorylated aspartate residue; this distinguishes P-type pumps from V-type ATPases.

In the E1-like conformation the transporter binds a cytosolic proton. ATP-dependent phosphorylation changes the accessibility and affinity of the proton-binding site, enabling release outward in an E2-like conformation. Dephosphorylation and structural resetting complete the cycle. Exact microscopic steps vary with the pump and its regulatory state. The net reaction is ATP + H₂O + H⁺cyt → ADP + Pi + H⁺apo (schematic; biochemical proton balance depends on ionization).

The C-terminal regulatory domain of many AHA pumps is autoinhibitory. Phosphorylation of a conserved penultimate threonine promotes binding of 14-3-3 proteins and activation. Blue light can activate guard-cell H⁺-ATPases through phototropin-dependent signaling. Proton export hyperpolarizes the membrane, favors inward K⁺ transport, and helps establish osmotic conditions for water entry and stomatal opening. Auxin-induced wall acidification can also facilitate cell expansion by activating acid-dependent wall loosening processes, provided turgor and wall extensibility permit growth.

The pump does not directly import potassium, nitrate or sucrose. It provides the proton motive force and voltage that other transporters exploit. Increased proton pumping may not always increase net nutrient uptake if another transporter is limiting, if ATP supply is insufficient, or if the external environment buffers proton concentration strongly.

5. Proton motive force and secondary active transport

For proton entry, using pH = −log10aH⁺, ΔGH,in = 2.303RT(pHout − pHin) + FVm. Both an acidic exterior and a negative interior favor entry. The proton gradient stores free energy rather than ATP molecules. A symporter can harness this free energy by linking proton entry to otherwise unfavorable uptake of another solute.

For a symporter transporting n protons and one neutral solute inward per cycle, ΔGcycle = nΔGH,in + RT ln(Sin/Sout). Transport is favorable in the specified direction when the sum is negative, not necessarily when each component is negative. At equilibrium the sum is zero. Real transport is limited by leaks, finite turnover and metabolic consumption, so a theoretical accumulation ratio is an upper bound, not a guaranteed measured concentration.

Consider a cytosol at pH 7 and an apoplast at pH 5.5 with Vm = −120 mV at 25°C. Proton entry contributes approximately −8.56 kJ mol⁻¹ chemically and −11.58 kJ mol⁻¹ electrically, totaling −20.14 kJ mol⁻¹. For a 1:1 H⁺/neutral-sucrose symporter, the ideal equilibrium accumulation ratio is exp(20.14/2.478) ≈ 3.4 × 10³. A 100-fold sucrose accumulation costs only RT ln(100) ≈ 11.41 kJ mol⁻¹, so the coupled cycle is favorable in this example.

For nitrate, the negative voltage opposes anion entry, and the electrical terms of H⁺ and NO₃⁻ cancel in a hypothetical 1:1 cycle. Different nitrate transporters can have different coupling characteristics, so always use the stoichiometry supplied by a question. NPF6.3/NRT1.1 is a nitrate transporter and sensor; NRT2 family proteins contribute to high-affinity uptake, often requiring partner proteins. Affinity and capacity are distinct properties: a lower apparent Km indicates higher apparent affinity, not necessarily a larger maximal flux.

Antiporters use the favorable movement of one ion in the opposite direction to another. At the plasma membrane SOS1 helps export Na⁺ coupled to H⁺ entry during salt stress. Tonoplast NHX exchangers use proton gradients to exchange cations with the vacuole; they contribute to K⁺ homeostasis, pH control and, depending on context, Na⁺ sequestration. A transporter may reverse if the combined gradients reverse; its usual physiological direction is not an immutable property.

6. The tonoplast and vacuolar homeostasis

A mature vacuole is an osmotic reservoir, a storage compartment, a site of degradation and a buffer against fluctuations in cytosolic ions. Its membrane contains two important primary proton pumps: V-type H⁺-ATPase, which uses ATP, and H⁺-translocating pyrophosphatase (V-PPase), which uses inorganic pyrophosphate. Both normally pump protons from cytosol to vacuolar lumen. Unlike the plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase, their physiological destination is the vacuolar interior.

V-type ATPases use a rotary mechanism involving ATP hydrolysis in a peripheral catalytic sector and proton movement through a membrane sector. They do not use the P-type phosphorylated-aspartate cycle. V-PPase couples PPi hydrolysis to H⁺ transport. These pumps establish a proton electrochemical gradient that supports secondary transport of ions, organic acids and other metabolites into or out of the vacuole. The exact voltage depends on other permeabilities, including counterion channels.

Vacuolar sequestration can reduce cytosolic toxicity during salt stress, but retaining Na⁺ in the vacuole requires compatible osmotic adjustment in the cytoplasm to avoid damaging water-potential differences. Calcium accumulation in the vacuole contributes to maintenance of very low resting cytosolic Ca²⁺; controlled release and reuptake help shape calcium signals. Calcium pumps and Ca²⁺/H⁺ exchangers contribute differently to this regulation.

Water crosses the tonoplast through TIP aquaporins as well as the lipid bilayer. Solute accumulation can lower vacuolar water potential and promote water entry until pressure and osmotic effects balance. The pumps move ions, not bulk water; the water response follows its own potential gradient.

7. Aquaporins: water permeability without water pumping

Rapid water transport in biological membranes was explained by the discovery of aquaporins. Peter Agre and colleagues identified a water channel in erythrocytes and demonstrated water permeability by expressing it in Xenopus oocytes; plant aquaporins belong to the wider major intrinsic protein (MIP) family. Their role is particularly important in plants because water flux must adjust quickly across plasma membranes and tonoplasts during growth, transpiration and stress.

A typical aquaporin monomer contains six membrane-spanning helices and two half-helices carrying conserved NPA motifs. Four monomers usually assemble as a tetramer; each monomer contains its own principal water pore. The narrow aromatic/arginine selectivity region and electrostatic features around the NPA region allow single-file passage of water while normally preventing passage of hydrated ions and disrupting proton hopping through a continuous hydrogen-bond network. The protein is not an ATP-powered motor.

Plant aquaporins are divided into subfamilies such as PIPs (plasma membrane intrinsic proteins), TIPs (tonoplast intrinsic proteins), NIPs (nodulin 26-like intrinsic proteins), SIPs and XIPs in species that possess them. PIPs contribute to cell and tissue water permeability; TIPs facilitate vacuolar exchange. Some members transport other small neutral molecules, including glycerol, urea, H₂O₂, CO₂ or boric acid, depending on isoform and selectivity. It is incorrect to infer that every aquaporin transports every such substrate or that aquaporins are ordinary ion channels.

Activity can be regulated by phosphorylation, cytosolic pH, Ca²⁺, redox signals, trafficking, heteromerization and abundance. During oxygen deprivation, cytosolic acidification can close certain root PIPs, reducing root hydraulic conductivity. Drought or salinity may alter expression or trafficking in a tissue- and time-dependent manner. Aquaporin activity is therefore not universally increased or decreased by stress.

Water flux can be represented as Jv = Lp(Ψout − Ψin) for a simplified membrane with appropriate osmotic assumptions, where Jv is inward volume flux per area and Lp is hydraulic conductivity. For a membrane partially permeable to solute, the reflection coefficient σ modifies the effective osmotic term: Jv = Lp[(Pout − Pin) − σ(πout − πin)]. A perfectly reflected solute has σ = 1, whereas σ approaches zero for a freely permeating solute in this simplified treatment. Opening aquaporins primarily raises water permeability, not the equilibrium water-potential difference. If driving force is zero, increased permeability cannot produce sustained net water flux.

8. Apoplast, symplast and repeated membrane crossing

Plant cells are encased in walls but are not isolated. The apoplast is the extracellular continuum of cell walls and intercellular spaces, including xylem conduits. The symplast is the interconnected cytoplasm of living cells linked by plasmodesmata. The transmembrane route involves successive crossings of plasma membranes and may include tonoplast crossings, depending on the path. A molecule can change routes; these terms describe segments of a trajectory rather than mutually exclusive identities of a solute.

Consider a mineral ion outside a root hair. It can diffuse through the hydrated wall without crossing a plasma membrane. Once it enters the cytoplasm through a specific transporter, it can move through open plasmodesmata into adjacent cytoplasm. It may subsequently be exported into the apoplast again. Cell walls bind some ions and their porosity influences movement, so apoplastic passage is not entirely unrestricted. In roots the lignified Casparian strip blocks much of the unrestricted apoplastic bypass at the endodermis, requiring selective membrane entry before arrival in the stele by the typical radial route.

Symplastic movement does not require crossing a lipid bilayer at every cell boundary; plasmodesmata provide a cytoplasmic connection. However, initial entry into the symplast, later exit, and compartmental exchange remain membrane-dependent. Bulk movement through tissues depends on driving forces and pathway resistances. Water moves through both routes; their relative contributions vary with species, root age, driving force and anatomy.

9. Plasmodesmata: structure, gating and selective trafficking

Plasmodesmata are narrow plasma-membrane-lined channels traversing the wall between plant cells. A central desmotubule is derived from endoplasmic reticulum and remains connected to ER on either side. Cytoplasmic sleeves around the desmotubule provide important pathways for intercellular transfer. Their fine structure varies with development; a simplified sleeve diagram is useful but should not be interpreted as a fixed open cylinder in all tissues.

Primary plasmodesmata originate during cytokinesis when ER strands become trapped in the developing cell plate. Secondary plasmodesmata form across existing walls later. Simple pores may become branched or reorganized as tissues mature. The density, architecture and opening state collectively determine tissue-level symplastic connectivity.

Callose, a β-1,3-glucan, accumulates around plasmodesmatal neck regions. Callose synthases promote deposition, which generally restricts connectivity, whereas β-1,3-glucanases remove callose and can increase permeability. Developmental signals, immune responses, calcium and reactive oxygen species can regulate these processes. Plasmodesmata can act as adjustable gates that separate symplastic domains rather than permanently uniting every cell of the plant.

Small metabolites, ions and some signaling molecules can pass through open plasmodesmata. Larger molecules are constrained by effective size exclusion, shape and interactions with transport machinery. Specific proteins and RNAs can move in regulated ways; examples include SHORT-ROOT protein movement from stele to endodermis and movement of some small RNAs between cells. FT protein movement through phloem is an important long-distance flowering signal; local entry and exit are distinct trafficking steps. Plant viruses may encode movement proteins that modify plasmodesmatal permeability. This does not imply that all macromolecules freely diffuse through every pore.

Fluorescent tracer studies often compare passage of small dyes with larger dextrans or fluorescent proteins. A size exclusion limit is an operational measurement in a particular tissue and condition, not a single universal molecular weight. Failure of a tracer to move may reflect loading failure, binding, degradation or closure; appropriate controls are essential.

10. Integrated examples: guard cells, roots and phloem

Guard-cell opening and closure

In the light, H⁺-ATPase activation promotes hyperpolarization. Inward-rectifying K⁺ channels permit K⁺ influx, and counterions such as Cl⁻ and malate help maintain charge balance. Solute accumulation lowers guard-cell osmotic potential, water enters, turgor increases and the pore opens because of specialized guard-cell geometry and walls. During ABA signaling, anion channels such as SLAC1 can promote anion efflux and depolarization; outward K⁺ channels such as GORK then facilitate K⁺ loss, water exits and the pore closes. Different species and conditions modify these contributions. Water channels influence response kinetics but are not the source of the driving force.

Root mineral acquisition

Root epidermal and cortical transporters select nutrients from the apoplast. At relatively high external K⁺, channels may support uptake when the voltage is favorable; at low external K⁺, high-affinity systems such as HAK5 are important in Arabidopsis. Nitrate uptake couples to proton motive force. Phosphate uptake frequently involves proton-coupled transporters, and iron uptake mechanisms differ between grasses and many non-grass plants. These mechanisms link nutrient availability, membrane energetics and root architecture.

Phloem loading

In apoplastic loading, SWEET facilitators can release sucrose from exporting cells into the apoplast when the sucrose gradient favors that direction. SUT/SUC symporters then use proton entry to concentrate sucrose in the sieve element–companion cell complex. In symplastic loading, sucrose enters through plasmodesmata; polymer trapping in some species involves conversion to larger raffinose-family sugars, helping limit back diffusion through specialized connections. Not all plants use the same loading strategy. The subsequent long-distance pressure flow is a different process from molecular transport across a membrane.

11. Transport kinetics and experimental interpretation

For a simplified saturable carrier, initial uptake v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]). At [S] = Km, v = Vmax/2. If Vmax = 60 nmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ and Km = 0.2 mM, uptake is 20 at 0.1 mM, 30 at 0.2 mM and 50 at 1 mM. An increase in transporter abundance may increase Vmax without changing intrinsic affinity, whereas a mutation affecting binding may alter apparent Km. Whole-root uptake can contain multiple overlapping transport systems, so fitting a single curve does not necessarily identify one molecular transporter.

Patch-clamp recordings reveal membrane currents and voltage-dependent channel gating. A reversal potential close to the calculated Nernst potential supports ion selectivity; changing external ion concentration and observing the predicted reversal shift strengthens the inference. Current direction depends on charge and convention: inward anion movement corresponds to outward conventional current. An uptake assay measures molecular flux, which is not always identical to electrical current when electroneutral transport contributes.

Heterologous expression in yeast or oocytes can test a candidate transporter's function. Compare expressing cells with empty-vector or uninjected controls under matched gradients, check membrane localization, and verify that observed uptake is not due to altered endogenous transport. In an aquaporin swelling assay, transfer into hypotonic medium promotes water entry; faster swelling at equal osmotic difference indicates increased water permeability. Failure to swell may result from poor membrane expression rather than loss of intrinsic pore function.

Protonophores collapse proton gradients and can inhibit proton-coupled nutrient uptake. They may also alter ATP production and membrane potential, so their effect alone does not establish direct H⁺ coupling. Orthovanadate can inhibit P-type ATPases but has other targets; genetic perturbation, complementation, ATP measurements and pH or voltage monitoring provide stronger evidence. A cytosolic or apoplastic pH sensor reports local pH changes, not necessarily the exact transport stoichiometry.

For cell-to-cell transfer, introduce a tracer into one cell and measure spread to neighbors over time. Compare wild type with altered callose deposition or degradation, normalize initial loading, verify cell viability, and distinguish extracellular leakage from cytoplasmic movement. FRAP measures fluorescence recovery after bleaching, which may reflect diffusion, binding, transport and geometry; it is not a direct count of plasmodesmata.

12. Common misconceptions and reasoning shortcuts

  • A negative membrane voltage favors cation entry, but a sufficiently large opposing concentration gradient can reverse the net direction.
  • Facilitated diffusion uses proteins without direct energy expenditure; secondary active transport uses another gradient and is not synonymous with facilitated diffusion.
  • Aquaporins change the rate of water movement, not the thermodynamic direction or equilibrium by themselves.
  • The plasma membrane proton pump exports H⁺; tonoplast proton pumps normally acidify the vacuole.
  • SWEET proteins facilitate sugar movement along gradients; SUT/SUC proteins use H⁺ coupling. Their direction is determined by gradients and mechanism, not simply by the gene name.
  • Plasmodesmata are selective, regulated connections, not unrestricted holes. Their permeability is not fixed across all cell types.
  • Apoplastic movement does not mean that every solute can bypass selective membranes; endodermal barriers constrain radial flow.
  • A protonophore-induced decline in transport is evidence for energetic dependence but is not, by itself, proof of transporter stoichiometry.

13. Original CSIR-NET and SET practice

Attempt the problems before expanding each explanation. Assume ideal dilute solutions and 25°C unless specified.

1. MCQ — At 25°C, K⁺ is 100 mM inside and 10 mM outside. Vₘ is −120 mV. Which is correct? A. K⁺ enters through an open selective channel; B. K⁺ exits; C. K⁺ is at equilibrium; D. ATP must bind the channel.

Answer and reasoning: A. Eₖ ≈ −59 mV. The more negative membrane potential makes the inward electrochemical free energy negative.

2. MCQ — Which transporter directly hydrolyzes ATP to export protons from plant cytoplasm? A. SWEET; B. PIP; C. AHA; D. SUT.

Answer and reasoning: C. AHA is a P-type H⁺-ATPase; the other proteins facilitate or couple transport.

3. MCQ — Which pump uses pyrophosphate? A. V-PPase; B. V-ATPase; C. AKT1; D. TIP.

Answer and reasoning: A. V-PPase uses PPᵢ hydrolysis to pump H⁺ into the vacuole.

4. MCQ — Which feature is most associated with plasmodesmatal closure? A. Callose removal; B. Callose deposition at necks; C. ATP binding to PIP; D. Increased xylem tension alone.

Answer and reasoning: B. Callose deposition usually reduces the effective pore permeability.

5. MCQ — In an aquaporin-expressing oocyte, hypotonic swelling is faster than in controls. What is demonstrated most directly? A. ATP-driven water transport; B. Increased osmotic water permeability; C. Increased solute concentration necessarily; D. Reversal of osmosis.

Answer and reasoning: B. The same osmotic driving force produces greater water flux.

6. MCQ — Which protein can facilitate sucrose export downhill in apoplastic phloem loading? A. SWEET; B. GORK; C. TIP; D. AHA.

Answer and reasoning: A. SWEET facilitates sugar transport down the sugar gradient; SUT/SUC couples uptake to H⁺.

7. Numerical — Calculate Eₖ for 1 mM outside and 100 mM inside at 25°C.

Answer and reasoning: Eₖ = 59.16 log₁₀(0.01) = −118.32 mV. At Vₘ = −90 mV, outward K⁺ movement is favored.

8. Numerical — At 298 K, pH outside = 6, pH inside = 7, Vₘ = −100 mV. Calculate ΔG for H⁺ entry.

Answer and reasoning: Chemical term = −5.71 kJ/mol; electrical term = −9.65 kJ/mol. Total ≈ −15.36 kJ/mol.

9. Numerical — For that proton gradient, can a 1:1 symporter accumulate neutral sucrose 1000-fold?

Answer and reasoning: RT ln(1000) ≈ 17.12 kJ/mol; sum ≈ +1.76 kJ/mol, unfavorable at that ratio. The ideal equilibrium ratio is exp(15.36/2.478) ≈ 492.

10. Numerical — A membrane has Lₚ = 2 × 10⁻⁷ m s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ and inward water-potential difference 0.3 MPa. What is Jᵥ? If Lₚ doubles?

Answer and reasoning: Initially 6 × 10⁻⁸ m/s; after doubling, 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ m/s at unchanged driving force.

11. Numerical — A carrier has Vmax = 120 units and Kₘ = 2 mM. Find rates at 1 and 6 mM.

Answer and reasoning: At 1 mM: 120/3 = 40. At 6 mM: 720/8 = 90 units.

12. Assertion–reason — Assertion: opening PIPs can accelerate water uptake. Reason: PIPs establish water potential by hydrolyzing ATP.

Answer and reasoning: Assertion true, reason false. PIPs facilitate passive movement; osmotic and pressure differences drive flux.

13. Assertion–reason — Assertion: H⁺-ATPase inhibition can reduce nitrate uptake. Reason: nitrate transport can depend on proton motive force.

Answer and reasoning: Both true and reason explains assertion, although the exact response depends on transporter and conditions.

14. Assertion–reason — Assertion: all cells in a plant organ share one unrestricted symplast. Reason: plasmodesmatal gating changes during development.

Answer and reasoning: Assertion false, reason true. Gating can establish distinct symplastic domains.

15. Data interpretation — A mutant shows normal plasma membrane H⁺ extrusion but reduced sucrose uptake, restored by SUT expression. What defect is supported?

Answer and reasoning: A sucrose transport defect downstream of gradient generation; confirm localization and matched voltage/pH.

16. Data interpretation — A protonophore reduces nitrate uptake by 70%, while respiration and ATP also decrease. Can you infer exact nitrate/H⁺ stoichiometry?

Answer and reasoning: No. Multiple variables changed. Measure flux and electrical/pH gradients under controlled conditions and isolate transporter function.

17. Data interpretation — Small tracer spreads in wild type but remains in the loaded cell after callose induction. What controls are necessary?

Answer and reasoning: Verify equal loading, viability, tracer stability and localization; test whether callose removal restores spread; exclude leakage.

18. Analytical — Why can vacuolar ion storage increase turgor without a water pump?

Answer and reasoning: Ion accumulation lowers osmotic potential. Water moves passively until pressure and osmotic terms balance; tonoplast aquaporins change rate.

19. Analytical — Compare a K⁺ channel and a K⁺/H⁺ symporter at extremely low external K⁺.

Answer and reasoning: Channel entry becomes energetically unfavorable when Eₖ is more negative than Vₘ. Proton coupling can supply additional free energy for uptake.

20. Analytical — Why is a negative membrane potential not enough to conclude that nitrate enters passively?

Answer and reasoning: Nitrate has negative charge, so voltage opposes inward movement; chemical and electrical terms must be combined.

21. Analytical — Trace sucrose from a source cell through an apoplastic loading route.

Answer and reasoning: Export via a gradient-dependent facilitator such as SWEET; H⁺-coupled uptake via SUT/SUC; osmotic water entry and pressure-driven transport follow.

22. Analytical — A TIP mutation lowers vacuolar water permeability but equilibrium solute gradients are unchanged. Predict the phenotype of an osmotic step.

Answer and reasoning: Slower vacuolar volume adjustment, with potentially similar final equilibrium under otherwise unchanged conditions.

23. Analytical — Why might two cells show different plasmodesmatal permeability despite having the same number of pores?

Answer and reasoning: Differences in neck callose, architecture, gating, cytoplasmic sleeve and cargo-specific regulation can alter conductivity.

24. Integrative — Explain the connection between proton pumping, K⁺ uptake and guard-cell turgor.

Answer and reasoning: H⁺ export hyperpolarizes; K⁺ enters through favorable channels, counterions accumulate, osmotic potential decreases and water enters, increasing turgor.

25. Integrative — Why does root endodermal barrier formation make membrane transporters especially important?

Answer and reasoning: It reduces apoplastic bypass, so many ions must cross selective membranes before entering the stele; symplastic and transmembrane flow become important.

14. Rapid revision and concept map

ATP hydrolysis at the plasma membrane drives proton export → the apoplast acidifies and the cytosol becomes relatively negative → proton entry and electrical gradients power secondary uptake and ion-channel flux → cytosolic and vacuolar solute concentrations change → water responds to water potential through lipid and aquaporin pathways → turgor, growth and long-distance transport adjust. Between cells, apoplastic movement and regulated plasmodesmatal transfer determine the route. At the tonoplast, ATP and PPi drive proton accumulation in the vacuole and support solute storage.
