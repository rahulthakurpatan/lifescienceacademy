1. The problem of continuing respiratory oxidation

A germinating seed may grow for several days without any effective photosynthesis. Its stored carbon supports elongation of the root, synthesis of membranes, protein turnover and uptake of mineral ions. These activities consume ATP even though the seed has not yet developed a photosynthetically competent leaf. A mature leaf faces the same energetic problem at night, and its nonphotosynthetic cells face it during the day as well. The energy contained in organic molecules is not itself ATP. Cells must transfer a controlled portion of that energy to a form that can support work, while maintaining a supply of oxidized cofactors for subsequent reactions. The mitochondrial electron transport chain connects these two requirements. It allows reducing equivalents from respiratory metabolism to be oxidized and uses the energy released during electron transfer to establish an electrochemical difference across a membrane.

The preceding chapter follows respiratory carbon into acetyl-CoA and the citric acid cycle. The cycle generates reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, NADH, through several different reactions. Succinate oxidation also reduces the flavin cofactor of succinate dehydrogenase. To understand the problem posed by these products, imagine a matrix in which NADH is produced continuously but never oxidized. The available pool of NAD+ would become progressively depleted. Reactions requiring NAD+ would eventually slow even if organic substrate remained abundant. The fact that ATP is needed does not by itself restore the missing cofactor. NADH must lose its reducing equivalents to an acceptor, and the resulting NAD+ must return to metabolism. The electron transport chain performs this regeneration through a succession of carriers that terminate, under aerobic conditions, in the reduction of molecular oxygen.

Respiration in a green plant is often misunderstood because photosynthesis and mitochondrial respiration have opposite overall gas-exchange signatures. A chloroplast can release oxygen during water oxidation while a mitochondrion in the same cell consumes oxygen at cytochrome c oxidase. There is no contradiction: the processes occur in distinct membrane systems, and the source of energy for each differs. Chloroplast photochemistry raises the energy of electrons using absorbed light. Mitochondria capture some of the energy already available when reduced substrates transfer electrons toward oxygen. In illuminated leaves, the oxygen production of photosynthesis can exceed respiratory oxygen consumption, so the net oxygen signal is positive despite ongoing consumption. Net gas exchange is therefore not equivalent to the mitochondrial respiratory rate. In roots, tubers and many seed tissues, the photosynthetic contribution is absent, making mitochondrial energy metabolism particularly apparent.

The term respiratory chain denotes electron-transfer reactions organized around proteins and mobile carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The chain is not a single polypeptide or a rigid row in which every electron necessarily passes through every numbered protein. Rather, it is a network with distinct entry points and a shared quinone pool. Complex I receives electrons from matrix NADH; Complex II receives reducing equivalents from the oxidation of succinate. Both conventional entry routes deliver electrons to ubiquinone. Ubiquinol subsequently supplies Complex III, which passes electrons to cytochrome c. The reduced cytochrome c delivers them to Complex IV, where oxygen is converted to water. In plant mitochondria, additional NADH- or NADPH-oxidizing enzymes can feed the quinone pool without operating the proton-pumping machinery of Complex I. A separate terminal route, alternative oxidase, can draw electrons from ubiquinol without using Complexes III and IV. This last branch is introduced here only to explain the network; its regulation and physiology are the subject of a later chapter.

To see why this arrangement matters, distinguish electron transport, proton translocation and ATP synthesis. Electron transport is the transfer of reducing equivalents between chemical groups. Proton translocation is the net movement of positive charge and hydrogen ions across the membrane through energy-coupled reactions. ATP synthesis is the formation of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate through the membrane enzyme ATP synthase. The activities are related but not identical. Complex II transfers electrons without directly pumping protons. ATP synthase uses an existing electrochemical difference without accepting electrons from NADH. If the membrane loses its ability to maintain a proton gradient, some electron transfer may continue even when ATP production is inefficient. A student who equates oxygen consumption directly with ATP production will therefore struggle to interpret mitochondrial experiments.

Plant physiology also demands attention to metabolic flexibility. Unlike an idealized closed reaction sequence, a living mitochondrial network receives fluctuating amounts of reducing equivalents from carbon metabolism and, in illuminated C3 leaves, from glycine oxidation during photorespiration. Cells encounter variable oxygen availability, temperature, sugar supply and biosynthetic demand. Consequently, the most useful question is not simply how many molecules of ATP are formed per glucose, but which routes electrons can take under particular conditions, how those routes affect the gradient, and how experiments identify them. The chapter develops the molecular map first, then connects the map to quantitative proton accounting and respiratory observations. ATP synthase catalysis and the detailed costs of ATP synthesis belong to the following chapter.

The biological advantage of a chain rather than a single unrestricted reaction can be appreciated from the chemistry of oxygen. Reduced organic compounds and oxygen have a large difference in electron affinity under appropriate conditions. A direct reaction would not automatically direct energy toward the movement of ions or the regeneration of a particular intracellular cofactor. The organized membrane network instead offers successive catalytic sites, so that substrate oxidation, intermediary electron transfer and the terminal reaction can be coordinated. Some stages are linked to vectorial proton movement. Other stages principally provide a chemical connection between available electron donors and the conserved quinone pool. The distinction between those functions explains why the overall redox reaction is not enough to predict the amount of energy conserved by a mitochondrion.

2. Membrane organization and compartment identity

Before following a single electron, establish the orientation of the mitochondrial membranes. An intact mitochondrion has an outer membrane, an inner membrane, an aqueous intermembrane space between them, and a matrix inside the inner membrane. The inner membrane forms folds called cristae, which increase its surface area and organize many respiratory proteins. The matrix contains enzymes for several respiratory transformations, including most soluble enzymes of the citric acid cycle. The respiratory chain is associated with the inner membrane rather than distributed freely through the matrix. The outer membrane helps define the organelle and supports exchange with the cytosol, but it is not the principal barrier across which the respiratory proton motive force is maintained.

The importance of the inner membrane lies in its selective permeability. A phospholipid bilayer provides a hydrophobic interior that resists the unrestricted movement of hydrated ions. Proteins allow selected molecules and charges to cross through specified routes. If protons were able to move freely across the entire inner membrane, every proton moved outward by respiratory reactions could promptly return without doing useful work. Sustained separation of charge would become difficult. The relatively low passive proton permeability of the inner membrane therefore allows respiratory complexes to establish a difference in proton electrochemical potential between its two aqueous faces.

It is useful to call the matrix-facing surface the N side and the intermembrane-space-facing surface the P side. During conventional forward electron transport, positive charge is displaced toward the P side, and the matrix generally becomes electrically negative relative to the intermembrane space. The abbreviations identify membrane orientation, not universal names for all intracellular compartments. For example, in the thylakoid membrane the side that accumulates protons is the lumen, and ATP is synthesized on the stromal side. Transferring chloroplast orientation directly to a mitochondrion is a common error. In mitochondrial respiration the corresponding proton return is from intermembrane space into matrix, where the catalytic head of ATP synthase operates.

Several respiratory proteins extend into one aqueous compartment while also spanning or associating with the hydrophobic interior of the inner membrane. Complex I has a peripheral arm projecting into the matrix, where soluble NADH can reach its catalytic site, and a membrane arm that participates in proton pumping. Complex II presents its succinate-oxidizing machinery to the matrix and anchors it to the membrane. Complex III interacts with lipid-soluble ubiquinol at membrane-accessible sites and with soluble cytochrome c on the P side. Complex IV accepts electrons from cytochrome c at the P side and receives chemical protons for water formation from the matrix. A diagram of these complexes should not be interpreted as a literal scale model: relative sizes, distances and membrane protein positions vary, and the membrane is a three-dimensional structure.

Ubiquinone is particularly suited to electron transfer among membrane-associated sites because it contains a polar redox-active ring attached to a hydrophobic isoprenoid tail. The tail favors association with the lipid phase, while the ring participates in reversible oxidation and reduction. Cytochrome c, by contrast, is a small water-soluble heme protein associated with the intermembrane-space-facing surface of the inner membrane. It receives electrons from Complex III and transfers them to Complex IV. It would be incorrect to draw cytochrome c as a lipid-soluble quinone traveling through the bilayer. Nor should ubiquinone be drawn as a freely soluble matrix cofactor analogous to NADH. These physical distinctions follow from molecular structure and are essential when locating inhibitor effects.

The outer membrane contains channels that permit exchange of many small solutes between the cytosol and the intermembrane space. This permeability does not make the matrix chemically identical to the cytosol because the inner membrane imposes an additional selective barrier. In particular, NADH itself does not simply diffuse through an intact inner membrane from the cytosol into the matrix. Plants can exploit alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases whose catalytic sites face the outer side of the inner membrane, thereby accessing reducing equivalents outside the matrix through the appropriate compartment. This is different from importing intact cytosolic NADH into the matrix. Metabolic shuttles can also transfer reducing equivalents indirectly, but those pathways are not the same reaction as membrane NADH transport.

Topology becomes especially important in isolated mitochondrial experiments. If the outer membrane ruptures, intermembrane-space proteins such as cytochrome c may be lost, and respiration through the conventional chain may decrease even though the catalytic centers of Complexes III and IV are still present. Conversely, inner membrane damage can collapse the electrochemical gradient while leaving some redox activity measurable. A biochemical preparation cannot be assumed to behave like an intact mitochondrion simply because it contains mitochondrial proteins. Before attributing a low oxygen-consumption rate to a missing respiratory enzyme, consider whether the preparation still contains its mobile carriers, appropriate substrates and an intact coupling membrane.

Cristae are not independent miniature mitochondria. Their membranes are continuous with the inner mitochondrial membrane, and their luminal spaces communicate with the intermembrane space through junctions. This permits extensive respiratory membrane area inside the organelle while retaining the relevant separation from the matrix. The exact local distribution of complexes and protons may vary with membrane geometry and physiological conditions. For examination purposes, a two-compartment representation is usually sufficient to explain the direction of proton translocation, carrier accessibility and electrochemical coupling. The representation is a functional abstraction, not a claim that the concentrations and electrical conditions are identical at every point of a real crista.

A simple orientation exercise clarifies the relationship between structure and function. Suppose a membrane-impermeant inhibitor is introduced on the matrix side of an oriented inner-membrane vesicle. It may reach a matrix-facing enzyme site while leaving a P-side catalytic site inaccessible. If the vesicles are inverted during preparation, the same added reagent can act on a different set of exposed domains. A change in accessibility therefore offers experimental evidence about protein orientation, provided the investigator controls membrane integrity and vesicle orientation. The outcome cannot be interpreted merely by comparing inhibitor concentration. Location is part of the causal mechanism, and a compartment map is the foundation of reliable reasoning about respiratory reactions.

3. Redox chemistry and the direction of electron transfer

Oxidation and reduction are complementary processes. In a redox reaction a donor loses reducing equivalents, while an acceptor gains them. An oxidation reaction cannot proceed in isolation without some corresponding reduction. In the mitochondrial chain, substrate-derived electrons are conveyed between cofactors that alternate between oxidized and reduced states. The oxidation of NADH restores NAD+; the final reduction of oxygen produces water. The respiratory proteins contain intermediary cofactors that provide chemically suitable ways for these transfers to occur, often handling one electron at a time even when the original donor transfers two.

A reduction potential expresses the tendency of a redox couple to accept electrons under specified conditions. The more positive the potential, the stronger the relative tendency of its oxidized member to be reduced under those conditions. If a donor couple has a lower reduction potential than the acceptor couple, the transfer can be energetically favorable. The relationship between potential difference and Gibbs free energy is ΔG = −nFΔE, with ΔE defined as the acceptor reduction potential minus the donor reduction potential, n the number of moles of electrons transferred per mole of reaction, and F approximately 96.485 kilocoulombs per mole of electrons. This equation connects a measurable redox property to the maximum non-expansion work available under the specified thermodynamic conditions.

Consider an illustrative biochemical-standard comparison between the NAD+/NADH couple at about −0.32 V and the oxygen/water couple at about +0.82 V. Their potential difference is about 1.14 V. If two electrons are transferred, the calculated free-energy change is approximately −220 kJ per mole of NADH oxidized under those standard conditions. The negative sign means that the net electron transfer is thermodynamically favorable. It does not specify the fraction of this energy captured by proton translocation, nor does it yield the exact ATP output of a real mitochondrion. Real potentials depend on pH, substrate concentrations and the relative amounts of oxidized and reduced cofactors. The standard calculation is a useful reference for the energetic scale of the process, not an invariant physiological measurement.

The tendency to transfer electrons is conditional on the state of both the donor and the acceptor pools. If most NAD is already in the oxidized form and very little NADH is present, then the rate and thermodynamic driving force of NADH oxidation may differ from those in a matrix rich in NADH. If the quinone pool is predominantly reduced, acceptance of additional electrons by quinone becomes less favorable. These effects are understood through the Nernst relation: for a simple half reaction written as Ox + ne− → Red at fixed pH, E = E°′ + (RT/nF) ln([Ox]/[Red]), using activities in rigorous thermodynamic treatment. As the ratio of oxidized to reduced forms increases, the effective reduction potential rises. If protons explicitly appear in the half reaction and pH changes, their activities must also be accounted for.

The diagrammatic arrow from a donor toward oxygen therefore represents the predominant direction of forward respiratory flux, not an absolute law that every carrier step is irreversible. Many redox reactions are reversible in appropriate circumstances. A large proton motive force and a sufficiently reduced quinone pool can, for example, provide conditions favorable to reverse electron transfer through Complex I. Such reverse transfer is not the standard pathway for aerobic matrix NADH oxidation, but its possibility shows why redox chemistry and membrane energetics have to be understood together. Simply ranking proteins in numerical order does not determine the actual direction of every chemical step.

Cofactors also differ in how they carry reducing equivalents. NADH normally transfers a hydride equivalent to a suitable enzyme-bound acceptor. Flavin cofactors can participate in both two-electron and single-electron reactions, which helps connect NADH oxidation to one-electron carriers such as iron-sulfur centers. Quinones can exist in fully oxidized, semiquinone and fully reduced forms. A heme iron in cytochrome c alternates between ferric and ferrous oxidation states, accepting or donating one electron per molecule. These differences help explain why a simple equation involving a pair of electrons may require two successive cytochrome c reduction events. Electron balance must be respected at every intermediate stage, even when the overall pathway is drawn as a single arrow.

The conventional pathway also depends on the existence of oxygen as a final acceptor. Oxygen does not normally accept electrons from every carrier in the chain, because the proteins organize specific catalytic contacts and reaction pathways. At Complex IV, a specialized catalytic center enables the coordinated four-electron reduction of oxygen to water. At other points, particularly where partially reduced redox intermediates occur, some electrons can react with oxygen adventitiously and generate reactive oxygen species. The controlled terminal reaction and this side reaction must not be confused. Oxygen consumption is not necessarily accompanied by a fixed amount of superoxide formation, and production of reactive oxygen species is not the intended function of the conventional chain.

For quantitative reasoning, count electrons before counting protons. One NADH supplies a pair of electrons. Each reduced cytochrome c carries one electron. One molecule of O2 accepts four electrons in complete reduction to two water molecules. Thus a pair of electrons corresponds to half a molecule of O2 in molar stoichiometry. The use of a fractional oxygen coefficient does not imply a physical enzyme molecule breaks oxygen in half; it is a normalized chemical equation. Two NADH molecules provide the four electrons required for one O2. This simple bookkeeping prevents confusion between a mole of oxygen molecules, a mole of oxygen atoms and a mole of electron pairs.

Finally, free energy and rate are different properties. A large favorable ΔG indicates that a reaction can proceed in the forward direction under defined conditions; it does not tell how rapidly the reaction occurs. Enzymes influence rates by lowering activation barriers, and the organization of the membrane determines whether the reaction is linked to proton transport. A respiratory inhibitor can greatly slow a thermodynamically favorable process without changing the underlying standard free-energy difference between NADH and oxygen. A student interpreting experiments should separate the driving force for the overall redox process from the kinetic accessibility of each individual catalytic route.

4. Complex I: NADH oxidation, quinone reduction and proton pumping

The first conventional entry point is NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase, usually called Complex I. Its function is defined by the two substrates in that name. NADH is oxidized, and ubiquinone is reduced. The reaction occurs through a large multi-subunit enzyme embedded in the inner membrane. Structural studies show the general organization of an L-shaped complex with a matrix-exposed hydrophilic arm and a membrane-embedded arm. The hydrophilic region contains the redox cofactors, and the membrane region enables proton translocation. Plant Complex I shares this conserved functional architecture with other eukaryotic mitochondria, although plant-specific accessory components and assembly features exist.

A soluble matrix NADH molecule reaches the enzyme's NADH-binding site. Its reducing equivalents are transferred to flavin mononucleotide, FMN, which is associated with the complex. A flavin can accept the two reducing equivalents of NADH and pass electrons successively to one-electron carriers. A chain of iron-sulfur centers provides a pathway through the hydrophilic arm toward the quinone reaction region. Iron-sulfur centers are coordinated metal cofactors capable of reversible changes in electronic state. They do not need to diffuse between the respiratory complexes; their positions within the protein support short-distance electron transfer. The electron relay brings the reducing equivalents to the region where ubiquinone is reduced.

At the quinone site, oxidized ubiquinone accepts two electrons and the appropriate protons to become ubiquinol. This reaction is distinct from the protons that are translocated through the membrane arm. Writing the chemical reaction without proton pumping helps separate the bookkeeping: NADH + H+ + Q → NAD+ + QH2. When NADH donates a hydride equivalent, the additional hydrogen ion in the net equation is supplied by the matrix. The electron and proton contents of the reactants and products balance. In the physiological forward reaction the conserved conventional stoichiometry is approximately four protons translocated from matrix to P side per electron pair, in addition to the chemical chemistry represented by the net quinone-reduction reaction.

Energy coupling poses an interesting molecular problem. NADH oxidation and quinone reduction occur predominantly in the hydrophilic arm, whereas the proton-translocating components extend along the membrane arm. How can redox chemistry drive the movement of protons at sites physically separated from the electron-transfer centers? The answer involves communication between quinone-site chemistry, protein conformational states, electrostatic changes and proton pathways within the membrane domain. Exact molecular details remain the subject of structural and biochemical research. At CSIR-NET level, the established functional conclusion is that energy released by favorable NADH-to-quinone electron transfer is coupled to vectorial proton translocation through Complex I. It would be inaccurate to assert that an electron physically travels along each proton channel, or that the four protons are simply carried across the membrane by the NADH molecule.

The four-proton coupling convention is normalized to the oxidation of one NADH, equivalent to two electrons. It is not the number of electrons in the NADH molecule. When succinate-derived electrons enter the quinone pool through Complex II, they do not automatically travel backward through Complex I to obtain this contribution. Likewise, a plant alternative type II NADH dehydrogenase that reduces Q without pumping protons does not make the same contribution merely because the initial substrate is NADH. The energetic contribution is a property of the route, not a fixed property of a free electron or an NADH molecule regardless of its path.

Complex I is sensitive to inhibitors such as rotenone that interfere with quinone-linked electron transfer. If the conventional chain is the only relevant route and respiration depends on matrix NADH, blocking Complex I prevents efficient donation of those electrons to Q. NADH may accumulate as continued substrate oxidation supplies reducing equivalents, and downstream carriers may become more oxidized if they retain access to oxygen but lack electron input. The proton-translocating activity associated with Complex I falls, and oxygen consumption through the conventional NADH-dependent route declines. This prediction assumes that upstream substrate is available and that the remaining respiratory machinery is intact. If no substrate is being oxidized, an inhibitor may cause little detectable change because there was little electron flux to begin with.

Plant mitochondrial respiratory measurements are especially instructive because rotenone sensitivity does not necessarily account for all NADH-related oxygen consumption. Alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases can provide other entry pathways to ubiquinone. If they are active and can access the available reducing equivalents, some electron flow may persist after Complex I inhibition. This persistence does not imply that rotenone has somehow converted Complex I into a non-pumping enzyme. It indicates that another route may carry the electrons. The degree of residual respiration depends on the specific preparation and substrate conditions, so a partial inhibitor response cannot by itself establish an exact allocation of flux between enzymes.

A useful causal prediction follows from comparing two mitochondrial suspensions. Both receive a matrix NADH-generating substrate, oxygen and the conditions needed for coupled respiration. Suspension A contains active Complex I; in suspension B the quinone reaction of Complex I is inhibited, while succinate-dependent Complex II remains functional. If no other NADH entry route operates, B will show reduced oxygen consumption with the first substrate. If succinate is then provided in a way that permits its oxidation, oxygen consumption can resume via Complex II. The rescue demonstrates that the downstream chain may still be intact. It does not restore the four protons associated with the normal Complex I route. Comparing oxygen consumption alone would miss that energetic difference; a gradient or ATP measurement offers additional information.

Plant Complex I also has structural features beyond the minimal conserved catalytic machinery. Some accessory subunits form domains related to carbonic anhydrase, particularly in plant and other photosynthetic eukaryotic complexes. Their presence illustrates the integration of conserved energy-converting machinery with lineage-specific cellular history. For the present learning objective, it is enough to distinguish these structural features from the core NADH oxidation, electron relay and proton-translocation functions. The existence of additional protein components does not justify inventing extra pumping sites or adding unverified proton stoichiometries to the conventional respiratory accounting.

An additional distinction concerns reactive oxygen formation. A flavin or other redox site held in a highly reduced state can interact with oxygen in side reactions, particularly when normal electron acceptance is restricted. However, the amount of superoxide formed depends on the redox state, membrane potential and exact inhibitory or substrate conditions. It is not correct to assume that every inhibition of Complex I invariably raises reactive oxygen formation by the same mechanism. Some interventions decrease the supply of electrons to a particular ROS-producing site, whereas others increase its reduction state. A mechanistic prediction should identify the site, specify the availability of reducing equivalents and distinguish a possible tendency from a universal rule.

5. Complex II: a connection between carbon metabolism and the quinone pool

Succinate dehydrogenase is a particularly useful enzyme because it belongs to both the citric acid cycle and the respiratory chain. In the cycle it catalyzes the oxidation of succinate to fumarate. In the respiratory network it transfers the resulting reducing equivalents to ubiquinone. These are two aspects of the same enzyme reaction rather than separate reactions carried out by a soluble succinate dehydrogenase and an unrelated Complex II. The protein's position in the inner mitochondrial membrane enables a matrix-accessible metabolic substrate to deliver electrons to the lipid-associated quinone pool.

Oxidation of succinate removes two hydrogen equivalents from adjacent carbon atoms, leaving the carbon-carbon double bond characteristic of fumarate. The four carbon atoms remain in the organic product; no carbon dioxide is produced at this step. The enzyme-bound flavin adenine dinucleotide, FAD, accepts the reducing equivalents. Unlike NADH, which is a freely exchanging substrate of numerous dehydrogenases, the flavin in succinate dehydrogenase remains associated with the enzyme through catalytic turnover. The shorthand statement that the citric acid cycle produces FADH2 is useful for electron accounting, but the student should not imagine a freely diffusing FADH2 molecule traveling from the matrix to a separate binding site on Complex II. The succinate dehydrogenase reaction already takes place within the complex itself.

The reduced flavin transfers electrons through iron-sulfur centers toward the quinone-binding region. Quinone accepts a pair of electrons and becomes quinol. The net redox reaction can be written as succinate + Q → fumarate + QH2. This equation highlights the fact that succinate and quinone are the donor and acceptor of reducing equivalents, respectively. The flavin and iron-sulfur centers are catalytic intermediaries and are restored during turnover. Additional membrane-associated components anchor and organize the quinone site. Plant Complex II contains distinctive structural subunits compared with the standard simplified description from animal mitochondria, but the principal function of succinate-to-quinone electron transfer remains conserved.

The most important energetic feature of Complex II is what it does not do: it does not directly pump protons across the inner membrane. There is no equivalent four-proton membrane arm performing the work of Complex I. The redox potential difference for succinate-to-quinone electron transfer is also much smaller than for NADH-to-quinone transfer under the usual biochemical conditions. Although the exact thermodynamics depend on concentrations, the route functions as a non-pumping connection from carbon oxidation to Q. The two hydrogen equivalents ending up on QH2 should not be counted as two pumped protons from matrix to intermembrane space. At this stage they are part of the chemical quinol product. Their role in vectorial charge transfer is realized during downstream quinol oxidation.

This distinction is particularly important when comparing NADH-dependent and succinate-dependent respiration. If both routes subsequently use Complexes III and IV, the downstream proton-translocating processes are shared. The succinate route lacks the four-proton contribution of Complex I, so under idealized conventional coupling it contributes six rather than ten protons per electron pair to the total outward proton translocation. The fact that both routes can support oxygen consumption does not make them energetically equivalent. Conversely, the fact that Complex II is not a pump does not imply that succinate oxidation cannot contribute to mitochondrial ATP synthesis; downstream complexes generate an electrochemical gradient from its electrons.

Malonate provides a classical way to reason about succinate dehydrogenase activity. It is structurally similar enough to succinate to compete at its substrate-binding region, but it is not converted by the normal succinate-to-fumarate reaction. Under an ideal competitive inhibition model, adding more succinate can partially overcome inhibition, whereas changing an unrelated donor does not overcome the same competition at that site. In a mitochondrial suspension receiving succinate as the principal electron donor, sufficiently strong inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase reduces electron delivery to Q and therefore decreases conventional oxygen consumption. If NADH-linked electron entry through Complex I is intact, a substrate producing matrix NADH may support respiration independently of Complex II. Such experiments help locate the inhibited step relative to the shared quinone pool.

An inhibitor of Complex II can affect more than a single respiratory rate. Because succinate oxidation participates in the citric acid cycle, changes in its activity may alter the relative amounts of succinate, fumarate and other cycle intermediates. Predicting the steady-state concentration of each metabolite requires more than simply locating the blocked reaction: alternative consumption routes, substrate supply and feedback control may alter the pattern. In a simplified short-term experiment where succinate is continually supplied and fumarate formation is primarily through Complex II, succinate is expected to accumulate relative to control, and the rate of fumarate formation to decrease. In an intact plant cell the wider metabolic network can modify those observations. The distinction between immediate mechanistic consequences and long-term steady-state concentrations is valuable for Part C questions.

The reaction can be interpreted by comparing two experiments with the same downstream machinery. A mitochondrial preparation supplied with succinate and oxygen consumes oxygen even if Complex I is blocked, provided the necessary transport and enzymatic steps remain functional. A second preparation supplied with a matrix NADH-generating substrate can consume oxygen when Complex II is blocked, provided Complex I and downstream pathways function. These independent routes converge at Q. A block of Complex III or IV therefore affects both conventional routes, unless another terminal pathway is available. This logical convergence is more useful than memorizing a list of inhibitor names because it lets the student predict the outcome of unfamiliar experimental manipulations.

The succinate/fumarate reaction also reminds us that thermodynamic direction and physiological role can vary with context. A protein capable of transferring reducing equivalents between two couples may, under suitable conditions, catalyze reactions in the reverse direction. The standard plant respiration map in this chapter focuses on forward succinate oxidation and Q reduction under aerobic conditions. It should not be expanded into a claim that the complete citric acid cycle necessarily operates in one fixed cyclic pattern in every plant tissue or metabolic state. The electron transport network participates in determining the redox balance within which the carbon-metabolic pathways operate.

6. Ubiquinone and cytochrome c: mobile carriers

The respiratory chain has multiple entry points but must communicate between proteins distributed throughout the membrane. A mobile carrier allows different enzymes to share a common redox pool. Ubiquinone, abbreviated Q, is the oxidized form of coenzyme Q. Ubiquinol, QH2, is its fully reduced form. Reduction involves addition of two electrons and two protons to the quinone ring. A one-electron intermediate, semiquinone, is possible in the catalytic mechanisms of quinone-associated enzymes. The hydrophobic tail holds the molecule within the membrane environment, so diffusion and local protein interactions allow Q and QH2 to reach appropriate enzyme-binding regions.

Complex I, Complex II and plant alternative dehydrogenases can all reduce the oxidized quinone pool. Complex III can oxidize QH2 and transfer the electrons onward to cytochrome c. Plant alternative oxidase also oxidizes QH2 but transfers the reducing equivalents directly toward oxygen by a distinct terminal pathway. The pool is therefore a junction, not merely an intermediate that is used by Complex I alone. Its degree of reduction reflects the balance between the input and output pathways. If Q reduction increases while QH2 oxidation does not, the reduced fraction of the pool tends to increase. If terminal oxidation is accelerated without a corresponding increase in input, the pool can shift toward the oxidized form.

The expression 'the Q pool is reduced' does not necessarily mean every quinone molecule is reduced. It usually describes a population with a relatively high QH2 fraction. For a simple accounting example, if an illustrative pool contains 100 molecules of quinone in total, with 70 in the QH2 state and 30 in the Q state, its reduced fraction is 70 percent when semiquinone is negligible. If twenty additional Q molecules are reduced and none are reoxidized during the observation interval, the fraction rises to 90 percent. The example is a teaching model, not experimental data. A real mitochondrion continuously turns over quinone, and changes in redox state reflect the difference between simultaneous reduction and oxidation fluxes.

Cytochrome c plays a different role. It is a heme-containing protein found on the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane. Its heme iron cycles between oxidized Fe3+ and reduced Fe2+ states. Each cytochrome c accepts one electron from Complex III, then donates one electron to Complex IV and becomes oxidized again. Unlike ubiquinone, it is not a proton-carrying lipid-soluble molecule that cycles between Q and QH2. Its role as a mobile one-electron carrier is essential in connecting the two conventional cytochrome complexes. The difference in carrier capacity explains why oxidation of a single QH2 must be matched by the reduction of two cytochrome c molecules when the overall Complex III reaction is considered.

The two mobile carriers also permit a useful localization of defects. In a system where Complex I and II are active and downstream Complex III is blocked, electron input can drive the Q pool toward reduction, while cytochrome c downstream of the block may remain relatively oxidized if Complex IV can still accept its remaining electrons. If instead Complex IV is blocked while Complex III continues functioning for a short interval, cytochrome c becomes more reduced because its oxidation route is impaired. These tendencies assume continuing electron supply, enough oxygen, and no alternative pathways or loss of carriers. The reasoning is akin to observing a bottleneck: material before the block can accumulate, and supply after the block may decline. Unlike an inert material in a pipe, however, electrons are transferred among reversible redox couples whose state affects subsequent flux, so the analogy has limits.

Experimental assays can sometimes distinguish changes in carrier redox state from changes in total respiration. Cytochromes absorb light differently depending on whether their heme iron is oxidized or reduced. Difference spectroscopy exploits this property to compare samples or follow respiratory changes. A difference in absorbance is interpretable only if the appropriate wavelengths, sample baseline and contributions from other chromophores are considered. A whole-chain oxygen electrode reports net oxygen consumption; it cannot directly identify whether Q or cytochrome c is predominantly oxidized. Combining optical measurements with respiratory assays can therefore narrow down the region at which electron transfer becomes restricted.

The organization of electron transport may also involve associations among several respiratory complexes, often termed supercomplexes. Such associations can influence organization and stability, but the presence of a supercomplex should not lead to the conclusion that ubiquinone and cytochrome c have lost their identities as carriers or that all electrons must pass through a single fixed sequence of physically fused enzymes. At the level required here, the conserved functional routes and their compartmental orientation provide the essential map. Plant-specific assembly and regulation offer context, while detailed structural subunit catalogs are unnecessary for solving the central questions about flux and coupling.

7. Complex III and the Q-cycle

At the point where ubiquinol reaches Complex III, the chain must solve a mismatch between two kinds of electron carrier. A molecule of ubiquinol carries two reducing equivalents and two protons, while each cytochrome c carries only one electron. The cytochrome bc1 complex, also termed ubiquinol:cytochrome c oxidoreductase, resolves this mismatch through an organized reaction sequence known as the Q-cycle. The cycle couples electron transfer to vectorial proton movement, making Complex III an important contributor to the respiratory electrochemical gradient. Understanding this mechanism requires tracing the two electrons from each incoming quinol separately rather than treating the Q-cycle as a simple one-step oxidation.

Complex III contains several redox-active components. Cytochrome b has two hemes conventionally called bL and bH, reflecting their relative redox properties. The Rieske iron-sulfur protein contains a specialized 2Fe–2S center that participates in one branch of quinol oxidation. Cytochrome c1 transfers electrons toward the mobile cytochrome c carrier. Two quinone reaction sites occur at different positions relative to the membrane: the Qo site, also called Qp, faces the P side, and the Qi site, also called Qn, faces the matrix or N side. Their names refer to the locations and typical functions of quinone reactions in the forward cycle. It is essential to distinguish these two sites; a diagram in which both quinol oxidation and quinone reduction occur on the same aqueous side misses the basis of vectorial proton chemistry.

Follow the first turnover. A QH2 molecule binds at the Qo site, where its two hydrogen ions are released toward the intermembrane space as its two electrons enter different redox pathways. One electron moves through the Rieske iron-sulfur protein toward cytochrome c1 and then to an oxidized mobile cytochrome c, producing a reduced cytochrome c. This is commonly described as the high-potential branch. The second electron travels through the hemes of cytochrome b, from bL toward bH, reaching a quinone at the Qi site on the matrix-facing region of the complex. Reduction of that quinone by one electron produces a semiquinone intermediate. The exact protonation state of an enzyme-bound intermediate depends on its environment; the central exam-relevant result is that the Qi quinone has not yet received the second electron needed to complete its conversion to QH2.

The bifurcation of electron transfer has two consequences. First, only one cytochrome c has been reduced during this first Qo turnover, even though the incoming quinol donated two electrons. Second, part of the reducing power remains in the quinone chemistry at the Qi site, rather than being passed to oxygen through cytochrome c immediately. The oxidized quinone resulting from the first Qo event can return to the membrane pool. The Qi intermediate remains available for the next appropriate electron transfer. This arrangement is why the cycle must be completed before the net reaction is calculated.

In the second turnover, another QH2 molecule is oxidized at Qo. Two additional protons are released toward the P side. As before, one electron passes through the Rieske center and cytochrome c1, reducing a second mobile cytochrome c. The other electron travels along the cytochrome b route, supplying the remaining reducing equivalent to the Qi intermediate. With uptake of two protons from the N side over the completed cycle, the Qi quinone is converted to QH2. This regenerated ubiquinol can return to the common membrane pool and eventually undergo oxidation again. The cycle has oxidized two quinol molecules at Qo, reduced one quinone at Qi and reduced two molecules of cytochrome c.

Now compare inputs and outputs. At Qo, two QH2 molecules are oxidized to two Q molecules; at Qi, one Q molecule becomes one QH2 molecule. Cancel the regenerated QH2 against one input QH2 and cancel one Q against one output Q. The net reaction is QH2 + 2 cytochrome c(oxidized) + 2 H+(N) → Q + 2 cytochrome c(reduced) + 4 H+(P). The chemical protons initially contained in the net incoming QH2 are included in the four released on the P side. Two additional N-side protons participate in regeneration of quinol at Qi. The result is a conventional effective contribution of four protons to outward vectorial translocation per pair of electrons delivered to cytochrome c. This convention is appropriate for comparison with the contributions of other respiratory complexes.

There is an important distinction between Complex III and a pump described as a conformational machine that carries protons through internal channels. Complex III generates its vectorial effect through the topology of quinol oxidation, quinone reduction and redox cycling. The common phrase that Complex III pumps four protons per pair is often used in textbook shorthand; it means a net coupling contribution to the gradient, not that all four hydrogen ions travel through a conventional transmembrane pump channel during a single isolated catalytic event. The Q-cycle accomplishes charge separation because proton-linked chemistry occurs on opposite sides of the membrane in a coordinated manner. This mechanistic understanding is necessary for answering questions that contrast direct proton pumps and redox-loop mechanisms.

A frequent mistake is to count all protons twice: once when ubiquinol forms at Complex I or Complex II, and again when they are released by Complex III. The net Q-cycle equation shows exactly which quantities have to be counted. The two hydrogen atoms carried by the quinol are chemical components of that substrate before the cycle. Complex I's separate four-proton pumping contribution is distinct from the chemistry that produces QH2. When the entire chain is considered, the conventional total is obtained by summing the effective vectorial contributions of I, III and IV, not by adding every hydrogen ion appearing in every half reaction. Careful cancellation of shared intermediates is more reliable than counting symbols by sight.

The Q-cycle can be understood through inhibitor logic. Antimycin A acts at the Qi region of Complex III, interfering with the route that normally delivers the lower-branch electrons to quinone. If the low-potential branch cannot operate normally, repeated Qo turnover becomes restricted even if cytochrome c and Complex IV remain present. Quinol oxidation, cytochrome c reduction and associated proton translocation can decline. An inhibitor acting at Qo, such as myxothiazol under appropriate conditions, instead interferes with electron transfer from incoming quinol. The two inhibitors can have different effects on the population of partially reduced intermediates and hence on reactive oxygen species formation. It is not sufficient to state that both are Complex III inhibitors and therefore must produce identical redox states under all conditions.

Suppose isolated mitochondria receive an electron donor that reduces Q, and oxygen is abundant. After inhibition of the conventional Complex III pathway, continued donation from upstream tends to increase the reduction state of Q, while cytochrome c can become more oxidized as it transfers its remaining electrons to Complex IV. If the same mitochondria have an active alternative oxidase, some quinol oxidation and oxygen consumption can persist without the normal Complex III or IV reactions. The residual respiration is then not evidence that Complex III continues to translocate its normal proton contribution. An additional measurement of membrane potential, or a carefully designed pathway-specific assay, is needed to distinguish electron flow from energy conservation.

Complex III is also a potential source of reactive oxygen species. The semiquinone intermediates involved in quinol oxidation have chemical properties that permit unwanted electron transfer to oxygen in some circumstances. A block that changes the lifetimes and reduction states of those intermediates can alter ROS formation. However, the magnitude and direction of the change depend on which site is affected and how much electron supply reaches it. A very strong restriction of electron input may reduce the opportunity for oxygen reduction at a given site even though the overall chain is inhibited. It is therefore better to predict the likely redox state of the relevant site and explicitly identify the conditions than to memorize a universal association between inhibition and increased ROS.

The idea of a Q-cycle provides a general lesson about scientific models of membrane bioenergetics. A structural model tells us which cofactors and reaction sites are positioned near particular membrane faces. A kinetic model explains how repeated turnovers connect the electron-transfer branches. A net reaction accounts for the conservation of electrons and protons over the completed sequence. These are related but distinct descriptions. For a correct answer, all three should be compatible. An electron diagram that creates an extra electron, a net equation that fails to account for Qi proton uptake, or a structural map that places cytochrome c on the matrix side contradicts the established functional scheme.

8. Complex IV and the controlled reduction of oxygen

The final enzyme of the conventional cytochrome pathway is cytochrome c oxidase, or Complex IV. Its central function is to accept electrons from reduced cytochrome c and use them to reduce molecular oxygen to water. The term oxidase refers to the enzyme's use of oxygen as an electron acceptor; the enzyme simultaneously oxidizes cytochrome c. Its catalytic chemistry must be understood in terms of both electron conservation and proton movement, since these aspects are related but contribute differently to the overall membrane energy balance.

Reduced cytochrome c reaches its binding region on the intermembrane-space side of Complex IV. It transfers a single electron to redox centers within the enzyme and becomes oxidized, after which it can participate in another turnover of Complex III. The conserved electron-transfer pathway in cytochrome c oxidase involves a copper center called CuA, heme a, and the catalytic heme a3–CuB center at which oxygen reduction occurs. The exact ordering and internal kinetics of electron and proton delivery are more elaborate than the elementary diagram, but this map explains the connection between a soluble one-electron donor and the final oxygen chemistry in a membrane-integrated catalyst.

Molecular oxygen requires four electrons and four chemical protons for complete reduction to two water molecules: O2 + 4 e− + 4 H+ → 2 H2O. Since one cytochrome c transfers one electron, four reduced cytochrome c molecules are required per oxygen molecule under the normal net stoichiometry. Oxygen reduction proceeds through enzyme-bound intermediates rather than requiring release of stable free partially reduced oxygen products into the matrix during each normal catalytic cycle. Specialized metal centers and proton delivery pathways support controlled completion of the reaction. The existence of oxygen as a terminal acceptor does not imply that every encounter between oxygen and a reduced mitochondrial component results in safe water formation.

In addition to consuming chemical protons in water formation, conventional Complex IV translocates protons across the membrane. The usual accounting is four pumped protons per O2 reduced, or two per electron pair. The complete idealized reaction can be written using distinct matrix and P-side labels: 4 cytochrome c(reduced) + O2 + 8 H+(N) → 4 cytochrome c(oxidized) + 2 H2O + 4 H+(P). Four matrix protons are consumed in the water product and four are translocated. Normalizing to a pair of electrons gives two chemical protons for one water molecule and two pumped protons. Counting all four matrix protons in the normalized equation as pumped protons would overstate the conventional contribution of Complex IV.

The significance of this separation becomes clear when evaluating whole-chain equations. Matrix chemical proton consumption can influence the proton balance, and the oxidation of redox substrates also involves chemical protons. The widely used respiratory pumping convention counts the vectorial protons associated with the route: four at Complex I, four effective at Complex III and two at Complex IV per pair of electrons. Other accounting conventions may describe charge separation across particular enzyme segments using electrochemical bookkeeping that includes vectorial electron movements. When using the textbook total of ten protons for the NADH-to-oxygen cytochrome route, consistently apply the conventional normalization rather than combining incompatible definitions.

Complex IV is a terminal bottleneck in the conventional path. If oxygen availability falls, the capacity for the normal terminal reaction falls when oxygen becomes limiting. Upstream electron carriers may become more reduced because electron delivery continues while electron removal slows. NADH oxidation can be restricted, and pathways requiring NAD+ may face a redox limitation. The membrane potential may decrease if proton-translocating flux can no longer compensate for proton return and other dissipative processes. The exact time course depends on respiratory substrates, alternative routes, initial gradient, membrane conductance and other energy-consuming processes. For a well-posed examination question, the assumptions about these factors should be clear.

Cyanide and azide are familiar examples of substances that inhibit cytochrome c oxidase. Cyanide interferes with the terminal oxygen-reduction chemistry at the catalytic region of the enzyme. In a preparation in which only the conventional cytochrome pathway operates, sufficiently strong inhibition sharply restricts oxygen consumption driven by both NADH and succinate, because both routes rely on Complex IV after convergence at Q. Providing more succinate does not bypass the terminal block. Neither does adding matrix NADH. The appropriate mechanistic observation is that the common downstream oxygen-reduction route has been disabled.

Plant mitochondria introduce a qualification. Alternative oxidase can accept electrons from ubiquinol and reduce oxygen without using cytochrome c oxidase. Consequently, oxygen consumption that persists after cyanide treatment may reflect alternative respiratory capacity rather than failure of the conventional terminal reaction to be inhibited. This interpretation requires appropriate controls because inhibitor sensitivity is not perfectly specific in every biological setting. The fraction of oxygen consumption remaining under an inhibitor condition is not automatically identical to the physiological flux through that branch before inhibition. Blocking one outlet can alter the redox state of the Q pool and redistribute electron flux to a previously less active outlet. Alternative oxidase is examined in detail in 6.B.04; its role here is to prevent the mistaken prediction that a terminal cytochrome block necessarily eliminates all plant mitochondrial respiration.

A worked electron-counting example illustrates the connection between this enzyme and the complete chain. If an isolated ideal conventional system transfers 12 micromoles of electron pairs from matrix NADH to oxygen, there are 24 micromoles of individual electrons. Complete oxygen reduction requires four electrons per molecule, so 6 micromoles of O2 are reduced, forming 12 micromoles of water in the terminal reaction. Complex IV contributes 24 micromoles of pumped protons in the conventional accounting, because each pair supports two. The total conventional chain contribution is 120 micromoles of outward protons if all pairs entered through Complex I. The 24 micromoles of chemical protons used in water formation are not added to that number as extra pumped protons. The distinction between individual electrons, electron pairs and oxygen molecules is indispensable when converting respiratory data into energetic quantities.

The catalytic function of Complex IV can be investigated using a donor system that supplies reduced cytochrome c without requiring ordinary upstream NADH and succinate oxidation. In a conceptual assay, if oxygen reduction persists when Complex I or Complex III is disabled but is abolished when cytochrome c oxidase is blocked, the data support the position of Complex IV downstream of the point bypassed. Actual artificial donor assays require blanks because the reducing reagents may react with oxygen independently of the mitochondrial enzyme. A student should not infer that an oxygen-electrode signal represents exclusively enzyme-dependent respiration without accounting for chemical oxygen consumption in the absence of mitochondria. This principle applies broadly to respiratory experimental design.

Terminal oxygen reduction is essential to the forward conventional path, but it is not a source of carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide released during respiration arises primarily from decarboxylating transformations in carbon metabolism; Complex IV receives electrons and protons and converts oxygen to water. The common overall equation for glucose oxidation includes both CO2 and water, but the products are formed at different biochemical stages. By separating the carbon transformations of the previous chapter from the electron-transfer reactions here, we can explain why oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide release may vary in relative rates when plant respiratory substrates, biosynthetic pathways or photorespiratory processes change.

9. Alternative NAD(P)H dehydrogenases in plant mitochondria

The conventional four-complex representation is a useful starting point, but it is not an adequate map of the plant mitochondrial respiratory network. Plant mitochondria possess type II NAD(P)H dehydrogenases that can transfer electrons from reduced nicotinamide cofactors to ubiquinone without the proton-pumping machinery of Complex I. These enzymes add flexibility at the entrance to the chain. Their existence is especially important in interpreting rotenone-insensitive NADH oxidation, compartment-specific substrate use and the energetic consequences of changing electron routes.

The notation NAD(P)H refers collectively to NADH and NADPH, not to one molecular species containing both. NADH and NADPH are related coenzymes, differing in a phosphate group on the adenosine ribose region. Although their nicotinamide groups participate in similar redox chemistry, enzymes can discriminate between them. Some alternative dehydrogenases favor NADH, some favor NADPH and their specific activity can depend on the isoform and physiological conditions. Therefore, the general label NAD(P)H dehydrogenase does not mean every member oxidizes both substrates with equal efficiency. Conventional Complex I is primarily the matrix NADH-oxidizing proton-pumping enzyme, while the alternative family provides additional access to the quinone pool.

The location of the catalytic site determines which pool of reducing equivalents an alternative enzyme can access. An internally oriented alternative dehydrogenase presents its catalytic activity toward the matrix. It can oxidize suitable reduced cofactors in that compartment and deliver electrons to quinone. An externally oriented alternative dehydrogenase faces the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane. Because the outer mitochondrial membrane permits exchange of many small metabolites, external activity can provide a route for oxidation of cytosolic reducing equivalents without requiring NADH to cross the inner membrane. The two activities therefore solve different compartmental redox problems even though they converge on the same Q pool.

In Arabidopsis and other plant models, nomenclature such as NDA and NDB is commonly used for groups of type II dehydrogenases, with internal and external orientations represented in the family. Other family members and targeting patterns also occur. For examination purposes, it is more important to establish the orientation, cofactor preference and non-pumping nature of a specified enzyme than to memorize a complete catalog of every isoform across all species. If a question explicitly assigns an enzyme to the matrix face or external face, base the prediction on that assignment rather than assuming a general family name determines all possible properties in every plant.

Alternative dehydrogenases reduce Q to QH2 but do not translocate protons in the way Complex I does. This difference is easily overlooked because both enzyme types can perform the overall chemical transfer of electrons from NADH to quinone. Similar net redox chemistry does not guarantee similar energy coupling. The type II enzyme lacks the large proton-translocating machinery characteristic of Complex I. Its reaction therefore provides an additional route for maintaining the redox turnover of cofactors, while omitting the four-proton contribution that would have been made by conventional Complex I.

Consider two routes using the same matrix NADH donor. Route A proceeds through Complex I, Q, Complex III, cytochrome c and Complex IV. Route B uses an internal alternative NADH dehydrogenase, Q, Complex III, cytochrome c and Complex IV. If every electron pair is transferred to oxygen and the normal downstream complexes remain coupled, Route A contributes ten outward protons per pair and Route B contributes six. Both oxidize NADH and reduce oxygen; the difference is in energy conservation. If the measured oxygen consumption is equal in the two systems, it does not follow that their ATP output is equal. Under equivalent coupling conditions, the route with Complex I has a larger conventional proton-translocation contribution per unit oxygen reduced.

The advantage of the alternative route becomes apparent under changing metabolic conditions. A cell does not always need to maximize ATP yield per electron. It also needs to maintain oxidized nicotinamide cofactors for metabolic reactions, prevent prolonged accumulation of reducing equivalents and accommodate fluctuations in substrate availability. The availability of a non-pumping route can provide additional opportunities for oxidation when the usual route is limited or when the redox needs of the cell differ from its instantaneous ATP demand. This is a tradeoff rather than a simple replacement of a defective conventional chain. In a given tissue, whether alternative activity is used depends on enzyme expression, substrate availability, regulation and the state of downstream electron acceptors.

An external NADH-dependent respiration experiment illustrates why topology and transport must be considered together. Add NADH to a suspension of intact plant mitochondria, assuming that the outer membrane is sufficiently permeable to allow its access to the external surface of the inner membrane. If an external NADH dehydrogenase is active and downstream pathways operate, oxygen consumption may increase even though NADH does not freely pass into the matrix. If the same experiment is performed with mitochondria lacking the relevant external activity, a smaller response may occur. A comparison with disrupted mitochondria is informative because membrane disruption can expose matrix-facing catalytic regions that were inaccessible in the intact preparation. The difference between the two preparations reflects accessibility as well as enzyme activity.

Calcium can regulate some plant alternative dehydrogenases, particularly certain externally facing forms, and substrate preference or redox regulation can vary across family members. A study comparing oxygen consumption in the presence and absence of calcium may therefore detect changes in alternative activity. However, a calcium-dependent change in a whole mitochondrial preparation does not by itself prove direct calcium binding by a particular dehydrogenase. Calcium can influence other mitochondrial processes and transport activities. A more direct inference requires isolating the relevant enzyme-dependent response or using appropriate genetic and biochemical controls. The NET-level principle is that plant-specific entry pathways may respond to conditions and help balance the cellular redox state.

Rotenone is often used to distinguish the proton-pumping Complex I route from alternative entry. Imagine that matrix NADH-dependent respiration in an intact mitochondrial preparation falls from an illustrative rate of 100 units to 35 units after addition of a concentration of rotenone that effectively inhibits conventional Complex I. If the remaining oxygen consumption is substrate dependent, one possibility is electron entry through a rotenone-insensitive dehydrogenase. Other explanations, including incomplete inhibition, alternative substrates or non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption, must be excluded before attributing the entire residual rate to the alternative pathway. Even if the residual activity is convincingly assigned to a type II enzyme, the measurement reflects flux under the inhibited condition, not necessarily the pre-inhibition division of electrons.

A complementary genetic approach could compare a wild-type plant with a line deficient in a defined external dehydrogenase. If the deficient mitochondria have a reduced ability to oxidize externally supplied NADH while their succinate-dependent respiration remains similar to the wild type, the result supports the specific role of the missing activity in external electron entry. The succinate assay serves as a control for the functionality of shared downstream respiratory components. Restoring the relevant gene and observing rescue strengthens the causal interpretation. Such an experiment does not establish that every external NAD(P)H oxidation event depends on a single enzyme, because related isoforms may compensate.

The alternative entry enzymes must not be confused with alternative oxidase. An alternative dehydrogenase provides electrons to Q; alternative oxidase takes electrons away from QH2 and reduces oxygen. These are opposite positions in the network. Bypassing Complex I is not the same as bypassing Complexes III and IV. An electron entering through an alternative dehydrogenase can still follow the conventional cytochrome terminal path and contribute six outward protons per pair through the downstream complexes. Conversely, an electron entering through Complex I can exit through alternative oxidase and retain the upstream four-proton pumping contribution while omitting the downstream conventional six. This distinction provides a concise route-based framework for plant respiration without preempting the full treatment of AOX in the later chapter.

Alternative electron entry illustrates that mitochondrial function has to be understood at several scales. At the molecular scale an enzyme oxidizes a cofactor and reduces quinone. At the organelle scale its position controls access to reducing equivalents and its coupling properties affect the electrochemical gradient. At the whole-plant scale, those properties contribute to the ability of tissues to respond to changes in light, growth and metabolic demand. The same enzyme can therefore be described in terms of its chemical reaction, its topological position and its physiological function. A correct explanation connects these scales rather than replacing the molecular mechanism with a vague statement about stress tolerance.

A published test of the external entry route comes from Sweetman and colleagues (2019), who compared Arabidopsis thaliana plants with altered expression of AtNDB2. They isolated leaf mitochondria and measured oxidation of externally supplied NADH. Mitochondria from a T-DNA knockout lacking detectable AtNDB2 protein showed very low external NADH oxidation, below 3 nmol per minute per milligram of mitochondrial protein in the reported assay, both with and without added calcium. The genetic intervention and the measured biochemical defect support the conclusion that this enzyme supplies much of the external NADH oxidation capacity in that model. The result applies to the defined species, tissue and assay, not to every possible plant NAD(P)H dehydrogenase. It also demonstrates why it is useful to distinguish external NADH from matrix NADH when assigning a respiratory defect. Coexpression experiments involving AtNDB2 and AOX further established the possibility of a functional alternative entry-to-exit path. The study provides evidence for capacity; determining the natural fraction of respiratory flux through that path in a living plant requires additional measurements. [See reference 9.]

10. Route-specific proton accounting and the electrochemical gradient

The previous sections establish that the conventional cytochrome chain has several electron entry routes and a common terminal reaction. We can now derive its basic proton accounting. Take one electron pair supplied by matrix NADH. Its passage through Complex I contributes four outward protons, the effective Q-cycle at Complex III contributes four and Complex IV contributes two. Summing these gives ten conventional vectorially translocated protons per electron pair. If the pair enters through Complex II instead, the initial contribution is zero; the two downstream contributions are unchanged under the same assumptions, giving a total of six. An alternative non-pumping NAD(P)H dehydrogenase followed by the conventional III–IV path also gives six. The calculation explicitly assumes forward transport through intact, normally coupled complexes and excludes leakage or other transport demands.

Oxygen consumption can be expressed in the same framework. One O2 molecule accepts four electrons, so reduction of one O2 corresponds to two electron pairs. If all electrons originate through Complex I and all exit through III and IV, the conventional translocation contribution is twenty protons per O2 molecule. If all enter through a non-pumping route and leave through the conventional cytochrome path, the corresponding contribution is twelve. These numbers describe nominal proton translocation, not the number of protons that remain accumulated in the intermembrane space. Protons can return through ATP synthase, leak through the membrane, or participate in coupled transport. A steady-state gradient can coexist with substantial continuous proton flux because rates of outward movement and return are balanced.

A mixture of routes produces an intermediate average. Suppose 60 percent of the electron pairs enter through Complex I and 40 percent enter through non-pumping dehydrogenases, while all leave through the cytochrome pathway. The weighted contribution is 0.60 × 10 + 0.40 × 6 = 8.4 protons per pair. A fractional number here does not describe a single molecular turnover moving a fractional proton. It represents the mean over many turnover events with different routes. If 100 micromoles of electron pairs follow this distribution, the nominal total is 840 micromoles of vectorially translocated protons. Oxygen consumption would be 50 micromoles of O2 if all those pairs undergo complete terminal reduction. Stating the route distribution is essential; without it the energetic contribution cannot be inferred from oxygen consumption alone.

The proton gradient generated by the chain is an electrochemical difference. There are two components because a proton carries a positive electrical charge and also has a concentration-dependent chemical potential. Denote the P-side and N-side electrical potentials by ψP and ψN. The free-energy change for moving one mole of protons from N to P is RT ln(aHP/aHN) + F(ψP − ψN), where aH denotes hydrogen ion activity. Since pH = −log10(aH), the chemical term can be written as 2.303RT(pHN − pHP). Thus if the P side is more acidic and more positive than the matrix, both terms oppose additional outward proton movement. The equation describes the energetic cost of increasing or maintaining charge separation, while the reverse movement can release free energy to drive appropriate coupled processes.

For an illustrative calculation at 298 K, let the P side be 0.15 V more positive than the matrix and let pHN exceed pHP by 0.5 pH units. The electrical term per mole of protons is 96.485 kJ V−1 mol−1 × 0.15 V = 14.47 kJ mol−1. The chemical term is approximately 2.303 × 8.314 J mol−1 K−1 × 298 K × 0.5, or 2.85 kJ mol−1. The total outward energetic cost is approximately 17.32 kJ mol−1 of protons under these assumed conditions. Moving ten moles of protons per mole of NADH oxidized would correspond to roughly 173 kJ mol−1 of electrochemical work, if the gradient remained at those values throughout the calculation. This is compatible with the earlier standard redox free-energy scale but is not an experimentally determined energy efficiency. Actual substrate potentials, membrane conditions and route stoichiometry must be specified for such an efficiency measurement.

If proton return is restricted while the respiratory chain initially remains active, the electrochemical difference tends to increase. An increased gradient makes further outward proton movement energetically more demanding. The rates of energy-coupled electron-transfer reactions may then decrease because their driving force must overcome a larger opposing electrochemical cost. This is one basis of respiratory control. Conversely, if a pathway dissipates the gradient, the energetic opposition to outward proton movement is reduced and electron transfer can accelerate when oxygen, substrate and respiratory capacity are available. A low gradient does not always imply an inhibited chain; it may result from faster proton return or increased leak. The following chapter develops this coupling logic in relation to ATP synthase.

It is instructive to contrast a chain inhibitor with a membrane uncoupler. A chain inhibitor reduces electron transfer through a defined route, decreasing the capacity of that route to generate the gradient. An uncoupler increases dissipation of the gradient without necessarily blocking electron transfer at a respiratory complex. Under suitable conditions, uncoupling can increase oxygen consumption even while ATP synthesis falls. Both interventions may lower the membrane potential, but they do so by different mechanisms. Measuring only the potential cannot uniquely identify the causal defect. An oxygen-consumption measurement, an ATP synthesis measurement and knowledge of the added substrate can help discriminate between reduced pumping capacity and increased proton conductance.

The relative contributions of the electrical and pH components are also important. A change in membrane potential does not directly specify the magnitude of the entire proton electrochemical gradient if the pH difference changes independently. For example, selective movement of other ions can alter electrical charge separation and allow a different distribution of the chemical and electrical components. Mitochondrial membrane potential-sensitive dyes are useful indicators of one aspect of energetic state, but the interpretation depends on calibration, dye behavior and membrane integrity. An examiner may supply a simplified assumption that the pH component is constant; in that case, relative changes in electrical potential can be used more directly. Without that assumption, avoid equating an electrical signal to a complete measurement of all proton motive force components.

The terminal alternative oxidase route adds a second reason that proton yield cannot be inferred from oxygen use alone. If electrons enter through Complex I and then leave QH2 through AOX, the nominal contribution of Complex I remains four, but the effective four-proton Q-cycle and two-proton Complex IV contribution are bypassed. If electrons enter through a non-pumping route and leave through AOX, none of these conventional proton-translocation contributions is made by that electron path. This does not mean the mitochondrion as a whole must have zero gradient; other concurrent routes or processes may maintain it. The prediction is route-specific. Keeping the distinction between an individual path and the complete organelle prevents incorrect statements such as 'AOX always abolishes all mitochondrial ATP synthesis.'

We can express the general idealized account in terms of two independent questions for each pair: did the pair enter through Complex I, and did it exit through the conventional cytochrome pathway? Each pair passing through Complex I contributes four, and each passing through the complete downstream III–IV route contributes six. For N electron pairs, of which NI use Complex I and NC use the complete conventional terminal route, the total nominal contribution is 4NI + 6NC. This remains valid if the two subsets overlap in any proportion, under the stated simplifying assumptions. The relation captures the modularity of entry and exit in the plant respiratory chain. It cannot substitute for measurements of ATP yield because it does not include the ATP synthase stoichiometry, metabolite transport or proton leakage.

As a final comparison, consider a short illustrative interval in which two mitochondrial samples consume the same amount of oxygen. Sample X carries 20 micromoles of electron pairs, all through Complex I and the cytochrome pathway, giving 200 micromoles of nominal proton translocation. Sample Y carries the same number of pairs, ten through Complex I and ten through non-pumping entry, with only twelve pairs using III and IV and eight exiting through AOX. The nominal result is 4 × 10 + 6 × 12 = 112 micromoles of protons. Each sample reduces 10 micromoles of O2, but their nominal translocation contributions differ substantially. This calculation is not a measured physiological partition; it demonstrates why two plants with similar oxygen consumption rates may differ in the extent of respiratory energy conservation.

11. Experimental foundations and respiratory measurements

The pathway described in this chapter was not established simply by observing that plants consume oxygen. The development of mitochondrial bioenergetics required investigators to connect chemical changes in redox carriers with the architecture of cellular membranes and the synthesis of ATP. Early respiratory enzyme research identified oxidation reactions and oxygen-dependent cellular metabolism. David Keilin's 1925 work on cytochromes [reference 6] helped establish the importance of reversibly oxidized and reduced cellular pigments in biological oxidation. The absorption properties of heme proteins allowed investigators to follow changes in the respiratory system optically. A carrier could be identified not only by its presence in a tissue but also by its changes in oxidation state under conditions that altered respiratory activity.

The separation of mitochondria from other cellular components was another crucial advance. An isolated organelle preparation allows a researcher to provide selected respiratory substrates, vary oxygen and adenylate availability, and examine the consequences of inhibitors without all the complexity of simultaneous whole-cell pathways. The ability to recover oxygen consumption and coupled energy conservation in an intact isolated membrane system supports the idea that these processes depend on the organization of the organelle itself. It also reveals limitations: a damaged preparation may consume oxygen while conserving little energy, and enzymes or carriers lost during isolation may cause a result that is not representative of the intact cell.

Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic proposal in 1961 [reference 7] offered a coherent explanation for the connection between electron transfer and phosphorylation. Instead of requiring a single freely diffusible high-energy chemical intermediate linking every respiratory oxidation to ATP synthesis, it proposed that energy-conserving membrane reactions establish an electrochemical proton difference that can drive ATP synthesis. The concept predicts that electron transport, membrane integrity and the proton gradient must be related experimentally. The decisive logic is not merely that electron transport and ATP formation occur together. Interventions that independently change electron transfer or proton permeability should alter their relationship in systematic ways.

Classic membrane studies, biochemical reconstitution and the characterization of respiratory complexes subsequently strengthened the mechanistic account. The Q-cycle model associated with Mitchell (1975) [reference 8] explains how a quinone redox loop and electron bifurcation can produce a vectorial proton effect. Modern structural and functional studies identify cofactors and quinone reaction regions in positions compatible with the essential cycle. Plant studies add a further line of evidence through the identification of mitochondrial type II dehydrogenases and alternative oxidase, establishing that an energy-conserving respiratory chain may share its carriers with non-pumping branches. At the examination level, the important historical continuity is the transition from observation of oxygen consumption to experimentally constrained models of carrier identity, membrane orientation and energetic coupling.

A useful respiratory measurement records dissolved oxygen concentration against time. In a closed, well-mixed chamber, a decreasing oxygen concentration can reflect oxygen consumption by the sample. If the chamber volume is 2.0 mL and dissolved oxygen decreases from 200 to 170 micromolar over three minutes, the concentration slope is −10 micromolar per minute. Multiplying its magnitude by 0.002 L gives a consumption rate of 0.020 micromoles, or 20 nanomoles, of O2 per minute. If the chamber contains 0.50 mg of mitochondrial protein, the normalized rate is 40 nanomoles O2 per minute per milligram protein. These figures are hypothetical and are chosen to illustrate units and normalization. The concentration slope itself is not an amount per unit time until the chamber volume is included.

The oxygen trace should ideally be interpreted over an interval where the rate is reasonably stable and oxygen is not severely limiting. A nonlinear trace may reflect changing substrate availability, product inhibition, oxygen limitation, a time-dependent inhibitor effect or sample deterioration. If a sample is compared against another using different amounts of mitochondrial protein, the raw oxygen slopes are not directly comparable as specific respiratory rates. Researchers normalize to an appropriate measure, and they must still consider whether mitochondrial enrichment and membrane integrity differ between preparations. Normalization to protein mass does not automatically prove that every milligram contains the same amount of functional respiratory machinery.

The choice of substrate is an experimental probe. Pyruvate with a suitable supporting substrate can promote matrix NADH formation; succinate can support Complex II-dependent electron input; externally supplied NADH can probe appropriate external alternative dehydrogenase activity in an intact plant mitochondrial preparation. Substrate choice is not a guarantee of perfect pathway isolation because metabolism and membrane transport can create additional reducing equivalents. For conceptual examination experiments, the question may explicitly assume that the designated route is the only electron input. In interpreting real research, substrate specificity must be supported by the experimental design, and the effects of parallel metabolism cannot simply be ignored.

An inhibitor experiment can be designed to locate a functional restriction by varying both the donor and the inhibitor. A preparation that loses NADH-dependent conventional oxygen consumption after rotenone but maintains succinate-dependent oxygen consumption probably retains a functional shared downstream pathway. If a later intervention blocks both donor-dependent conventional rates, its target may be a common downstream component, a necessary shared condition or a major loss of membrane integrity. To distinguish these possibilities, one can add appropriate controls that examine a different donor region, measure carrier redox states, or establish whether the membrane remains intact. A respiratory rate alone is not a direct measurement of the abundance of a single complex.

In classical optical reasoning, a carrier located before an effective block tends to become reduced if electron input continues, whereas a carrier beyond the block tends to become oxidized if it can still transfer its electrons to oxygen. This rule is an excellent initial prediction, but it depends on the direction of ongoing flux. For example, the Q pool need not become strongly reduced after an upstream entry inhibitor when downstream oxidation continues, and inhibition of Complex IV may alter the entire network over time. Multiple entry and terminal paths in plants further weaken the assumption of one isolated linear chain. When a problem asks for the oxidation state of a specific carrier, first draw the active routes and identify which acceptor remains available.

Measuring membrane potential alongside oxygen uptake makes several otherwise ambiguous observations more informative. If a perturbation reduces oxygen consumption and dissipates membrane potential, a severe restriction of electron transport is a possible explanation. If oxygen consumption increases while membrane potential falls, increased proton conductance is a stronger candidate when substrates and oxygen remain available. If oxygen consumption persists but the potential is lower than expected for conventional Complex I-dependent respiration, a non-pumping entry route or a terminal bypass is possible. None of these combinations is uniquely diagnostic without controlled conditions. They guide the next experimental question rather than replacing direct evidence.

The same logic applies to genetic studies of plant respiratory complexes. A mutant with low mitochondrial oxygen consumption could have a defect in a respiratory subunit, mitochondrial protein import, complex assembly, substrate metabolism or organelle abundance. Demonstrating reduced activity of a particular complex with normal downstream donor-supported respiration narrows the explanation. Detecting the corresponding protein and comparing activity per amount of complex can distinguish reduced complex abundance from reduced catalytic performance. Restoration of the relevant gene provides further causal support. These experimental distinctions are sufficient for examination-level reasoning without requiring specialist protocols for complex purification or structural determination.

Finally, consider what the experiment actually measures in an illuminated plant. A whole-leaf oxygen trace combines oxygen production and oxygen consumption by several processes. Photorespiration, mitochondrial oxygen reduction and chloroplast-associated oxygen metabolism can all contribute under appropriate conditions. Measurements of isolated mitochondria more directly address respiratory electron transfer, but the isolation changes the physiological setting and may change substrate availability and regulation. Whole-tissue and isolated-organelle observations answer complementary questions. An accurate conclusion identifies the experimental scale and avoids treating the capacity of a pathway measured under imposed conditions as its exact flux in the intact plant.

12. Respiratory regulation, redox balance and reactive oxygen species

The plant mitochondrial electron transport chain operates within a continuously changing metabolic environment. The matrix receives respiratory substrates as sugars are mobilized, and the availability of reduced cofactors depends on the activities of dehydrogenases and the demand for metabolic intermediates. The rate of electron transfer depends on the ability of downstream carriers to accept electrons and on the energetic opposition imposed by the proton gradient. A large quantity of respiratory substrate therefore does not guarantee proportionately high oxygen consumption if another requirement becomes limiting. The network responds to both the supply of reducing equivalents and the capacity to use or dissipate the energy conserved during their oxidation.

In a growing root, ATP-consuming processes such as active transport, biosynthesis and cellular maintenance contribute to the generation of ADP. When mitochondria have access to ADP and inorganic phosphate, ATP synthase can use the proton gradient, allowing continued respiratory proton translocation. If ATP demand decreases and proton return slows, the gradient can become more restrictive to additional pumping. Electron transfer then slows under appropriate conditions, despite the continuing presence of oxygen and substrate. This is why substrate availability and cellular energy demand have to be considered together when interpreting a change in respiration.

The redox state of the matrix also interacts with carbon metabolism. The NAD+/NADH ratio influences the ability of NAD+-dependent enzymes to oxidize their substrates. When downstream electron transfer is restricted, NADH oxidation may become inadequate relative to production. A decline in the availability of oxidized NAD can restrict some dehydrogenase reactions and alter the distribution of metabolic intermediates. In a plant, these metabolites can also feed biosynthetic pathways, so changes in respiratory redox state may influence carbon allocation rather than merely the rate of complete oxidation to carbon dioxide. Alternative electron entry and terminal branches are among the features that permit adjustment to these varying demands.

Photosynthetic activity creates further opportunities for interaction between compartments. The chloroplast, cytosol and mitochondrial matrix have distinct nicotinamide cofactor pools. Reducing equivalents can be exchanged indirectly through metabolite reactions and transport systems, and external mitochondrial NAD(P)H dehydrogenases can accept suitable reducing equivalents outside the matrix. In an illuminated C3 leaf, mitochondrial glycine oxidation associated with photorespiration produces NADH in the matrix. The respiratory chain can participate in its reoxidation. The detailed carbon and nitrogen salvage itinerary is reserved for 6.B.05, but the connection demonstrates why mitochondria remain metabolically important during illumination. An illuminated chloroplast does not simply remove every need for mitochondrial redox regulation.

A high level of reduction in certain respiratory cofactors can increase the likelihood of side reactions with oxygen. The one-electron reduction of oxygen forms superoxide, O2•−. This differs from the coordinated four-electron reduction of oxygen to water at Complex IV. Respiratory Complex I and Complex III contain sites at which such side reactions can occur under appropriate conditions. Reduced flavin or quinone-associated intermediates may transfer electrons to oxygen before those electrons complete the normal terminal pathway. The probability of these reactions depends on oxygen availability, the lifetime and occupancy of reduced intermediates, the membrane energetic state and the detailed reaction mechanism.

Superoxide is a reactive oxygen species, but it is not identical to hydrogen peroxide. Superoxide dismutase catalyzes the conversion of two superoxide species together with protons to hydrogen peroxide and oxygen. Hydrogen peroxide can subsequently be removed by peroxide-metabolizing enzymes. These antioxidant reactions influence the amount of ROS detectable in a mitochondrial preparation. A measured ROS signal reflects both production and removal; it is not necessarily a direct measurement of electron leakage at one respiratory complex. Excess ROS can damage proteins, lipids and nucleic acids, while regulated changes in redox signaling can participate in cellular responses. Detailed stress signaling and antioxidant systems are addressed elsewhere in the plant physiology unit.

When terminal electron acceptance is restricted but upstream reduction continues, some carriers can become highly reduced. This can favor ROS formation at susceptible sites, but the outcome is conditional. For instance, restricting electron entry may decrease the reduction of a downstream ROS-generating site, even though total oxygen consumption falls. Inhibiting a quinone reaction may change the accumulation of a semiquinone intermediate differently depending on the site inhibited. The appropriate prediction therefore depends on the intervention. A statement that all inhibitors increase superoxide equally, or that higher respiration always means higher oxidative stress, ignores the chemistry of the individual carrier and the balance of antioxidant processes.

An alternative terminal pathway can help oxidize the Q pool when conventional downstream flow is restricted, provided the alternative enzyme is active. A less reduced pool may reduce the likelihood of some ROS-generating conditions. This provides a mechanistic basis for discussing the protective potential of alternative oxidase without making the stronger and unjustified claim that every increase in AOX activity necessarily reduces all ROS or increases plant fitness. Its physiological role depends on the balance between energy conservation, redox regulation and other metabolic requirements. Similarly, increasing a non-pumping entry activity can influence redox turnover, but its effect on Q reduction depends on the ability of the terminal routes to accept the additional electrons.

A well-controlled hypothetical experiment might compare mitochondria with identical substrate supply under two conditions. In the first, the conventional chain and alternative pathways remain functional. In the second, the conventional terminal route is partially restricted. If Q becomes more reduced and hydrogen peroxide accumulation increases in the second condition, the observations are consistent with a link between restricted electron removal and altered ROS metabolism. To demonstrate that the extra hydrogen peroxide originated specifically from Complex III, however, the investigator would need evidence that separates that site from other sources and accounts for antioxidant removal. A correlation between Q reduction and hydrogen peroxide concentration is informative but not a unique assignment of molecular origin.

The network is assembled from proteins encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. Appropriate expression, import, assembly and maintenance of respiratory components are necessary for sustained function. Plant-specific structural features and alternative enzymes modify the network while retaining the central principles of redox transfer and energy coupling. A defect in assembly may reduce the number of active complexes even when some individual subunits are present. This distinction between abundance and functionality is valuable when examining genetic data. An increase in transcript abundance for a respiratory subunit is not sufficient to infer a corresponding increase in mitochondrial respiration, because protein assembly, substrate availability and respiratory control also matter.

The plant-specific perspective can now be condensed into one causal chain. Carbon metabolism provides reducing equivalents; respiratory dehydrogenases transfer them to the quinone pool; conventional downstream complexes deliver electrons to oxygen while establishing an electrochemical gradient; alternative routes change the degree of energy conservation; and the capacity to oxidize reduced cofactors affects the continuation of metabolic reactions. The whole system is regulated by both substrate supply and energetic demand. This view links the biochemical and physiological roles of respiration without reducing the entire process to a fixed ATP yield.

The next chapter focuses on what happens to the gradient once it has been established. ATP synthase does not belong to the sequence of electron carriers from NADH to oxygen. It is a separate membrane enzyme whose activity is driven by the electrochemical difference created and maintained by respiratory reactions. That distinction explains how electron flow can be diverted, inhibited or uncoupled while ATP formation changes in different ways. The present chapter supplies the carrier map, the redox logic and the proton-accounting foundation required to understand those effects quantitatively.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All electrons visit complexes I, II, III and IV in order.

Correct concept: I and II are independent entrances into Q.

Exam reasoning: Roman numerals resemble a linear sequence. In an inhibitor question, map the donor before selecting the path.

Complex II receives a freely diffusible FADH₂ made elsewhere.

Correct concept: Its FAD is enzyme-bound; succinate is the immediate substrate.

Exam reasoning: The TCA yield shorthand hides cofactor location. A question may ask which metabolite actually enters II.

Complex II pumps two protons because QH₂ forms.

Correct concept: Q reduction is chemical proton transfer; II has no direct vectorial pumping.

Exam reasoning: Distinguish hydrogens in the chemical product from membrane translocation.

Every NADH pair gives ten protons.

Correct concept: Only the fully coupled I–III–IV route gives ten in the stated convention.

Exam reasoning: NADH is a donor, not a path specification. Identify alternative entry and exit first.

Cytochrome c carries two electrons and two protons.

Correct concept: A cytochrome c heme carries one electron; QH₂ carries two electrons and two protons.

Exam reasoning: Carrier names are learned without redox capacity. Count two cyt c molecules per pair.

Q is a soluble matrix protein.

Correct concept: Q is a lipid-associated quinone; cyt c is the small mobile heme protein on the P side.

Exam reasoning: Both are called mobile carriers, but their solvent and location differ.

Complex III's first Qo event completes the Q-cycle.

Correct concept: Two Qo turnovers produce two reduced cyt c and regenerate one QH₂ at Qi.

Exam reasoning: The first event supplies only one Qi electron. Track both turns before cancelling intermediates.

All four matrix protons per pair at IV are pumped.

Correct concept: Two are chemical protons and two are pumped in the conventional normalized reaction.

Exam reasoning: The full equation contains both categories. Distinguish product protons from translocated protons.

One O₂ accepts two electrons.

Correct concept: O₂ requires four electrons for two waters.

Exam reasoning: Confusing oxygen atoms with molecules produces a twofold numerical error.

ATP synthase passes electrons to oxygen.

Correct concept: Complex V uses the gradient and is not an electron carrier.

Exam reasoning: The numbering of complexes encourages a false linear continuation beyond IV.

Cyanide must eliminate all plant oxygen uptake.

Correct concept: AOX can support cyanide-resistant QH₂ oxidation; nonrespiratory sinks may also exist.

Exam reasoning: A conventional mammalian diagram is not the complete plant network. Specify pathways and controls.

Rotenone-resistant NADH oxidation proves Complex I remains active.

Correct concept: Alternative type II dehydrogenases can bypass I; incomplete inhibition must also be excluded.

Exam reasoning: Residual flux is not a molecular identity test by itself.

An externally added NADH molecule must enter the matrix.

Correct concept: External dehydrogenases can oxidize it on the P-facing inner membrane surface.

Exam reasoning: External substrate accessibility is not proof of inner membrane permeability.

All respiratory inhibitors increase ROS to the same extent.

Correct concept: ROS depends on electron supply, individual site chemistry and antioxidant removal.

Exam reasoning: Avoid extrapolating a result from one inhibitor to every block.

Equal oxygen uptake means equal ATP output.

Correct concept: Route-specific proton contributions and coupling efficiency may differ.

Exam reasoning: Convert oxygen to pairs, identify routes and distinguish pumping from ATP formation.

Illuminated leaves do not respire.

Correct concept: Mitochondrial respiration can continue while photosynthesis produces oxygen.

Exam reasoning: Net gas exchange combines multiple processes; identify the experimental compartment.

A lower membrane potential uniquely identifies an ETC block.

Correct concept: A chain block, proton leak or altered ion movement may lower potential.

Exam reasoning: Use oxygen flux and complementary measurements to distinguish causes.

Examination practice

Original practice questions; these are not claimed to be previous-year questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

Part B

1. The conventional electron acceptor for Complex I is
  1. cytochrome c
  2. ATP synthase
  3. ubiquinone
  4. oxygen

Correct answer: C — ubiquinone.

Complex I transfers matrix NADH reducing equivalents to Q. Cytochrome c is downstream of III, oxygen is the terminal acceptor at IV, and ATP synthase is not a redox acceptor.

2. Which component belongs to both the TCA cycle and the respiratory chain?
  1. Succinate dehydrogenase
  2. Cytochrome c oxidase
  3. Complex I
  4. Complex III

Correct answer: A — Succinate dehydrogenase.

Succinate dehydrogenase oxidizes succinate to fumarate and feeds electrons to Q. The other complexes perform respiratory electron transfer without catalyzing that TCA step.

3. The initial flavin acceptor of NADH at Complex I is
  1. FAD in Complex II
  2. heme a
  3. FMN
  4. CuB

Correct answer: C — FMN.

FMN accepts NADH reducing equivalents in the matrix arm of I. FAD is associated with II, whereas heme a and CuB are components of IV.

4. The mobile one-electron carrier connecting III to IV is
  1. cytochrome c
  2. fumarate
  3. NADPH
  4. ubiquinol

Correct answer: A — cytochrome c.

Cytochrome c cycles between ferric and ferrous heme states on the P side. QH₂ feeds III, not IV directly in the cytochrome pathway.

5. Which conventional complex does not directly pump protons?
  1. III
  2. IV
  3. II
  4. I

Correct answer: C — II.

II transfers succinate-derived electrons to Q without direct pumping. III has an effective vectorial contribution through the Q-cycle; I and IV are pumps.

6. The conventional Complex I contribution per NADH pair is
  1. 2 H⁺
  2. 10 H⁺
  3. 6 H⁺
  4. 4 H⁺

Correct answer: D — 4 H⁺.

I contributes four. Ten is the whole conventional NADH route, six the conventional downstream contribution, and two the IV contribution.

7. The Qo site of Complex III primarily supports
  1. NADH oxidation in matrix
  2. oxygen reduction to water
  3. quinol oxidation on the P side
  4. ATP synthesis

Correct answer: C — quinol oxidation on the P side.

Qo oxidizes QH₂ and releases protons on the P side. The Qi site participates in Q reduction; neither site forms ATP or performs terminal oxygen reduction.

8. The Qi site is located toward the
  1. cytosolic plasma membrane
  2. outer mitochondrial membrane
  3. matrix-facing side of the inner membrane
  4. P-facing surface only

Correct answer: C — matrix-facing side of the inner membrane.

Qi is on the N side, where quinone reduction is coupled to matrix proton uptake.

9. Two complete Qo oxidations in a Q-cycle reduce how many mobile cyt c molecules?
  1. four
  2. two
  3. zero
  4. one

Correct answer: B — two.

One high-potential branch electron reduces cyt c per Qo turnover. The second electron in each turnover feeds the Qi branch.

10. The terminal catalytic center of Complex IV contains
  1. FAD and fumarate
  2. cytochrome bL only
  3. heme a₃ and CuB
  4. FMN and Q

Correct answer: C — heme a₃ and CuB.

The heme a₃–CuB center catalyzes oxygen reduction. FMN belongs to I; bL is part of III.

11. How many electrons are needed to fully reduce one O₂?
  1. four
  2. eight
  3. one
  4. two

Correct answer: A — four.

O₂ plus four electrons and four chemical protons gives two water molecules. Two electrons are sufficient for only half an O₂ in normalized full reduction.

12. At Complex IV, chemical protons are
  1. all pumped
  2. incorporated into water
  3. all released as superoxide
  4. taken from the P side for ATP

Correct answer: B — incorporated into water.

The four chemical protons per O₂ enter water; four separate protons are pumped in the conventional account.

13. Which plant enzyme supplies electrons to Q while bypassing I?
  1. ATP synthase
  2. type II NAD(P)H dehydrogenase
  3. AOX
  4. cytochrome c

Correct answer: B — type II NAD(P)H dehydrogenase.

Alternative dehydrogenases provide an entry branch. AOX is a terminal QH₂ oxidation branch.

14. A typical external type II dehydrogenase catalytic site faces
  1. intermembrane space
  2. thylakoid lumen
  3. chloroplast stroma
  4. matrix only

Correct answer: A — intermembrane space.

The external face of the inner mitochondrial membrane accesses cofactors outside the matrix. Chloroplast compartments are unrelated to this topology.

15. Which pathway does AOX bypass?
  1. III and IV
  2. all TCA reactions
  3. I and II only
  4. ATP synthase and no ETC complex

Correct answer: A — III and IV.

AOX oxidizes QH₂ and reduces oxygen without the normal cytochrome III–IV path. Upstream I can still operate.

16. Rotenone primarily interferes with the conventional
  1. ATP synthase
  2. Complex I Q-linked reaction
  3. Complex II substrate site
  4. Complex IV

Correct answer: B — Complex I Q-linked reaction.

Rotenone targets the quinone-linked region of I, whereas malonate probes succinate oxidation and cyanide inhibits IV.

17. Malonate is used to probe
  1. succinate dehydrogenase
  2. Complex I FMN
  3. AOX alone
  4. cytochrome c binding

Correct answer: A — succinate dehydrogenase.

Malonate competes with succinate at II. It does not specifically inhibit the cyt c or AOX pathways.

18. Antimycin A classically acts at
  1. Complex IV copper
  2. ATP synthase F₁
  3. Complex III Qi region
  4. Complex I NADH site

Correct answer: C — Complex III Qi region.

Antimycin A interferes with the lower branch of the Q-cycle at Qi, disrupting III turnover.

19. The most direct terminal respiratory product of complete O₂ reduction is
  1. carbon dioxide
  2. succinate
  3. water
  4. ATP

Correct answer: C — water.

Oxygen receives electrons and chemical protons to form water. CO₂ comes from carbon metabolism, ATP is a coupling product and succinate is a substrate.

20. Which statement about mitochondria in illuminated leaves is correct?
  1. They synthesize oxygen at IV.
  2. They no longer have a proton gradient.
  3. They stop oxidizing substrates whenever chloroplasts function.
  4. They may respire concurrently with photosynthesis.

Correct answer: D — They may respire concurrently with photosynthesis..

Oxygen production by photosynthesis and consumption by mitochondria can coexist. Net leaf oxygen exchange does not measure respiration alone.

21. The typical outward pumping direction is
  1. P to N
  2. matrix to intermembrane-space side
  3. cytosol to chloroplast
  4. lumen to stroma

Correct answer: B — matrix to intermembrane-space side.

In conventional mitochondria I, III and IV contribute outward vectorial proton movement from N to P. Proton return through V is in the opposite direction.

22. Superoxide is generated by
  1. ATP hydrolysis alone
  2. one-electron reduction of oxygen
  3. complete oxidation of water
  4. four-electron reduction of O₂ to water

Correct answer: B — one-electron reduction of oxygen.

An adventitious electron transfer to O₂ forms O₂•−. The complete terminal reaction instead gives water.

23. Which enzyme uses the gradient without carrying respiratory electrons?
  1. ATP synthase
  2. Complex I
  3. Complex III
  4. Complex II

Correct answer: A — ATP synthase.

ATP synthase couples proton return to phosphorylation. I–III are redox enzymes.

24. Which redox carrier is shared by conventional and alternative respiratory entry routes?
  1. CuA
  2. Cytochrome c exclusively
  3. matrix NAD⁺ exclusively
  4. the Q pool

Correct answer: D — the Q pool.

I, II and type II dehydrogenases all deliver electrons to Q. Cyt c is downstream of III and is bypassed by AOX.

25. The reduced form of cytochrome c has heme iron in the
  1. metal-free state
  2. Fe²⁺ state
  3. Fe⁰ state
  4. Fe³⁺ state

Correct answer: B — Fe²⁺ state.

Reduction adds one electron to ferric heme, forming ferrous iron. It does not remove iron or produce metallic iron.

Part C

26. A mitochondrial preparation lacks active alternative routes. Rotenone reduces NADH-linked oxygen use almost to zero; succinate restores it. Which interpretation fits?
  1. Q is absent
  2. I is impaired but the Q-to-O₂ route functions
  3. II cannot oxidize succinate
  4. IV is inhibited

Correct answer: B — I is impaired but the Q-to-O₂ route functions.

Succinate enters downstream of I through II and uses Q, III and IV. Rescue excludes a complete common downstream block.

27. Three samples use identical amounts of O₂. A uses I and III–IV; B uses II and III–IV; C uses II and AOX. Rank nominal proton contributions.
  1. A > B > C
  2. A = B = C
  3. B > A > C
  4. C > B > A

Correct answer: A — A > B > C.

The per-pair contributions are 10, 6 and 0 respectively. Equal oxygen use fixes electron-pair throughput, not pumping per pair.

28. Twelve micromoles of NADH pairs follow the conventional cytochrome path. The nominal outward proton amount is
  1. 72 μmol
  2. 24 μmol
  3. 12 μmol
  4. 120 μmol

Correct answer: D — 120 μmol.

Each pair contributes 4 + 4 + 2 = 10; multiply 12 × 10 = 120. The 24 value is IV alone.

29. A conventional system reduces 8 μmol O₂ using only succinate entry. The nominal proton amount is
  1. 48 μmol
  2. 80 μmol
  3. 16 μmol
  4. 96 μmol

Correct answer: D — 96 μmol.

Eight O₂ means sixteen electron pairs; each supplies six nominal protons through III and IV, giving 96.

30. Of 50 electron pairs, 30 use I and 40 leave through III–IV. Find the total nominal proton contribution.
  1. 240
  2. 500
  3. 360
  4. 120

Correct answer: C — 360.

Use 4NI + 6NC = 4×30 + 6×40 = 360. The overlap between the subsets need not be known for this additive account.

31. A conventional chain is blocked at III while I receives substrate and IV can oxidize its remaining cyt c. The earliest expected change is
  1. Q tends reduced; cyt c tends oxidized
  2. both immediately vanish
  3. both pools must remain constant
  4. Q becomes oxidized and cyt c reduced

Correct answer: A — Q tends reduced; cyt c tends oxidized.

The upstream Q pool receives electrons but cannot normally discharge through III. Downstream cyt c can transfer its remaining electrons to IV. The result assumes no alternative outlets.

32. In a conventional preparation IV is inhibited, with continued upstream supply. Which immediate tendency is most plausible?
  1. cyt c becomes entirely oxidized while donating rapidly to O₂
  2. O₂ is produced at IV
  3. Complex II turns into a pump
  4. cyt c accumulates in the reduced state

Correct answer: D — cyt c accumulates in the reduced state.

Reduced cyt c cannot efficiently donate to the terminal enzyme and accumulates. The other possibilities contradict the blocked oxidation route.

33. An external NADH response disappears in a mutant, while succinate respiration is preserved. Which control-based conclusion is strongest?
  1. matrix NADH crossed the inner membrane
  2. All complexes are absent
  3. external NADH entry may be defective while common downstream components function
  4. AOX is necessarily deleted

Correct answer: C — external NADH entry may be defective while common downstream components function.

Succinate tests an independent entry into shared downstream pathways. The selective defect supports external entry; gene rescue and integrity checks would strengthen the inference.

34. A plant preparation retains 40% O₂ uptake after rotenone. What is the best inference?
  1. Complex I necessarily pumps 40% more protons
  2. a residual route is possible, but inhibitor and substrate controls are needed
  3. Exactly 40% used type II ND before treatment
  4. all mitochondria are uncoupled

Correct answer: B — a residual route is possible, but inhibitor and substrate controls are needed.

Residual flux may be alternative entry or incomplete inhibition. Redistribution after blockade prevents treating residual capacity as the original fraction.

35. A chain inhibitor and protonophore both lower potential. Only the protonophore increases oxygen uptake under abundant donor and O₂. Why?
  1. dissipation decreases the energetic backpressure on electron transfer
  2. it directly activates II pumping
  3. It creates electrons from ATP
  4. it makes IV produce O₂

Correct answer: A — dissipation decreases the energetic backpressure on electron transfer.

Proton conductance lowers the opposition to pumping, so flux can rise without increased ATP output. Inhibiting the chain restricts electron transfer instead.

36. A 2 mL chamber loses 30 μM O₂ over 3 min, with 0.5 mg protein. Normalized consumption is
  1. 20 μmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  2. 10 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  3. 60 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹
  4. 40 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹

Correct answer: D — 40 nmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹.

Slope = 10 μM/min. Multiply by 0.002 L = 0.02 μmol/min = 20 nmol/min. Divide by 0.5 mg to obtain 40.

37. An illustrative Q pool of 100 has 70 QH₂. If 20 Q are reduced and 15 QH₂ oxidized, the new reduced fraction is
  1. 95%
  2. 75%
  3. 55%
  4. 90%

Correct answer: B — 75%.

Net reduced population changes by +20 −15 = +5, giving 75/100. Both simultaneous reactions matter.

38. For ΔE°′ = +1.14 V, n = 2 and F = 96.485 kJ V⁻¹ mol⁻¹, ΔG°′ is closest to
  1. +110 kJ/mol
  2. −220 kJ/mol
  3. +220 kJ/mol
  4. −110 kJ/mol

Correct answer: B — −220 kJ/mol.

ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′ = −2×96.485×1.14 ≈ −220. The negative sign follows from a positive acceptor-minus-donor difference.

39. At 298 K, ψP−ψN = +0.15 V and pHN−pHP = +0.5. Approximate outward proton cost is
  1. 17.3 kJ/mol
  2. 11.6 kJ/mol
  3. −17.3 kJ/mol
  4. 2.85 kJ/mol

Correct answer: A — 17.3 kJ/mol.

Electrical cost ≈ 14.47 and chemical cost ≈ 2.85 kJ/mol. Both oppose outward movement, giving a positive total ≈17.32.

40. A mutant loses normal Qo activity. Which Q-cycle process is directly restricted?
  1. matrix NADH synthesis by TCA
  2. oxygen binding to IV only
  3. ATP binding to F₁ only
  4. P-side quinol oxidation

Correct answer: D — P-side quinol oxidation.

Qo is the quinol oxidation and bifurcation site. The other processes may be affected indirectly but are not its immediate catalytic function.

41. If the first Qo turnover has occurred but the second cannot occur, which event remains incomplete?
  1. initial release of two P-side protons
  2. initial electron delivery to the Qi site
  3. completion of Qi quinol regeneration
  4. reduction of one cyt c

Correct answer: C — completion of Qi quinol regeneration.

One low-branch electron is insufficient to fully reduce Qi quinone. A second turnover and net proton uptake complete QH₂ generation.

42. A mitochondrial preparation loses cyt c during isolation. Addition of cyt c improves O₂ uptake. The most defensible interpretation is
  1. outer membrane generates all proton motive force
  2. Q must be dissolved in the matrix
  3. I was converted into AOX
  4. loss of an accessible carrier limited III-to-IV transfer

Correct answer: D — loss of an accessible carrier limited III-to-IV transfer.

Loss and replacement of cyt c can restore the connection between III and IV when the added carrier can reach its site. The assay does not establish other claims.

43. An intervention raises H₂O₂ accumulation while Q becomes more reduced. What does this establish?
  1. ROS removal is necessarily unchanged
  2. a correlation consistent with changed redox/ROS balance, not a unique source
  3. Complex IV pumps eight extra protons per pair
  4. All H₂O₂ originates exclusively at III

Correct answer: B — a correlation consistent with changed redox/ROS balance, not a unique source.

The observed level reflects generation and scavenging. Site assignment requires additional experiments.

44. A mutation lowers total O₂ uptake and abundance of assembled I, with normal specific catalytic activity of remaining I. Which mechanism is supported?
  1. fewer assembled active I complexes
  2. oxygen is no longer terminal acceptor
  3. the enzyme's catalytic chemistry must be abolished
  4. II directly pumps four additional protons

Correct answer: A — fewer assembled active I complexes.

Lower assembled abundance with normal specific activity supports fewer functional complexes rather than necessarily reduced turnover per complex.

45. A plant line shows cyanide-resistant respiration that is suppressed by an independently validated AOX inhibitor. Which interpretation is supported?
  1. Q no longer accepts electrons
  2. IV became cyanide-resistant and pumped twice as much
  3. a terminal alternative branch is functioning under the assay conditions
  4. all respiration is mediated by I

Correct answer: C — a terminal alternative branch is functioning under the assay conditions.

The two interventions are consistent with QH₂ oxidation through AOX. This measures activity under assay conditions, not unperturbed flux without additional evidence.

46. In an isolated conventional system with only succinate supplied, adding a competitive malonate inhibitor lowers O₂ uptake. Increasing succinate partially restores it. This supports
  1. a cyt c deficiency
  2. competition at II substrate binding
  3. terminal IV inhibition
  4. a proton leak

Correct answer: B — competition at II substrate binding.

Competitive inhibition can be overcome partly by greater substrate concentration. The observed dependence fits a succinate-binding effect at II.

47. A membrane-impermeant NADH analog inhibits an internal dehydrogenase in inverted vesicles but not intact mitochondria. What follows?
  1. orientation and accessibility explain the different response
  2. AOX is the direct target
  3. the dehydrogenase is necessarily absent in intact organelles
  4. NADH diffuses freely across the membrane

Correct answer: A — orientation and accessibility explain the different response.

Inversion can expose matrix-facing catalytic sites to added reagents. An intact membrane limits access, so inhibition depends on orientation.

48. If O₂ declines sufficiently while donor supply continues and all terminal outlets are O₂-dependent, what is the likely early sequence?
  1. ATP synthase creates O₂
  2. NAD⁺ increases indefinitely
  3. cyt c immediately becomes a proton pump
  4. terminal electron removal slows; reduced carriers accumulate; pumping may decrease

Correct answer: D — terminal electron removal slows; reduced carriers accumulate; pumping may decrease.

The terminal acceptor is limiting, so carrier oxidation and coupled proton translocation may fall. NADH reoxidation is restricted.

49. An artificial donor drives IV-dependent O₂ uptake after III inhibition. Which control best distinguishes chemical autooxidation?
  1. omit oxygen
  2. increase mitochondrial protein without measuring a baseline
  3. measure only pH
  4. measure donor-dependent O₂ uptake in an otherwise identical chamber without mitochondria

Correct answer: D — measure donor-dependent O₂ uptake in an otherwise identical chamber without mitochondria.

A donor-only blank tests nonenzymatic oxygen consumption, so the mitochondrial component can be interpreted.

50. A leaf has positive net O₂ exchange in light. After darkening, net O₂ becomes negative. Which conclusion is valid?
  1. respiration was absent in light
  2. photorespiration and ETC are identical
  3. IV releases O₂ in light
  4. photosynthetic production previously exceeded consumption; respiration may have been active in light

Correct answer: D — photosynthetic production previously exceeded consumption; respiration may have been active in light.

Net exchange is the difference among production and consumption. It cannot show absence of one component during illumination.

Assertion–reason, integration and SET practice

51. Assertion: A type II dehydrogenase can sustain conventional terminal O₂ reduction. Reason: It feeds Q without pumping protons at entry.
  1. both true; reason explains assertion
  2. both true; reason unrelated
  3. assertion true, reason false
  4. assertion false, reason true

Correct answer: A — both true; reason explains assertion.

QH₂ can be oxidized by III and IV regardless of whether it came from I or an alternative entry enzyme. The entry mechanism explains the bypass.

52. Assertion: Complex II is not part of the TCA cycle. Reason: It directly reduces Q.
  1. both true; reason explains assertion
  2. both true; reason unrelated
  3. assertion true, reason false
  4. assertion false, reason true

Correct answer: D — assertion false, reason true.

II is succinate dehydrogenase, a TCA enzyme and respiratory Q reducer. The assertion is false despite the true reason.

53. Choose the correct sequence for conventional NADH respiration.
  1. NADH → II → cyt c → Q → IV
  2. NADH → I → Q → III → cyt c → IV → O₂
  3. NADH → V → O₂ → Q
  4. NADH → IV → III → II

Correct answer: B — NADH → I → Q → III → cyt c → IV → O₂.

I passes electrons to Q; III reduces cyt c; IV reduces oxygen. II is an independent succinate-linked entry.

54. Consider statements: (i) Cyt c transfers one electron; (ii) QH₂ carries two; (iii) IV uses four electrons per O₂; (iv) V directly oxidizes NADH. Which set is correct?
  1. i and iv
  2. ii and iv
  3. i, ii and iii
  4. all four

Correct answer: C — i, ii and iii.

The first three statements describe electron stoichiometry correctly. V is ATP synthase, not an NADH oxidase.

55. A student claims 2 μmol O₂ consumption equals 2 μmol pairs. Correct the calculation.
  1. 4 μmol pairs
  2. 1 μmol pairs
  3. 8 μmol pairs
  4. 2 μmol pairs is correct

Correct answer: A — 4 μmol pairs.

Each O₂ accepts four individual electrons, equivalent to two pairs; 2 × 2 = 4 μmol pairs.

Revision and mastery

The plant respiratory chain reoxidizes metabolic cofactors while transferring electrons to O₂. Complexes I, III and IV contribute to the conventional electrochemical gradient; II and alternative type II dehydrogenases feed the Q pool without directly pumping. The Q-cycle bridges two-electron quinol and one-electron cyt c. IV uses four electrons per O₂; separate chemical proton uptake from pumping. The branch structure explains variable coupling.
