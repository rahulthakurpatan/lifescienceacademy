1. The biological problem: reaching a living plant cell

A leaf may contain millions of bacterial cells on its outer surface without showing a visible disease. A fungal spore may land on the same leaf, produce a short germ tube and cease growing before it reaches a suitable site. A virus particle may remain infectious outside a plant cell but cannot manufacture additional virus particles there. These observations reveal three distinct obstacles to infection: reaching a suitable host, crossing the physical boundaries of that host and establishing a biological relationship with its living cells. A microorganism that succeeds at the first obstacle has not necessarily succeeded at the second or third. This distinction explains why neither a count of microbes on a leaf nor the presence of a microbial molecule inside a leaf establishes the entire mechanism of disease.

The word pathogen describes an agent capable of causing disease under suitable conditions. The qualification is important because the ability to cause damage is not independent of the environment or the host. A strain may encounter a resistant plant that arrests it before multiplication, while a susceptible plant may permit extensive colonization. A microbe that occupies the outer surface of a plant is an epiphyte in that location; a microbe residing inside plant tissues without causing apparent damage can be described as an endophyte. These names describe ecological relationships or positions, not permanently fixed molecular identities. Pathogenic bacteria may pass through an epiphytic stage before entering a leaf. Consequently, finding them on a healthy plant is not logically inconsistent with their ability to cause disease.

The term colonization refers to establishment and persistence of an agent at a site, often involving multiplication when the agent is a cellular microorganism. Penetration denotes passage across a physical barrier. Initial infection is best understood as successful establishment of the relevant host–pathogen interaction, rather than as a synonym for any encounter. Disease refers to a disturbance of normal structure or function. The stages may overlap in time, but they answer different questions. An assay of adherence measures a prerequisite for some entry routes; an assay of internal bacterial population measures a later property; and a measure of chlorosis or loss of tissue function examines consequences that can occur after colonization.

A plant cell is bounded by a plasma membrane and, outside that membrane, a mechanically important cell wall. Most aerial epidermal cells have an additional outer cuticular covering. Thus, contact with the outside of an intact leaf is not equivalent to contact with the plasma membrane of a living epidermal cell. If bacteria move through a natural opening into an air space below the epidermis, they have entered the tissue but still occupy an extracellular compartment. If a fungal hypha passes through a host wall but becomes surrounded by a host-derived membrane, it has crossed the wall without acquiring unrestricted access to host cytoplasm. If a plant virus genome becomes available to host ribosomes and replication factors, it must have gained intracellular access, even when the initial encounter occurred at a wound or during vector feeding.

An animal cell may internalize material by remodeling its plasma membrane around extracellular particles. The general principles of animal bacterial entry and viral receptor-mediated membrane penetration studied earlier therefore do not provide universal models for plant infection. Plant cells possess endocytic machinery, membrane receptors and an actin cytoskeleton, but these features operate behind a wall that ordinarily limits access by large external structures. The wall does not make plants immune to microbes. Instead, it changes the relative importance of external openings, growing pathogen structures, wall-degrading processes and physical discontinuities in the host surface. A successful plant pathogen need not reproduce the cellular invasion strategy of an animal pathogen.

It is useful to picture three successive compartments: the external environment, extracellular space within the plant and the contents of living host cells enclosed by their membranes. The second compartment is part of the plant body but not part of host cytoplasm. At each transition, different properties become limiting. Surface moisture may influence whether a spore germinates or a bacterium survives; tissue anatomy may determine whether an external agent reaches internal spaces; membrane access may determine whether a viral genome can be expressed. Finally, host compatibility and defensive responses determine whether the agent persists after reaching its destination. No single observation proves that all these transitions have occurred.

Host range describes the set of hosts an agent can successfully infect under defined circumstances. Tissue tropism is a preference for particular tissues or cell types within a host. Neither property can be inferred solely from the distribution of a receptor. In plants, a susceptible cell may be inaccessible from an external surface; in a different host, physical access may be possible but the intracellular environment may fail to support virus multiplication. A third host may allow initial entry but activate effective resistance before the microorganism becomes established. Thinking in successive barriers provides a more accurate explanation of host specificity than treating all failure to infect as a single defect.

The chapter therefore distinguishes the plant surface, the extracellular space inside the tissue, the wall and the host membrane. These spatial distinctions provide a framework for interpreting guard-cell responses, bacterial apoplastic growth, fungal penetration structures, oomycete haustoria and the delivery of plant viruses. Later chapters can then ask how a pathogen modifies a cell or spreads through an organ without repeating the question of how the first site was reached.

2. Plant barriers from the cuticle to the plasma membrane

The first surface encountered by many leaf pathogens is not cellulose itself but the cuticle. This is a hydrophobic extracellular layer associated with the outer epidermal wall. Cutin forms an important polymeric framework, while waxes contribute to surface properties. The cuticle reduces uncontrolled water loss and influences the retention and movement of droplets. These same functions change the opportunities available to microorganisms. A water droplet that runs off quickly may leave a different distribution of cells or spores from one retained in a depression near a vein. The surface is therefore both a physical barrier and an ecological habitat, and its effects cannot be described by a single measurement of thickness.

Below this surface lies a wall composed principally of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix containing other polysaccharides and proteins. In a typical growing primary wall, hemicelluloses and pectins contribute to the organisation and physical properties of the matrix. The wall withstands forces exerted by the pressurized protoplast while remaining permeable to water and various dissolved substances. Its mechanical strength and its permeability are distinct properties. A hydrated polymer network can allow the movement of ions and small molecules but restrict the passage of an intact bacterial cell. A microbial protein or small molecular signal can therefore approach host cell-surface receptors without the whole organism crossing the wall.

Between adjacent plant cells, the middle lamella is enriched in pectic material and participates in adhesion. An organism advancing between cells may encounter wall surfaces and the material joining cells without penetrating either plasma membrane. Weakening intercellular adhesion is not the same event as perforating a plasma membrane. If the structure of the middle lamella deteriorates, adjacent cells may become less firmly attached even before their membrane integrity is lost. This helps explain why wall-associated changes may contribute to later tissue damage without implying that every pathogenic cell grows inside plant cytoplasm.

The apoplast comprises extracellular wall spaces and intercellular spaces, together with conducting spaces such as mature xylem conduits. In contrast, the symplast comprises the interconnected cytoplasm of living cells joined by plasmodesmata. A molecule may travel through water in wall spaces without crossing a plasma membrane, whereas reaching the cytoplasm from the apoplast requires passage across a membrane. The tonoplast surrounding the vacuole is an additional membrane and should not be confused with the plasma membrane. In a microscopic image, a large central vacuole can displace cytoplasm toward the cell periphery, making an external structure close to the cytoplasm appear intracellular unless membrane boundaries are labelled.

An intercellular air space does not normally contain the same concentrations of nutrients or water as cytoplasm. Mesophyll cells are adapted for gas exchange, and the internal leaf architecture allows carbon dioxide to diffuse toward photosynthetic cells. An aerobic bacterium in this region may encounter oxygen yet lack sufficient water or available carbon for population expansion. The extracellular compartment is thus an active physiological environment rather than an empty cavity. Host membrane transport helps determine its chemical composition, while transpiration and other processes influence hydration. Entry into this space provides access to potential resources but is not automatically sufficient for growth.

Physical and chemical exclusion work together. A surface compound can inhibit microbial growth, but a chemically inert barrier could still be effective if it excludes an entire organism. Conversely, a surface that is physically traversable can be unfavorable because of desiccation, nutrient limitation or other conditions. When comparing host genotypes with altered surfaces, it is necessary to distinguish changes in adhesion, changes in permeability and changes in defense. A thinner cuticle might increase solute leakage in one setting but also alter microbial detection or induce compensatory responses. It is not sound to conclude that every reduction in barrier thickness produces greater infection.

Young tissues and old tissues need not offer identical barriers. A growing organ changes its wall properties as cells expand and differentiate. A mature root region may acquire suberin-rich barriers; a young root tip has different protective architecture and exposure. A wound changes the surface by creating a discontinuity but also activates host responses. Consequently, an anatomical diagram should be read as the representation of a particular region and developmental stage. The location of the pathogen in that diagram matters more than the simple label of leaf or root.

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable lipid bilayer. Its proteins mediate transport, perception and communication. If microbial recognition takes place at a membrane receptor, the ligand must reach the receptor's extracellular domain, but the receptor does not thereby become an entrance for the microbe. This point is particularly important when comparing bacterial flagellin perception by a plant receptor with the binding of an animal virus to a cellular attachment receptor. Both involve molecular recognition at a membrane; the biological consequences can be quite different. One can initiate defense without admitting the organism, while the other may facilitate an entry event.

The geometry of the plant surface also explains the importance of local heterogeneity. A cuticle interrupted by a pore has a different topology from an uninterrupted surface, even when the cuticle elsewhere has the same chemical composition. A microbe found within an internal cavity may have followed an existing passage rather than broken through a wall. A continuous barrier should therefore be distinguished from the surrounding tissue as a whole. Loss of a single local point of continuity can provide access without changing the bulk composition of the organ.

3. From observation to evidence for routes of infection

In early plant pathology, the visible association of a fungus with damaged tissue was an insufficient explanation by itself. A fungus could have caused damage, or it could have appeared after tissue had already deteriorated for another reason. A causal investigation required distinguishing an initiating biological agent from an opportunistic colonizer. This problem remains relevant to modern imaging and molecular detection. Detecting a microbial sequence or a hypha at the edge of a lesion establishes presence, but the spatial and temporal relationship between that agent and the beginning of injury requires additional evidence.

Research on tobacco mosaic disease was especially important because the causal agent did not behave like a conventional cellular microorganism. In 1892 Dmitri Ivanovsky reported that infectivity could remain in material after filtration through a barrier intended to retain bacteria. In 1898 Martinus Beijerinck interpreted the transmissible agent in a way that helped establish the modern distinction between viruses and cellular microbes. These findings concerned the nature of infectious agents rather than a complete molecular explanation of plant cell entry. Their conceptual importance for this chapter is that an infectious agent need not be able to grow independently outside the host cell.

The tobacco mosaic findings also demonstrate the distinction between infectious material and a visible cellular structure. Filterability alone cannot identify the shape of the infectious particle or prove how it enters a leaf. A filter separates entities according to physical properties under specific conditions; it does not report their interaction with an epidermis. The later development of virology made it possible to ask whether the infectious unit was a nucleic acid, a particle containing nucleic acid, or another biologically active entity. These questions differ from how a microbial cell navigates a natural pore.

A different line of evidence came from investigations of specialized fungal infection structures. Howard and colleagues reported in 1991 that the rice blast fungus could penetrate hard artificial surfaces using a specialized appressorium and that indirect measurements indicated very high internal pressure. The experimental value of an artificial substrate was that penetration could be studied without the full complexity of a living plant. The work supported a substantial mechanical contribution to penetration in that fungal system, rather than attributing all crossing of plant surfaces to chemical dissolution. It did not establish that every fungus uses the same pressure or that wall-degrading enzymes never participate.

By the beginning of this century, genetic analysis of host cells had revealed membrane-associated recognition systems. Gómez-Gómez and Boller identified FLS2 in Arabidopsis in 2000 through studies of flagellin perception. Genetic variation in response to a defined microbial signal linked the host gene to perception of that signal. Importantly, demonstrating a receptor's contribution to perception does not require the bacterium to occupy host cytoplasm. A microbial component reaching the outside face of the plasma membrane can carry information about the presence of a pathogen before any cellular invasion has occurred.

A major change in the interpretation of stomatal entry followed the work of Melotto and colleagues in 2006. Stomata had long been recognized as anatomical passages, but experiments demonstrated that stomatal apertures themselves could change in response to microbial signals. In the studied Arabidopsis and Pseudomonas system, microbial recognition was associated with closure, and a bacterial virulence factor could counteract this response. The guard cells surrounding the pore were therefore recognized not merely as structures through which microbes happen to pass but as a regulatory component of the early host barrier.

The historical evidence spans different levels of organization. Filter experiments addressed the character of an infectious agent; studies of fungal penetration addressed the physical capacity to cross a surface; receptor genetics addressed host perception; and stomatal observations addressed regulation of a tissue passage. None of these methods alone can establish an entire infection sequence. Their combination provides the mechanistic framework used today. For examination reasoning, the most valuable lesson is to identify the particular causal link supported by a finding, rather than memorize the discovery as an isolated name and year.

4. Stomata, hydathodes and wounds as distinct entry routes

A stoma is an adjustable pore in the epidermis bordered by a pair of guard cells. Changes in guard-cell geometry change the effective size of the pore. During normal plant physiology, stomatal adjustment balances carbon dioxide uptake against the loss of water vapor. The existence of this exchange surface creates an unavoidable tradeoff: a leaf optimized for gas exchange cannot remain entirely sealed from its surroundings. A bacterial cell near an open pore may therefore gain access to the space below the epidermis without needing to dissolve an intact cuticle.

The substomatal cavity connects to intercellular spaces associated with the mesophyll. After a bacterium reaches this cavity, its physical relationship to the host changes from external surface residence to internal extracellular residence. Crossing the plane of the epidermis has not transported the bacterium through the membrane of a guard cell or mesophyll cell. Indeed, a functional stoma is useful for gas exchange precisely because it connects extracellular spaces across the epidermal boundary. A drawing that shows a bacterium entering a stoma and then immediately labels it as cytoplasmic would omit the intervening host wall and membrane.

An anatomical passage is not necessarily open at every moment. Changes in the movement of ions and water affect guard-cell turgor and hence aperture. Closure can restrict access through that particular route while leaving wound routes unaffected. A host that recognizes microbial signals can sometimes alter the balance between access and exclusion without constructing a new physical wall. Closure is therefore an example of a physiological change that produces a mechanical consequence. The response must be distinguished from killing microbes: a closed pore can exclude an otherwise viable bacterium that remains on the surface.

Hydathodes are associated with the release of liquid water, called guttation, often near leaf margins or tips. In a typical epithemal hydathode, water pores communicate with a loose tissue associated with vascular endings. The structure differs in function from an ordinary regulated gas-exchange stoma. An agent adapted to this route may encounter an aqueous internal microenvironment relatively near xylem. The relevance of this arrangement is topological: a short anatomical connection between an exterior opening and vascular tissue creates a different initial colonization opportunity from a passage into ordinary mesophyll air spaces.

Hydathodes should not be treated as permanently undefended pores. Cerutti and colleagues investigated bacterial colonization of cauliflower hydathodes and linked local host immune responses to restriction of systemic infection. Their observations distinguish the existence of a route from the ability of a particular organism to expand after reaching its internal tissue. A later study of hydathode immunity likewise emphasizes that local multiplication is subject to host defense. The geometry of a passage may provide access, yet the tissue at the far end can still restrict establishment.

Wounds represent a third category because they create discontinuities in barriers rather than exploit a normal physiological opening. The depth and location of a wound determine which boundaries have been interrupted. A superficial defect may expose the epidermal wall without exposing the cytoplasm, whereas a deeper injury can damage plasma membranes. The fact that an agent enters through a wound does not specify its final compartment. Extracellular bacteria can remain between cells after gaining access, whereas virus particles or genomes may become available inside living cells adjacent to a site of injury.

Host cells severely damaged at a wound can die, which changes their usefulness to different pathogens. A virus requires a living cellular environment capable of supporting its gene expression and replication; delivery into an irreversibly destroyed cell does not necessarily establish infection. A pathogen dependent on resources supplied by a living plant cell also encounters a problem if the cell dies before the interaction is established. Other organisms can exploit dead or damaged material. Thus, injury can simultaneously increase physical access and reduce the suitability of some of the newly exposed cells.

A natural opening and a wound are often discussed together because both can bypass continuous surface barriers. Their origins and regulation differ, however. A stoma is a normal dynamic structure, a hydathode serves a water-release function and a wound is an acquired discontinuity. They may expose different tissues and generate different host responses. When evaluating an experiment that compares two routes, the investigator must avoid interpreting all resulting differences as differences in pathogen adhesion. The routes also differ in the compartment reached and the host's physiological state.

5. Root interfaces and tissue-specific opportunities

A root faces an environment very different from that of a mature aerial leaf. Soil particles, organic material, dissolved minerals and diverse microorganisms surround its growing regions. The root cannot perform its principal functions if it is completely isolated from this environment. It must take up water and inorganic ions, expand into new soil and allow controlled interactions with beneficial organisms. Consequently, the relevant barriers include the organization of tissues within the root as well as the properties of its outermost cells. The process by which a microorganism reaches the root surface should be distinguished from the process by which it gains access to the cortex or vascular tissues.

The rhizosphere is the region of soil influenced by a root. Compounds released from roots alter the chemical environment of nearby microorganisms. This can support microbial growth or influence the movement of motile organisms, but it does not identify those organisms as pathogens. Many root-associated microbes have neutral or beneficial relationships with the plant. A concentration gradient of a nutrient is a possible source of directional information for a microorganism with a suitable sensory system. Following that gradient may bring cells close to a root; the capacity to recognize and metabolize a compound therefore need not imply the capacity to infect a living plant.

A root's surface changes along its length. The region near a growing tip contains a root cap and young differentiating tissues. In the zone of maturation, epidermal cells may form root hairs that extend into the surrounding environment. A root hair is an extension of a living epidermal cell, not a pore connecting the outside directly with its cytoplasm. The increased area of contact with soil permits physiological exchange and creates opportunities for microbial interactions, yet its plasma membrane and wall continue to define a cellular boundary. A microorganism attached to a root hair is therefore outside the host cytoplasm unless additional evidence shows cellular penetration.

The internal organization of a typical young root includes an epidermis, cortex and endodermis surrounding the central vascular region. The Casparian strip interrupts unrestricted apoplastic flow through the endodermal cell walls. Water and many solutes moving toward the central cylinder must encounter selective membrane transport at some point rather than follow an entirely unrestricted extracellular path. The arrangement is especially relevant for pathogens that first occupy cortical spaces and later become associated with vascular tissues. Reaching the cortex does not establish that a pathogen has already reached xylem. The endodermis introduces a further anatomical barrier even after the exterior of the root has been crossed.

A lateral root develops from internal cells and emerges through overlying tissues. This normal developmental process requires local changes in walls and cell arrangements. Such sites may offer discontinuities or transiently altered barriers in some plant–microbe interactions. Their importance lies in the fact that a structure can be more accessible during one developmental process than another without representing a generalized weakness throughout the root. A microorganism observed near a lateral root base may be exploiting its anatomy, responding to locally available substances, or both. Location alone does not discriminate these possibilities.

Mature roots in many plants acquire additional protective modifications as development proceeds. Suberin deposited in appropriate tissues can restrict movement of water and solutes. Secondary growth can replace the original epidermal covering in woody tissues. These differences explain why a pathogen's association with one root region cannot be extrapolated to all organs. The plant's development changes the sequence of barriers encountered from the environment toward the vascular system. Moreover, damage produced by other organisms or environmental injury can alter those barriers without changing the genome of the plant.

Root-associated bacteria, fungi and oomycetes are not equivalent in their mode of access. A bacterium moving through an intercellular passage remains extracellular to living plant cells. A filamentous organism may grow along or between host cells and in some cases produce specialized structures that traverse walls. A vector-associated virus faces the additional requirement of intracellular delivery. The root provides a shared ecological location, but it does not provide a shared mechanism of infection. Each organism must be evaluated according to its size, architecture, capacity for growth and dependence on host cellular machinery.

A common examination problem concerns a plant whose surface-associated microbial abundance is unchanged but whose vascular colonization is reduced. This pattern could result from a defect in passage from the surface into the root, restriction within the cortex, or an inability to establish a population after reaching vascular tissue. It would not be sufficient to infer that attachment failed. A spatially resolved observation at intermediate locations would be needed to narrow the explanation. Conversely, a reduction in rhizosphere abundance alone does not establish that an internal barrier became stronger. The two observations concern different compartments and different stages in the causal sequence.

The physiological role of a root barrier can be remembered through its selective nature. A barrier designed to exclude all water and minerals would prevent the root from supporting the plant, just as a leaf unable to exchange gases would not perform ordinary photosynthesis. The organization of a healthy root permits beneficial exchange while limiting access to sensitive tissues. This general principle explains why host entry is frequently governed by anatomy and regulation together, not by a single impermeable outer layer.

6. Surface residence, adhesion and the transition to tissue entry

Before crossing an external barrier, a microbial cell or spore must encounter a suitable site. Passive transport through air, water or contact can bring organisms to a plant. Retention then depends on the physical properties of the organism and the surface. The collision of a bacterial cell with an epidermis is not yet stable attachment. The surface may be dry, a droplet may remove the organism or a competing microorganism may already occupy the relevant space. The terms arrival and attachment should therefore be kept separate, even when a simplified diagram uses a single arrow to represent both.

Molecular attraction at a surface can arise from several classes of interactions. Electrostatic forces depend on charges and the local ionic environment. Hydrophobic interactions influence the association of nonpolar regions with one another in an aqueous setting. Hydrogen bonds and specific interactions between proteins and carbohydrates may contribute to recognition and attachment. A biological surface typically presents a heterogeneous combination of such properties rather than one ideal ligand. A conclusion that attachment is receptor-mediated requires evidence beyond the simple observation that microbial cells remain associated with a leaf after washing.

Some bacteria express cell-surface structures that help them attach to host-associated surfaces. Extracellular polysaccharides can contribute to retention in a hydrated matrix. Other bacterial surface proteins can participate in interactions with local molecules. The effect of such a structure may not be restricted to attachment, because it can also influence aggregation, stress tolerance or recognition by the host. If a mutant has fewer bacteria remaining on a leaf, it is therefore necessary to consider whether the mutation altered adherence directly or affected survival during the observation period. A count of attached viable organisms is a joint measurement of multiple biological properties.

Plant surfaces frequently exhibit microscopic variation in height, composition and wetness. A region near an epidermal junction can present different local conditions from the exposed top of a cell. Bacteria within a cluster may experience different levels of desiccation from isolated cells. A spore attached to a surface may perceive its physical surroundings and respond by changing growth direction or differentiation. These examples illustrate why the mean properties of a leaf do not entirely determine the circumstances of every individual microorganism. An experiment averaged over a large area can obscure local events that contribute to successful attachment and access.

Flagella permit motility in many bacteria, whereas pili and other surface structures can participate in adhesion or different modes of movement. Flagellar motility requires metabolic energy and an appropriate physical environment. In the absence of sufficient liquid connectivity, a bacterium cannot necessarily translate its swimming capacity into movement across a leaf. Motility is therefore a property of the bacterium, while the ability to move in a specific location emerges from interaction with the environment. The ability to move toward a favorable site is distinct from the ability to grow after reaching that site.

For filamentous microorganisms, attachment of a propagule can be followed by polarized growth. A fungal germ tube extends by adding material preferentially at its tip. Its position relative to the host surface changes as growth continues, even if the original spore remains attached. The resulting route is not equivalent to the movement of an entire bacterial cell. The mechanical and chemical properties of the developing fungal structure can determine whether it remains outside, follows a surface contour or crosses a barrier. The same plant anatomy can consequently support quite different entry strategies.

A useful distinction is between an entry site and an attachment site. A microorganism can first attach at a location from which it cannot enter, then reach a nearby opening. Another can attach and develop its penetration structure at the same location. An externally applied tracer may also concentrate at a pore without crossing it. Thus, spatial association with an opening increases the plausibility of a route but is not definitive evidence of successful crossing. Microscopy at the far side of the barrier provides information that a surface image cannot provide.

The external habitat may support an established epiphytic population without internal infection. This is not a paradox: the nutritional requirements of an extracellular bacterium are not identical to the requirements of a virus, and some microbes can remain viable using resources outside the living plant cell. Other organisms survive on the surface in dormant or nonmultiplying forms. An organism's capacity to persist externally affects the probability of eventual contact with a suitable entry site. Nevertheless, surface fitness and internal fitness can be genetically and physiologically separable.

In a comparison of two bacterial populations, a difference in total cells associated with a plant may arise from differential arrival, retention, survival or growth. If the populations are indistinguishable externally but differ in the internal compartment immediately after exposure, the evidence favors a difference in access or in very early survival. If they enter at comparable levels and subsequently diverge, the explanation must consider internal establishment. These distinctions motivate the quantitative analysis of separate stages rather than a single measure of abundance at the end of an interaction.

7. Bacterial colonization of the apoplast

For many foliar bacterial pathogens, the host cytoplasm is not the principal habitat of the intact bacterial cell. The bacterium occupies spaces outside the plasma membranes of living cells after it gains access to internal tissue. This extracellular position still permits intimate interactions with the host because small molecules, nutrients and signals can move between the bacterial environment and host membrane surfaces. The bacterium can affect host physiology without becoming an intracellular organism. The topology is therefore similar in one respect to extracellular bacterial relationships in animals, but the plant wall and internal air-space system introduce additional spatial constraints.

The distinction between extracellular and intracellular location is not a distinction between biologically active and biologically inactive pathogens. An extracellular bacterium may possess its own metabolic machinery and ribosomes, allowing it to synthesize proteins and replicate independently of the host's ribosomes when suitable conditions are available. A virus lacks the independent machinery needed for translation. Consequently, an apoplastic bacterium may increase its population without entering a living host cell, whereas a virus in the same extracellular region cannot complete its multiplication merely by accessing soluble nutrients. The dependence of each pathogen on host cellular resources helps explain why their routes of establishment differ.

The apoplast is neither equivalent to a rich laboratory growth medium nor a completely nutrient-free environment. Cells transport substances across their plasma membranes for normal physiological reasons. Soluble carbohydrates, inorganic ions and other metabolites may be present in extracellular regions at concentrations different from those inside living cells. A bacterium must obtain necessary materials and maintain its own internal chemical conditions in this environment. Its membrane transport systems are relevant to that process, but the overall supply of nutrients also depends on host metabolism and the accessibility of local resources.

Water availability has special importance because cellular metabolic reactions require an aqueous environment. The presence of an air-filled space next to a host cell does not mean a bacterium has abundant liquid water in which to grow. The hydrated wall matrix, small liquid films and locally altered water distribution can provide microenvironments with different properties. Plant physiological changes may influence these conditions. Consequently, a plant with well-developed intercellular spaces can still restrict bacterial multiplication if the chemical or physical quality of those spaces is unfavorable.

The term water potential describes the tendency of water to move under specified conditions relative to a reference. Solute concentration, pressure and interactions with surfaces can all contribute to the potential in a biological system. Water generally moves from a region of higher water potential toward one of lower water potential when an appropriate pathway is present. A bacterium in an environment with low water availability must maintain cellular hydration and membrane function despite that constraint. A hydrated environment is not automatically favorable if the osmotic or ionic conditions exceed the organism's tolerance.

Plant cells can modify extracellular conditions during defense. Restriction of nutrient availability or the appearance of unfavorable chemical conditions may limit microbial population expansion after entry. The distinction between preinvasive defense and postinvasive restriction is particularly useful here. A bacterium unable to pass an epidermal opening fails before reaching the apoplast. A bacterium that reaches an internal space but fails to multiply has overcome the earlier physical barrier without overcoming the later biological restriction. These stages can be affected independently by different host traits.

A bacterial cell in the apoplast is separated from host cytoplasm by the host wall and plasma membrane. Some bacterial systems allow particular microbial proteins to be delivered across host boundaries, but delivery of a protein should not be confused with delivery of the entire bacterium. A soluble molecule released into the apoplast may interact with the exterior of a host receptor without ever entering the cytoplasm. The outcomes of these different interactions belong to later discussion of host-cell manipulation. Here the essential spatial conclusion is that microbial occupancy and molecular access have different meanings.

Pseudomonas syringae provides an instructive model for the transition from leaf surface residence to internal extracellular colonization. Its study has established that the plant surface is not simply a passive covering. The ability of a particular population to reach internal leaf spaces and establish itself there reflects interactions between microbial properties, the plant and its physiological state. A result obtained with one host genotype or bacterial strain should not be generalized to every member of the bacterial species complex. The representative system is valuable precisely because it allows particular stages to be distinguished.

Some bacterial pathogens instead occupy specialized vascular environments. Mature xylem vessel elements lack living cytoplasm, and their conducting lumina are extracellular in the physiological sense. A bacterium in such a lumen is inside an organ but not inside the cytoplasm of a living xylem vessel element. The neighboring living vascular cells can nevertheless influence the environment and respond to microbial signals. Phloem-associated microorganisms encounter a distinct cellular organization: mature sieve elements remain living although highly specialized. The example prevents the overgeneralization that every internal plant compartment is equivalent to the mesophyll apoplast.

The initial internal population is often much smaller than the total population associated with the plant surface. Its subsequent behavior depends on the balance of cellular division, death and movement. An increasing internal viable count is compatible with net growth, but the count can also increase through continued entry from an external reservoir. In the absence of a measurement separating those processes, the slope of the population curve is not a direct measurement of cell division. The meaning of a bacterial count is therefore inseparable from its spatial and temporal definition.

8. Plant recognition of microbial patterns before and after entry

Plant cells need to respond to microorganisms even when the intact microbial cell remains outside the host membrane. A molecular recognition system permits that response because the receptor does not need to contact the entire organism. A microbial component released into the surrounding environment or exposed at an accessible surface can convey information about a potential threat. Such components can include fragments of structures conserved across classes of microorganisms. The biological significance of perception is not that the plant identifies a particular microbial species with certainty, but that it recognizes a molecular feature associated with a relevant biological encounter.

A pathogen-associated molecular pattern, or PAMP, is a molecular feature associated with a class of pathogens that can be detected by a host recognition system. The broader term microbe-associated molecular pattern, or MAMP, is often appropriate because related features are also present in microbes that do not cause disease. Bacterial flagellin is a well-known example. Its principal role in the bacterium is structural participation in the flagellum, not communication with the plant. A conserved portion of that protein can nevertheless function as a signal to a host receptor. A microbial structure can therefore simultaneously serve a function for the microbe and provide information to its host.

Pattern recognition receptors are host proteins that detect such features and initiate responses. Many plant cell-surface pattern receptors have an extracellular recognition region, a membrane-spanning segment and a mechanism for transmitting information into the cell. Some contain an intracellular kinase domain, while others rely on associated signaling components. The extracellular location of recognition is particularly appropriate for the plant body because molecular signals can reach a membrane through extracellular wall spaces even when an entire bacterium cannot. The receptor's structure links detection on one side of the plasma membrane to a response on the other.

FLS2 is an Arabidopsis receptor-like kinase involved in flagellin perception. A conserved peptide region of bacterial flagellin, commonly represented experimentally by flg22, can elicit responses in plants with the appropriate recognition machinery. The receptor participates in a signaling complex rather than acting as a channel through which flagella or bacteria enter the cytoplasm. A plant defective in FLS2 can lose or reduce particular flagellin-triggered responses while retaining other barriers and recognition systems. Therefore, a defect in this receptor is not equivalent to complete absence of plant immunity.

Another example is recognition of chitin-derived material. Chitin is an important structural polysaccharide in many true fungal walls. Fragments generated or exposed during host–fungus interactions can be perceived by appropriate plant receptor systems, including LysM-domain receptors. Chitin perception illustrates recognition of a molecular pattern different from a protein-derived flagellin peptide. It is not a universal detector of every filamentous plant pathogen, because wall composition differs among biological groups. Oomycetes, for example, are not true fungi and commonly have walls rich in glucans and cellulose rather than the typical chitin-rich fungal wall organization.

Recognition and adhesion are sometimes confused because both can involve binding at a cell surface. Adhesion describes the retention of an object at a surface, whereas perception describes the generation of a host response following detection. A receptor that binds a microbial ligand may promote defense instead of pathogen uptake. An adhesin may attach an organism to extracellular material without activating the particular defense pathway being measured. A single interaction can sometimes affect both phenomena, but the terms should not be used as synonyms. Their difference is defined by biological outcome rather than by whether a binding event occurs.

Initial recognition can induce rapid physiological changes, including altered ion fluxes and production of signaling molecules. At the level necessary to understand entry, these responses can restrict access to a tissue opening or change the suitability of the extracellular space. Detailed downstream pathways are not required to establish the causal principle. If perception is defective, the plant may not implement a normally induced barrier even though the underlying anatomical structures remain intact. Conversely, a plant whose physical barrier is damaged may still recognize microbial signals and restrict the subsequent internal population.

A damage-associated molecular pattern originates from the host rather than directly from a microorganism. Fragments released from host wall polymers are one possible source of information about tissue disturbance. Such information can be generated even when the initiating injury is not caused by a pathogen. The distinction is useful because the plant may respond to microbial molecules and to changes in the integrity of its own tissue. Recognition of damage does not establish the taxonomic identity of an infectious agent. An experiment that induces a response through mechanical damage alone therefore demonstrates neither microbial adhesion nor entry.

Plant recognition should also be distinguished from the adaptive immune mechanisms of vertebrates. Plants do not use circulating B lymphocytes or rearranged immunoglobulin receptors as their standard mode of pathogen recognition. Their cells use inherited recognition machinery, and responses can influence neighboring cells and more distant tissues. This does not make plant defenses merely structural or nonspecific. They include molecular discrimination, regulated responses and interactions with particular pathogen features. The comparison is helpful because it separates a general biological principle, recognition followed by response, from the particular molecular machinery used in different organisms.

One may observe a host recognition response without observing successful infection. A purified microbial component can activate an appropriate receptor even in the absence of a living microbe. Likewise, a bacterium that never reaches a favorable internal habitat may expose recognizable molecules on the plant surface. A signal is therefore evidence of perception, not proof of successful microbial growth. In the opposite direction, failure to detect a particular response does not establish that no recognition occurred: the selected assay may measure only one output among several or may be conducted at a time when the response has changed.

9. Guard-cell physiology and the logic of an entry checkpoint

Guard cells are living cells whose changes in shape control the effective area of a stomatal pore. Their operation depends on interactions between membrane transport, osmotic properties, wall mechanics and pressure. Solute accumulation in guard cells can lower their water potential, favor water uptake and increase turgor under appropriate conditions. Because guard-cell walls and their geometry constrain expansion, changes in turgor translate into a change in pore aperture rather than simple uniform enlargement. During closure, changes in solute and water relations reduce the force supporting an open pore. The essential physical principle is the conversion of cellular physiology into tissue-scale geometry.

A microbial signal perceived by a guard cell or associated signaling system can alter the normal regulation of aperture. This is a particularly clear example of recognition preceding access to an internal habitat. The plant does not have to engulf the bacterium before responding to its presence. If closure occurs while the bacterium is still outside, the response can reduce the probability of movement through the pore. Its effectiveness depends on the timing and magnitude of the response relative to the movement of the microorganism and the availability of alternative routes.

Stomatal closure is not a free protective measure. A pore that is closed for longer periods reduces the conductance available for gas exchange under otherwise comparable conditions. The host must therefore regulate this response in the context of other signals, including water status and light-dependent physiological requirements. Infection-related behavior cannot be interpreted as though stomata were structures whose sole function was to block pathogens. A constitutively closed pore might alter plant growth and leaf physiology even if it reduces a particular entry route. Changes in disease outcomes could consequently reflect multiple effects.

The landmark stomatal study by Melotto and colleagues showed that the interaction is dynamic. Microbial signals can induce closure, and in the particular bacterial system studied a bacterial factor can counteract closure. The presence of a defense response at one time and an open pore at a later time is not necessarily contradictory. The two observations can represent different phases of an interaction. A single endpoint image of an open stoma cannot distinguish a pore that never closed from a pore that closed and then reopened.

The appropriate causal prediction depends on the route being considered. A host change that reduces pore aperture should most directly influence organisms whose access depends on that opening. The same change need not prevent infection at a wound that bypasses the pore. Similarly, an organism may retain the capacity for subsequent multiplication even when its access through an intact leaf is limited. Separating the effect of a barrier on entry from the effect of internal conditions on multiplication is central to the interpretation of plant infection experiments.

Aperture width is not identical to the total amount of tissue colonization. Pore number, local distribution of microorganisms, the direction and continuity of liquid movement, microbial attachment and postentry survival all contribute to the outcome. Even under an idealized geometric model, doubling the width of a pore would not necessarily double the number of organisms that reach the mesophyll. If a simplified problem assumes that flux is proportional to accessible area, that assumption must be stated. The biological conclusion is conditional on the model rather than a general law relating pore width to disease.

A pore can also remain open enough for gas exchange while still being restrictive to particular microbial structures. Molecular gases and bacterial cells differ enormously in size and modes of transport. The geometry relevant to diffusion of a small molecule is not identical to the geometry relevant to passage of an elongated cell or a growing hypha. Therefore, a measure of stomatal conductance provides valuable physiological information but is not a direct count of bacterial entry events. Experimental interpretation improves when these quantities are measured and discussed separately.

10. Fungal penetration: growth, differentiation and force

A fungal spore differs fundamentally from a bacterium moving through an existing opening. It is a propagule capable, under suitable conditions, of developing into a growing filament. The filament is called a hypha, and a network of hyphae is a mycelium. A germ tube is an early outgrowth from the spore. Because the hypha grows in a polarized manner, its tip can reach a location different from the initial site of spore attachment. The organism is thus able to change its physical form during the encounter with the host. This property explains why fungal entry can involve the construction of an infection structure rather than movement of an already formed cellular body through a pore.

Germination alone is not proof of successful penetration. A spore may generate a germ tube on a surface that is unsuitable for subsequent infection. A germ tube may grow across the epidermis without encountering a favorable anatomical site, or the plant may restrict the formation of a successful infection structure. The stages of spore survival, germination, polarized growth, differentiation and wall crossing can therefore be distinguished experimentally. When a fungal mutant forms germ tubes normally but does not penetrate the host, its defect occurs after germination, although the exact molecular cause remains to be established.

Many fungal plant pathogens form an appressorium, a specialized structure associated with adhesion and penetration. It develops from a germ tube or related fungal structure and can establish a localized interaction with the host surface. Its architecture varies across fungal groups. In some systems it supports direct mechanical penetration, in others it helps position further growth at a natural opening, and not all successful fungal pathogens form the same type of appressorium. The term denotes a differentiated infection-associated structure rather than a single molecular mechanism shared by every fungus.

Consider a fungus that develops an appressorium on an intact epidermis. The broad structure can remain attached while a much narrower penetration peg extends from a restricted region. By localizing growth at this region, the organism concentrates interactions with the host barrier. The position and shape of the penetration peg matter because the fungus must create a passage through a surface with finite strength. A broad germ tube pressing against a resistant surface is mechanically different from a narrow specialized extension growing at a precisely defined contact. The differentiation of the appressorium and penetration peg coordinates adhesion, geometry and localized growth.

In the rice blast model, high turgor pressure in the appressorium contributes to mechanical penetration. Turgor arises when water inside a bounded cell exerts pressure on its enclosing structures. Solute accumulation can favor water uptake when the surrounding conditions allow it, while the wall resists unlimited expansion. The mechanical consequence depends on maintaining a difference between internal and external conditions. In a structure unable to retain the solutes that contribute to the pressure difference, the relationship between osmotic accumulation and mechanical force can be altered. A visible appressorium is therefore not necessarily a mechanically functional one.

The melanized wall of the appressorium in the rice blast model has an important relationship with the maintenance of high internal pressure. It helps provide a barrier to loss of relevant solutes, while the wall architecture and specialized site of peg emergence allow penetration to occur in a localized manner. The presence of melanin is not a universal requirement for all fungal infection structures. Some fungi penetrate by different combinations of growth force and enzymatic activities, and some use natural openings instead. A student should connect the importance of melanization to the particular mechanistic system in which it has been demonstrated.

Pressure and force describe different physical quantities. Pressure is force per unit area and has units of pascals. Force has units of newtons. An idealized pressure acting on a specified area can be related to force through multiplication by that area. This simple relationship is useful when interpreting the physical scale of a problem, but an actual fungal penetration peg is not an ideal piston. Its wall, geometry, material properties and growing tip contribute to the local mechanical interactions. Penetration depends on whether stress at the contact can overcome the host material's resistance, rather than on the internal pressure value alone.

Chemical activity can contribute to the same biological event. A fungal organism may secrete enzymes capable of modifying cuticular or wall-associated material. Hydrolysis of polymers can reduce local resistance or alter the cohesion of the host wall. The effectiveness of an enzyme depends on substrate accessibility, chemical conditions and the particular polymer targeted. An enzyme capable of hydrolyzing a wall component does not automatically dissolve the entire wall, which is a composite structure. Local chemical modification and mechanical growth can cooperate without either being the exclusive cause of penetration.

These processes can be distinguished conceptually by asking which property an experimental perturbation changes. A defect that prevents development of an appressorium alters the architecture of the infection structure. A defect that preserves its shape but reduces pressure may primarily affect its mechanical function. A change affecting the modification of the host surface may instead alter the resistance encountered by the growing tip. The expected phenotype depends on which stage is rate-limiting in the particular fungus–host system. Similar reductions in disease can therefore arise through distinct mechanisms.

The study by Howard and colleagues is valuable because it separated part of the mechanical problem from the complexities of living host defenses. Penetration of an artificial material provided evidence that the fungal infection structure itself could exert substantial mechanical effects. The reported pressure estimate was inferred from osmotic behavior rather than obtained by directly reading a pressure gauge inside each appressorium. That distinction illustrates how a physical property may be estimated indirectly from an experimentally accessible consequence. It also cautions against transferring a measured pressure from one experimental system to all plant pathogens.

Fungal access does not always require direct penetration through an unbroken epidermis. Some rust fungi recognize features of the leaf surface associated with stomata and establish infection structures near those openings. Their growing structures can then reach internal spaces through an anatomical passage. A fungus using this route may still need to cross walls later to establish a specialized relationship with individual host cells. The early passage into tissue and the later development of a cell-associated structure are distinct events. The presence of an appressorium near a stoma does not prove that the fungus drilled directly through the cuticle at that point.

Other fungi can enter damaged tissues, where the original external barrier has already been disrupted. In that case, the contribution of an infection structure can differ from its contribution during direct penetration of an intact surface. The important comparison is not that one pathogen is universally stronger than another, but that different strategies address different physical and biological obstacles. A pathogen restricted by one surface property may be unaffected by a different property if it does not encounter that particular barrier along its natural route.

After penetration, the fungus must establish its initial relationship with host tissue. This may involve intercellular growth, growth within damaged cellular regions or formation of specialized interfaces associated with living host cells. The same word penetration is sometimes applied to all of these processes, but the barriers crossed are different. To determine the mechanism, one must identify the position of the fungal wall and membrane relative to the host wall and membrane. This spatial reasoning becomes particularly important in the study of haustoria.

11. Oomycetes and comparative filamentous entry

Oomycetes were historically grouped with fungi because many form filamentous structures and have similar ecological roles. Modern classification places them in a distinct evolutionary lineage within the stramenopiles. Their resemblance to fungi reflects, in part, the biological usefulness of growing filaments in absorbing nutrients and exploring tissues. A hypha-like shape is not by itself sufficient evidence of close evolutionary relationship. This example is useful in examinations because it requires distinguishing a shared functional solution from a shared taxonomic identity.

The cell walls of many oomycetes contain cellulose and glucans, whereas true fungal walls commonly contain chitin and glucans. These are broad comparative patterns rather than absolute descriptions of every stage of every species. The compositions influence the molecular structures available for host perception and the material properties of the pathogen. A host receptor system recognizing a typical fungal chitin-derived signal is therefore not a complete explanation of recognition of all filamentous pathogens. The plant must encounter a molecular feature that is actually accessible in the relevant biological interaction.

Some oomycetes produce motile zoospores with flagella during their life cycle. A zoospore is a dispersal form, and in an appropriate aqueous environment its movement can contribute to reaching a host surface. It may subsequently encyst and produce a germ tube. The motile stage and the growing stage solve different problems: the first can change the position of the organism in a suitable liquid environment, while the second can establish a localized physical interaction with tissue. Not every oomycete infection follows an identical sequence, and the presence of a motile stage should not be inferred solely from the observation of filamentous growth.

In plant-associated oomycetes, entry may involve natural openings, wounds or direct interactions with epidermal tissues, depending on the pathogen and organ. A growing structure may advance between cells and subsequently form specialized interfaces. Because these organisms and fungi both include filamentous pathogens, they can encounter analogous mechanical problems even though their cellular structures and evolutionary histories differ. The physical barrier is defined by the host, whereas the means of overcoming it is constrained by the pathogen's own developmental and biochemical properties.

Phytophthora species provide familiar examples of plant-pathogenic oomycetes. The purpose of introducing them here is to distinguish the initial interaction with the plant from the later consequences of infection. Their capacity to produce filamentous growth or specialized host-associated structures does not mean they are true fungi, and the initial entry process does not require a disease symptom to have already developed. A microscopic observation of a propagule or germ tube establishes an early stage; a later lesion reflects a different level of biological organization.

Comparative entry also reveals that there is no universal definition of successful penetration based purely on the appearance of a host surface. An organism may be able to advance between adjacent cells without breaching their membranes. Another may cross a wall and establish a membrane-bound interface within the outline of a host cell. A third may exploit tissue already damaged by an earlier event. These outcomes are determined by the combination of the entry route and the subsequent requirements of the pathogen. Describing each compartment is more informative than assigning all filamentous organisms to a single invasive category.

A useful way of interpreting an image is to identify structures that belong to the organism and structures that belong to the host. A thick line outside a pathogen's membrane may represent its own wall rather than the host wall. A membrane surrounding a pathogen-derived structure may belong to the host, the pathogen or another compartment, depending on the preparation. Without identifying these boundaries, a two-dimensional image cannot establish whether a filament lies in intercellular space or has become associated with a living host cell in a specialized way.

12. Haustoria and the meaning of intracellular position

A biotroph obtains resources from living host tissues and depends on a sustained relationship with viable host cells. A necrotroph derives resources from killed host tissue, often after contributing to tissue injury. A hemibiotroph has a phase associated with living host tissue and a subsequent phase associated with tissue death. These categories describe nutritional relationships and infection strategies, not fixed entry routes. A biotrophic fungus can cross a physical wall without immediately destroying the host cell, while other organisms may be able to establish themselves in damaged tissue.

Some biotrophic fungi and oomycetes produce haustoria. A haustorium is a specialized pathogen-derived structure associated with a host cell and adapted for exchange and communication. It may arise after a filament has already gained access to the tissue. Formation of the haustorium is thus not necessarily the first entry event into the plant. Its relevance to this chapter is that it demonstrates how an organism can cross the host wall yet remain separated from the living host cytoplasm by a membrane. This apparently unusual spatial relationship is central to understanding the interface.

A typical diagram shows a pathogen-derived protrusion extending through a region of the host wall. Around that protrusion is a host-derived membrane called the extrahaustorial membrane. Between the pathogen surface and the extrahaustorial membrane lies an interfacial region known as the extrahaustorial matrix. The pathogen's own plasma membrane and wall remain distinct from these host-associated structures. Starting from the host cytoplasm and proceeding toward the pathogen cytoplasm, one would encounter the extrahaustorial membrane, the intervening matrix, the pathogen wall and the pathogen plasma membrane, with variations according to the particular system.

This arrangement illustrates the difference between geometrical location and membrane topology. A structure may extend within the outline defined by a plant cell wall and appear intracellular in a low-resolution image, yet it may occupy a compartment topologically outside the host cytoplasm. The host-derived membrane continues to separate it from the host cytosol. Calling the structure intracellular without qualification can therefore be misleading. The correct interpretation is that a pathogen-derived structure has invaded the wall-bounded region of a host cell while retaining a specialized membrane interface between the two cytoplasms.

The interface is not necessarily identical in composition to an ordinary region of the host plasma membrane. Host membrane components and trafficking processes can contribute to formation of a specialized compartment. The resulting boundary has structural and physiological significance because substances passing between the host and pathogen encounter selective interfaces. It is not simply an open hole through which the cytoplasm of one organism mixes with the cytoplasm of the other. The spatial arrangement permits exchange while preserving the individuality of the two living systems.

The extrahaustorial matrix likewise should not be treated as ordinary host cytoplasm. It occupies the region between distinct biological surfaces and may have a composition influenced by both organisms. In some systems the interface is relatively isolated from the surrounding apoplast by specialized structures near the narrow region of the haustorium. Such organization can affect how substances move into or out of the compartment. However, the exact architecture differs among fungi and oomycetes, and the presence of a particular sealing structure should not be asserted for every haustorium.

A biotrophic relationship places constraints on the pathogen. If a living host cell is required for the continued supply of useful resources, immediate unrestricted destruction of its membrane and metabolism could be disadvantageous. Establishment of an organized interface provides a way to interact with the cell while preserving its viability for a period of time. The host can also respond to this interaction. The observed structure is therefore the outcome of a dynamic relationship between the growing pathogen and the host cell, not a static object inserted into an inert container.

It is important to distinguish a haustorium from an appressorium. The appressorium is commonly associated with localized attachment and penetration of a surface. The haustorium is associated with exchange across a specialized interface with living host tissue. Some organisms produce both structures at different stages, but one is not simply another name for the other. An appressorium located on the outer epidermal surface may help an organism reach internal tissue, while a haustorium subsequently formed in association with an individual host cell supports a different phase of the relationship.

The distinction also prevents an error when comparing haustoria with bacterial colonization. An apoplastic bacterium can interact with a host cell across extracellular material without developing a haustorium. A haustorial pathogen, in contrast, develops a specialized filament-derived structure intimately associated with a host membrane. Both may exchange substances with the host, but their physical arrangements and the mechanisms supporting them are not equivalent. The examination-relevant common principle is the possibility of extensive host interaction without free mixing of host and pathogen cytoplasm.

Microscopic evidence for a haustorial interface must resolve more than the general outline of a host cell. An image showing a fungal structure inside the outline of the host wall provides evidence of wall crossing. Demonstrating an intervening host-derived membrane requires additional structural or molecular evidence identifying that membrane. A marker associated with the host plasma membrane may not be retained uniformly at a specialized interface, so absence of one marker alone does not necessarily prove absence of the membrane. Structural evidence and appropriate localization markers are complementary rather than interchangeable.

The concept of a membrane-bounded interface provides a bridge to later discussions of nutrient exchange and pathogen-driven changes in the host cell. For the present purpose, the central conclusion is topological. An organism can enter a tissue, penetrate a host wall and form a structure deep within the wall-bounded space while still being excluded from direct access to the host cytoplasm. A virus that must express its genome inside a plant cell faces a different problem, because it cannot establish productive replication merely by occupying an extracellular interface.

13. The special problem of plant virus delivery

A virus particle contains a genome, either RNA or DNA, associated with viral proteins and, in some virus groups, additional components such as a lipid-containing envelope. The protein coat, or capsid, protects the genome and can participate in interactions necessary for transmission. A viral particle outside a host cell is not an independently growing microbial cell. It lacks the complete machinery necessary to manufacture its proteins and reproduce its genome autonomously. The distinction explains why an extracellular space that supports bacterial multiplication cannot by itself support productive plant virus infection.

Animal viruses often encounter a plasma membrane exposed at the surface of a cell or accessible through tissue fluids. They may use attachment molecules, receptors and membrane-associated entry mechanisms. The intact wall surrounding most plant cells is a major obstacle to the same general strategy. Reaching the plant apoplast does not by itself bring an intact virion into productive contact with cytoplasmic translation machinery. The cell wall, plasma membrane and living state of the recipient cell must be considered separately. The existence of membrane receptors in plants is not evidence that ordinary animal-virus entry routes operate in plant cells.

Many plant viruses gain initial access through injury to surface barriers or through the action of an organism that carries virus material between hosts. A wound may permit viral material to reach the contents of cells in the vicinity of the disruption. The recipient cell must remain sufficiently functional to support subsequent viral activity. A virus particle trapped in a dead cell does not become productive simply because the wall and membrane no longer form an effective barrier. The crucial biological outcome is the availability of a functional viral genome within a living compatible cell.

Tobacco mosaic virus illustrates a plant virus for which mechanical transmission is an important classical concept. Its rod-shaped particles contain an RNA genome enclosed by repeated protein subunits. The structure protects the RNA during an extracellular phase, but the genome must become accessible to host machinery after delivery. Infectivity and particle integrity are therefore related but not identical properties: a genome packaged in an intact particle may survive outside the cell, yet that genome cannot direct synthesis of proteins while remaining inaccessible to the relevant intracellular machinery. Entry and uncoating solve different problems.

Uncoating refers to removal or rearrangement of protective viral protein components in a manner that makes the genome available for its next intracellular function. It need not occur in one instantaneous event. Different viruses have different genome organizations and dependencies, so the subsequent events cannot be generalized from a single RNA virus. A positive-sense RNA genome can serve as a template for translation in appropriate circumstances, whereas a DNA genome has different requirements for generating usable RNA. The specific transcription, translation and replication pathways belong to earlier and later curriculum topics. Here they establish why simply detecting an external particle is insufficient evidence of productive infection.

A vector is an organism that mediates transmission of a pathogen from one host to another. In plant virology, many vectors are arthropods, including aphids and other groups with feeding behaviors that bring them into close association with internal plant tissues. Other biological vectors exist, and not all plant viruses require a vector. The importance of a vector is not simply movement over distance: its interactions with plants can provide physical access to tissues or cells otherwise separated from the environment by wall and membrane barriers.

Vector transmission has at least three distinguishable stages: acquisition from an infected source, association or retention in the vector, and delivery to a recipient host. A vector may be competent for one virus but not another because viral and vector components differ in their compatibility. The capacity of the recipient plant to support the virus is a separate matter. A virus can therefore fail to infect a recipient even if the vector acquired it successfully. Conversely, a plant cell may be permissive after intracellular delivery even when a particular vector cannot transmit that virus to the plant.

The terms nonpersistent, semipersistent and persistent transmission describe broad differences in how long a virus remains transmissible in a vector and in the biological relationship involved. In noncirculative transmission, viral material can associate with regions of the feeding apparatus or alimentary tract without circulating through the vector body. Circulative transmission involves passage through internal vector barriers and delivery via relevant tissues. Some circulative viruses replicate in their vectors, whereas others do not. Persistent transmission does not by itself prove replication within the vector, and the three retention categories should not be treated as a universal substitute for mechanistic classification.

A vector may be an animal host for some plant viruses, yet the virus's interaction with animal cells in that vector and its initial access to the plant cell are distinct biological events. A virus that has an envelope may use mechanisms appropriate to infection of vector cells in some systems. That does not imply that an intact plant epidermis is crossed by the same membrane fusion event. The plant cell wall remains a physical obstacle. The proper question is which barrier is being crossed and in which organism the relevant molecular event takes place.

A recipient plant's susceptibility is conditional on delivery to the appropriate cellular context. The virus must encounter necessary host factors, avoid or withstand restrictions and establish initial expression or replication. The first successfully infected cell is not automatically evidence of systemic infection. Additional cell-to-cell and long-distance movement requirements must be satisfied before a distant tissue becomes infected. Plasmodesmata are channels connecting the cytoplasm of neighboring plant cells; they are important for later virus movement but are not simply open pores connecting the external environment to the cytoplasm of an intact leaf.

Some plant viruses can also be transmitted through reproductive material or vegetative propagation. In these circumstances, infectious material may already be present in tissues that contribute to the next plant. Such transmission differs from the initial penetration of an intact external leaf by an incoming particle. It prevents the overgeneralization that all plant virus infections must begin with insect feeding or visible external wounds. The shared requirement is a viral genome reaching a living cellular environment in which the necessary biological functions can occur.

When evaluating a proposed route of plant virus entry, distinguish the presence of viral material, its access to cells and the production of additional viral material. The first may be demonstrated by a detection assay; the second requires information about cellular location; the third requires evidence consistent with new biological synthesis rather than persistence of the original material. Each is a different claim. A diagnostic signal measured shortly after an encounter can reflect input particles, while a later increase could reflect new synthesis or redistribution unless the observation is interpreted in the context of the experimental design.

14. Host range and the sequence of compatibility filters

The same species of plant may vary in susceptibility among organs, developmental stages or genotypes. This variation can arise before a microorganism enters tissue or after it reaches a cellular environment. A plant with a particular surface architecture may be exposed to fewer successful entry events. A different plant may permit physical access but restrict growth through recognition or unfavorable internal conditions. A virus may reach a living cell and still fail to express or replicate its genome because essential biological interactions are absent. The host range observed in nature is therefore the combined result of multiple filters.

A compatibility filter is a useful conceptual term for a requirement that must be satisfied before a later stage becomes possible. It need not correspond to a single gene or molecule. Physical access through a tissue opening, availability of water to an extracellular microbial cell, formation of a suitable fungal interface and productive intracellular viral expression are examples of different requirements. The organism's phenotype influences whether a requirement is met, and host architecture and physiology influence the circumstances in which the phenotype is expressed.

The biological meaning of a host restriction depends on which filter has failed. If an organism cannot attach to the surface, it may never reach a tissue opening. If it attaches but does not cross the surface barrier, an internal assay may remain negative despite abundant surface cells. If it enters but is unable to sustain itself, the initial internal abundance may be detectable and then decline. These distinct trajectories can lead to the same final observation of little or no disease. A conclusion based on disease outcome alone therefore cannot assign the mechanism of resistance.

Some restrictions are shared widely among plants because important structural and physiological features are conserved. Others depend on a particular interaction between a microbial component and a host recognition system. A plant lacking recognition of one microbial molecule can still possess other recognition systems and physical barriers. Likewise, a pathogen capable of establishing a specialized interface in one plant may fail in another because the relevant biological requirements differ. The term host specificity should not be reduced either to absolute physical exclusion or to a universal receptor compatibility rule.

Environmental variation interacts with the compatibility filters. A plant may retain the same genotype but display different physiological states at different times. The state of its stomata, hydration of external and internal spaces and development of its tissues can all change. A microorganism may retain the same genetic capacity for motility or polarized growth while its ability to express that capacity in the environment differs. Host range defined from one condition is therefore an observation under those conditions, not an unconditional list of every possible interaction.

The disease triangle is a classical conceptual model in plant pathology that relates a susceptible host, an agent capable of causing disease and an environment favorable to their interaction. It is not a mathematical law predicting lesion size. Rather, it summarizes the conditional character of disease. For the early infection stage, the model can be expanded by identifying which barrier or resource is affected by each component. The environment might change the physical accessibility of a surface, the host might change recognition or tissue physiology, and the pathogen might differ in the biological requirements for establishment.

Comparative biology helps distinguish shared and divergent solutions. Animal and plant cells both have plasma membranes, rely on selective transport and perceive external molecular information. Plant cells additionally have a wall and are arranged in tissues with characteristic extracellular pathways. A bacterium can establish extracellular populations in both kinds of host, but the relevant anatomy and environmental constraints differ. Animal viruses often exploit specific interactions at accessible cell membranes; many plant viruses rely on delivery across disrupted or bypassed barriers. A filamentous plant pathogen can use growing structures to address obstacles that a virus cannot independently overcome.

These comparisons also help explain why the study of entry should not be confused with the study of later pathogenic activity. An agent may have an effective route into a tissue but little capacity to modify the host or spread further. Another may cause substantial cellular disturbance once delivered into a suitable compartment but rarely achieve that delivery in a particular ecological context. The frequency of entry and the consequences of each successful entry are different variables. The next chapter examines the latter processes after the initial site has been established.

15. Experimental reasoning: separating access from establishment

A strong mechanistic interpretation begins by identifying the variable an observation actually measures. A microscope image of a leaf surface can reveal attachment or the formation of a fungal structure, but it cannot alone establish whether an organism has reached internal tissue. A tissue-level signal may reveal the presence of a microbial molecule without resolving whether it is located outside or inside living host cells. An assay for viable microbial cells addresses another question because biological viability is not the same as the presence of a molecular marker. The conclusions must therefore be tied to the type of evidence obtained.

For an extracellular bacterial population, an internal viable count at an early time can be compared with a later internal count. If the external reservoir and continued influx are negligible, the change in count reflects the net balance of bacterial multiplication and loss. When influx remains possible, the same change has multiple potential causes. The most informative reasoning considers surface populations, initial internal populations and later internal populations separately. These observations help distinguish altered surface fitness, altered access and altered postentry establishment without assuming that all effects are independent.

The interpretation of a host genotype with altered stomatal behavior illustrates the logic of a route comparison. If two genotypes have similar surface-associated bacterial abundance but one has a smaller early internal population, an access-related restriction becomes plausible. If the difference disappears when the stomatal stage is absent from the comparison, the evidence is stronger that the original phenotype depended on that stage. However, a difference in early internal survival could still contribute. Independent evidence of the anatomical route and host physiology is necessary before claiming that aperture alone accounts for the phenotype.

A receptor-associated phenotype requires a different set of comparisons. A host with a defect in perception of a defined bacterial component can be evaluated for its response to that component independently of the whole organism. If the response is reduced, the finding supports a role in perception or signaling. If the same host also shows altered access through an epidermal opening, the two observations may be related. But a single receptor defect can influence several outputs, and the absence of one response does not identify the complete mechanism of susceptibility. A functional restoration that restores the response strengthens the connection between genotype and phenotype.

Fungal penetration can be evaluated by considering distinct morphological transitions. If the proportion of spores germinating is unchanged but fewer germ tubes form appressoria, a defect in differentiation is suggested. If appressoria form normally but crossing of the host surface is reduced, the mechanism may involve pressure, peg formation or modification of the encountered material. If penetration is similar but establishment of haustoria differs, the relevant defect occurs later. These predictions are not equivalent to demonstrating a particular molecular cause; they identify the stage at which additional evidence is most informative.

A well-designed interpretation of haustorial images considers the origin and continuity of membrane boundaries. If a structure crosses a host wall and a separate host-associated membrane remains visible around it, wall penetration has occurred without free exposure of pathogen cytoplasm to host cytosol. An image lacking a visible membrane at low resolution does not establish that membrane disruption occurred. The optical method may lack sufficient resolution or contrast. Agreement between structural observations and independent localization evidence strengthens the conclusion that the compartment is membrane bounded.

For a virus, detection of a viral genome does not necessarily distinguish the material delivered from material synthesized within a host cell. An increase in a marker after entry is more informative when its biological origin is known and the measurement is interpreted alongside evidence of intracellular localization. An assay that measures viral protein production can help address expression, but the particular protein and source of the signal matter. A protein carried by the original particles is not identical evidence to a protein newly produced by the recipient cell. The distinction illustrates a general principle: a marker must report the event about which the investigator wishes to reason.

A simple probability framework can clarify the serial nature of infection. Suppose an encounter has probability p of reaching internal tissue and, conditional on reaching that tissue, probability q of establishing an initial population. The probability of both events is p multiplied by q. This follows from the definition of conditional probability and does not require the two events to be independent. If a third event must occur, such as subsequent cellular access for a virus, its conditional probability must also be considered. The important assumption is that the events have been defined in sequence and each conditional probability refers to the correct population.

The model is useful because it separates early access from subsequent compatibility. A change in the fraction of successful encounters could arise through a change in p, q or both. If only the overall outcome is measured, the individual probabilities cannot usually be recovered uniquely. For example, an overall establishment probability of one tenth could arise from one half of encounters reaching the tissue and one fifth of those becoming established, or from one fifth reaching the tissue and one half of those becoming established. These cases imply different restrictions despite the same overall fraction. The model is descriptive and does not assume that real infection events are identical or that microbial cells act independently.

Population changes also require careful definition. For a closed population experiencing approximately constant net per capita growth rate r, the change in abundance over time can be represented by dN/dt = rN. Separation of variables gives dN/N = r dt. Integration between an initial abundance N0 and abundance Nt at time t yields ln(Nt/N0) = rt. This is a natural-logarithm relationship, with r expressed in reciprocal units of time. It describes a net population rate; it is not automatically the rate of cell division when losses occur. The model does not apply indefinitely if resources become limiting or the rate changes substantially.

The distinction between a fitted rate and a causal explanation is especially relevant to plant tissues. In an open compartment, the population can receive additional cells from elsewhere or lose them through movement. A more general description includes a term for influx and a term for losses not already represented by the net rate. A curve of total abundance is then insufficient to identify all underlying processes without further information. A single time point is even less informative because it combines the starting population with all subsequent changes. Quantitative examination questions become tractable when assumptions about the compartment and time interval are explicitly provided.

Comparisons between two populations should consider both absolute and relative changes. A population increasing from a small initial value can display a large fold increase while remaining smaller in absolute terms than a population with a high initial value. Conversely, a larger final abundance might arise entirely from a larger starting population if the relative net growth is the same. When the question concerns internal establishment, normalizing later abundance to the appropriate early internal abundance can be informative. When the question concerns total disease burden, absolute abundance may be more relevant. The correct denominator depends on the biological question.

Finally, an experiment that identifies an early restriction does not automatically predict the ultimate consequences for the host. Later pathogen activity, host compensation and spread can modify the relationship between the early population and disease. A plant may tolerate a population without severe visible injury, or a restricted population may still have a substantial physiological effect. The early infection sequence is therefore a foundation for disease reasoning rather than a complete description of all later outcomes. By tracking the compartments, timing and measured variables, a student can distinguish the mechanism of access from the mechanisms of host alteration and spread.
