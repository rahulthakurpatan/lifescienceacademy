1. Why plants need respiration in light and darkness

A green leaf in bright sunlight is capable of producing organic matter from carbon dioxide, yet each living cell of that same leaf needs to release usable chemical energy from organic substrates. This is not a contradiction. Photosynthesis changes the supply of energy and reduced carbon available to a plant; respiration changes the form and location in which that energy can be used. A root tip growing underground cannot perform ordinary light-driven carbon fixation, but its cells can divide, synthesize proteins and pump ions because carbon compounds from photosynthetic tissues supply respiratory metabolism. A seedling growing before its first leaves expand depends on stored reserves. Even a fully illuminated leaf must maintain cellular processes outside the chloroplast. Understanding mitochondrial respiration therefore begins with the demands of a living plant rather than with a list of enzyme names.

The word respiration sometimes denotes gas exchange in elementary descriptions, but cellular respiration refers here to a collection of reactions that oxidize substrates and conserve a portion of the released free energy. Gas exchange is a measurable consequence, not the mechanism itself. For example, an illuminated C3 leaf may absorb carbon dioxide from surrounding air while its mitochondrial enzymes are releasing carbon dioxide. The observed exchange represents the difference between several simultaneously operating pathways. It cannot establish that respiration is absent. Conversely, a darkened leaf has ceased light-driven photosynthesis but still supports growth and maintenance by using previously synthesized compounds. Respiration also takes place in living nongreen tissues. The relative rates depend on developmental stage, substrate availability, temperature, oxygen supply and the demand for energy and biosynthetic intermediates.

Energy enters a photosynthetic cell as radiation and is conserved partly in reduced carbon compounds. The Calvin–Benson cycle supplies phosphorylated carbon products that contribute to sucrose and starch formation. These are not interchangeable with ATP in an immediate biochemical reaction. A molecule of sucrose cannot simply drive a membrane pump by binding to the pump in place of ATP. Its carbon must first be metabolized through enzymes that transfer electrons to suitable acceptors and couple favorable reactions to energy conservation. ATP then participates directly in phosphorylation, mechanical work or ion transport. NADH, in contrast, is a reduced electron carrier, not an energy currency that can be inserted into every ATP-requiring reaction. ATP and NADH have connected yet distinct roles.

In a plant cell, the mitochondrion is central to the oxidation of respiratory substrates, whereas the chloroplast is central to photosynthetic carbon assimilation. ATP made in the chloroplast supports the Calvin cycle and other chloroplast processes. Mitochondrial ATP supports mitochondrial work and can be exchanged with the cytosolic nucleotide pool. Neither organelle is a completely isolated energy factory. They exchange carbon compounds, and reducing equivalents can be redistributed indirectly through metabolic reactions and selective transporters. However, their membranes and internal redox states mean that the concentrations of ATP, NADH and NADPH in different compartments are not automatically identical. Consequently, an increase in photosynthetic ATP synthesis does not by itself prove that a particular mitochondrial or cytosolic ATP-requiring reaction is supplied adequately.

Two linked but experimentally separable processes occur during aerobic respiration. The first is the oxidation of organic carbon, through reactions such as pyruvate dehydrogenation and the citric acid cycle. This generates reduced carriers and carbon dioxide. The second is mitochondrial electron transport, in which the electrons ultimately reach oxygen and are used to establish conditions for ATP synthesis. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the ordinary aerobic respiratory chain, but molecular oxygen is not a direct substrate in each citric acid cycle reaction. This distinction becomes crucial when a question asks what happens after oxygen depletion. The cycle slows because oxidized cofactors become difficult to regenerate, not because oxygen is normally added to every carbon intermediate.

The complete aerobic oxidation of glucose is summarized by glucose plus six molecules of oxygen yielding six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water. This equation is a useful overall accounting identity, not an elementary enzyme reaction. A substantial amount of the original chemical energy is transferred through several stages before oxygen is reduced. Some energy is dissipated as heat and the fraction conserved in ATP varies with metabolic organization. The equation also presupposes complete oxidation of the carbon. If a plant withdraws citrate for biosynthesis or retains part of its sugar as a new cell wall, the whole organism will not obey this respiratory equation as a description of every carbon atom entering metabolism.

Why is the citric acid cycle particularly important? It is a metabolic meeting point rather than simply a furnace that burns sugar. Its intermediates can feed amino acid synthesis, participate in carbon and nitrogen coordination, and be returned to the cycle through replenishing reactions. A growing tissue must balance oxidation with construction. If it oxidized every available carbon molecule to carbon dioxide, little would remain for making new membranes, proteins and structural carbohydrates. If it retained all carbon without producing adequate ATP and oxidized cofactors, cellular construction would stall. The citric acid cycle helps resolve these competing demands by providing both respiratory reducing equivalents and carbon skeletons for growth.

2. Carbon supply from leaves, reserves and glycolysis

In vascular plants, mature photosynthetic leaves generally act as sources of assimilated carbon and many growing or storage organs act as sinks. Sucrose is an important transported carbohydrate. A developing root or seed can obtain sucrose from the phloem, whereas a mature leaf may draw on carbohydrate made locally. The source–sink distinction describes net movement over a given interval; it is not a fixed identity of an organ under every condition. A young leaf can be a sink until it becomes capable of exporting more carbon than it receives. A storage organ can later become a source when stored reserves are mobilized for new growth. In all these settings, the carbon ultimately enters central metabolism through reactions determined by the tissue and its physiological state.

Plants can use sucrose through invertase or sucrose synthase pathways. Invertase hydrolyzes sucrose into glucose and fructose, while sucrose synthase catalyzes a reversible reaction involving sucrose and UDP to produce UDP-glucose and fructose. These reactions supply carbon for metabolism or biosynthesis in different forms. Hexose sugars need suitable phosphorylation steps before they can enter the usual glycolytic sequence. It would be incorrect to assume that every molecule of sucrose imported into a cell immediately produces four molecules of mitochondrial pyruvate. Carbon may instead be incorporated into cell walls, stored or diverted through other routes. Four pyruvates per sucrose is the idealized carbon yield only if both of its hexose units are fully processed by glycolysis.

Starch plays a particularly important role in understanding the day–night connection. In a photosynthetically active leaf, some assimilated carbon accumulates as transitory starch in chloroplasts. During darkness, starch breakdown can supply sugars that support respiration and export. The biochemical breakdown and transport routes need not be identical to those used by a storage organ such as a germinating cereal grain. A useful general principle is that the reserve is converted into smaller, metabolically accessible compounds before its carbon reaches respiration. Insoluble starch granules do not pass through the mitochondrial inner membrane. A dark leaf with depleted carbohydrate reserves may show constrained respiration even though all the respiratory enzymes are still present.

Glycolysis provides a common starting point for the oxidation of hexose carbon. In its familiar ATP-dependent textbook form, one glucose molecule yields two pyruvate molecules, two net ATP and two NADH when the pathway runs to completion. The hexose is first converted through phosphorylation and isomerization steps into a form that can be cleaved into triose phosphates. Oxidation of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate reduces NAD+ and introduces a high-energy phosphorylated intermediate. Subsequent phosphate transfer to ADP and carbon rearrangements lead to pyruvate. The early ATP investment and later ATP generation are distinct; net ATP is obtained by subtracting consumption from production. Plant cells possess glycolytic reactions in cytosolic and plastidic contexts and can use additional enzyme variants, so this ideal accounting should not be imposed uncritically on all plant carbon flux.

A useful boundary between the glycolytic pathway and mitochondrial oxidation is the production of pyruvate. Pyruvate is a three-carbon alpha-keto acid: it contains a carboxylate group adjacent to a keto group and a methyl group. Its structure allows oxidative decarboxylation, in which one carbon is released while the remaining two form an acetyl group. It is also a branching metabolite. If oxygen limitation prevents efficient respiratory cofactor recycling, pyruvate can be diverted into fermentative processes that help regenerate cytosolic NAD+. In a well-aerated tissue it often supplies mitochondrial oxidation, but its partitioning is not an all-or-none switch dictated solely by oxygen. The use of pyruvate depends on transport, enzyme activity, cellular demand and competing pathways.

The fact that glycolysis creates NADH in the cytosol does not imply that this NADH molecule freely enters the mitochondrial matrix. The inner mitochondrial membrane is selective, and cytosolic and matrix redox pools are distinct. Cells can transfer reducing equivalents indirectly by linking metabolite interconversion to transport, and plant mitochondria also have electron-transfer routes able to oxidize some external reducing equivalents. Those electron transport pathways will be examined in the next chapter. At present, the key reasoning is to label both the substrate and the compartment before assigning its metabolic fate. Cytosolic pyruvate and cytosolic NADH are products of the same pathway but reach the mitochondrial respiratory system by different mechanisms.

Suppose a leaf begins to export sucrose rapidly after illumination. Carbon allocation to export might increase, but we cannot infer from that observation alone whether mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation also increases. It depends on how much carbon is directed into glycolysis and on competing pathways, as well as on the redox and energy conditions of the mitochondrion. Concentration is a pool size; flux is a rate of movement through a pathway. A small pool of pyruvate can sustain a large flux if consumption closely matches production, while a large pool can indicate impaired consumption. This distinction will recur when mitochondrial enzyme activity is inferred from metabolite measurements.

3. The mitochondrial matrix and selective carbon transport

A plant mitochondrion is enclosed by an outer membrane and an inner membrane. The aqueous region within the inner membrane is the matrix. The space between the two membranes is the intermembrane space, and folds of the inner membrane form cristae. These regions matter because biochemical reactions can occur only where their substrates and enzymes meet. The matrix contains many enzymes of pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle. The inner membrane contains the electron-transfer complexes and ATP synthase, as well as carriers controlling the exchange of metabolites. An enzyme that oxidizes a soluble substrate may nevertheless be part of an inner-membrane complex, as happens with succinate dehydrogenase.

The outer mitochondrial membrane is comparatively permeable to many small metabolites through channels. The inner membrane is much more selective. Its low permeability to ions is required for the electrochemical gradient that drives ATP synthesis. The same barrier also prevents the assumption that a charged organic acid reaches the matrix by unrestricted diffusion. Pyruvate, predominantly anionic near physiological pH, is transported by mitochondrial pyruvate carriers in the inner membrane. Transport is therefore a possible control point even when cytosolic pyruvate is abundant and matrix enzymes remain functional. The inner membrane makes mitochondrial respiration a problem in membrane transport as well as enzyme chemistry.

Plant mitochondria can also generate pyruvate within the matrix. NAD-dependent malic enzyme converts malate into pyruvate, carbon dioxide and NADH; alanine aminotransferase can transfer the amino group of alanine to 2-oxoglutarate, generating pyruvate and glutamate. These pathways allow alternative substrate entry when cytosolic pyruvate import is restricted, although their quantitative importance is tissue- and condition-dependent. Research on Arabidopsis mitochondrial substrate use distinguishes imported and internally produced pyruvate and shows why loss of a single transport route cannot always be equated with complete loss of respiratory pyruvate supply. These alternatives should be treated as routes with their own substrate and redox requirements, not as a universal guarantee of full compensation.

Consider the malate route in more detail. Malate is a four-carbon dicarboxylate. NAD-dependent malic enzyme removes a carbon as carbon dioxide while oxidizing the substrate and generating a three-carbon pyruvate molecule. Pyruvate can then undergo oxidative decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA. If both reactions operate in sequence, the original four-carbon malate has already yielded two carbon dioxide molecules and two NADH before the acetyl group enters the citric acid cycle. This is not the same as the malate dehydrogenase reaction, which oxidizes four-carbon malate into four-carbon oxaloacetate without carbon loss. The distinction is readily tested by counting carbon atoms and identifying the oxidation product.

Likewise, alanine aminotransferase does not directly produce NADH. It transfers an amino group between an amino acid and a keto acid. Its products link carbon metabolism to nitrogen metabolism. If alanine gives its amino group to 2-oxoglutarate, the resulting glutamate retains the five-carbon skeleton of 2-oxoglutarate, and the resulting pyruvate retains the three-carbon skeleton of alanine. The reaction need not release free ammonium. An examination problem that compares malic enzyme and aminotransferase can therefore be solved by determining whether each reaction transfers electrons, transfers nitrogen or removes carbon. Both may generate pyruvate without having the same effect on matrix redox balance.

Several other charged intermediates, including malate and citrate, are exchanged across mitochondrial membranes by specialized transport systems. The identity and direction of transport depend on the available gradients and carrier properties. Oxaloacetate, malate and aspartate can interconvert through reactions that support indirect exchange of carbon and reducing equivalents. Consequently, drawing a pathway on a single flat page without membrane boundaries can obscure the actual route followed by a carbon atom. When a substrate is labeled in the cytosol, the investigator must consider which compounds carry that label into the matrix. The mere appearance of label in a mitochondrial intermediate does not prove direct transport of the original compound.
