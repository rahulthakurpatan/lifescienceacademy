1. Why plants need respiration in light and darkness

A green leaf in bright sunlight is capable of producing organic matter from carbon dioxide, yet each living cell of that same leaf needs to release usable chemical energy from organic substrates. This is not a contradiction. Photosynthesis changes the supply of energy and reduced carbon available to a plant; respiration changes the form and location in which that energy can be used. A root tip growing underground cannot perform ordinary light-driven carbon fixation, but its cells can divide, synthesize proteins and pump ions because carbon compounds from photosynthetic tissues supply respiratory metabolism. A seedling growing before its first leaves expand depends on stored reserves. Even a fully illuminated leaf must maintain cellular processes outside the chloroplast. Understanding mitochondrial respiration therefore begins with the demands of a living plant rather than with a list of enzyme names.

The word respiration sometimes denotes gas exchange in elementary descriptions, but cellular respiration refers here to a collection of reactions that oxidize substrates and conserve a portion of the released free energy. Gas exchange is a measurable consequence, not the mechanism itself. For example, an illuminated C3 leaf may absorb carbon dioxide from surrounding air while its mitochondrial enzymes are releasing carbon dioxide. The observed exchange represents the difference between several simultaneously operating pathways. It cannot establish that respiration is absent. Conversely, a darkened leaf has ceased light-driven photosynthesis but still supports growth and maintenance by using previously synthesized compounds. Respiration also takes place in living nongreen tissues. The relative rates depend on developmental stage, substrate availability, temperature, oxygen supply and the demand for energy and biosynthetic intermediates.

Energy enters a photosynthetic cell as radiation and is conserved partly in reduced carbon compounds. The Calvin–Benson cycle supplies phosphorylated carbon products that contribute to sucrose and starch formation. These are not interchangeable with ATP in an immediate biochemical reaction. A molecule of sucrose cannot simply drive a membrane pump by binding to the pump in place of ATP. Its carbon must first be metabolized through enzymes that transfer electrons to suitable acceptors and couple favorable reactions to energy conservation. ATP then participates directly in phosphorylation, mechanical work or ion transport. NADH, in contrast, is a reduced electron carrier, not an energy currency that can be inserted into every ATP-requiring reaction. ATP and NADH have connected yet distinct roles.

In a plant cell, the mitochondrion is central to the oxidation of respiratory substrates, whereas the chloroplast is central to photosynthetic carbon assimilation. ATP made in the chloroplast supports the Calvin cycle and other chloroplast processes. Mitochondrial ATP supports mitochondrial work and can be exchanged with the cytosolic nucleotide pool. Neither organelle is a completely isolated energy factory. They exchange carbon compounds, and reducing equivalents can be redistributed indirectly through metabolic reactions and selective transporters. However, their membranes and internal redox states mean that the concentrations of ATP, NADH and NADPH in different compartments are not automatically identical. Consequently, an increase in photosynthetic ATP synthesis does not by itself prove that a particular mitochondrial or cytosolic ATP-requiring reaction is supplied adequately.

Two linked but experimentally separable processes occur during aerobic respiration. The first is the oxidation of organic carbon, through reactions such as pyruvate dehydrogenation and the citric acid cycle. This generates reduced carriers and carbon dioxide. The second is mitochondrial electron transport, in which the electrons ultimately reach oxygen and are used to establish conditions for ATP synthesis. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in the ordinary aerobic respiratory chain, but molecular oxygen is not a direct substrate in each citric acid cycle reaction. This distinction becomes crucial when a question asks what happens after oxygen depletion. The cycle slows because oxidized cofactors become difficult to regenerate, not because oxygen is normally added to every carbon intermediate.

The complete aerobic oxidation of glucose is summarized by glucose plus six molecules of oxygen yielding six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water. This equation is a useful overall accounting identity, not an elementary enzyme reaction. A substantial amount of the original chemical energy is transferred through several stages before oxygen is reduced. Some energy is dissipated as heat and the fraction conserved in ATP varies with metabolic organization. The equation also presupposes complete oxidation of the carbon. If a plant withdraws citrate for biosynthesis or retains part of its sugar as a new cell wall, the whole organism will not obey this respiratory equation as a description of every carbon atom entering metabolism.

Why is the citric acid cycle particularly important? It is a metabolic meeting point rather than simply a furnace that burns sugar. Its intermediates can feed amino acid synthesis, participate in carbon and nitrogen coordination, and be returned to the cycle through replenishing reactions. A growing tissue must balance oxidation with construction. If it oxidized every available carbon molecule to carbon dioxide, little would remain for making new membranes, proteins and structural carbohydrates. If it retained all carbon without producing adequate ATP and oxidized cofactors, cellular construction would stall. The citric acid cycle helps resolve these competing demands by providing both respiratory reducing equivalents and carbon skeletons for growth.

2. Carbon supply from leaves, reserves and glycolysis

In vascular plants, mature photosynthetic leaves generally act as sources of assimilated carbon and many growing or storage organs act as sinks. Sucrose is an important transported carbohydrate. A developing root or seed can obtain sucrose from the phloem, whereas a mature leaf may draw on carbohydrate made locally. The source–sink distinction describes net movement over a given interval; it is not a fixed identity of an organ under every condition. A young leaf can be a sink until it becomes capable of exporting more carbon than it receives. A storage organ can later become a source when stored reserves are mobilized for new growth. In all these settings, the carbon ultimately enters central metabolism through reactions determined by the tissue and its physiological state.

Plants can use sucrose through invertase or sucrose synthase pathways. Invertase hydrolyzes sucrose into glucose and fructose, while sucrose synthase catalyzes a reversible reaction involving sucrose and UDP to produce UDP-glucose and fructose. These reactions supply carbon for metabolism or biosynthesis in different forms. Hexose sugars need suitable phosphorylation steps before they can enter the usual glycolytic sequence. It would be incorrect to assume that every molecule of sucrose imported into a cell immediately produces four molecules of mitochondrial pyruvate. Carbon may instead be incorporated into cell walls, stored or diverted through other routes. Four pyruvates per sucrose is the idealized carbon yield only if both of its hexose units are fully processed by glycolysis.

Starch plays a particularly important role in understanding the day–night connection. In a photosynthetically active leaf, some assimilated carbon accumulates as transitory starch in chloroplasts. During darkness, starch breakdown can supply sugars that support respiration and export. The biochemical breakdown and transport routes need not be identical to those used by a storage organ such as a germinating cereal grain. A useful general principle is that the reserve is converted into smaller, metabolically accessible compounds before its carbon reaches respiration. Insoluble starch granules do not pass through the mitochondrial inner membrane. A dark leaf with depleted carbohydrate reserves may show constrained respiration even though all the respiratory enzymes are still present.

Glycolysis provides a common starting point for the oxidation of hexose carbon. In its familiar ATP-dependent textbook form, one glucose molecule yields two pyruvate molecules, two net ATP and two NADH when the pathway runs to completion. The hexose is first converted through phosphorylation and isomerization steps into a form that can be cleaved into triose phosphates. Oxidation of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate reduces NAD+ and introduces a high-energy phosphorylated intermediate. Subsequent phosphate transfer to ADP and carbon rearrangements lead to pyruvate. The early ATP investment and later ATP generation are distinct; net ATP is obtained by subtracting consumption from production. Plant cells possess glycolytic reactions in cytosolic and plastidic contexts and can use additional enzyme variants, so this ideal accounting should not be imposed uncritically on all plant carbon flux.

A useful boundary between the glycolytic pathway and mitochondrial oxidation is the production of pyruvate. Pyruvate is a three-carbon alpha-keto acid: it contains a carboxylate group adjacent to a keto group and a methyl group. Its structure allows oxidative decarboxylation, in which one carbon is released while the remaining two form an acetyl group. It is also a branching metabolite. If oxygen limitation prevents efficient respiratory cofactor recycling, pyruvate can be diverted into fermentative processes that help regenerate cytosolic NAD+. In a well-aerated tissue it often supplies mitochondrial oxidation, but its partitioning is not an all-or-none switch dictated solely by oxygen. The use of pyruvate depends on transport, enzyme activity, cellular demand and competing pathways.

The fact that glycolysis creates NADH in the cytosol does not imply that this NADH molecule freely enters the mitochondrial matrix. The inner mitochondrial membrane is selective, and cytosolic and matrix redox pools are distinct. Cells can transfer reducing equivalents indirectly by linking metabolite interconversion to transport, and plant mitochondria also have electron-transfer routes able to oxidize some external reducing equivalents. Those electron transport pathways will be examined in the next chapter. At present, the key reasoning is to label both the substrate and the compartment before assigning its metabolic fate. Cytosolic pyruvate and cytosolic NADH are products of the same pathway but reach the mitochondrial respiratory system by different mechanisms.

Suppose a leaf begins to export sucrose rapidly after illumination. Carbon allocation to export might increase, but we cannot infer from that observation alone whether mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation also increases. It depends on how much carbon is directed into glycolysis and on competing pathways, as well as on the redox and energy conditions of the mitochondrion. Concentration is a pool size; flux is a rate of movement through a pathway. A small pool of pyruvate can sustain a large flux if consumption closely matches production, while a large pool can indicate impaired consumption. This distinction will recur when mitochondrial enzyme activity is inferred from metabolite measurements.

3. The mitochondrial matrix and selective carbon transport

A plant mitochondrion is enclosed by an outer membrane and an inner membrane. The aqueous region within the inner membrane is the matrix. The space between the two membranes is the intermembrane space, and folds of the inner membrane form cristae. These regions matter because biochemical reactions can occur only where their substrates and enzymes meet. The matrix contains many enzymes of pyruvate oxidation and the citric acid cycle. The inner membrane contains the electron-transfer complexes and ATP synthase, as well as carriers controlling the exchange of metabolites. An enzyme that oxidizes a soluble substrate may nevertheless be part of an inner-membrane complex, as happens with succinate dehydrogenase.

The outer mitochondrial membrane is comparatively permeable to many small metabolites through channels. The inner membrane is much more selective. Its low permeability to ions is required for the electrochemical gradient that drives ATP synthesis. The same barrier also prevents the assumption that a charged organic acid reaches the matrix by unrestricted diffusion. Pyruvate, predominantly anionic near physiological pH, is transported by mitochondrial pyruvate carriers in the inner membrane. Transport is therefore a possible control point even when cytosolic pyruvate is abundant and matrix enzymes remain functional. The inner membrane makes mitochondrial respiration a problem in membrane transport as well as enzyme chemistry.

Plant mitochondria can also generate pyruvate within the matrix. NAD-dependent malic enzyme converts malate into pyruvate, carbon dioxide and NADH; alanine aminotransferase can transfer the amino group of alanine to 2-oxoglutarate, generating pyruvate and glutamate. These pathways allow alternative substrate entry when cytosolic pyruvate import is restricted, although their quantitative importance is tissue- and condition-dependent. Research on Arabidopsis mitochondrial substrate use distinguishes imported and internally produced pyruvate and shows why loss of a single transport route cannot always be equated with complete loss of respiratory pyruvate supply. These alternatives should be treated as routes with their own substrate and redox requirements, not as a universal guarantee of full compensation.

Consider the malate route in more detail. Malate is a four-carbon dicarboxylate. NAD-dependent malic enzyme removes a carbon as carbon dioxide while oxidizing the substrate and generating a three-carbon pyruvate molecule. Pyruvate can then undergo oxidative decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA. If both reactions operate in sequence, the original four-carbon malate has already yielded two carbon dioxide molecules and two NADH before the acetyl group enters the citric acid cycle. This is not the same as the malate dehydrogenase reaction, which oxidizes four-carbon malate into four-carbon oxaloacetate without carbon loss. The distinction is readily tested by counting carbon atoms and identifying the oxidation product.

Likewise, alanine aminotransferase does not directly produce NADH. It transfers an amino group between an amino acid and a keto acid. Its products link carbon metabolism to nitrogen metabolism. If alanine gives its amino group to 2-oxoglutarate, the resulting glutamate retains the five-carbon skeleton of 2-oxoglutarate, and the resulting pyruvate retains the three-carbon skeleton of alanine. The reaction need not release free ammonium. An examination problem that compares malic enzyme and aminotransferase can therefore be solved by determining whether each reaction transfers electrons, transfers nitrogen or removes carbon. Both may generate pyruvate without having the same effect on matrix redox balance.

Several other charged intermediates, including malate and citrate, are exchanged across mitochondrial membranes by specialized transport systems. The identity and direction of transport depend on the available gradients and carrier properties. Oxaloacetate, malate and aspartate can interconvert through reactions that support indirect exchange of carbon and reducing equivalents. Consequently, drawing a pathway on a single flat page without membrane boundaries can obscure the actual route followed by a carbon atom. When a substrate is labeled in the cytosol, the investigator must consider which compounds carry that label into the matrix. The mere appearance of label in a mitochondrial intermediate does not prove direct transport of the original compound.

4. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: the irreversible entry reaction

The transition from pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is an important change in chemical form. Pyruvate contains a carboxyl group that can be released as carbon dioxide. The remaining two-carbon fragment must be oxidized and transferred to a suitable carrier before it can condense with oxaloacetate. The mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) coordinates these chemical transformations. Its overall equation, written with conventional biochemical proton bookkeeping, is pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD+ → acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+. One three-carbon substrate gives a two-carbon acetyl group and one carbon dioxide; one oxidized NAD+ accepts a pair of electrons. The equation includes no ATP consumption or production. Its energy-conserving product is acetyl-CoA, a thioester, in addition to NADH.

The acetyl group is not equivalent to free acetate. In acetyl-CoA it is covalently joined to the sulfur of coenzyme A. Hydrolysis of this thioester is favorable, and the chemical potential associated with the thioester helps drive the citrate synthase reaction that follows. CoA is a recyclable group carrier: its large molecular structure does not become part of citrate. In a carbon-accounting question, count two carbons contributed by acetyl-CoA, not the many carbon atoms in the entire coenzyme A molecule. CoA molecules cycle between acylated and unacylated states as metabolic reactions transfer acyl groups. If the free CoA pool is depleted, the supply of acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA can be limited despite adequate carbon substrates.

PDC is composed of interacting catalytic activities conventionally named E1, E2 and E3. E1 is pyruvate dehydrogenase, E2 is dihydrolipoyl transacetylase, and E3 is dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase. They employ thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoamide, coenzyme A, FAD and NAD+. The order in which these cofactors operate clarifies why a complex is advantageous. Pyruvate first binds to the thiamine-dependent enzyme. Decarboxylation generates a hydroxyethyl intermediate stabilized on thiamine pyrophosphate. It does not immediately release a free acetyl anion into the matrix. Instead, the remaining carbon fragment is transferred to the oxidized lipoamide group associated with E2. During this transfer the fragment is oxidized to an acetyl group and the lipoamide disulfide is reduced.

The lipoyl group is attached through an amino acid side chain to the protein and can move between catalytic sites. It carries the acetyl group to the transferase site, where the acetyl group is transferred to the sulfur of coenzyme A. Acetyl-CoA can then dissociate for use in downstream metabolism. The reduced lipoyl group must be regenerated before it can accept another hydroxyethyl group. E3 transfers electrons from the reduced lipoyl group through its bound FAD and finally to NAD+. The regeneration step explains why NADH is released by the overall reaction even though the first decarboxylation reaction does not directly transfer hydride to NAD+. FAD is a temporary internal carrier in this enzyme complex, rather than an additional net FADH2 product.

These stages show how oxidative decarboxylation differs from simple decarboxylation. A decarboxylase can remove carbon dioxide without necessarily oxidizing the residual carbon. In PDC, however, carbon loss is coupled to a redox reaction and thioester formation. If a question supplies a selective defect in E3, the initial decarboxylation reaction might proceed transiently, but the reduced lipoyl groups cannot be efficiently restored. Sustained acetyl-CoA formation therefore declines. It is insufficient to conclude that E3 is irrelevant to acetyl-CoA simply because it acts after the acetyl-transfer step. Every repeated turnover requires the catalytic system to return to its starting state.

PDC is generally treated as physiologically irreversible. Its strongly favorable overall reaction separates glycolytic pyruvate from the mitochondrial acetyl-CoA pool. This does not mean that a plant cannot synthesize three-carbon compounds from two-carbon reserves by any route. Plants can mobilize lipids in germinating oil seeds and use the glyoxylate cycle and gluconeogenic pathways to conserve carbon; the reverse PDC reaction is not the mechanism for that conversion. Such distinctions are essential when a problem asks whether acetyl-CoA can serve as a net precursor of carbohydrate. The answer depends on the available pathway network, not merely on the ability to write an equation backward.

Plant cells contain distinct mitochondrial and plastid pyruvate dehydrogenase systems. The mitochondrial complex supplies acetyl-CoA for respiratory metabolism, whereas plastid PDC provides acetyl groups that contribute to fatty acid synthesis. Products in one compartment cannot be assigned automatically to the other. For example, impaired mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation does not imply that plastid fatty acid production has lost its own source of acetyl-CoA. Both systems are regulated in their physiological contexts. Mitochondrial PDC activity responds to substrate and product availability and reversible phosphorylation of its E1 component. Phosphorylation by PDC kinase generally reduces mitochondrial complex activity; dephosphorylation restores it. Its response to illumination involves more than the direct presence or absence of photons at the enzyme.

A high matrix NADH/NAD+ ratio can hinder net oxidation because the oxidized acceptor NAD+ is less available relative to the reduced product. A high acetyl-CoA/free CoA ratio can also oppose continued formation of acetyl-CoA. These product and substrate relationships interact with phosphorylation-dependent regulation. A mitochondrion whose respiratory chain cannot recycle NADH may therefore have reduced PDC flux even with a large pyruvate pool. If electron transport resumes and regenerates NAD+, the same pyruvate pool can support renewed oxidation. This illustrates a common systems-level principle: controlling the consumption of a product can regulate an earlier reaction that produces it.

5. Citrate formation and the two oxidative decarboxylations

The citric acid cycle, also called the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle or Krebs cycle, is named after its six-carbon product citrate and the fact that the reaction sequence regenerates its starting four-carbon acceptor. Its discovery grew from studies of organic acid oxidation and the observation that certain metabolic intermediates stimulated the oxidation of larger amounts of respiratory substrate than could be explained by their own disappearance. Hans Krebs and William Johnson proposed a cyclic scheme in 1937. The important conceptual advance was that intermediates could act in a regenerative system. A small amount of an intermediate could support repeated oxidation of incoming acetyl units because it was restored by the later reactions. The cycle is not a closed container of fixed molecules: intermediates enter and leave the network, particularly in living plant tissues.

Citrate synthase begins the canonical sequence by joining acetyl-CoA with oxaloacetate. The four-carbon oxaloacetate contains a keto group that can accept the two-carbon fragment. The enzyme catalyzes a carbon–carbon bond-forming condensation, yielding an enzyme-bound citryl-CoA intermediate. Thioester hydrolysis releases citrate and free coenzyme A. The favorable hydrolysis helps drive the overall reaction, which consumes water. No NADH is generated at this first step, because there is no net transfer of electrons to NAD+. Its biological role is to commit available acetyl groups to the citrate pool when oxaloacetate is also available. This substrate dependence is why an abundant acetyl-CoA pool alone cannot guarantee rapid TCA activity.

Citrate is a six-carbon molecule with three carboxyl groups and a hydroxyl group on a tertiary carbon. Direct oxidation of that hydroxyl-bearing carbon is not suited to the following decarboxylation mechanism. Aconitase rearranges citrate into isocitrate through a dehydration and rehydration sequence involving cis-aconitate. There is no net carbon loss, and water consumed in the second stage balances water removed in the first. The rearrangement shifts the hydroxyl group to a position that permits oxidation. Aconitase uses an iron–sulfur cluster to assist catalysis. That cluster helps bind and rearrange substrate; it is not functioning as a net electron donor to the respiratory chain in the aconitase reaction.

A common mistake is to treat citrate and isocitrate as two names for the same structure because both have six carbons and the same overall formula. They are structural isomers with different arrangements of substituents and therefore different catalytic possibilities. Aconitase is stereospecific. The enzyme imposes orientation on the otherwise symmetric citrate framework so that the subsequent reactions follow a defined chemical path. At a conceptual level, this establishes why carbon labeling experiments can distinguish the fate of carbons that are structurally equivalent in a free molecule but become distinguishable in an enzyme-bound orientation. The cycle cannot be understood correctly by using only carbon counts; the location of functional groups also matters.

Isocitrate dehydrogenase performs the first oxidative decarboxylation of the cycle. The mitochondrial NAD-dependent enzyme oxidizes isocitrate to an unstable intermediate, oxalosuccinate, which loses carbon dioxide to form the five-carbon keto acid 2-oxoglutarate, also called alpha-ketoglutarate. NAD+ is reduced to NADH during this reaction. A metal ion participates in catalysis and stabilization of intermediates. The net result is a six-carbon substrate yielding a five-carbon product, one carbon dioxide and one reduced NAD carrier. Plant cells also contain NADP-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenases, so the cofactor and compartment must be specified when interpreting an enzyme assay. The canonical respiratory balance of three NADH per turn refers to the standard NAD-linked route.

The product 2-oxoglutarate is particularly significant in plant metabolism because it can provide the carbon skeleton used in glutamate synthesis during nitrogen assimilation. Its consumption is not restricted to the next TCA reaction. When ammonium is assimilated through the glutamine synthetase and glutamate synthase pathway, 2-oxoglutarate participates in the formation of glutamate. Increased demand for glutamate-family amino acids may therefore change the proportion of 2-oxoglutarate that proceeds to succinyl-CoA. In an actively growing tissue, an increase in the rate of 2-oxoglutarate production does not necessarily mean that the same increase will appear in the rate of carbon dioxide release from the following reaction.

The 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex catalyzes the second oxidative decarboxylation. Its overall reaction is 2-oxoglutarate + CoA-SH + NAD+ → succinyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+. Five-carbon 2-oxoglutarate loses one carbon and becomes a four-carbon succinyl group attached to CoA. This complex employs a catalytic logic related to PDC, using thiamine-dependent decarboxylation, lipoyl-group reactions and electron transfer to NAD+. The formation of a second high-energy thioester is important because its subsequent cleavage will be coupled to direct nucleotide phosphorylation. The strongly favorable overall reaction provides a directional component of the conventional cycle. Like PDC, its ability to maintain steady turnover depends on oxidized cofactor regeneration and free coenzyme A.

By this stage, the two carbons added in the citrate synthase reaction have been balanced by the loss of two carbon dioxide molecules. It is tempting to infer that those two released carbon atoms must be the newly added acetyl carbons. That inference is incorrect for the first turn with unlabeled oxaloacetate and labeled acetyl-CoA. In the canonical first passage, the two carbons released are derived from the oxaloacetate acceptor framework. The acetyl-derived carbons persist in the four-carbon product and will be redistributed over later turns. Net carbon accounting and atom-specific tracing answer different questions. The first asks how much carbon is lost; the second asks exactly which atoms are lost.

6. Regenerating oxaloacetate and recovering additional energy

Succinyl-CoA synthetase, also called succinate-CoA ligase, converts succinyl-CoA into succinate and releases free CoA. The energy associated with the succinyl thioester is used to form a nucleoside triphosphate from a nucleoside diphosphate and inorganic phosphate. In the canonical sequence an enzyme-bound phosphorylated intermediate participates in transferring phosphate to the nucleotide. This is substrate-level phosphorylation because energy from a chemical intermediate drives nucleotide phosphorylation without a transmembrane proton gradient. Textbooks often write the product as GTP for the GTP-forming enzyme, especially in mammalian tissue examples. ATP-forming enzymes also exist, and plant mitochondrial accounts commonly emphasize ATP formation. Unless the isoform is specified, one NTP per completed turn is the safest general stoichiometric statement.

A molecule of GTP is not itself ATP. Nucleoside diphosphate kinase can exchange phosphate groups between NTPs and NDPs, allowing energy in GTP to be transferred to ADP in an appropriate metabolic context. The exam question may request the direct nucleotide product or an ATP equivalent. The former requires identifying the enzyme specificity, while the latter allows an explicitly stated equivalence for energetic accounting. It is also necessary to distinguish one NTP produced at this step from the larger, variable amount of ATP that might later be obtained by oxidizing NADH and electrons from succinate. Only the substrate-level phosphorylation is counted as direct nucleotide synthesis within the TCA sequence.

Succinate is a four-carbon dicarboxylate. In the next step, succinate dehydrogenase removes two hydrogen equivalents to form fumarate, which contains a carbon–carbon double bond. The enzyme uses FAD as a bound prosthetic group. It is part of Complex II of the respiratory electron-transfer chain and is associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane, with its catalytic domain facing the matrix. This enzyme is the structural exception to the usual description of the citric acid cycle as a matrix pathway. Its electrons pass from reduced FAD through iron–sulfur centers toward ubiquinone. Unlike a freely diffusible NADH molecule, the FAD cofactor stays associated with the enzyme and is reoxidized as electrons move to the respiratory chain.

The usual notation of one FADH2 per cycle represents the two electrons transferred at the succinate oxidation step. In more mechanistic bookkeeping, one can write that the succinate dehydrogenase reaction reduces ubiquinone to ubiquinol when electron transfer is coupled to the membrane acceptor. Counting both one FADH2 and one QH2 as separate net respiratory products would double-count the same electron pair. A question that blocks ubiquinone reduction can therefore affect succinate oxidation even if FAD is present in the enzyme. The oxidized enzyme-bound FAD must become available again for repeated catalysis. Complex II does not pump protons directly, a distinction that will explain why its electrons support less conventional ATP production than those entering through proton-pumping Complex I.

Fumarase hydrates fumarate to form L-malate. The reaction adds the elements of water across the double bond in a stereospecific manner. Four carbons enter and four leave, and no NADH or carbon dioxide is formed. The double bond is transformed into an alcohol-containing structure appropriate for the final dehydrogenase reaction. The specificity of fumarase is a good example of why biological catalysts control not only the rate of reaction but also the identity of the stereochemical product. Plant tissues can also accumulate fumarate as an organic acid reserve in certain species and conditions. A measured fumarate pool need not represent only the small amount passing through the mitochondrial cycle at that instant.

Malate dehydrogenase catalyzes oxidation of L-malate to oxaloacetate with NAD+ reduction. The hydroxyl group on malate becomes the keto group of oxaloacetate. This supplies the third NADH of the canonical turn and regenerates the four-carbon acceptor required by citrate synthase. Under standard biochemical conditions, the free-energy change for malate oxidation in this direction is unfavorable. In functioning mitochondria, the reaction can proceed because metabolite concentrations differ markedly from standard conditions. Oxaloacetate may be maintained at low concentration by its rapid consumption in citrate synthesis and other reactions, and the NAD+/NADH ratio influences the redox driving force. This is an important case where the direction of cellular flux cannot be predicted solely from the sign of the standard free-energy change.

Oxaloacetate is regenerated chemically, but not necessarily retained as the same set of four carbon atoms. If some oxaloacetate is converted into aspartate or other metabolic products, the pool of cycle intermediates may decline unless replenished. Similarly, if citrate is exported before it is oxidized, the ordinary closed-turn stoichiometry will not describe that molecule. The pathway diagram should therefore be read as a reference map of reactions, not a rule that every citrate molecule must complete a full revolution. A cycle can be recognized by the potential for regeneration even when physiological flux is divided into branches. This distinction between reaction topology and actual flux is one of the most important plant-specific ideas in this chapter.

7. Carbon, electron and nucleotide accounting

The canonical cycle is most easily reconstructed by following three independent accounts. The carbon account records how many carbon atoms enter, remain in intermediates and leave as carbon dioxide. The redox account records the transfer of electron pairs to NAD+ and to the respiratory chain through succinate dehydrogenase. The nucleotide account records the single substrate-level phosphorylation. Keeping these accounts separate prevents several familiar mistakes. For example, the two carbon dioxide molecules are not a measure of the number of ATP molecules formed directly, and the absence of carbon loss during succinate oxidation does not mean that no energy is transferred.

Begin with one acetyl-CoA and one oxaloacetate. Citrate has six carbon atoms because it contains the four-carbon acceptor plus the two-carbon acetyl fragment. Isocitrate also has six. Oxidative decarboxylation produces 2-oxoglutarate with five. The next oxidative decarboxylation produces a four-carbon succinyl group. Succinate, fumarate, malate and regenerated oxaloacetate each contain four carbon atoms. The gross carbon input is six and the gross carbon output consists of four carbons in the regenerated acceptor and two as carbon dioxide. Subtract the regenerated acceptor from both sides of a net equation. The resulting net carbon balance is two carbons entering as an acetyl group and two carbons leaving as carbon dioxide. This subtraction is legitimate for a complete turn with no net loss of intermediates. It is not legitimate if an intermediate is withdrawn and not replaced.

The reducing equivalents arise from four distinct oxidation reactions. NADH is generated by NAD-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase, the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex and malate dehydrogenase. Succinate dehydrogenase transfers another pair of electrons to ubiquinone through bound FAD. Each two-electron transfer is counted once. Thus three NADH represent six electrons, and the succinate-derived electron pair adds two, for a total of eight electrons per acetyl unit. The balanced overall redox reaction for complete oxidation of an acetyl unit is consistent with removal of these eight electrons. Molecular oxygen can eventually receive four electrons per O2 molecule when reduced fully to water. In an idealized system in which these four electron pairs all reach oxygen, two molecules of oxygen are reduced per completed TCA turn. That is an indirect stoichiometric implication of electron recycling rather than direct oxygen consumption by the cycle enzymes.

The nucleotide account gives one ATP in a typical plant mitochondrial cycle using ADP-forming succinyl-CoA ligase in the direction of substrate oxidation, or one equivalent NTP in a generic biochemical description. The phosphate group comes from inorganic phosphate, not from cleavage of a phosphate group already attached to succinyl-CoA. This direct phosphorylation does not depend on ATP synthase, although sustained TCA flux depends on the surrounding metabolic conditions. One can combine these observations in a conventional net equation written with NAD+, FAD and a generic NDP: acetyl-CoA + 3 NAD+ + FAD + NDP + Pi + 2 H2O → 2 CO2 + 3 NADH + 3 H+ + FADH2 + NTP + CoA-SH. Proton and water notation can vary with conventions for the ionic species. For a more mechanistic membrane account replace the FAD/FADH2 pair with Q/QH2 in the net succinate oxidation step. Do not count both.

Now include the reaction that precedes the cycle. One pyruvate generates one acetyl-CoA, one NADH and one carbon dioxide through PDC. Addition of the complete TCA account gives four NADH, one succinate-derived electron pair, one NTP and three carbon dioxide per pyruvate. The carbon balance is now three carbons entering and three leaving. Ten electrons have been transferred to carriers, corresponding to two and a half molecules of oxygen reduced if every pair is passed to oxygen. Fractional stoichiometric coefficients are entirely acceptable in molar accounting: five moles of oxygen can accept the electrons from two moles of pyruvate in this ideal model. This does not mean a single oxygen molecule can physically be divided in an individual enzyme event.

For one glucose, glycolysis in its conventional form supplies two pyruvates, two net ATP and two cytosolic NADH. Two PDC reactions generate two NADH and two carbon dioxide in mitochondria. Two completed TCA turns yield six additional matrix NADH, two succinate-derived electron pairs, two NTP and four carbon dioxide. Combining these idealized stages gives ten NADH in total, two succinate-derived electron pairs, four net directly generated nucleotide triphosphates when the NTPs are counted as ATP equivalents, and six carbon dioxide. Distinguish the eight matrix NADH generated by PDC and TCA from the two NADH generated outside the matrix in the assumed cytosolic glycolytic pathway. They may differ in how their electrons enter respiratory electron transport.

The arithmetic can be used without assigning a universal ATP yield. Suppose an exercise explicitly stipulates a conventional effective yield of 2.5 ATP per matrix NADH and 1.5 ATP per succinate-derived electron pair. For one acetyl-CoA, the calculation is three times 2.5 plus one times 1.5 plus one direct nucleotide triphosphate, giving ten ATP equivalents. For one pyruvate followed by a full cycle turn, the additional matrix NADH from PDC adds 2.5, giving 12.5 ATP equivalents. These are model-dependent estimates and not measurements of the yield of a plant leaf. If electrons enter through alternative routes or leave the respiratory chain through alternative oxidase, if proton leakage occurs or if transport uses part of the electrochemical gradient, less ATP may be conserved. The next three chapters explain these mechanisms. Here the important result is that the amounts of respiratory reducing equivalents generated can be calculated independently of the eventual coupling efficiency.

A useful examination check is to ask which products would still be generated if the cycle stopped after the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase reaction. Starting with acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate, citrate synthase and aconitase would have generated no reduced carriers. The two oxidative decarboxylations would have generated two NADH and two carbon dioxide. But no succinyl-CoA-dependent nucleotide triphosphate, succinate-derived electrons or malate-derived NADH would yet have appeared. The acceptor would not have been regenerated. A full-turn yield is therefore invalid for an explicitly interrupted sequence. The correct account must be constructed for the specified segment rather than recalled as a single memorized number.

8. Tracing individual carbon atoms with isotopes

Carbon conservation tells us the number of carbon atoms appearing in each product, but a tracer experiment asks where particular atoms travel. This distinction is especially valuable for understanding the citric acid cycle because the two carbon dioxide molecules released during the first turn do not originate from the newly introduced acetyl carbons. To reason about tracing, distinguish a labeled molecule from a labeled position within that molecule. Carbon-13 is a stable isotope of carbon with one additional neutron compared with carbon-12. Carbon-14 is radioactive and can also be used as a tracer in appropriate experimental contexts. Neither tracer adds a new carbon atom to the molecule. It marks one or more existing positions so that their eventual distribution can be measured.

Pyruvate is conventionally numbered from its carboxyl carbon. Carbon 1 is the carboxylate carbon, carbon 2 is the keto carbon and carbon 3 is the methyl carbon. In the PDC reaction the carboxyl carbon is removed as carbon dioxide. The keto carbon becomes the carbonyl carbon of the acetyl group and the methyl carbon remains its methyl carbon. Thus, if [1-13C]pyruvate is delivered to a mitochondrion and is oxidized exclusively by PDC, labeled carbon dioxide may appear immediately. If [2-13C]pyruvate is used instead, the label initially appears in the acetyl group rather than in the carbon dioxide produced directly by PDC. The same applies to [3-13C]pyruvate. The experiment distinguishes reactions by carbon position, not simply by the total carbon concentration in a sample.

Consider a preparation initially containing unlabeled oxaloacetate that receives acetyl-CoA labeled in both acetyl carbons. Citrate synthase produces citrate containing two labeled carbon atoms and four unlabeled carbon atoms. The two oxidative decarboxylation reactions in the first canonical turn release carbons derived from the original oxaloacetate skeleton. The newly introduced acetyl carbons remain in the four-carbon succinyl product. If the reaction sequence completes, regenerated oxaloacetate retains the two labeled carbons. There is a net loss of two carbon atoms from the system, but the immediate identity of the lost atoms differs from the identity of the incoming atoms. The net equation remains correct because it does not promise anything about atom-by-atom provenance.

Succinate is symmetric at the level of its molecular structure, with chemically equivalent carboxyl ends and equivalent internal methylene groups. Once the acetyl-derived label passes through the symmetric intermediate, the orientation of labeled atoms in subsequent metabolites cannot be described simply by saying that each carbon retains one unique numbered identity around the ring. Fumarase and the later reactions are stereospecific, but the symmetry of the succinate/fumarate stage introduces positional redistribution in tracer accounting. As labeled oxaloacetate combines with a new acetyl group, some previously introduced labels become eligible to leave during later decarboxylations. A realistic population of cycling molecules will therefore display a time-dependent distribution of labels rather than a synchronized release of both acetyl carbons in the next turn.

In an illustrative pulse–chase experiment, a brief pulse of labeled substrate is supplied and then replaced by unlabeled substrate. A sample taken immediately after the pulse may show enrichment in early intermediates. A later sample may show enrichment in downstream intermediates, biosynthetic products or carbon dioxide. The unlabeled chase dilutes the incoming label and helps reveal the turnover of pools. If a metabolite retains enrichment over a long interval, that can result from slow turnover, exchange with a stored pool or continued recycling from another labeled compound. It is not sufficient evidence by itself that its associated enzyme has been inhibited. Similarly, a rapid decline in enrichment can result from mixing with unlabeled molecules without any decrease in the total concentration of the metabolite.

Mass spectrometry can distinguish molecules containing different numbers of carbon-13 atoms. Nuclear magnetic resonance can provide information about labeling positions in suitable experiments. Measuring the carbon isotopic composition of released carbon dioxide can reveal which parts of a supplied substrate contribute to decarboxylation. Each measurement answers a different question. If mass spectrometry detects citrate with two labeled carbons, that is compatible with entry of a doubly labeled acetyl group into unlabeled oxaloacetate, but it is not unique proof of that route after extensive labeling and recycling. A four-carbon intermediate could also be partially labeled, and mixing of labeled pools can produce the same total isotopologue mass.

Plant experiments complicate the interpretation because labeled substrates may be metabolized in more than one compartment. In a photosynthetically active leaf, labeled carbon dioxide released by a mitochondrial reaction may be fixed again by chloroplastic Rubisco. A label that eventually appears in sugar is not necessarily evidence of a direct reverse reaction from acetyl-CoA to glucose. The label may have traveled through carbon dioxide and subsequent photosynthetic assimilation. Experimental interpretation requires considering these recycling possibilities. The isotope studies of illuminated leaves described later are particularly valuable because they address the partitioning of carbon through several interacting metabolic routes, rather than assuming the textbook closed cycle is the only route available.

A sound Part C strategy is to identify the explicitly labeled position, write the first reaction that transforms the supplied molecule, and ask whether that labeled atom is lost, retained or transferred. Then consider the next reaction and any symmetric intermediate. If a question asks for labeled carbon dioxide in the first instant after a short pulse, distinguish direct PDC decarboxylation from later TCA turnover. If the question concerns long-term steady-state labeling, acknowledge that multiple turns and exchange reactions may obscure the original position. Avoid imposing an ideal first-turn result on a leaf that has been supplied with label for several hours.

9. Carbon skeletons, anaplerosis and nitrogen assimilation

The TCA cycle is amphibolic: it participates both in catabolic oxidation and in the supply of precursors for anabolic reactions. This dual function is particularly visible in plants because carbon fixation, amino acid production and respiratory metabolism must be coordinated. Carbon skeletons are the carbon frameworks onto which other chemical groups, including nitrogen-containing groups, are added. The five-carbon skeleton of 2-oxoglutarate is well suited to the production of glutamate, and oxaloacetate can accept an amino group to form the four-carbon amino acid aspartate. Removing these intermediates to make amino acids is useful to the plant, but it changes the amount of carbon remaining in the cycle. Such withdrawal is often called cataplerosis.

Anaplerosis is the complementary replenishment of cycle intermediates. The term does not mean that every reaction adding carbon to the cell is anaplerotic. A reaction is anaplerotic in the relevant accounting system when it increases the supply of intermediates that can replenish the cycle. Acetyl-CoA enters through citrate synthase, but under the conventional closed-turn scheme it does not provide a net gain in four-carbon acceptor because its input of two carbons is balanced by two decarboxylations. In contrast, formation of a four-carbon compound from a three-carbon substrate and bicarbonate can add a net intermediate to the system. This is why simply supplying extra acetyl-CoA may not overcome depletion of oxaloacetate.

In many plant tissues, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) plays an important anaplerotic role. The enzyme combines phosphoenolpyruvate with bicarbonate to produce oxaloacetate and inorganic phosphate. Phosphoenolpyruvate contains three carbons, while bicarbonate provides the fourth. The phosphate associated with phosphoenolpyruvate is released rather than being used to phosphorylate ADP in the PEPC reaction. This route differs from the pyruvate kinase reaction, which converts phosphoenolpyruvate into pyruvate and forms ATP. Competition between these pathways therefore involves both carbon allocation and energetic consequences.

Oxaloacetate formed in the cytosol can be reduced to malate by malate dehydrogenase, consuming NADH, and malate can participate in mitochondrial metabolism through selective transport. If imported malate is converted into oxaloacetate in the matrix, the newly formed four-carbon substrate can help replenish the acceptor pool. However, if that malate is instead oxidatively decarboxylated by malic enzyme and the resulting pyruvate is oxidized by PDC, the route has a different carbon outcome. The presence of malate import alone does not prove that the net effect is anaplerotic. A student must trace the fate of malate after entry. The pathway network includes both possibilities.

The central role of 2-oxoglutarate in nitrogen metabolism can be understood through the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase system. Glutamine synthetase incorporates ammonium into glutamate using ATP, forming glutamine. Glutamate synthase then uses glutamine and 2-oxoglutarate together with reducing power to produce two glutamate molecules. One glutamate can be viewed as regenerating the glutamate initially used by glutamine synthetase, and the other supplies newly assimilated nitrogen in a carbon skeleton. The net system therefore requires ammonium, a 2-oxoglutarate carbon skeleton, ATP and reducing equivalents. This explains why a leaf with plentiful nitrogen but restricted carbon metabolism may be unable to incorporate all available nitrogen into amino acids at a high rate. The detailed reactions and their cellular locations belong to the nitrogen metabolism subunit.

Aspartate formation similarly connects oxaloacetate to amino acid metabolism. Aspartate aminotransferase can transfer an amino group from glutamate to oxaloacetate, forming aspartate and 2-oxoglutarate. This is a reversible transamination and does not simply remove nitrogen from the system. It moves nitrogen from one carbon skeleton to another. Depending on the direction of net flux, it may supply aspartate for biosynthesis or contribute to the interconversion of oxaloacetate and related pools. Aspartate is a precursor for additional amino acids in plants. If a developing organ increases demand for those amino acids, the metabolic pressure on the oxaloacetate pool can change.

Citrate export provides another example of cataplerotic carbon movement. Citrate generated in the matrix may be transported to the cytosol and used in pathways that provide acetyl units or regenerate other intermediates. Cytosolic ATP citrate lyase can cleave citrate into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate at an ATP cost. The acetyl-CoA so produced can contribute to cytosolic biosynthetic processes. This is different from moving acetyl-CoA itself freely across the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is also distinct from plastid fatty acid synthesis, for which plastid pyruvate dehydrogenase is an important acetyl-CoA source. Correctly identifying compartment-specific acetyl-CoA is important in questions about whether citrate export supports a particular biosynthetic pathway.

Plant organic acid pools also have roles beyond central energy metabolism. Malate and citrate can contribute to osmotic balance, charge balance and pH-related processes, and their accumulation in vacuoles may be substantial in some tissues. Organic acids can participate in mineral nutrient interactions. Such pools need not be in instantaneous equilibrium with matrix metabolites. A whole-tissue measurement of malate may therefore largely reflect a storage or transport pool rather than the substrate concentration experienced by matrix malate dehydrogenase. For a biochemical inference about TCA flux, distinguish the compartment where the reaction occurs from the compartment dominating the measured concentration.

A simple mass-balance model clarifies the relation between withdrawal and replenishment. Let P denote the total amount of a defined set of cycle intermediates, measured in moles of intermediate molecules rather than carbon atoms. Let Jadd be the rate at which replenishing reactions add molecules to this set and Jwithdraw be the rate at which cataplerotic pathways remove molecules from it. If internal cycle conversions preserve the number of molecules belonging to the defined set, the rate of pool change is dP/dt = Jadd − Jwithdraw. In a steady pool, Jadd equals Jwithdraw even if both fluxes are large. If withdrawal occurs at 7 micromoles per gram per hour and replenishment at 5 in a hypothetical constant-rate interval, the pool decreases by 2 micromoles per gram per hour. In three hours the decrease would be 6 micromoles per gram, provided the pool remains positive and the rates do not change. The assumption of constant rates is a simplifying feature of the example; real enzymes respond as substrate concentrations change.

It is possible for the cycle's pool size to remain steady while respiration slows, or for the pool to increase while respiration accelerates. The pool equation contains the difference between net addition and withdrawal, not the rate of every internal conversion. Consequently, observing that a plant mutant has twice the concentration of citrate does not establish that citrate synthase flux has doubled. The mutation could impede citrate consumption, increase transport into a storage compartment or alter the balance of competing routes. The correct approach is to combine metabolite measurements with enzyme activity, labeling, respiratory gas exchange and physiological context. This is the bridge from a memorized reaction diagram to the interpretation of real plant metabolic data.

10. Regulation of carbon oxidation by energy and redox demand

The citric acid cycle is usually drawn as eight consecutive arrows of similar size. This visual convention is useful for remembering reaction order, but it hides the control exerted by substrates, products, cofactor regeneration and the demand for intermediates. In an isolated teaching model, all eight reactions have the same steady-state molar flux if no intermediates are added or removed. In a real plant, branches make this equality conditional. A regulator that affects one reaction may change the relative fluxes through several branches without suppressing every reaction equally. Understanding regulation requires first distinguishing enzyme capacity from the actual rate of a reaction in a cell.

Enzyme capacity is the maximum activity measured under a defined set of assay conditions, often with substrates and cofactors supplied at favorable concentrations. The in vivo flux is the rate at which the reaction proceeds in its normal cellular environment. A plant with abundant malate dehydrogenase may nevertheless show little net oxidation of malate if oxaloacetate is not consumed or if the matrix NADH/NAD+ ratio is high. Conversely, increasing the activity of an enzyme that already has excess capacity may produce almost no change in the pathway's overall flux. Control is distributed across a network, including transport and downstream product consumption. The classical enzymes described as irreversible or regulatory are important starting points for reasoning but should not be treated as the only possible controllers.

Citrate synthase requires both oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA. If the supply of either substrate falls substantially, the rate of condensation can decrease. This makes the reaction sensitive to upstream glycolysis and PDC as well as to oxaloacetate regeneration and replenishment. A tissue can possess active citrate synthase but be unable to sustain the usual cycle rate if carbon skeletons are withdrawn faster than they are replaced. High citrate accumulation can alter the balance of transport and enzyme activities, yet measuring citrate in a whole leaf does not reveal the exact product concentration in the matrix. In an examination model that specifies only substrate limitation, reason from the explicitly limited substrate rather than imposing an unrelated regulatory effect.

NAD-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase supply NADH and remove carbon dioxide. Their rates depend on both their carbon substrates and access to oxidized NAD+. High reduced-carrier levels often constrain dehydrogenation because the capacity to accept additional electrons is reduced. The balance of free and acylated coenzyme A also matters for PDC and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase. The same cofactor molecule may participate in successive reactions, so a shortage can influence several points in the network. This relationship makes respiratory control particularly sensitive to downstream electron transport, even if the electron-transfer complexes and dehydrogenases are not part of the same soluble enzyme assembly.

The NAD redox ratio is informative because it expresses the balance between reduced and oxidized carrier. For a fixed total pool of NADH plus NAD+, increasing the fraction present as NADH necessarily decreases the fraction present as NAD+. Suppose a simplified matrix contains a total of 100 arbitrary units of NAD carrier. With 20 units of NADH and 80 of NAD+, the ratio NADH/NAD+ is 0.25. If the distribution becomes 80 NADH and 20 NAD+, the ratio becomes 4. This sixteen-fold increase is caused by redistribution within a fixed total pool. It illustrates why a change in redox state need not require any change in total NAD synthesis. Such a redistribution can greatly alter the driving force for NAD-linked reactions. Real measurements must account for free and protein-bound pools and for the compartment from which the signal originates.

Thermodynamics provides a quantitative explanation. For the malate dehydrogenase reaction, malate + NAD+ ⇌ oxaloacetate + NADH + H+, the biochemical transformed reaction quotient at fixed pH can be written approximately as Q = ([oxaloacetate][NADH])/([malate][NAD+]), using activity ratios approximated by concentration ratios. The actual transformed Gibbs energy is ΔG′ = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q. R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature in kelvin, and ln the natural logarithm. The sign of ΔG′ determines the thermodynamic tendency under the specified conditions, not the reaction speed. Decreasing oxaloacetate or increasing the availability of NAD+ reduces Q and makes malate oxidation more favorable. The reverse direction may be favored if oxaloacetate and NADH accumulate.

For an illustrative calculation, use ΔG°′ = +29.7 kJ mol−1, T = 298 K, and R = 0.008314 kJ mol−1 K−1. If the dimensionless quotient is 10−6, RT ln Q is approximately 2.48 × (−13.82) = −34.3 kJ mol−1. The actual transformed Gibbs energy is therefore approximately −4.6 kJ mol−1. The forward oxidation is thermodynamically favorable under these hypothetical conditions despite its positive standard value. If Q instead rises to 10−3, the correction is approximately −17.1 kJ mol−1 and the net value is +12.6 kJ mol−1. The point is not that either quotient is a measured plant matrix condition; it is that metabolite ratios can change a reaction's thermodynamic direction. A thermodynamically favorable reaction can still have a low flux if enzyme capacity or substrate delivery is limited.

Energy demand also affects respiratory carbon metabolism. ATP consumption by cellular processes generates ADP and inorganic phosphate, which are substrates for oxidative phosphorylation. In a tightly coupled mitochondrion, an increase in ADP can increase ATP synthase turnover and allow the respiratory chain to oxidize NADH more rapidly. More NAD+ becomes available for dehydrogenation. The connection runs from ATP consumption to electron transfer to cofactor regeneration and then to upstream carbon oxidation. The response is not necessarily immediate or proportional in all tissues because additional constraints include oxygen supply, substrate transport and the presence of alternative respiratory pathways. It nevertheless explains why the TCA cycle may respond to demand even when the concentration of glucose outside the mitochondrion has not changed.

Adenylate energy charge provides one way of summarizing the distribution of adenine nucleotides. It is defined as ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). The numerator gives ATP a full weight, ADP a half weight and AMP no weight. The ratio is dimensionless, ranging from zero when all adenylates are AMP to one when all are ATP. In a hypothetical compartment with ATP, ADP and AMP concentrations of 6, 3 and 1 millimolar, respectively, the energy charge is (6 + 1.5)/10 = 0.75. If the distribution changes to 3, 5 and 2 millimolar, it becomes (3 + 2.5)/10 = 0.55. These values are illustrative rather than typical measurements. Energy charge is not identical to ATP concentration or ATP/ADP ratio and cannot alone describe the energy state of every organelle. It is useful for questions that supply all three nucleotide concentrations and ask for an integrated measure.

An increase in oxygen supply can relieve respiratory limitation when oxygen was previously restricting terminal electron transfer. If oxygen was already sufficient, adding more may have little effect on carbon oxidation. This is the difference between a necessary substrate and a limiting substrate. A necessary substrate participates in a process, whereas a limiting substrate is present at a level where increasing it raises the rate under otherwise specified conditions. In roots growing in waterlogged soil, oxygen diffusion to cells can be restricted, whereas illuminated leaves exposed to air often encounter other limitations. In both cases the mitochondrial matrix reacts to the consequences of oxygen availability through the regeneration of NAD+ and the redox state of the membrane electron carriers.

A plant-specific regulatory feature is the reversible phosphorylation of mitochondrial PDC. Photorespiratory metabolism can modify the mitochondrial environment, including production of NADH and ammonium, which can influence PDC activity and its kinase-dependent regulation. In an illuminated C3 leaf, this contributes to the coordination of pyruvate oxidation with other mitochondrial reactions. Do not confuse this regulation with a direct photochemical reaction in PDC. PDC does not capture light; illumination changes the metabolites, signals and cellular demands that regulate the enzyme. Nor should the mechanism be generalized as a complete stop of PDC in every plant tissue exposed to light. The balance differs with leaf species, carbon dioxide, oxygen and physiological state.

11. Why a cycle can become a branched network in an illuminated leaf

In the dark, a leaf must obtain most of its immediately usable energy from nonphotosynthetic metabolism of organic substrates. During illumination, chloroplasts generate ATP and NADPH for carbon fixation, and the pattern of carbon export from chloroplasts changes. The mitochondrial respiratory network responds to these altered conditions. In particular, the rate at which organic carbon is released as carbon dioxide through the TCA cycle may decline, while mitochondrial functions such as reductant exchange and supply of carbon skeletons continue. The existence of a functional TCA enzyme sequence does not require every enzyme to carry the same net flux in the light.

Consider a simplified dark-leaf model in which an acetyl unit proceeds through citrate formation, both oxidative decarboxylations and regeneration of oxaloacetate. If no substantial intermediates are withdrawn, the familiar full-cycle accounting is a reasonable starting model. Now consider a photosynthesizing leaf supplied with plenty of carbon and with a demand for nitrogen assimilation. Citrate or 2-oxoglutarate can be directed toward biosynthetic routes, while imported malate can contribute to oxaloacetate formation and exchange of reducing equivalents. Carbon entering the first part of the cycle may not necessarily pass through the second oxidative decarboxylation. The route is therefore an open network, with different rates in different segments. It may still contain cyclic flux in some cells or conditions, but an equal flux through every step is no longer guaranteed.

The phrase noncyclic TCA flux should be read carefully. It does not mean that the eight enzymes have all disappeared, that the reaction sequence cannot operate, or that the entire cycle universally runs in reverse in illuminated tissue. A network can have one segment operating in the conventional direction, another with reversible reactions responding to local concentration gradients, and net input or output at several nodes. In particular, reversible malate and fumarate interconversion does not prove that the strongly favorable citrate synthase reaction reverses to generate acetyl-CoA. The reaction directions in an open network depend on each enzyme and the surrounding concentrations. A question using the words noncyclic and reversed should not be answered until those meanings have been separated.

Mitochondrial metabolism in the light is closely linked to photorespiration. Rubisco can catalyze oxygenation of ribulose bisphosphate in addition to carboxylation. The resulting carbon salvage pathway spans chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria. In the mitochondrial portion of that pathway, conversion of two glycine molecules into one serine involves release of carbon dioxide and ammonium and generation of NADH. This reaction supplies mitochondrial reducing power even when pyruvate-derived carbon oxidation is reduced. The detailed sequence of photorespiration will be treated in Node 6.B.05. For the present chapter, note that the origin of mitochondrial NADH and carbon dioxide is not always the TCA cycle. The same measured respiratory electron transport can be supported by different upstream reactions.

The reducing equivalents generated by photorespiratory glycine oxidation can be used by the mitochondrial respiratory chain or redistributed through linked metabolite exchange. A high rate of glycine oxidation changes the matrix NAD redox balance and can influence the use of other substrates. This means that suppression of conventional TCA decarboxylation does not imply a corresponding suppression of all mitochondrial oxygen consumption. Electron transport receives reducing equivalents from several pathways. If one source decreases while another increases, the total oxygen consumption may change little, or it may change in a direction different from that predicted from the TCA flux alone. The same principle applies when alternate respiratory substrates contribute to electron flow during nutrient stress or sugar depletion.

One useful way to think about malate and citrate exchange is to separate carbon transport from reductant transport. NADH cannot generally pass directly through the inner mitochondrial membrane as a freely diffusing small metabolite. A redox reaction in one compartment may instead convert oxaloacetate into malate using NADH. After malate moves to another compartment and is oxidized to oxaloacetate, NAD+ is reduced there. The pair of reactions can transfer reducing equivalents indirectly, provided the carbon intermediates or their equivalents are exchanged in a way that sustains the process. The direction of net transfer depends on metabolite gradients, redox state and transport availability. A malate-based exchange process should not be described as the physical movement of an intact NADH molecule through the membrane.

Citrate export also connects compartments and can divert carbon from immediate oxidation toward biosynthetic needs. An illuminated leaf may have demand for organic acid-derived precursors even while its chloroplast is assimilating carbon dioxide. The chloroplast and mitochondrion consequently participate in an integrated carbon network. The existence of a citrate output from the mitochondrion is consistent with a relatively active citrate-producing segment and a less active downstream decarboxylating segment. However, citrate concentration alone cannot distinguish increased synthesis from decreased utilization. Measuring the export flux, tracing labeled carbon or comparing changes in several intermediates provides stronger evidence than a single metabolite measurement.

A simplified quantitative branch model demonstrates why equal enzyme fluxes cannot be assumed. Imagine that the citrate-forming segment supplies 12 micromoles of citrate equivalents per gram per hour. Of these, 5 are exported before conversion to 2-oxoglutarate. The remaining 7 proceed through isocitrate dehydrogenase, so this reaction generates 7 micromoles each of NADH and carbon dioxide per gram per hour. If 3 of those 7 micromoles of 2-oxoglutarate are withdrawn for biosynthesis and the remaining 4 are oxidized by 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase, that second reaction generates only 4 micromoles each of NADH and carbon dioxide. Assume steady intermediate pools, no other branch in the modeled segment and one product per reaction event. The two oxidative decarboxylations together generate 11 micromoles of carbon dioxide and 11 micromoles of NADH, not 24. Multiplying citrate synthesis by two would be wrong in this open network.

This model does not specify how the oxaloacetate pool is maintained, so it cannot by itself describe a complete steady-state mitochondrial carbon balance. Additional anaplerotic input or return of exported carbon would be required to account for the withdrawn intermediates over time. That limitation is pedagogically important: a local branch balance can be solved with the stated information, while a whole-system balance requires all relevant inputs and outputs. In a Part C question, identify whether a numerical problem describes a complete cycle, a pathway segment or an entire organ. The mathematical assumptions for one scope cannot automatically be used in another.

Light–dark transitions also provide a natural experimental perturbation. When a leaf is abruptly darkened, photosynthetic carbon fixation and photochemical generation of ATP and NADPH decline, but metabolites and enzyme regulatory states take time to adjust. Carbon dioxide efflux measured immediately after darkening can contain transient contributions that differ from a later steady dark rate. Conversely, the onset of illumination changes carbon dioxide fixation, photorespiration and mitochondrial metabolism at overlapping times. A comparison of instantaneous gas exchange before and after a light switch is therefore not a direct measurement of just the change in TCA activity. The time chosen for sampling matters.

Temperature and carbon dioxide availability further influence the pattern of leaf metabolism. Under conditions favoring Rubisco oxygenation, the photorespiratory pathway contributes more strongly to mitochondrial metabolism than under conditions suppressing oxygenation, all else being equal. Water stress can close stomata and alter internal carbon dioxide, while also changing growth and energy demand. These connected effects mean that mitochondrial respiration in the light cannot be predicted from light intensity alone. Students should recognize the direction of a particular controlled effect but avoid universal claims about net respiration in an intact plant subjected to a complex environmental change.

The distinction between net carbon assimilation and respiratory carbon oxidation is fundamental. Net carbon uptake from the air can be positive even though respiratory decarboxylation is occurring. A green leaf can use part of its newly fixed carbon to produce ATP and intermediates needed for continued growth. Respiratory carbon loss is therefore not simply wasted photosynthesis. It represents a cost of metabolism that supports processes necessary for the plant. The physiological question is how respiration, carbon allocation and nitrogen assimilation are balanced, not whether respiration can be eliminated while maintaining normal cellular function.

12. Evidence for plant respiratory flexibility

The canonical enzyme pathway was established through biochemical investigation of organic acid transformations, but demonstrating that a particular sequence is possible is not the same as determining how rapidly it operates in a living leaf. To make that distinction, plant physiologists use complementary kinds of evidence. Enzyme assays reveal catalytic capacity; mitochondrial preparations reveal substrate-dependent respiratory behavior under controlled conditions; tracer studies reveal carbon movement; and whole-tissue measurements place the reactions in their physiological environment. Each approach has strengths and limitations. An interpretation supported by several kinds of evidence is generally stronger than one based on a single measurement.

The work of Tcherkez and colleagues on respiratory metabolism of illuminated leaves is an instructive example. In a study published in Plant Physiology in 2005, the investigators supplied French bean leaves with glucose or pyruvate labeled at particular carbon positions and examined labeled products, including respired carbon dioxide. In a later study published in 2009, isotope-based analysis addressed the distribution of respiratory flux in illuminated leaves and supported the possibility of noncyclic operation under the studied conditions. The value of this approach is that different reactions remove different carbon positions. By examining the fate of the label, the researchers could obtain information unavailable from a measurement of total carbon dioxide exchange alone.

The experimental question can be restated in simpler terms. If pyruvate dehydrogenation and a fully cycling TCA pathway were both operating strongly, a tracer supplied in the carboxyl carbon of pyruvate would enter carbon dioxide rapidly through PDC, whereas tracers retained in the acetyl group would contribute through subsequent metabolism and later turns. If the decarboxylating portion of the TCA network is constrained in the light while PDC retains some activity, the labeling patterns can differ. The experiment compares those patterns with the predictions of alternative metabolic arrangements. Importantly, weak labeling in a particular product does not prove the complete absence of the relevant reaction. Dilution by unlabeled carbon, uptake differences and alternative pathways can all influence enrichment.

A strong tracer design would compare the same labeled substrate under light and dark conditions, account for uptake and initial enrichment, and measure more than one product. Unlabeled controls reveal background isotope abundance and help distinguish tracer signal from ordinary carbon dioxide production. Time-matched sampling limits confounding by changes in leaf physiology during the experiment. Measurements of photosynthetic carbon fixation and respiration are useful because label released as carbon dioxide may be recaptured. These are principles for interpreting the experiment, not a detailed specialist laboratory protocol. The published studies used isotope measurements in conjunction with metabolic analysis; their conclusions should not be transformed into an unsupported claim that every plant exhibits an identical light-dependent flux distribution.

A separate line of evidence comes from experiments on mitochondrial substrate supply in Arabidopsis. Studies discussed in the review literature and in work on distinct matrix pyruvate pools support the importance of mitochondrial pyruvate import as well as internally generated pyruvate from malate and alanine metabolism. The underlying reasoning is that an impairment in a carrier need not have the same physiological effect as a direct impairment of PDC. Removing the carrier reduces one supply route; removing PDC restricts use of pyruvate arriving from several routes. Even if externally added pyruvate is poorly oxidized by a mutant mitochondrion, oxidation of a substrate that generates pyruvate inside the matrix may remain possible. The comparison of substrates is informative because they share some downstream reactions while differing at the entry step.

To appreciate the logic of genetic evidence, imagine a mutant plant with reduced activity of the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier. An isolated mitochondrial preparation supplied with pyruvate displays lower oxygen consumption than the corresponding wild-type preparation, while succinate-supported oxygen consumption is comparatively preserved. If the organelles are equally intact and other conditions are matched, the pattern is compatible with a defect upstream of succinate dehydrogenase and downstream of extracellular pyruvate delivery. It does not uniquely prove a transport defect because PDC impairment could also selectively affect pyruvate-supported respiration. Direct transport measurements, PDC activity assays or restoration of the carrier by genetic complementation can distinguish the explanations. The reasoning illustrates why multiple experiments are necessary before assigning a mechanistic cause to a respiratory phenotype.

In another hypothetical comparison, a succinate dehydrogenase-deficient plant might display reduced oxidation of succinate to fumarate and accumulation of succinate under conditions where the upstream pathway continues to supply it. The expected accumulation is conditional on sustained input and absence of effective diversion or transport. If succinate production also falls, whole-tissue succinate need not increase. A reduction in succinate-supported oxygen consumption is compatible with loss of Complex II activity, but a downstream electron-transfer lesion could also reduce that oxygen consumption. Comparing NADH-generating substrates with succinate and measuring the relevant enzyme activity can help localize the defect. This is a model for interpreting pathway-block experiments without confusing an expected metabolic consequence with a universal observation.

Research on the regulation of TCA metabolism in photosynthetic and heterotrophic plant tissues also indicates that control of respiratory flux is context-dependent. Altering the capacity of an individual TCA enzyme may have different effects in an illuminated leaf and in a nonphotosynthetic storage organ. The key lesson for an exam is not to memorize a single rate-limiting enzyme for all plant respiration. Rather, distinguish a potentially important regulatory reaction from the actual location of flux control in a specified physiological system. An experiment that measures a change in overall respiratory rate after an enzyme perturbation provides evidence about that system and those conditions. Generalization requires additional comparisons across tissues and environments.

13. Respiration in growing roots, germinating seeds and oxygen-limited tissues

A useful test of plant-specific respiratory understanding is to compare a growing root with a mature illuminated leaf. Both contain mitochondria, but they encounter different carbon inputs and different metabolic demands. The root generally relies on imported or locally stored organic carbon rather than on photochemical carbon fixation. Growing root cells invest ATP in ion transport, macromolecular synthesis, cytoskeletal organization and maintenance of concentration gradients. Carbon skeletons are also required for amino acid synthesis and the construction of new tissue. A high respiratory rate in a growing root can therefore reflect the energetic cost of growth rather than unusually inefficient metabolism. If growth stops during nutrient deprivation, respiratory demand may change even before the supply of sugar is exhausted.

The effects of nutrient assimilation are especially informative. Nitrogen acquisition does not simply increase the plant's mass without affecting carbon metabolism. Ions must be transported, nitrogen may undergo chemical reduction, and amino groups must be incorporated into organic carbon skeletons. A root receiving nitrogen may consequently require additional respiratory energy and intermediates such as 2-oxoglutarate. The direction and magnitude of the change in oxygen consumption are not determined by nitrogen concentration alone; they depend on the availability of carbon, the chemical form of nitrogen and the plant's regulatory response. If a student is asked why adding nitrate might increase demand for respiratory metabolism, the appropriate explanation is that transport and assimilation consume energy and reducing power, not that nitrate directly substitutes for acetyl-CoA in citrate synthase.

Germinating seeds provide another contrast. Before emergence and effective photosynthesis, their growth depends on mobilization of reserves. In a starch-rich seed, mobilized carbohydrates can enter glycolysis and supply pyruvate. In a lipid-rich seed, fatty acids may undergo beta oxidation in specialized peroxisomal compartments. The resulting acetyl units can be used in the glyoxylate pathway, which allows conservation of carbon in four-carbon products rather than obligatory loss of two carbon dioxide molecules through the two ordinary TCA decarboxylations. Isocitrate lyase cleaves isocitrate into succinate and glyoxylate; malate synthase combines glyoxylate with an additional acetyl-CoA to form malate. These two characteristic reactions explain the carbon-saving property of the pathway.

The glyoxylate cycle is not a mitochondrial reversal of the citric acid cycle. Its characteristic enzymes bypass the ordinary oxidative decarboxylations, and its cellular organization involves peroxisomal and other compartments in germinating plants. The resulting organic acid intermediates can interact with mitochondrial oxidation and with pathways of carbohydrate synthesis. The important distinction for the present chapter is between oxidizing acetyl carbon for energy and conserving acetyl carbon for construction. A germinating oil seed must do both. If every acetyl unit were oxidized completely through the conventional TCA cycle, its carbon could not simultaneously be retained in a net carbohydrate product via that same closed cycle. If carbon were conserved but insufficient energy were made available, seedling development would also be constrained.

The concept of respiratory quotient provides a simple way to relate the nature of a respiratory substrate to gas exchange. Respiratory quotient, RQ, is the molar ratio of carbon dioxide released to oxygen consumed. For complete glucose oxidation in a system where these gases reflect only respiration, six moles of CO2 are released for six moles of O2 consumed, so RQ is one. A more reduced substrate may require more oxygen per carbon dioxide produced. For example, the ideal complete oxidation of palmitic acid can be written as C16H32O2 + 23 O2 → 16 CO2 + 16 H2O; RQ is 16/23, approximately 0.70. This calculation describes complete oxidation of the specified compound, not a claim that mitochondrial beta oxidation of fatty acid is the usual initial step in plants.

A measured gas-exchange ratio from a living plant may deviate from these ideal substrate values. Carbon dioxide can be retained in organic acids, reassimilated by photosynthesis, released by photorespiration or formed by decarboxylation pathways that do not consume oxygen directly. The plant may simultaneously oxidize mixed substrates and divert intermediates into biosynthesis. During fermentation, carbon dioxide can be released without the corresponding oxygen consumption associated with aerobic oxidation. The denominator may become very small, making the ratio difficult to interpret. Respiratory quotient is consequently most informative when the experimental conditions constrain the relevant processes, rather than as an unqualified test for which substrate a whole illuminated plant is oxidizing.

Oxygen availability can become a primary constraint on respiration when roots are surrounded by waterlogged soil. Oxygen diffuses much more slowly through water than through air, and oxygen can also be consumed by microbes in the root environment. If delivery cannot meet consumption, the tissue becomes hypoxic. Mitochondrial electron transport through oxygen-reducing terminal pathways is restricted, and reoxidation of NADH becomes more difficult. The supply of matrix NAD+ decreases relative to NADH, making sustained PDC and TCA dehydrogenation difficult. This mechanism explains the decline in aerobic oxidation without incorrectly identifying oxygen as a direct reactant of citrate synthase or malate dehydrogenase.

Cytosolic glycolysis can continue for some time under restricted oxygen supply if NAD+ is regenerated through other reactions. In plant alcoholic fermentation, pyruvate decarboxylase converts pyruvate to acetaldehyde and carbon dioxide, and alcohol dehydrogenase reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol using NADH. The latter reaction returns NAD+ to the cytosolic pool so glycolysis can proceed. The fermentation reactions themselves do not add the large ATP yield characteristic of oxidative phosphorylation. The energy benefit is the ability to maintain some glycolytic ATP production. Plant cells may also show lactate production under some conditions, and the balance of products varies with the tissue and duration of oxygen limitation. Neither route should be mistaken for operation of the normal closed mitochondrial TCA cycle without oxygen.

When aeration returns after a hypoxic episode, the respiratory system may resume NADH oxidation and increase the potential for carbon oxidation. Recovery is not guaranteed to be instantaneous because substrates, enzymes, membranes and cellular homeostasis may have changed during the stress period. Different plant species and organs tolerate oxygen limitation differently. Adaptations such as air-filled internal tissue spaces can help improve oxygen delivery in some plants, but these adaptations do not change the requirement for an electron acceptor in ordinary aerobic mitochondrial respiration. The physiological response to oxygen stress belongs in greater detail to the stress physiology subunit. The essential concept here is how environmental oxygen delivery becomes a biochemical constraint through respiratory redox recycling.

14. Solving integrated respiratory experiments

Examination problems frequently combine a pathway diagram with measurements rather than asking for a single reaction name. A systematic approach begins with the preparation being studied. An isolated mitochondrial preparation can receive selected substrates, whereas an intact leaf has multiple compartments and gas-exchange processes. In a purified enzyme assay, the investigator may supply NAD+ and a substrate directly to an enzyme. Each preparation answers a different question. Failure to identify the preparation can lead to an incorrect inference about membrane transport, regulation or substrate access.

Consider an illustrative dataset in which three isolated mitochondrial preparations receive pyruvate plus a source of oxaloacetate, or receive succinate, under matched oxygen and ADP availability. Wild type mitochondria oxidize oxygen at 80 arbitrary rate units with the pyruvate-based treatment and 65 with succinate. Mutant A gives 20 and 62, respectively. Mutant B gives 75 and 15. The rates are hypothetical and are intended solely to demonstrate diagnostic reasoning. In mutant A, the strong reduction with pyruvate but relative preservation with succinate suggests a lesion in pyruvate uptake, pyruvate oxidation or another part of the NAD-linked substrate route. In mutant B, selective reduction with succinate suggests impairment of succinate utilization or the route of electron entry through Complex II. Neither pattern alone uniquely identifies an enzyme. A direct activity assay, transport measurement or genetic rescue is needed to discriminate between alternatives.

The statement that succinate oxidation is comparatively preserved in mutant A does not prove that oxidative phosphorylation is normal in the intact plant. The measured variable is oxygen consumption under one supplied-substrate condition. Alternative electron-transfer pathways and proton coupling can influence ATP production independently of oxygen uptake. ATP formation would need to be measured, or inferred under well-defined assumptions about coupling. This is a common examination trap: two preparations can exhibit similar oxygen consumption but conserve different amounts of ATP. The chapter on alternative oxidase will develop the underlying reason in more detail.

Now consider the effect of reduced malate dehydrogenase activity in a simplified closed-cycle model. Immediately after inhibition, malate oxidation and oxaloacetate regeneration can decline. If upstream fumarase continues to generate malate, the malate pool may increase. As oxaloacetate becomes limiting, citrate formation can slow. The eventual effect on upstream and downstream pools depends on the availability of anaplerotic input and alternative routes. In a real plant, imported or cytosol-derived organic acids and compartment-specific isozymes can modify this simple expectation. A prediction about metabolite accumulation is strongest when the problem states that other inputs, outputs and enzyme activities are initially unchanged.

A related question may provide results from a spectrophotometric assay. NADH absorbs ultraviolet light near 340 nm more strongly than NAD+. In a reaction that reduces NAD+ to NADH, an increase in absorbance at this wavelength can monitor NADH formation when interfering absorbers are controlled. The Beer–Lambert law is A = εlc, where absorbance A is dimensionless, ε is the molar absorption coefficient, l is optical path length, and c is concentration. If a hypothetical NAD-linked enzyme assay has a path length of 1 cm, ε = 6.22 mM−1 cm−1 and an absorbance increase of 0.1244 per minute, the NADH formation rate is 0.1244/(6.22 × 1) = 0.0200 millimolar per minute. In a reaction volume of 2 millilitres, this corresponds to 0.0400 micromoles per minute. If the preparation contains 0.10 mg protein, the specific activity is 0.400 micromoles per minute per mg protein.

This assay measures the rate of net NADH accumulation, not automatically the in vivo rate of TCA turnover. A crude extract may contain multiple enzymes that produce or consume NADH. To attribute the signal to one reaction, the assay should control substrate identity, cofactor availability, blank absorbance and interfering reactions. For example, omission of the specific organic acid substrate provides an important control for substrate-independent changes in absorbance. If NADH is simultaneously oxidized by another enzyme, net accumulation can underestimate its production rate. The distinction between gross production and net accumulation resembles the distinction between respiration and net gas exchange at the leaf level. Both require clear accounting of simultaneous opposing processes.

An intact leaf experiment presents a different challenge. Suppose net assimilation is 15 micromoles CO2 per square metre per second and an independently estimated nonphotorespiratory respiratory carbon dioxide release is 2 in the same units. If photorespiratory carbon dioxide release is 3, then under a deliberately simplified gas-exchange balance the gross carboxylation flux is 20: net uptake equals gross carboxylation minus the two release terms. This arithmetic does not separate all possible recapture and transport processes. It merely shows that a positive measured net uptake is compatible with simultaneous production of carbon dioxide. If the question instead gives only the net exchange, gross carboxylation and respiratory release cannot be uniquely determined from that single measurement.

Finally, remember that a model of respiration is useful only when its assumptions match the problem. A complete turn requires acceptor regeneration, a closed carbon accounting boundary and the stipulated reaction directions. An ATP estimate needs a coupling assumption. A tracer prediction needs the labeled position, time and potential for exchange. A physiological prediction requires a tissue and environmental context. An enzyme-inhibition interpretation requires knowledge of alternative pathways and the condition of upstream substrate supply. These are not additional facts to memorize independently; they are the conditions under which the reaction sequence becomes a reliable explanation.
