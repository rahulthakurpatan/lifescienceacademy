1. The developmental problem: from one cell to an organized plant

A young bean seedling has a root that extends into the soil and a shoot that rises into the light. Between them lies a region that joins the two systems. A cross-section of either axis reveals tissues arranged in reproducible positions: a surface that separates the plant from its surroundings, internal tissues that perform metabolic and mechanical work, and conducting tissues that connect distant regions. None of these arrangements exists as a miniature organ inside the newly fertilized egg. They are developmental achievements. The first major challenge of plant embryogenesis is therefore not simply to increase cell number but to establish a spatial organization that will remain useful throughout the life of the plant.

Embryogenesis is the process by which a fertilized egg develops into an embryo. In flowering plants, the process occurs within the ovule, surrounded by tissues whose origins and functions differ from those of the embryo itself. The embryo is the young sporophyte, the generation that will ultimately make flowers and spores. The endosperm, when present, is a separate product of fertilization with supporting functions. The integuments are maternal sporophytic tissues that will contribute to the seed coat. Seeing these three compartments together under a microscope does not imply that they belong to the same cell lineage. The embryo begins from the fertilized egg, whereas the other compartments have distinct origins. Keeping those origins separate is essential for interpreting developmental genetic experiments.

The central question becomes particularly interesting because plants possess cell walls. A typical plant cell cannot detach from its neighbors and crawl freely through an embryonic tissue as many animal cells do. New cells are created by building new walls within existing cells. Their relative positions are then modified predominantly through differential expansion and further divisions. A particular arrangement of daughter cells can thus persist as a record of a developmental decision. Yet physical immobility does not mean informational immobility. Small molecules and some regulatory proteins can move between cells, and a cell can respond to signals originating outside its own lineage. This combination of persistent cellular neighborhoods and mobile developmental information is a distinctive feature of plant pattern formation.

To understand why a division matters, distinguish three properties of an embryonic cell. Its lineage tells us from which earlier cell it descended. Its position tells us where it lies relative to neighboring cells and the external environment. Its fate describes what it will normally become. A lineage map can reveal striking correlations between these properties, but correlation does not prove that inheritance alone determines fate. A cell may adopt a particular fate because it occupies a position that receives a specific signal, even though it shares much of its genome and developmental capacity with nearby cells. Conversely, a signal may act only on a cell that has acquired the competence to interpret it. The embryo can use both inherited asymmetry and communication between cells to build a dependable pattern.

We will use Arabidopsis thaliana as the principal example because its early divisions are sufficiently reproducible to connect individual cells with gene activity and later anatomy. It is a small flowering eudicot, not a universal diagram of all seed plants. Its embryo passes through stages named for recognizable shapes, including the globular, heart and torpedo stages. The name of a stage is a morphological description, not an assertion that every cell in every species divides at the same moment. Embryos with different mature shapes may nonetheless face equivalent developmental problems. For example, a grass embryo with one cotyledon must still distinguish a future root pole from a future shoot pole and organize an internal transport system.

The concept of symmetry must be handled carefully. An organism is polar when two opposite ends along an axis are not equivalent. A plant embryo can be strongly polar along its future shoot-to-root axis while exhibiting an approximately radial arrangement around that axis. Later, two cotyledons emerge at particular locations near the shoot end of a eudicot embryo, establishing an approximately bilateral organization in that region. These properties are compatible because each describes a different spatial relationship. A student who treats radial symmetry and axial polarity as mutually exclusive will be unable to interpret a transverse section of a root or a longitudinal image of an early embryo.

There is also a difference between establishing a body plan and finishing the construction of the body. The plant embryo does not produce every leaf or lateral root that the adult will possess. It establishes primary tissues and organizing regions, including the precursors of the shoot and root apical meristems, from which growth can continue after germination. A meristem is a region that maintains cells capable of further proliferation while producing descendants that can differentiate. The establishment of these regions during embryogenesis explains how a relatively small seed can later generate a plant whose final size and architecture depend on its environment. The mechanisms of postembryonic meristem maintenance belong to later chapters; here our aim is to understand the developmental origins of those capacities.

2. What classical embryology and genetics revealed

Before developmental genes could be identified, embryologists learned the order of events by examining preserved ovules at different stages. If a series of specimens contains a fertilized egg, two-cell embryos, four-cell embryos and increasingly elaborate structures, a developmental sequence can be inferred. The method is powerful when development is stereotyped, but the images represent different individuals rather than a continuous record of one embryo. An unusual cell arrangement might represent a transient state, an abnormal specimen, or an alternative developmental route. Reliable interpretation requires repeated observations and comparison with a normal reference series.

Lineage diagrams developed from these observations were especially useful in flowering plants because neighboring cells usually remain adjacent after division. When a newly formed wall separates two cells, its orientation provides information about the earlier division plane. Repeated divisions can therefore be reconstructed with reasonable confidence in a stereotyped early embryo. Nevertheless, a wall observed in a fixed section does not report the gene expression of either cell. It also does not reveal whether a division was driven by geometric constraints, a local signal, or inherited intracellular organization. Modern developmental genetics added independent ways to address those questions.

A landmark Arabidopsis mutant analysis by Mayer and colleagues in 1991 demonstrated how seedling body organization could be used to investigate embryonic pattern formation. The investigators recovered mutants with reproducible defects affecting particular parts or arrangements of the embryonic body. The important inference was that the construction of an axis and the organization of tissues were genetically separable to some extent. A seedling lacking one region of the normal body did not necessarily represent a plant that was simply smaller in every dimension. Its phenotype could reflect disruption of information specifying a particular region or pattern. Such a distinction is fundamental to understanding developmental mutants.

Consider why a mutation screen is informative. A normal embryo contains thousands of interacting molecules, and observing any one protein in a particular location does not establish that it is necessary for development. A mutation that removes or alters the protein provides a perturbation. If an otherwise comparable embryo consistently fails to establish a specific structure, the altered gene is implicated in the process. The next task is to determine how it contributes. The protein could directly regulate the identity of the missing tissue, but it could also affect the viability of precursor cells, the delivery of a signal, or the shape of an earlier division. A morphological phenotype is a starting point for a causal explanation, not the complete explanation itself.

Early work on auxin transport offered a second kind of evidence. In 2003 Friml and colleagues linked the localization of PIN proteins, patterns of auxin-responsive gene expression, and axial defects in embryos. PIN proteins are membrane transport proteins that can contribute to auxin efflux, the movement of auxin out of a cell. When a transporter is concentrated on one cell face rather than distributed uniformly, auxin transport can become directional at tissue scale. Developmental patterning could consequently be studied as a problem involving the location of a hormone response and the machinery that establishes it, rather than only as a list of genes expressed in the embryo.

The logic of that work is important for examination reasoning. Observing a hormone-responsive reporter in a group of cells identifies a region of response under the conditions used. Observing a transporter concentrated on a particular cell face suggests a possible direction of transport. A mutation or inhibitor that changes both patterns and the eventual body axis strengthens the causal connection. None of these observations alone demonstrates all of the intermediate steps. A reporter can depend on cellular response competence as well as hormone supply, and the presence of a transporter does not measure the actual rate of transport. Combining independent observations makes the inference stronger than interpreting any one image in isolation.

A third step in the developmental story concerned communication across the boundary between the embryo proper and the suspensor. Weijers and colleagues in 2006 investigated the action of auxin response regulators in the cells adjacent to the future hypophysis, a cell that contributes to the root organizing region. Their results showed that relevant regulatory activity in one domain could influence the identity of a neighboring cell. This finding makes sense of a feature that may initially appear paradoxical: a cell descended from the basal daughter of the zygote can contribute to the embryo's future root, even though most descendants of that daughter form a temporary supporting structure. Position and intercellular communication help explain why one particular basal-lineage cell behaves differently from its neighbors.

These historical examples outline a sequence of increasingly informative questions. A morphological series asks what normally happens. A genetic perturbation asks what components are necessary for that outcome. A spatial reporter asks where and when the component or its downstream response is active. A restricted restoration or inhibition of gene function can ask in which cells the component must act. A well-supported developmental explanation integrates these levels. In the following sections we use this logic to connect the polarization of a single cell to the eventual arrangement of embryonic organs.

3. The fertilized egg and the establishment of intracellular polarity

The zygote originates when a sperm cell fuses with the egg during double fertilization. In a typical diploid flowering plant each gamete carries one chromosome set, so the new zygote carries two sets. The fusion event gives the embryo a paternal contribution and a maternal contribution to its nuclear genome. The surrounding endosperm arises through a separate fusion involving the central cell. The chromosomes of a normal zygote are therefore not those of the endosperm, and the first mitotic division of the zygote does not change its ploidy. This basic bookkeeping rules out one erroneous explanation for the different fates of the daughter cells: they do not become different tissues because one becomes haploid and the other becomes diploid.

The egg already occupies an asymmetric environment before fertilization. It is positioned toward the micropylar end of the female gametophyte and is associated with synergid cells that participate in fertilization. Its intracellular components are not necessarily distributed uniformly. A fertilized egg nevertheless undergoes substantial reorganization rather than simply dividing in the exact form inherited from the unfertilized egg. The direction from the ovular attachment region toward the future embryo apex becomes a meaningful developmental reference. In Arabidopsis the basal daughter will be nearer the micropylar side, while the apical daughter will be displaced inward toward the embryo sac.

A polarized cell has distinguishable regions. One side may contain a different concentration of organelles or a different distribution of regulatory proteins from the opposite side. Polarization can include the placement of the nucleus, rearrangement of the vacuoles, and reorganization of the cytoskeleton. In Arabidopsis the zygote elongates along its prospective apical–basal direction and establishes a highly unequal internal organization before its first division. The nucleus moves toward the apical region, while much of the vacuolar volume lies basally. The contrast between a relatively cytoplasm-rich apical region and a more vacuolated basal region prepares the cell to partition its contents unequally.

Nuclear position and vacuole distribution cannot be explained by a passive drawing of a cell. The plant cytoskeleton includes actin filaments and microtubules, polymers whose organization can change during development. Actin-dependent intracellular movement contributes to nuclear positioning and organelle organization, while microtubules participate in cell geometry and division-related organization. The vacuolar system also changes its configuration as the zygote elongates. These features are interdependent: expanding vacuoles alter the available cytoplasmic space, and changes in the cytoskeleton influence the movement and positioning of organelles. The resulting polarity is a dynamic state that must be established and maintained, not simply a fixed arrangement from the moment of fertilization.

Zygote elongation requires physical work. Water enters a cell when the relevant water-potential difference favors entry. Turgor pressure acts against the cell wall, which resists uncontrolled expansion. If the wall can yield more readily in one direction than another, an initially shorter cell can become elongated. The direction of growth depends on properties of the wall and its organization, together with pressure and material supply. There is no need to invoke an unusual source of energy for embryonic polarity: metabolism supplies energy for biosynthesis, transport and cellular organization, while water uptake and wall mechanics permit changes in cell shape. A living zygote integrates these processes with developmental regulation.

It is important to separate the causes and consequences of elongation. A long zygote is not automatically a correctly polarized zygote. One might imagine a cell that elongates normally but fails to move its nucleus to the appropriate location. Another might reorganize its nucleus and cytoplasm but form a division wall in an abnormal plane. Either disturbance could produce abnormal daughter cells, even if the final overall length resembles a normal specimen. Conversely, a short mutant zygote may retain part of its molecular polarity. Developmental experiments must therefore measure multiple characteristics rather than using length as the sole proxy for correct patterning.

The polarity of the embryo is also not synonymous with the gravitational orientation of a germinating root. The developing embryo lies inside an ovule, and its axial relationships are defined by the organization of the zygote and surrounding tissues. Later, root gravitropism involves sensing gravity and differential growth. That later physiological response can help orient a root in soil, but it is not the mechanism that originally distinguishes the two ends of the embryo. An embryo whose root pole points in an arbitrary direction in a microscopic image can still have entirely normal apical–basal polarity.

4. Asymmetric first division: geometry, inheritance and daughter identities

Once the elongated zygote has prepared for division, its nucleus undergoes mitosis. Sister chromatids segregate so that the two daughter nuclei normally receive equivalent chromosome complements. Cytokinesis then partitions the mother cell by building a cell plate between the daughter nuclei. Vesicles contribute membrane and wall material to the growing plate, which expands and eventually joins the parental cell surface. The location of this partition is critical because the zygote is elongated and internally polarized. A wall placed near the middle would produce daughters of approximately equal longitudinal extent; a wall displaced toward the apical end produces a smaller apical daughter and a larger basal daughter.

In Arabidopsis this unequal transverse division is the first conspicuous cellular manifestation of embryonic apical–basal organization. Transverse means that the division plane cuts across the long axis. It does not mean that the plane is horizontal relative to gravity or the microscope stage. A transverse wall can appear vertical if the specimen is rotated. A longitudinal division, by contrast, creates side-by-side regions with respect to the long axis. This vocabulary is important for reading embryo diagrams in which the orientation of the specimen may vary.

There are two different senses of asymmetry in an asymmetric cell division. Geometric asymmetry describes the unequal size or shape of the daughters. Developmental asymmetry describes a difference in their identities or future behaviors. These properties frequently occur together, as they do in the normal Arabidopsis zygote, but one cannot be deduced automatically from the other. A larger cell may inherit more vacuolar volume without inheriting more of a particular transcription factor. Two equal-sized daughters might still receive distinct signaling inputs or different intracellular determinants. To establish developmental asymmetry experimentally, a researcher must identify differences in marker expression, division behavior, or eventual fate as well as size.

The small apical daughter normally produces the major portion of the embryo proper. It undergoes further divisions in planes that build a three-dimensional multicellular structure. The larger basal daughter follows a contrasting developmental program, producing a file of cells called the suspensor. The differences in these programs illustrate why the first division cannot be interpreted as merely a method of making two equivalent copies of the zygote. The two cells occupy different positions and establish distinct patterns of gene expression. They are also brought into a new relationship: the embryo proper now develops adjacent to a genetically related supporting structure, from which it can receive signals.

The uppermost suspensor cell is a notable exception to the simplified rule that the basal lineage only makes temporary extraembryonic tissue. This cell, the hypophysis, is recruited into the development of the root pole. Its fate is influenced by signals from adjacent embryonic cells. Thus the statement that the apical daughter makes the entire mature plant is inaccurate. It is much more precise to say that the apical daughter generates most of the embryo proper, whereas the basal daughter generates the suspensor and contributes through the hypophysis to part of the embryonic root region. A student who understands this exception can predict the outcome of experiments that mark the two lineages with different fluorescent labels.

A convenient hypothetical example illustrates why the distribution of a regulatory molecule cannot be inferred from daughter volume alone. Suppose the apical and basal daughters occupy volumes of 20 and 80 arbitrary units. If a regulator was uniformly distributed in the parent and remains stable through cytokinesis, the larger daughter would receive approximately four times the total amount. However, both daughters would begin with the same concentration because concentration is amount per volume. If the regulator had accumulated preferentially around the apical nucleus before division, the smaller daughter could inherit a greater concentration despite its smaller size. These alternatives would have different effects if a downstream gene is activated only above a threshold concentration. The distinction between amount and concentration is useful whenever embryonic asymmetry is discussed.

Another reason not to equate geometry with fate is that an abnormal early wall need not lead to exactly the same phenotype in every mutant. If downstream positional signals remain functional, subsequent divisions and fate decisions may partially compensate for the original geometric defect. If the mutated gene also disrupts intercellular signaling, compensation may be much less effective. The first division is therefore a strong contributor to the normal developmental trajectory, but its geometry is not a complete specification of every later cell identity. Evidence from mutant embryos must be interpreted in the context of all the developmental roles of the affected gene.

5. Regulatory logic of the zygote: YODA and WOX transcription factors

How does fertilization lead to a zygote capable of elongating and dividing asymmetrically? Arabidopsis genetics has identified a regulatory module involving YODA, a mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase. A kinase catalyzes transfer of a phosphate group, commonly from ATP, to a target molecule. In a kinase cascade, one kinase activates another, which can in turn modify additional proteins. Such an organization allows a cellular event to be coupled to a coordinated response rather than requiring a single regulator to carry out every task. The YODA pathway is associated with normal zygote elongation and development of the basal lineage.

The SHORT SUSPENSOR gene provides an instructive example of information delivered at fertilization. In Arabidopsis, SSP messenger RNA can be delivered by the sperm and translated after fertilization, contributing to activation of YODA-dependent signaling in the zygote. It is important to distinguish this situation from a simple claim that all zygote polarity is paternal. The sperm-derived contribution acts within a cell containing maternal cellular structures, maternal gene products and a newly combined genome. It participates in a network of inputs. The resulting development depends on the integration of those inputs rather than exclusively on either parent's contribution.

Loss of YODA activity can impair elongation and the normal distinction between apical and basal development. The name of a mutant is a shorthand for an observed genetic phenotype, not a mechanistic explanation. If a mutant forms a shortened zygote and a defective suspensor, those observations support a role for the gene in the relevant processes. To infer that YODA acts before another regulator, one needs additional evidence such as downstream phosphorylation, changes in expression, or genetic relationships. For an examination, distinguish an observation about the mutant's geometry from a conclusion about the identity of the downstream signal.

A second useful regulatory system involves WUSCHEL-related homeobox proteins. Homeobox genes encode transcription factors with a characteristic DNA-binding domain. Their biological significance arises from the genes whose expression they regulate and the cells in which they act. WOX2 and WOX8 are expressed in early Arabidopsis development; their expression domains become differentiated between the apical and basal lineages after the zygote divides. WOX2 is associated with the apical lineage, while WOX8 has an important basal-lineage role. These expression patterns accompany the divergence in developmental behavior of the daughters.

One route connecting zygote signaling to transcription involves the transcription factor WRKY2 and regulation of WOX8. Experimental studies indicate that paternal and maternal regulatory contributions can converge on the activation of developmental transcription in the zygote. This is significant because it shows that the fertilized cell is not merely executing a mechanically fixed partition of pre-existing materials. Changes in gene activity are part of the process. The developing zygote can synthesize new proteins and establish a regulatory state before the first division. The exact behavior of individual factors depends on genotype and developmental stage.

A comparison between expression and function is particularly revealing for transcription factors. If a reporter driven by the WOX8 promoter is active in the basal daughter, it demonstrates activity of that regulatory construct in that location. It does not show that WOX8 alone is sufficient to convert any cell into a basal daughter. Sufficiency would require a suitable gain-of-function or ectopic expression experiment. Necessity requires a perturbation that compromises the relevant function. Even a loss-of-function phenotype may be masked by a related gene that performs a partially redundant role. The correct interpretation of a nearly normal single mutant is therefore not necessarily that the gene is irrelevant.

At the molecular level, the emerging difference between daughter cells can be maintained through a combination of transcriptional regulation, protein turnover and cell-to-cell signals. Gene products that influence the apical region need not remain exclusively in the cells where their genes are expressed, and a basal-lineage regulator can indirectly influence apical development. The regulatory distinction is consequently more subtle than a pair of isolated fate switches. Understanding that relationship prepares us for the later evidence that signals generated in the embryo proper influence the fate of the hypophysis.

For examination purposes the central causal sequence is fertilization followed by zygote reorganization and elongation, asymmetric division, and divergence of developmental programs. YODA-related signaling and WOX-related transcription are experimentally supported elements of that sequence in Arabidopsis. They should not be treated as universal, completely linear pathways for every flowering plant. Nor should a student confuse WOX2 or WOX8 with WUSCHEL itself, a related transcription factor whose later function in organizing the shoot meristem is distinct. The family name indicates molecular relatedness, not identical developmental roles.

6. The proembryo and the suspensor: constructing a cellular framework

After the asymmetric first division, the embryo contains two distinct cells but not yet the recognizable architecture of a seedling. To build that architecture, the apical lineage must produce cells occupying different positions within a coherent three-dimensional structure. The basal lineage must support that process without simply becoming a second embryo of equal size. In the normal Arabidopsis trajectory, the first divisions of the apical cell are longitudinal. They generate side-by-side daughters. Further longitudinal division in an approximately perpendicular orientation produces a group of four cells. A subsequent transverse division partitions that group into upper and lower tiers, yielding the eight-cell, or octant, embryo proper. This account refers to the principal divisions of the apical lineage, not to the total number of cells derived from the zygote.

The distinction between embryo proper and total embryo is necessary when cell counts are used as stage names. During the development of the apical lineage, the basal daughter is also dividing. Its transverse divisions produce a row of cells, one above another along the embryo's axis. This suspensor is attached toward the micropylar region. Thus an embryo described as being at the eight-cell stage normally contains additional cells in the suspensor. The suspensor does not appear only after the octant is complete. Its development overlaps with the growth and organization of the embryo proper.

An analogy with building a stack of boxes helps clarify division geometry, although the actual cells have curved walls and complex shapes. A longitudinal division of a cylinder would yield two side-by-side volumes. A second longitudinal cut oriented perpendicular to the first would produce four sectors. A transverse division of each sector would produce upper and lower sets. The resulting eight compartments have different axial positions but the same number of outer neighbors under an idealized geometry. A later division that separates an outer compartment from an inner one is qualitatively different: it establishes radial positional differences. This progression illustrates why the order and direction of divisions are more informative than a simple series of powers of two.

The term proembryo is often used for the early developmental structure before the major embryonic organs become recognizable. Terminology varies among descriptions, so a diagram should explicitly identify whether a cell count refers to the embryo proper or includes the suspensor. At this stage it would be premature to call every apical-tier cell a cotyledon cell or every lower-tier cell a differentiated root cell. A prospective fate can be assigned from a lineage map, but the actual differentiated structures develop later. A cell's location and expression of specific markers supply additional information about its commitment.

The suspensor can be understood as a developmental interface. It occupies a position between the embryo proper and the micropylar region of the developing seed. Depending on the species, it may contribute to the movement of nutrients and regulatory substances toward the embryo, help maintain its position in the surrounding tissue, and form a route through which developmental signals are exchanged. These functions do not imply that the suspensor is the only route through which an embryo receives resources. Endosperm and maternal tissues contribute to the embryonic environment in ways that vary during development and among species. The suspensor is especially prominent early, before the embryo has an extensive internal transport system.

In some flowering plants the suspensor contains large specialized cells and may reach a remarkable size relative to the embryo proper. In Arabidopsis it is much simpler, forming a relatively short cell file. The variation indicates that the suspensor should be defined by its developmental origin, position and biological roles rather than by an exact number of cells. The large suspensor of another species is not evidence of an abnormal Arabidopsis-type embryo. Comparative biology asks whether the structures perform related developmental tasks, even when their morphology differs.

Most of the suspensor is temporary. As the embryo develops, the need for the early supporting structure changes, and most suspensor cells degenerate. But a developmental structure can be temporary while its effects are permanent. If a suspensor transports a signal necessary for specification of the root region, its disappearance after the event does not erase the developmental consequence. This is a useful general principle: the time during which a signal is required need not be the same as the time at which its phenotype is visible. A mutant seedling may reveal a defect caused much earlier in embryogenesis.

The cell of greatest interest is the suspensor cell adjacent to the embryo proper. Its position differs from that of the rest of the suspensor because it lies directly against the developing basal region of the embryo proper. This cell becomes the hypophysis. Although it belongs genealogically to the basal daughter lineage, it participates in the formation of permanent embryonic structures. This behavior is not an exception to the usefulness of lineage tracing; rather, it demonstrates what lineage tracing can reveal when interpreted together with positional information.

An embryo with a severely defective suspensor can consequently exhibit both general and specific developmental abnormalities. Nutritional limitation may reduce proliferation throughout the embryo. A defect in the signaling environment of the hypophysis may interfere with the root pole in a more selective way. A researcher should not assume that a small embryo necessarily has a failure of axial specification or that a rootless seedling necessarily had a nutritionally inadequate suspensor. Distinguishing these alternatives requires a developmental series, tissue-specific markers and appropriate comparisons with the normal embryo.

7. Directional auxin transport and the changing axial signal

Auxin is a class of plant signaling molecules that includes indole-3-acetic acid, usually abbreviated IAA. The hormone participates in many processes, but its relevance here is the ability of a tissue to establish spatially unequal auxin signaling. A small embryo has no fully developed vascular transport network of the type found in an adult stem. Nevertheless, neighboring cells can generate a local pattern of hormone distribution by regulating production, uptake, efflux and metabolism. Such a pattern can provide positional information on the scale of only a few cells.

To understand how directed movement is possible, consider the chemical properties of IAA. It has a carboxyl group that can either retain its proton or lose it to form a negatively charged ion. The proportion in each form depends on the acidity of the environment. The extracellular space of many plant tissues is more acidic than the cytosol, so a greater fraction of IAA is protonated outside than inside. Uncharged IAA can cross a lipid membrane more readily by passive diffusion than its anionic form. Uptake carriers can also contribute to entry. Once inside, where the pH is higher, much of the IAA exists as an anion whose exit is facilitated by transport proteins.

This chemical asymmetry is a useful foundation for understanding polar auxin transport, but it cannot by itself specify the direction along the embryo. If efflux proteins were distributed uniformly on all cell faces and all other conditions were equivalent, no particular axial direction would be selected by the arrangement of those proteins. A tissue can create directional transport by concentrating transport capacity at selected plasma-membrane faces. PIN proteins are especially important examples of efflux-associated transport machinery with such spatial localization. Their membrane distribution can change during development. A PIN protein found preferentially on the side facing the shoot pole is described as apically localized in the relevant embryonic context; basal localization indicates the opposite orientation.

The distinction between the position of a cell and the polarity of its transporter is easy to overlook. An apical cell may have a protein at its basal membrane. A basal-lineage cell may have a protein at its apical membrane. Thus the statement that PIN7 is found apically in the early basal cell does not mean that PIN7 is located in the apical cell. It describes a particular face of the basal cell. This localization is compatible with preferential delivery of auxin toward the adjacent apical daughter and contributes to an early region of auxin response in the developing embryo proper. Drawing the two cells and labeling both their identities and their membrane faces eliminates much of the apparent ambiguity.

The developmental system later changes. During globular embryogenesis, PIN localization patterns are reorganized, and directional transport increasingly supports a region of auxin response toward the basal pole of the embryo proper and the hypophysis. PIN1 in developing internal embryonic cells becomes associated with transport toward the prospective root region. Changes in PIN7 polarity in the suspensor also accompany the reorganization. The earlier apical response and the later basal response are not contradictory results. They describe different stages of a dynamic process. The embryo needs different spatial information when establishing an initial apical domain and when specifying the root organizing region.

Why should a transport system change direction? A fixed hormone distribution might help initiate an initial fate difference, but subsequent developmental decisions occur in a structure whose geometry and cell identities have changed. A new internal cell population has emerged, the hypophysis occupies a particular boundary, and the embryo is beginning to establish tissue domains. Reorganizing transport allows a locally acting hormone to be reused for different decisions. Regulation of transporter placement is therefore a developmental mechanism in its own right rather than a secondary detail of hormone action.

A response maximum must also be distinguished from an absolute concentration maximum. Auxin-responsive transcription depends not only on the amount of hormone reaching a cell but on the state of its receptors, transcriptional regulators and downstream response machinery. A reporter such as DR5 is designed to reflect auxin-responsive transcription. Strong signal from the reporter indicates strong activity of that construct under the conditions observed, not a direct concentration measurement in micromoles per litre. A cell with abundant auxin but defective transcriptional response may show a weak reporter signal. A cell with more responsive regulatory machinery may show stronger signal than another exposed to the same concentration.

The classic evidence connecting PIN localization with axis formation involves several complementary observations. Spatial expression and localization studies identify the relevant transport proteins and their positions. Reporter experiments reveal changes in hormone response patterns as embryogenesis proceeds. Perturbations of transport machinery can alter those patterns and cause axial defects. The combination supports a causal role for directed auxin movement. It does not imply that auxin is the only information required to specify every axial region. Developmental identities also depend on competence, transcriptional state and communication with neighboring cells.

The chemistry of weak acids permits a useful calculation without pretending to model the entire embryo. For a monoprotic weak acid at equilibrium, the ratio of deprotonated to protonated form is ten raised to the power pH minus pKa. If the illustrative pKa of IAA is taken as 4.75, an extracellular pH of 5.5 gives a ratio of approximately 5.62. The protonated fraction is one divided by one plus that ratio, or about 0.151. At cytosolic pH 7.0, the ratio is approximately 178 and the protonated fraction about 0.0056. The calculation explains why the relative abundance of membrane-permeable neutral acid differs across the membrane. It does not specify the intracellular auxin concentration, because active transport, metabolic reactions, actual pH values and compartment volumes also matter.

This is an instance where a simple chemical model clarifies a physical constraint without replacing the biology. It would be wrong to calculate a universal flux from the two protonated fractions alone. Flux is the amount moving per unit time, sometimes normalized to membrane area. It depends on concentration differences, permeability and transport activity. The direction of net movement in a multicellular embryo depends on multiple cell faces and the surrounding sources and sinks. Energy-dependent processes maintain proton gradients and regulate membrane transport; the weak-acid equilibrium itself describes a distribution of chemical forms, not an independent supply of metabolic energy.

A useful thought experiment distinguishes transport capacity from signaling competence. Imagine two mutant embryos. In the first, PIN localization is disrupted, but the hormone response machinery remains intact. Auxin-responsive transcription could still occur if sufficient hormone reaches a competent cell, although its spatial distribution might be abnormal. In the second, transport is correctly polarized but the relevant response regulator is inactive. The hormone may arrive at the correct site without eliciting the transcriptional program required for fate specification. The two mutants may both lack an embryonic root, but their primary defects differ. This is why rescue by adding hormone cannot be assumed to repair every developmental defect associated with auxin.

8. From hormone perception to the hypophysis and root organizing region

For directional auxin transport to influence cell fate, a cell must convert information about auxin availability into a regulatory response. The canonical nuclear auxin-response mechanism provides an understandable example of regulated protein turnover. Auxin binds within a receptor complex involving members of the TIR1/AFB family of F-box proteins. In the presence of auxin, interaction between the receptor machinery and certain AUX/IAA transcriptional repressors is promoted. The repressors are targeted for ubiquitin-dependent degradation by the proteasome. When repression is relieved, auxin response factors, called ARFs, can regulate the transcription of target genes in the appropriate cellular context.

Notice the apparent reversal in this signaling scheme. Auxin can activate target-gene expression by promoting destruction of a negative regulator. It does not need to phosphorylate every activated transcription factor directly. The arrangement makes protein stability a control point: a repressor that remains unusually stable can block a response even when auxin is present. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why some mutations in an auxin-response pathway have dominant or gain-of-function behavior. A stabilized repressor retains its inhibitory influence rather than merely losing a normal positive function.

In embryonic Arabidopsis, MONOPTEROS, also called ARF5, is a particularly informative auxin response factor. BODENLOS, also known as IAA12, is an AUX/IAA repressor associated with its regulation. In a normal relevant domain of the embryo proper, auxin-dependent relief of repression allows MONOPTEROS to promote expression of genes needed for embryonic patterning. If MONOPTEROS function is lost, these transcriptional responses are compromised. If BODENLOS becomes stabilized in a form that resists normal auxin-dependent turnover, repression can persist. These are different molecular perturbations that can interfere with related developmental outcomes, including formation of the embryonic root.

It is tempting to imagine that the root founder cell must express every regulator necessary for its own fate. The hypophysis provides a classical reason to reject that assumption. Relevant MONOPTEROS activity occurs in cells of the embryo proper adjacent to the future hypophysis. The regulatory program in those cells contributes to signals that act on the neighboring suspensor-derived cell. Thus a gene can be required for a fate decision without acting exclusively within the cell whose fate is being specified. This is called a non-cell-autonomous contribution. It contrasts with a cell-autonomous requirement, in which the relevant gene function must occur within the affected cell itself.

One component of intercellular communication is the movement of the small transcription factor TMO7, a target associated with MONOPTEROS-dependent regulation. TMO7 can move from the embryo proper toward the hypophysis and contributes, with auxin signaling and other factors, to the appropriate developmental outcome. The important principle is that a hormone response in one cell can lead to production of a separate mobile regulatory signal. Consequently, simply supplying auxin to the neighboring cell may not reproduce the entire message normally generated by the upstream tissue. The signaling chain includes transport, response competence and downstream communication.

The hypophysis undergoes a characteristic asymmetric division during globular-stage development. Its smaller, lens-shaped upper daughter contributes to the quiescent center of the root apical meristem, a group of cells with an organizing role. Its lower daughter contributes to the columella lineage in the root cap region. Adjacent descendants of the embryo proper contribute other root tissues and stem-cell populations. The future root is therefore constructed through coordinated contributions of the two original zygotic lineages. The hypophysis does not form the entire root by itself, and the suspensor as a whole does not become the root.

At this point the plant's two axes can be considered together. The apical–basal axis already distinguishes the prospective shoot end, intermediate embryonic regions and the root end. Within the basal end, however, cells must also acquire different positions across the radial dimension. A functioning root meristem requires not just an organizing center but appropriately located neighboring tissues that can respond to it. The specification of a hypophysis is consequently a necessary component of normal root organization in the model system, not a complete explanation of root organogenesis. Detailed meristem maintenance and later root architecture belong to Subunit 5.D.

Experimental reasoning can separate some of the steps in this pathway. Suppose a loss-of-function mutant fails to specify the hypophysis. If expression of the intact gene solely in the adjacent embryo-proper domain restores the normal hypophyseal division, the result supports an upstream role in that domain. If the gene product is then observed moving toward the hypophysis, mobility becomes a plausible mechanism. However, restoration in one domain could also act indirectly through another signaling molecule. A protein movement experiment would need to distinguish the direct movement of the protein from activation of its gene in neighboring cells. A promoter reporter and a tagged protein can give complementary information because they report different biological events.

A further approach is to compare the phenotype of a normal protein with that of a modified protein whose movement is restricted but whose biochemical function remains intact. If the mobile version restores hypophysis specification and the restricted version does not, the evidence supports a role for movement. The interpretation requires care: a tag that prevents movement might also reduce protein stability, change subcellular localization or interfere with DNA binding. Controls confirming comparable production and retained function within the expressing cell are therefore important. The broader lesson is that a negative rescue experiment is informative only when alternative reasons for failure have been considered.

An examination may describe an auxin reporter that is normal in a mutant embryo even though the hypophysis fails to divide correctly. The best conclusion is not automatically that auxin is irrelevant to the root. The observation suggests that at least the reporter's measured response remains, while a subsequent or parallel required process may be defective. A second reporter, lineage markers and a selective genetic restoration could narrow the cause. Developmental phenotypes are endpoints of interacting processes, and different molecular failures can converge on superficially similar anatomy.

9. Radial patterning and the origin of primary tissue systems

An embryo can possess a clear shoot–root direction and still lack the arrangement of tissue layers needed for a functional seedling. Axial patterning identifies positions along the length of the embryo. Radial patterning identifies positions from its external surface toward its interior. The two spatial problems are complementary. An outer cell near the shoot pole and an outer cell near the root pole share an external position but can develop different specialized properties. Likewise, an internal cell near the root pole and an adjacent surface cell share an axial position but belong to different tissue domains.

A major early transition occurs when the octant embryo proper undergoes divisions separating outer cells from inner cells. In the idealized sixteen-cell stage, an outer layer of eight cells encloses eight internal cells. The outer cells form the protodermal domain, the precursor of the epidermal tissue system. The internal cells subsequently establish domains associated with ground tissue and vascular precursors. The actual order and geometry of divisions can be more complicated than a simultaneous split of every octant cell, but the simplified arrangement highlights the positional distinction between surface and interior.

The terms periclinal and anticlinal describe division orientation relative to a tissue surface. A periclinal division produces a new wall approximately parallel to the local surface, generating daughters at different depths. An anticlinal division produces a wall approximately perpendicular to that surface, often increasing cell number within an existing layer. These terms refer to a local surface rather than the embryo's global shoot–root direction. A periclinal division on the side of an embryo can have a different orientation in an image from a periclinal division near its tip. Therefore, it is important to identify the reference surface before classifying a division plane.

The protoderm is a primary embryonic tissue domain, not yet a fully differentiated adult epidermis containing all the specialized cell types seen in a leaf or root. Its development establishes an outer cell identity. Molecular markers associated with surface identity can help identify it before mature functional characteristics emerge. In Arabidopsis, the homeodomain-leucine zipper IV transcription factors ATML1 and PDF2 are associated with epidermal specification. They help illustrate how a particular position in the early embryo is linked to a gene regulatory state. The surface location of a cell is not itself a transcription factor; it supplies or constrains signals that the cell interprets through its regulatory machinery.

Epidermal identity has physical and physiological significance. A coherent surface separates the plant from its external surroundings, regulates exchange, and provides a context in which specialized surface structures can later differentiate. The outer tissue also participates in the mechanical behavior of the growing organ. However, a very early embryo is enclosed within the seed environment and does not yet face the same challenges as an exposed leaf. Its protoderm should therefore be understood as a developmental foundation, not as an already functioning adult protective surface.

Internal to the protoderm, divisions and positional signals establish precursors of the ground and vascular tissue systems. The ground tissue contributes, among other derivatives, to cortex and related internal tissues; the vascular precursor or procambial domain gives rise to conducting tissues such as xylem and phloem. The precise mature arrangement varies with organ and species. It is useful to imagine an embryonic cross-section as containing an outer surface domain, an intermediate internal domain and a central conducting domain. This is a conceptual model of tissue organization, not a claim that every transverse plane through a globular embryo already contains mature concentric cylinders.

Ground tissue specification is an instructive case of how radial and axial signaling intersect. Studies of Arabidopsis embryogenesis have implicated MONOPTEROS-dependent auxin response in initiating a ground tissue program. A transcription factor that was introduced to explain axial patterning can therefore also participate in other spatial decisions in a context-dependent manner. Later aspects of ground tissue patterning involve other regulators, including the SHORT-ROOT and SCARECROW network. SHORT-ROOT can move from an internal vascular-associated domain into adjacent cells, where it participates in regulatory events that contribute to ground tissue pattern. The initial specification of a tissue and its subsequent subdivision are distinct processes.

A failure of ground tissue subdivision need not remove the entire ground tissue domain. For example, a defect in a later formative division may leave a single layer where two distinct layers would normally occur. In contrast, failure to establish the earlier ground tissue identity could affect the existence or behavior of the domain from the outset. An examination question that provides cross-sections and molecular markers may ask students to distinguish these possibilities. It is not sufficient to count the number of internal rings. One needs to know which identities are present in each ring and which developmental step is affected.

Similarly, an embryo may possess an approximately normal external shape while having a serious tissue identity defect. Surface cell number and surface identity are different observations. If outer cells continue to divide but fail to express a group of epidermal markers, the outer geometric layer exists without the expected differentiation program. Conversely, a marker can be expressed before the associated morphological specialization is apparent. The relationship between pattern and morphology is often temporally offset because transcriptional decisions precede changes in wall structure, cell shape and physiological function.

The three plant primary tissue systems should not be mapped directly onto the three animal germ layers. The protoderm, ground meristem and procambium are plant developmental domains that contribute to tissue systems. Animal ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm are generated in relation to gastrulation and have different lineage and evolutionary meanings. A superficial resemblance between an outer plant tissue and an outer animal layer does not establish homology. The meaningful comparison is that both embryos generate spatially organized regions of distinct identity, while their mechanisms, tissue properties and developmental histories differ.

For a quantitative illustration, imagine the idealized sixteen-cell embryo proper. Eight cells occupy the outer domain and eight the inner domain, giving an outer-cell proportion of one half. If each outer cell divides anticlinally once while the inner cells do not divide, the embryo proper contains sixteen outer and eight inner cells. The outer-cell proportion becomes two thirds. Yet the outer layer remains one cell thick under the stated assumptions. By contrast, if each outer cell divides periclinally, new inner daughters are generated and the arrangement of layers changes. Both scenarios add eight cells, but they create different spatial patterns. The example demonstrates why total cell number is an inadequate measure of tissue organization.

10. The globular embryo as a meeting point of axial and radial information

The globular stage is recognizable by the rounded form of the embryo proper. It is a major conceptual transition because multiple types of pattern are being established within a small volume. The apical and basal regions have acquired different developmental relationships. The surface is distinguishable from the interior. The hypophysis is being specified at the interface with the suspensor, and internal cell populations begin to contribute to future primary tissues. These events overlap rather than occurring as a perfectly sequential list of completed modules.

A globular outline can be misleading. A spherical object appears to have no preferred direction if only its exterior is considered. But a globular embryo is not an unmarked sphere. The suspensor establishes a recognizable basal attachment, the cells have distinct lineages and molecular identities, and hormone-response domains have spatial organization. A rounded embryo can therefore exhibit strong internal polarity. To determine whether polarity has been established, a researcher must inspect landmarks and molecular patterns, not merely whether the silhouette looks elongated.

At the transition toward the heart stage, two lateral domains near the future shoot end begin to expand and form cotyledon primordia. A primordium is an early recognizable region that will develop into an organ. The shoot apical meristem region becomes positioned between these two developing structures. This arrangement requires the embryo to distinguish not only the apical pole from the basal pole but also particular positions within the apical region. The emerging paired structures provide a visible sign that the previously rounded body is acquiring bilateral organization.

Auxin distribution again contributes to the selection and development of organ initiation sites. The direction of transport and the capacity of particular cells to respond can generate local activity patterns associated with cotyledon initiation. The formation of two cotyledon primordia should not be interpreted as a simple consequence of two equal copies of the zygote's first apical daughter. Many rounds of division and regulatory events intervene. The developing primordia are regions within a multicellular tissue rather than single cells whose identities were permanently labeled at fertilization.

A boundary between the cotyledons and the shoot organizing region is developmentally important. If all cells in the apical region entered the same organ-development program, the embryo might fail to preserve the distinct central region needed for continued shoot growth. CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON genes are relevant examples of regulators involved in cotyledon separation and shoot meristem establishment in Arabidopsis. Their names reflect visible mutant phenotypes, including fused cotyledons in appropriate mutant combinations. The critical principle is that an organ boundary is an actively regulated region, not simply the empty space left over when two growing tissues happen to diverge.

Distinguish organ initiation from organ separation. An embryo with two initially specified cotyledon domains could later exhibit fusion because the boundary program fails. Another embryo might lack one domain from the beginning. A third might establish both domains and the boundary but show abnormal expansion because of impaired growth. Each could yield an abnormal cotyledon arrangement at the mature embryo stage. Developmental series and stage-specific markers are needed to distinguish them. A single mature phenotype cannot reliably reveal whether the initiating defect involved tissue specification, boundary maintenance or later morphogenesis.

The shoot meristem region is often portrayed as the central zone between cotyledons in a heart-stage diagram. This is an important spatial relationship, but one must distinguish the early expression of meristem-associated regulators from a fully functioning postembryonic meristem. Establishing an organizing center, maintaining a stem-cell population and producing later leaf primordia are related but separable processes. The embryo establishes the starting organization; a detailed treatment of WUSCHEL–CLAVATA regulation and leaf initiation belongs in later plant organogenesis chapters.

By the end of this transition, the embryo displays polarity in several senses: intracellular polarization has generated unequal zygotic descendants, tissue-scale transport has helped organize a longitudinal axis, divisions have established radial domains, and lateral organ initiation is creating a new geometric asymmetry relative to the earlier globular stage. These are not competing explanations. They represent distinct scales of patterning that become coordinated over developmental time. Recognizing the scale at which a question is posed is one of the most useful habits in developmental biology.

11. Heart, torpedo and mature embryo: pattern becomes architecture

At the heart stage of Arabidopsis embryogenesis, the two cotyledon primordia project from the apical region, and the body of the embryo begins to acquire a more definite elongated form. Cotyledons are embryonic organs often called seed leaves. They must be distinguished from the first true foliage leaves, which develop later from the shoot apical meristem. In many eudicot seedlings the cotyledons contribute stored resources or perform photosynthesis after emergence; in other species they remain inside the seed and transfer reserves. The developmental significance of the heart stage does not depend on a single nutritional role of the cotyledons. It depends on their establishment as distinct organs in a reproducible relation to the future shoot.

A longitudinal section through a heart-stage embryo in the plane of the cotyledons shows two lobes separated by a central region. A section perpendicular to that plane can show a rather different outline. This matters in classical embryological descriptions. Suppose two images of similar-sized embryos have different silhouettes, with paired protrusions apparent in only one image. They may represent different developmental stages, but they could also represent different planes of section through the same stage. A serial set of sections or three-dimensional image can distinguish these explanations. Stage assignment should use the combination of organ landmarks, tissue organization and orientation rather than overall width alone.

As the embryo proceeds toward the torpedo stage, longitudinal growth of the embryonic axis becomes conspicuous. The cotyledons elongate, the hypocotyl becomes more clearly recognizable, and the root region acquires an increasingly organized anatomy. The hypocotyl is the embryonic axis below the cotyledon attachment and above the root. It is neither the whole shoot nor the whole root. The radicle is the embryonic root region that will give rise to the primary root upon germination. The arrangement of the hypocotyl and radicle provides continuity between the upper and lower parts of the embryonic body.

Internal tissue organization develops concurrently with organ elongation. A conducting precursor strand running through the axis and into the cotyledons provides a framework for later vascular continuity. It is not necessary that the earliest procambial cells already transport water at the rate of mature xylem vessels. Their developmental significance is the establishment of a spatially coherent precursor system. Ground tissue and epidermal derivatives also develop in relation to this axial framework. Thus the mature plant's continuous systems are built through both local fate decisions and coordination across neighboring regions.

The transition from a round embryo to an elongated one depends on both cell proliferation and differential expansion. Cell proliferation increases the number of units available to construct a tissue. Expansion increases the size of those units. The distinction is experimentally observable: a longer organ could contain more cells of the same average length, the same number of longer cells, or a combination of both. A mutation that affects division need not have the same cellular consequences as one that affects wall extensibility. By counting cells and measuring cell dimensions along equivalent anatomical regions, one can investigate which process has changed.

Growth direction is similarly important. If cells expand more along the long axis than across it, a structure can become elongated without a proportionate increase in width. This is anisotropic growth. Isotropic growth, in an idealized sense, would increase dimensions equally in every direction. Plant cell-wall organization constrains expansion, and pressure supplies a physical driving force. But the actual expansion rate is regulated by cell wall remodeling, water relations and developmental control. Merely observing high turgor does not establish that one direction of growth must dominate. The organization of the wall and the mechanical context of neighboring cells also matter.

An illustrative geometric calculation makes the distinction concrete. If a simplified embryo region begins with a length of 50 micrometres and a width of 40 micrometres, its aspect ratio, defined as length divided by width, is 1.25. Suppose a later region measures 100 micrometres in length and 50 micrometres in width. Its aspect ratio has become 2.0. Length doubled, while width increased by only one quarter. The observation is consistent with anisotropic net tissue growth. It does not demonstrate whether the anisotropy arose through oriented cell divisions, individual cell expansion, or mechanical interactions among tissues. Additional cellular measurements are required.

A further idealization treats the longitudinal dimension as the sum of the lengths of cells arranged in one file. If ten cells of mean length five micrometres form a file, its length is approximately fifty micrometres. If the same number of cells later average ten micrometres, the length doubles without an increase in cell number. If twenty cells each average five micrometres, the same final length is achieved through proliferation alone. Real embryos have irregular cell shapes and three-dimensional arrangements, so this equation is not a full growth model. It is a practical means of identifying alternative explanations for a measured anatomical change.

As Arabidopsis embryo development continues, the elongating cotyledons and axis become curved within the developing seed. This bending reflects growth in a spatially constrained environment and is not evidence that axial polarity has been lost. The mature embryo contains a root pole, a hypocotyl, cotyledons and a shoot organizing region. Its reserves and physiological state change during maturation, but those events must be distinguished from the earlier establishment of basic structure. An embryo can have a recognizable body plan before becoming capable of surviving seed drying.

Maturation is consequently a developmental boundary rather than the end of growth potential. A viable mature embryo can resume proliferation and expansion under suitable germination conditions. In this sense, embryogenesis establishes an organized developmental starting point. Germination does not create a shoot–root axis from nothing. It reveals and extends an organization built in the seed. The events that protect the embryo during storage, dormancy and germination are considered in Node 5.B.13, while the coordination of the developing embryo with the endosperm and maternal tissues is the subject of the next chapter.

12. Comparative embryogenesis: what the Arabidopsis model does and does not predict

A textbook diagram often shows a sequence from a polarized zygote through globular, heart and torpedo stages to a bent mature embryo. It is an excellent description of the principal trajectory in Arabidopsis and many familiar eudicot examples. However, developmental biology requires a distinction between a model organism and a universal rule. If every stage name and division pattern were imposed on every plant, important biological differences would appear to be anomalies rather than adaptations. The correct comparative question is which developmental problem is shared and which cellular solution is specific to a lineage.

The most familiar contrast is between eudicot and monocot embryonic organization. A typical eudicot embryo possesses two cotyledons. A grass embryo possesses a single cotyledon, the scutellum. This specialized structure lies in relation to the developing endosperm and participates in nutrient transfer during germination. In a mature grass embryo, the shoot and root regions are also associated with characteristic protective structures, including the coleoptile and coleorhiza. These are not universal names for tissues at the two poles of every flowering plant. They describe particular features of grass embryonic architecture.

The difference in cotyledon number has a geometric consequence. An Arabidopsis heart stage is defined by the outgrowth of two cotyledon primordia. A grass embryo need not pass through an identical paired-lobe silhouette. Its embryo may show strong bilateral or dorsiventral organization associated with the position of the scutellum and embryonic axis, rather than the same transition observed in a eudicot. The existence of a single cotyledon does not imply that the embryo lacks an apical–basal axis. The future shoot and root still occupy distinguishable regions. It also does not imply that grass tissues are arranged without radial organization; the later root, for example, has recognizable surface and internal tissue systems.

A useful examination distinction concerns cotyledon count and polarity. If an embryo is experimentally modified so that it forms an abnormal number of cotyledons, that phenotype alone does not demonstrate that the apical–basal axis has disappeared. One must inspect whether a recognizable shoot meristem, hypocotyl and root pole remain. A defect may be confined primarily to organ initiation or boundary formation. Conversely, a severely abnormal axis may indirectly affect cotyledon number because the apical domain itself is disrupted. The same numerical phenotype can therefore arise from different developmental causes.

In addition to the major contrast between monocots and eudicots, early cell-division patterns vary among flowering plants. Some species have a conspicuous suspensor made of many specialized cells. Others possess a relatively inconspicuous suspensor. The first zygotic division may differ in orientation and degree of size asymmetry. Later cell divisions can follow different sequences while ultimately producing a comparable body plan. These variations show that the exact geometry of a particular early cleavage is not identical to the entire causal program of embryogenesis.

The evolution of plant developmental mechanisms can be understood through conservation with modification. Related plant lineages often possess homologous families of regulatory genes and transport proteins. Their patterns of expression, timing of activity, and interactions may differ. The presence of a homolog of an Arabidopsis gene in a grass genome is therefore evidence of evolutionary relatedness, not automatic proof that the gene acts in precisely the same embryonic cells. Functional experiments and expression analysis are needed to establish conservation of a particular mechanism. For NET-level reasoning, it is enough to recognize the distinction between gene homology and demonstrated developmental function.

A second comparative question concerns animals. In a typical animal embryo, cell movements during gastrulation establish the positions of major germ layers. Cells may change shape, move relative to one another and enter internal positions. In plants, cytokinesis produces walls that usually maintain local cell relationships. Tissue pattern can emerge when differently positioned descendants interpret signals and divide in oriented planes, without undergoing the extensive migrations characteristic of many animal gastrulation events. The absence of a comparable gastrulation movement is not evidence that plants lack a mechanism of tissue internalization. New internal cells arise when division planes partition previously surface-exposed cells into outer and inner daughters.

Some developmental principles remain comparable across kingdoms. Both plant and animal embryos establish axes, use intercellular communication, and regulate gene expression differently in different regions. Both can exhibit asymmetric cell division and dependence of cell identity on positional information. In both cases, a mutation affecting an early organizer may have effects that appear much later. The molecular signals and mechanical mechanisms implementing these principles differ, so comparisons should be made at the level of developmental logic rather than by assuming one-to-one equivalence of named structures.

This comparative perspective also helps explain the long-term difference between the embryonic and adult body. Many animal embryos establish a relatively large fraction of the adult organ layout during embryogenesis. A flowering plant generally preserves meristematic regions from which numerous later organs can develop. This contrast is not absolute: animals differ in their postembryonic development and regenerative capacities, while plant embryos establish important primary organs. It is a useful explanation of why the correct establishment of shoot and root organizing regions has such enduring importance in a plant.

13. Embryonic growth inside the seed: signaling, resources and developmental autonomy

A plant embryo does not develop in an empty container. It is surrounded by tissues that supply resources and influence the conditions in which it grows. The fertilized egg has its own developmental program, yet that program operates within a changing environment created by the endosperm and maternal ovule. The question of autonomy is therefore not whether an embryo needs nothing from other tissues. It is whether a particular developmental decision can be carried out by the embryo's own machinery when necessary environmental conditions are supplied, or whether an external tissue provides specific instructive information.

The term permissive signal describes an influence that enables a developmental process to occur without necessarily specifying its pattern. Adequate water, metabolic substrates and a suitable ionic environment are often permissive requirements. An instructive signal provides information that can influence which developmental choice is made. A particular external molecule may have different roles depending on the experiment and context. For example, a transported metabolite might supply energy for cell proliferation, while a signaling molecule might alter transcription in a particular region. Supplying more of the first would not necessarily replace the positional information carried by the second.

During early embryo development, the suspensor and surrounding tissues can contribute to the delivery of materials. The endosperm develops in parallel rather than being produced by the zygotic lineage. Its activity influences the internal environment of the seed. Maternal tissues provide the physical and nutritional setting in which both fertilization products develop. The embryo's position near one region of the ovule places its different surfaces in distinct local relationships. Such relationships may contribute to the spatial context of development, but it would be too strong to claim that maternal geometry alone determines every embryonic identity.

Genetic experiments can distinguish embryonic defects from defects in surrounding tissues. Suppose a mutant mother produces abnormal embryos even when the embryos inherit a functional paternal copy of the candidate gene. Several explanations remain possible. The gene might act in the maternal sporophyte, the female gametophyte, or through products accumulated before fertilization. It might also be subject to a parent-of-origin effect in the developing seed. Reciprocal crosses, segregation patterns and tissue-specific restoration of gene function can help discriminate among these possibilities. A maternal association is not by itself proof of genomic imprinting.

Conversely, if embryos inheriting two nonfunctional copies of a gene are abnormal irrespective of which parent provided a mutant allele, the result may indicate a recessive zygotic requirement. This is especially informative if normal and abnormal embryos develop within the same maternal plant, because they share much of the maternal environment. However, sibling seeds may also differ in endosperm genotype and local resource conditions. The strongest interpretation depends on the design of the cross and the tissues in which the candidate gene is active. This is why developmental genetics is closely connected to inheritance biology.

Consider a hypothetical mutation that reduces nutrient transfer to the embryo. At early stages, the embryos exhibit slower cell proliferation but retain the normal arrangement of tissue identity markers and a correctly specified root pole. Later they arrest at a small size. This result differs from a patterning mutation that generates approximately normal early growth but fails to establish a root founder population. The first suggests a general limitation affecting progression; the second suggests a defect in spatial developmental information. In reality a nutritional disturbance can eventually cause patterning defects and a patterning disturbance can alter growth. The earliest detectable abnormality, together with selective rescue, is more informative than the final arrest alone.

The embryo's relationship to the endosperm changes as development proceeds. The embryo increases its size, forms organ primordia and differentiates tissue domains; the endosperm follows its own developmental trajectory. Depending on species, much endosperm can remain at maturity or much of its contents can be transferred into embryonic storage structures. These differences influence mature seed organization but do not require a different definition of the embryo proper. The three compartments of the seed must be distinguished at all times by developmental origin. A detailed treatment of endosperm cellularization, storage and the coordination of seed size belongs to Node 5.B.12.

At the applied level, embryonic developmental capacity is important for plant regeneration. Under suitable conditions, some somatic plant cells can be reprogrammed to generate embryo-like structures, a process called somatic embryogenesis. These structures can establish a shoot–root axis even though they did not originate from sperm–egg fusion. This is useful evidence that plant developmental programs are not permanently restricted to cells produced by fertilization. It is not evidence that every differentiated cell readily forms an embryo under ordinary conditions. Regeneration depends on species, genotype, cell state and external conditions.

A somatic embryo and a zygotic embryo share certain developmental outcomes but differ in their initiation and surroundings. A somatic embryo does not necessarily have the normal suspensor, maternal ovule environment or endosperm of a zygotic embryo. The existence of an alternative route to a bipolar body plan therefore illustrates both developmental flexibility and the importance of distinguishing a developmental program from its normal starting conditions. This observation connects with the earlier Academy chapters on cell potency and reprogramming. Detailed plant tissue culture methods are outside the present chapter's scope.

14. Reading an experiment: identity, division and developmental time

When a question provides images of normal and mutant embryos, a productive first step is to decide what the images measure. A cell-wall stain reports boundaries, a nuclear label reports the locations of nuclei, a promoter-driven fluorescent reporter reports activity of a regulatory construct, and a translational fusion may report the location of a particular protein. Each measurement has a different relationship to the process of interest. Confusing the readouts can produce a biologically incorrect conclusion even when the figure has been read accurately.

Suppose a fluorescent reporter controlled by a protoderm-associated promoter is normally restricted to outer cells. In a mutant it is also detected in several internal cells. The observation is consistent with altered spatial regulation of the marker. It is not sufficient to prove that all internal cells have become functional epidermis. One marker can be misregulated without a complete change of tissue identity. A stronger conclusion requires several independent markers, appropriate structural features and, if possible, later behavior of the cells. This distinction between marker expression and actual identity is central to developmental genetics.

A promoter reporter and a protein reporter are especially important when a signal may move between cells. Imagine that a gene is transcribed in the embryo proper while its protein can enter the adjacent hypophysis. A transcriptional reporter constructed to remain in the cell of synthesis may illuminate the embryo-proper domain but not the hypophysis. A functional fluorescent fusion to the protein could illuminate both. This pattern is compatible with protein movement. Yet the interpretation depends on reporter properties, because a mobile reporter itself might create a misleading signal. Controls establishing localization and retained biological activity are needed.

Time is another source of confusion. A mutant embryo compared with a normal embryo at the same number of hours after fertilization may simply be developmentally delayed. Its lack of cotyledon protrusions is not necessarily a defect in cotyledon specification if it has not yet reached the equivalent globular-to-heart transition. A useful experimental comparison includes both age-matched and stage-matched samples. The first reveals whether progression is slower; the second helps determine whether spatial pattern is abnormal at an equivalent developmental landmark.

Stage matching is not always straightforward. If the feature used to identify a stage is itself affected by the mutation, defining the stage by that feature can make the analysis circular. For example, a mutation that prevents heart-shaped outgrowth cannot be evaluated by selecting only mutant embryos with a normal heart-shaped outline. Instead, investigators might compare the number of divisions, the arrangement of internal cells or independent markers of developmental progression. No single criterion is ideal, so the selected comparison should be explained.

A lineage-tracing experiment addresses a different question. If the apical daughter is labeled with a stable lineage marker after the first division, its descendants should retain the label under the assumptions that the marker remains detectable and does not move into other lineages. Labeling the basal daughter instead allows the contributions of the suspensor lineage to be examined. If label appears in the root organizing region, that finding supports a basal-lineage contribution but does not prove that every root cell has that origin. The location of the label and the identity of individual cells must be mapped precisely.

The effect of cell position can be investigated by perturbing a neighboring signaling domain without directly altering the receiving cell. For example, if a regulatory protein is restored specifically in the embryo proper of a mutant and the hypophysis resumes its normal developmental behavior, the receiving cell's fate can depend on activity elsewhere. Such an experiment can identify a non-cell-autonomous requirement. It does not immediately reveal the identity of the moving signal. Further perturbations that discriminate candidate signals are necessary for a detailed mechanistic explanation.

Loss-of-function and gain-of-function alleles must also be interpreted differently. A gene encoding a positive regulator may cause a developmental failure when its activity is lost. A gene encoding a negative regulator can produce a similar failure if a mutation makes the regulator excessively active or resistant to degradation. Thus two rootless mutants may have opposite changes in the activities of different proteins. The auxin-response examples involving MONOPTEROS and stabilized BODENLOS illustrate this point. An examination question may supply the molecular behavior of an allele rather than requiring memorization of a particular phenotype. The student should then reason through the pathway.

An epistasis experiment uses combinations of mutations to explore the relationships among regulatory components. Consider a hypothetical simple pathway in which a kinase activates a transcription factor and the transcription factor promotes specification of a basal identity. A constitutively active version of the transcription factor might restore a basal identity marker in a kinase-deficient background. This result would be consistent with the factor acting downstream of the kinase in the branch of the pathway that controls the marker. It would not prove that all kinase functions are mediated by that factor. A persistent defect in cell elongation could reveal an additional kinase-dependent branch.

The distinction between rescue of a marker and rescue of organismal development is equally important. Suppose a manipulated mutant embryo restores expression of a root marker but remains unable to form an organized root meristem. The result demonstrates partial restoration of a molecular feature. It does not establish complete developmental rescue. Conversely, a seedling that survives and grows may have compensated for an earlier abnormality rather than following a fully normal embryonic trajectory. Recording several levels of phenotype provides a more accurate account of the biological effect.

Quantitative datasets should preserve the correct sampling unit. If a researcher measures two daughter cells in each of fifty embryos, there are fifty pairs of observations, not one hundred independent embryos. Within each pair, cell lengths and volumes may be related because the daughters share the same parent cell and environment. The ratio of basal to apical size for each embryo can therefore be useful for describing geometric asymmetry. When comparing ratios between groups, the distribution of values is more informative than a single representative photograph.

Imagine a hypothetical set of forty normal embryos and forty mutant embryos. Thirty-six normal embryos and twelve mutant embryos display a clearly recognizable hypophyseal division at an equivalent stage. The observed proportions are 0.90 and 0.30. The difference is 0.60, or sixty percentage points. The mutant frequency is one third of the normal frequency under these sampling conditions. These values summarize the observed phenotype but do not alone establish which molecular mechanism failed. The experiment should specify the genotype, stage criteria, marker or morphological definition of the division, and whether scoring was performed without knowledge of genotype.

Another dataset might report similar proportions but with different total sample sizes. A result of three out of ten and thirty out of one hundred both gives 30 percent, but the larger sample generally provides a more precise estimate under comparable independent sampling. Replication across independent parent plants or experimental batches matters because embryos from the same plant can share environmental and genetic influences. A student need not perform an advanced statistical analysis to recognize that percentages without denominators are insufficient for evaluating evidence. The appropriate interpretation is that the observed difference supports a hypothesis to be tested further rather than proving a specific causal pathway by itself.

15. Integrating symmetry, mechanics and genetic information

The word symmetry is sometimes used loosely to mean an attractive or balanced shape. In developmental biology it describes spatial relationships that can be compared under transformations. An ideal radial structure has repeated or equivalent organization around an axis, so rotation around that axis preserves the relevant pattern. A bilaterally symmetric structure has two corresponding sides separated by a plane. A polar structure has distinguishable ends, so reversing the direction along its axis changes the biological interpretation. These definitions allow a single embryo to be described accurately even as it changes shape.

The early globular Arabidopsis embryo has an approximately radial arrangement of outer and inner domains about an already specified apical–basal direction. The presence of a suspensor and different molecular domains makes its two ends inequivalent. During cotyledon initiation the upper region acquires an approximately bilateral arrangement. A transverse section of the developing root may continue to exhibit radial tissue organization. There is no contradiction between these statements because they refer to different regions, developmental stages and spatial transformations.

Symmetry breaking refers to the establishment of a difference between regions that previously were more nearly equivalent in a specified respect. The term must not imply that the unfertilized egg was necessarily a perfectly homogeneous sphere. It already has an environment and intracellular organization. Fertilization, zygote reorganization and subsequent signaling create or reinforce asymmetries relevant to the developing embryo. Later organ initiation breaks the approximate rotational symmetry of the apical region by selecting particular positions for outgrowth. Development consists of a series of such reorganizations rather than one unique symmetry-breaking event that determines every future feature.

Geometry constrains developmental signaling as well as being influenced by it. Consider a signal exchanged between neighboring cells across shared surfaces. When a division adds a wall, the number of cells and the arrangement of their contacts change. An internal cell may be separated from the external environment by a new outer neighbor. A signal that was once delivered directly to a surface cell might now reach an interior cell through additional transport steps. In this way a division can change the set of signals a descendant receives even if the original genetic contents were distributed equally. Geometry and gene regulation form a reciprocal relationship.

The same principle applies to auxin transport. A network of polarized cell faces creates a pattern of possible movement through a multicellular structure. If a division creates new membrane faces, the daughter cells must organize transporters in the new geometry. A transport pattern established in the two-cell embryo cannot simply remain unchanged as the tissue grows into a sphere with distinct internal layers. Reorganization is expected because the cells and their neighbors have changed. Directional transport and division geometry work together to establish a developmental coordinate system.

A physical model of growth clarifies why the coordinated expansion of neighboring cells matters. Each cell wall is mechanically connected to adjacent tissue. If one region expands faster than another, constraints at their boundary influence the shape of the whole structure. The final shape is not obtained by adding independent growth vectors from isolated cells without accounting for their attachments. An organ primordium can emerge when local proliferation, expansion and mechanical properties produce outgrowth relative to neighboring regions. Regulatory signals influence those local properties, but the visible shape is the product of a tissue-level process.

This also explains why a developmental mutation can change morphology indirectly. A gene affecting wall synthesis or cytokinesis may cause abnormal cell shapes and division arrangements that alter the signaling environment. Conversely, a regulatory mutation may alter growth patterns while leaving basic wall chemistry intact. An observed change in organ shape therefore has several plausible explanations. The most useful developmental analysis connects molecular events, cellular behavior and tissue architecture with evidence at each level.

A coherent model of plant embryogenesis begins with fertilization and the establishment of a polarized zygote. Unequal division initiates two lineages with distinct normal developmental roles. Oriented divisions construct the early cellular framework. Intercellular signals, including dynamic auxin transport and response, help specify axial regions and the hypophysis. Divisions that distinguish outside from inside provide the geometry for primary tissue domains. Regulatory programs refine these domains, and local organ initiation and differential growth produce the recognizable heart and torpedo stages. The mature embryo retains the spatial organization necessary for postembryonic development.

The limits of this model matter just as much as its usefulness. Arabidopsis is a representative species, not a universal cellular blueprint. A hormone-response maximum is not a direct concentration measurement. A marker is not equivalent to a complete fate. A division plane is not a complete fate specification mechanism. The embryo's genotype is not the only influence on its environment. These distinctions do not weaken the scientific explanation; they identify the evidence necessary to apply it correctly to a new observation.

At the next developmental scale, the embryo must be considered together with the seed compartments that support and protect it. A correctly patterned embryo still requires an appropriate physiological environment and coordinated seed growth. Endosperm and maternal integuments have different genetic origins from the embryo and follow their own developmental trajectories. The next chapter examines those relationships. The present chapter establishes why an embryo is already a spatially organized plant body before seed maturation and germination transform it into a growing seedling.

Example 1 — weak acid equilibrium

Assume IAA pKa = 4.75, external pH = 5.50, cytosolic pH = 7.00. For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH−pKa), so fraction HA = 1/(1 + 10^(pH−pKa)). At pH 5.50 the ratio is 5.623 and the fraction is 0.15098 (15.10%); at pH 7.00 the ratio is 177.83 and the fraction is 0.00559 (0.559%). This estimates equilibrium chemical fractions only, not concentrations or net flux. Actual cellular pH and transport vary.

Example 2 — partition of a determinant

Take apical volume 20 µm³ and basal volume 80 µm³ in a hypothetical embryo. A uniformly distributed solute at 3 arbitrary amount units per µm³ gives 60 units and 240 units respectively, with equal concentrations. If 120 units instead accumulate in the apical daughter and 180 in the basal daughter, the concentrations are 6 and 2.25 units/µm³. A response threshold of 4 units/µm³ would be reached only apically under these assumptions. No biological threshold is implied.

Example 3 — cell number and tissue layers

Begin with 8 outer and 8 inner cells. Each outer cell divides once anticlinally; the resulting counts are 16 and 8, total 24. Outer fraction = 16/24 = 66.7%. A periclinal division of each original outer cell instead produces 8 outer and 16 inner cells if all new internal daughters are counted as inner: outer fraction 8/24 = 33.3%. Equal total counts do not imply equal geometry. Assumes no additional divisions or cell death.

Example 4 — growth and aspect ratio

For a simplified region 50 µm long and 40 µm wide, L/W = 1.25. Later dimensions 100 µm and 50 µm give L/W = 2.0; length change = (100−50)/50 = 100%; width change = (50−40)/40 = 25%. The shape change is consistent with anisotropic growth but cannot distinguish oriented proliferation from expansion.

Example 5 — phenotype frequency

In the hypothetical dataset above, 36/40 = 0.90 and 12/40 = 0.30. Absolute difference = 0.60 or 60 percentage points; ratio of mutant to normal frequency = 0.30/0.90 = 1/3. The values are observed sample proportions, not proof of a specific molecular mechanism. Independent sampling, stage matching and scoring controls matter.

  1. 1. A diploid plant has 2n = 10. What is the chromosome number in each normal daughter of the first zygotic mitosis?

    1. 5
    2. 15
    3. 10
    4. 20
    Answer and explanation

    C — 10. Mitosis preserves 2n. Five is the haploid gamete number; 15 could represent triploid endosperm, and 20 confuses replicated chromatids with chromosome number.

  2. 2. Which statement best identifies the hypophysis in Arabidopsis?

    1. Uppermost suspensor cell adjacent to embryo proper
    2. Entire basal daughter lineage
    3. First apical daughter
    4. A maternal integument cell
    Answer and explanation

    A — Uppermost suspensor cell adjacent to embryo proper. The hypophysis is a particular basal-lineage cell; the entire basal lineage is broader, the apical daughter has a different ancestry, and integuments are maternal.

  3. 3. A periclinal division in an early embryonic surface cell primarily produces daughters differing in which dimension?

    1. Chromosome complement
    2. Paternal allele count
    3. Time of fertilization
    4. Depth relative to the local surface
    Answer and explanation

    D — Depth relative to the local surface. The new wall is parallel to the surface and creates outer and inner positions. The other properties do not follow from normal wall orientation.

  4. 4. Which structure normally arises from the central cell fertilization product rather than the zygote?

    1. Cotyledon
    2. Endosperm
    3. Hypocotyl
    4. Radicle
    Answer and explanation

    B — Endosperm. Endosperm and embryo have different fertilization origins; all three other structures belong to the embryo.

  5. 5. Which protein's early apical localization in the basal daughter is associated with auxin transport toward the apical lineage?

    1. ATML1
    2. PIN7
    3. BDL/IAA12
    4. CUC2
    Answer and explanation

    B — PIN7. PIN7 contributes to efflux; ATML1 is an epidermal regulator, BDL a repressor, and CUC2 participates in organ boundaries.

  6. 6. A heart-stage Arabidopsis embryo displays which characteristic?

    1. Three animal germ layers
    2. Two separate zygotes
    3. An entirely mature root vascular system
    4. Paired cotyledon primordia
    Answer and explanation

    D — Paired cotyledon primordia. The paired primordia account for the shape. Plant tissues are not animal germ layers; the embryo derives from one zygote, and the vasculature need not be mature.

  7. 7. Which of the following correctly separates lineage and fate?

    1. Lineage describes ancestry; fate describes normal developmental outcome
    2. Lineage denotes tissue shape; fate denotes chromosome number
    3. Lineage and fate are identical by definition
    4. Fate can be established only by measuring cell size
    Answer and explanation

    A — Lineage describes ancestry; fate describes normal developmental outcome. Ancestry and outcome are conceptually distinct; neither chromosome number nor size establishes fate.

  8. 8. What is the most accurate description of DR5 fluorescence?

    1. Direct absolute auxin concentration
    2. Direct ATP measurement
    3. Auxin-responsive transcription readout
    4. PIN protein movement rate
    Answer and explanation

    C — Auxin-responsive transcription readout. DR5 responds to auxin signaling. It does not directly quantify hormone concentration, ATP, or transport kinetics.

  9. 9. Which pair is associated with epidermal identity in Arabidopsis?

    1. IZUMO1 and JUNO
    2. Bicoid and Nanos
    3. Igf2 and H19
    4. ATML1 and PDF2
    Answer and explanation

    D — ATML1 and PDF2. ATML1/PDF2 are plant HD-ZIP IV factors. The alternatives belong to animal fertilization, fly axis formation and mammalian imprinting.

  10. 10. Which is a characteristic of a grass embryo?

    1. Two cotyledons defining a universal heart stage
    2. One cotyledon, the scutellum
    3. No root pole
    4. No vascular precursors
    Answer and explanation

    B — One cotyledon, the scutellum. The scutellum is the grass cotyledon; grasses still establish root and shoot domains and conducting tissues.

  11. 11. Which cells principally generate the embryo proper?

    1. Maternal integument descendants
    2. Central cell descendants
    3. Apical daughter descendants
    4. Synergid descendants
    Answer and explanation

    C — Apical daughter descendants. The apical lineage generates most embryo proper; the other choices are distinct tissues or gametophytic cells.

  12. 12. Which statement about axial polarity and radial pattern is correct?

    1. Both may coexist in one embryo
    2. Radial organization requires identical ends
    3. Polarity requires animal gastrulation
    4. Radial organization requires a haploid embryo
    Answer and explanation

    A — Both may coexist in one embryo. Different symmetry dimensions coexist. Neither animal gastrulation nor haploidy is required.

  13. 13. In the canonical nuclear auxin pathway, auxin promotes degradation of which regulatory class?

    1. All ARF transcription factors
    2. All PIN transporters
    3. AUX/IAA repressors
    4. Cellulose microfibrils
    Answer and explanation

    C — AUX/IAA repressors. Auxin promotes receptor-mediated AUX/IAA turnover; ARFs act downstream, PINs transport auxin, and cellulose is a wall component.

  14. 14. Which gene product is an ARF implicated in embryonic root initiation?

    1. MONOPTEROS
    2. YODA
    3. WOX8
    4. SSP
    Answer and explanation

    A — MONOPTEROS. MP is ARF5. YODA is a MAPKKK, WOX8 a homeobox transcription factor and SSP an upstream signaling component.

  15. 15. The upper lens-shaped daughter of the hypophysis contributes to which region?

    1. Entire cotyledon
    2. Seed coat
    3. Endosperm
    4. Quiescent center lineage
    Answer and explanation

    D — Quiescent center lineage. The upper daughter contributes to QC; the other structures have distinct identities and origins.

  16. 16. Which stage immediately follows the idealized four-cell apical-lineage stage?

    1. Mature bent embryo
    2. Octant
    3. Torpedo
    4. Germinating seedling
    Answer and explanation

    B — Octant. A transverse partition creates upper and lower tiers. The other stages occur substantially later.

    1. Absence of intercellular signals
    2. Oriented division with persistent cellular neighborhoods
    3. Absence of gene regulation
    4. All cells remaining undifferentiated
    Answer and explanation

    B — Oriented division with persistent cellular neighborhoods. Walls generally maintain neighbor relations, unlike extensive migration in many animal embryos. Plant development uses signals and differentiation.

  18. 18. Which statement correctly describes SSP in Arabidopsis zygote signaling?

    1. It is the only maternal organelle
    2. It directly becomes the root cap
    3. It is a mature xylem transporter
    4. Sperm-delivered RNA can contribute to YODA activation after fertilization
    Answer and explanation

    D — Sperm-delivered RNA can contribute to YODA activation after fertilization. SSP provides a paternal regulatory contribution, not an organelle, tissue or conducting system.

  19. 19. Which region lies between cotyledons and the root in the embryonic axis?

    1. Hypocotyl
    2. Endosperm
    3. Seed coat
    4. Pollen tube
    Answer and explanation

    A — Hypocotyl. The hypocotyl connects cotyledonary and root regions; the other structures are not that embryonic segment.

    1. Cell wall polysaccharide
    2. Auxin response factor ARF5
    3. MAP kinase kinase kinase
    4. Fluorescent reporter
    Answer and explanation

    C — MAP kinase kinase kinase. YODA is a MAPKKK; the others identify wall material, MP or an experimental readout.

    1. Chromosomes move wholesale between daughters
    2. Both cells must have identical promoter activity
    3. The reporter proves both cells have identical fate
    4. Protein movement between cells
    Answer and explanation

    D — Protein movement between cells. Protein localization may extend beyond transcription. Mobile reporters and retained function must be controlled; chromosome transfer and identical fate are not implied.

    1. Both necessarily lack all auxin biosynthesis
    2. A may disrupt transport; B may disrupt response despite hormone availability
    3. B lacks a membrane efflux transporter by definition
    4. A and B must have identical upstream lesions
    Answer and explanation

    B — A may disrupt transport; B may disrupt response despite hormone availability. PIN localization affects delivery; a stabilized repressor blocks transcriptional response. Similar phenotype does not imply the same lesion.

    1. 8
    2. 5
    3. 13
    4. 40
    Answer and explanation

    3. Four times greater in the apical daughter

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    C — A later or parallel requirement may be defective. The reporter readout is retained but the fate outcome is not. This does not exclude auxin or establish concentration.

    4. The root forms entirely from apical descendants
    Answer and explanation

    A — MP activity can contribute non-cell-autonomously to that division. Rescue locates a relevant signaling domain, but does not by itself identify the moving molecule or all MP functions.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    Answer and explanation

    4. Screen orientation of its axis
    Answer and explanation

    D — Screen orientation of its axis. Orientation on a screen changes; biological identities and ancestry do not.

  43. 43. A phenotype is observed in embryos from mutant mothers even after crossing to wild-type fathers. Which further test best distinguishes maternal tissue effects from zygotic effects?

    1. Reciprocal crosses plus segregation and tissue-specific rescue
    2. Count cotyledons only in the maternal plant
    3. Assume paternal alleles are silenced
    4. Conclude all embryos are triploid
    Answer and explanation

    A — Reciprocal crosses plus segregation and tissue-specific rescue. The reciprocal and tissue-specific tests localize requirements; maternal association alone does not establish imprinting or ploidy.

  44. 44. A mutant displays a ground tissue marker in one internal layer where normal embryos later show two layers. Which explanation best fits the evidence?

    1. No ground tissue identity at all
    2. Direct conversion of plant ground tissue into animal mesoderm
    3. Possible subdivision defect with retained initial ground identity
    4. Complete loss of zygote fertilization
    Answer and explanation

    C — Possible subdivision defect with retained initial ground identity. The marker suggests some retained identity, and layer number suggests a later division defect. Full identity needs multiple markers.

  45. 45. A grass embryo has a single cotyledon, but clear shoot and root poles. Which statement best interprets this?

    1. The embryo is apolar
    2. The embryo must be a defective eudicot
    3. The root pole cannot arise in monocots
    4. Cotyledon number and axial polarity are distinct properties
    Answer and explanation

    D — Cotyledon number and axial polarity are distinct properties. The shared axial plan accommodates different cotyledon architectures; neither defect nor absence of root follows.

  46. 46. Assertion–reason: A: An embryo can remain polar at the globular stage. R: A rounded outline does not require identical apical and basal identities.

    1. Both true; R does not explain A
    2. Both true; R explains A
    3. A true; R false
    4. A false; R true
    Answer and explanation

    B — Both true; R explains A. Shape alone does not determine internal pattern. The reason explains why polarity can coexist with a rounded outline.

  47. 47. Assertion–reason: A: DR5 intensity alone gives exact IAA concentration. R: DR5 measures auxin-responsive transcription.

    1. Both true; R explains A
    2. Both true; R unrelated
    3. A false; R true
    4. A true; R false
    Answer and explanation

    C — A false; R true. The reporter depends on downstream response and competence, not a direct calibrated measurement.

  48. 48. Which sequence correctly orders events in the representative Arabidopsis model?

    1. Zygote → two-cell → octant → globular → heart → torpedo
    2. Heart → octant → two-cell → zygote
    3. Globular → zygote → torpedo → heart
    4. Torpedo → heart → octant → globular
    Answer and explanation

    A — Zygote → two-cell → octant → globular → heart → torpedo. The first sequence follows early divisions, radial pattern and organ outgrowth; the alternatives reverse landmarks.

  49. 49. Multiple statements: I. Basal descendants contribute to hypophysis. II. Hypophysis makes all root cells. III. Apical descendants contribute most embryo proper. Which are correct?

    1. II and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I and III only
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and explanation

    C — I and III only. The hypophysis has restricted contributions, while other root cells derive from embryo proper; II is false.

  50. 50. Match 1. PIN7; 2. ARF5; 3. IAA12; 4. ATML1 with a. efflux-associated transport; b. auxin response factor; c. repressor; d. epidermal regulator.

    1. 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d
    2. 1-b, 2-a, 3-d, 4-c
    3. 1-d, 2-c, 3-b, 4-a
    4. 1-c, 2-d, 3-a, 4-b
    Answer and explanation

    A — 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d. Each match distinguishes transporter, ARF, AUX/IAA and epidermal regulator. All other mappings misassign at least one molecular role.

  51. 51. In Figure 10, the mutant bar is 30% and normal bar 90%. What is the mutant-to-normal ratio?

    1. 3
    2. 0.6
    3. 2/3
    4. 1/3
    Answer and explanation

    D — 1/3. 30/90=1/3. Three is the inverse, 0.6 is the absolute proportion difference and 2/3 is the fractional reduction.

  52. 52. Which combination of evidence best supports directional auxin transport in early embryogenesis?

    1. One mature seed photograph alone
    2. Polar transporter localization, reporter distribution, and perturbation-induced changes
    3. A count of chromosomes only
    4. A single cotyledon outline without controls
    Answer and explanation

    B — Polar transporter localization, reporter distribution, and perturbation-induced changes. Combining localization, response and functional perturbation links transport to development; isolated anatomical or chromosome data do not.

  53. 53. Assertion–reason: A: Two equal-sized daughters must have identical developmental fates. R: Fate also depends on regulatory state and position.

    1. Both true; R explains A
    2. A false; R true
    3. A true; R false
    4. Both false
    Answer and explanation

    B — A false; R true. Equal geometry does not ensure identical molecular inheritance or signaling context. The reason is correct.

  54. 54. A hypothetical inhibitor selectively abolishes cell division but leaves wall expansion intact briefly. What immediate observation could still occur?

    1. Production of many new cell walls by mitosis
    2. Automatic duplication of root meristem identity
    3. Formation of triploid endosperm from embryo
    4. Increase in cell size without increase in cell number
    Answer and explanation

    D — Increase in cell size without increase in cell number. Expansion can occur without division over a limited interval; the other consequences do not follow from the stated intervention.

Revision and mastery

Embryogenesis establishes the primary plant body through intracellular polarization, asymmetric zygotic division, cell lineage diversification, directional transport, positional signaling, oriented divisions and differential growth. The Arabidopsis model distinguishes apical–basal polarity, radial tissue pattern and the later bilateral apical organization.

Follow the sequence: zygote → two daughters → embryo proper and suspensor → octant → outer and inner domains → globular embryo and hypophysis specification → cotyledon initiation → heart → torpedo → mature embryo. Remember that stages overlap and the named shapes are model-specific.

Mechanistic anchors: SSP–YODA signaling supports early zygote development; WOX factors contribute to lineage regulation; PIN transport localization changes with stage; auxin promotes AUX/IAA turnover; MP-dependent signaling helps specify hypophysis; periclinal division produces depth differences; cotyledon boundary regulation preserves an organizing region.

Useful calculations: concentration = amount/volume; protonated fraction of a monoprotic weak acid = 1/(1 + 10^(pH−pKa)); aspect ratio = length/width; observed phenotype proportion = affected or qualifying count/total scored. State assumptions rather than using these as universal predictive models.
