1. One organelle family, many cellular identities

A thin slice of a potato tuber reveals rounded, iodine-staining starch bodies. A slice of a green leaf reveals numerous small green bodies arranged around the cell periphery. A ripe red tomato contains cells filled with coloured particles, even though the young fruit was green. At first these observations seem to involve unrelated structures: food reserves, photosynthetic machines and pigment stores. Cell biology connects them through a single family of organelles, the plastids. What changes is not simply their colour but the machinery installed inside them, the metabolites they make and the tasks that the surrounding tissue requires.

A plastid is a membrane-bounded organelle of the plant and algal cell lineage, originally derived from a photosynthetic bacterial ancestor. In a land plant the typical plastid has an envelope consisting of an outer and an inner membrane. It also possesses its own genetic system, although most proteins required for its operation are encoded in the nucleus. A chloroplast is one specialized kind of plastid. The relationship is therefore asymmetric: every chloroplast is a plastid, but many plastids do not photosynthesize. Colourless plastids are neither empty chloroplasts nor structures belonging to a completely independent organelle family.

Recall the concept of compartmentalization from the endoplasmic reticulum, lysosome and mitochondrion. A membrane permits a cell to maintain different chemical environments in adjacent regions, provided substances are transported selectively. Plastids make particularly good use of this principle because their biochemical activities include both membrane-associated energy conversion and soluble enzyme reactions. In a chloroplast these activities are separated into distinct membrane systems and aqueous spaces. In an amyloplast a different internal organization supports the accumulation of large quantities of carbohydrate without raising the concentration of dissolved sugar to an equally large extent.

Most ordinary animal cells have no plastids, while plants contain mitochondria as well as plastids. A green cell does not replace its mitochondria with chloroplasts. Energy captured during illumination is first used within the photosynthetic machinery and in carbon assimilation; mitochondria still support respiratory metabolism. A root cell, even though it is part of a photosynthetic organism, normally receives organic carbon from other tissues and uses respiration. Its plastids can nevertheless carry out biosynthetic and storage functions. Thus the appropriate unit of explanation is the tissue and the cell, not simply the organism.

By the end of this chapter, the aim is to predict the internal structure of a plastid from its function, recognize the limits of a microscopic observation, explain changes during differentiation and identify the precise membrane or aqueous compartment involved in a biochemical process. These are closely related problems: a structure is useful only when it helps explain the chemistry that happens in and around it.

2. The origin and continuity of the plastid family

An obvious question follows from the presence of two envelope membranes and a separate genetic system: why do plastids resemble small bacterial cells in certain respects? The primary endosymbiotic model explains these features by descent from a cyanobacterium that became established inside an ancestral eukaryotic host. Instead of being digested, the internal photosynthetic cell participated in a stable association with the host. Over evolutionary time this association became an integrated organelle rather than a pair of independently viable organisms. Modern plastids are descendants of earlier plastids, not cyanobacteria newly captured by each generation of plant cells.

Several independent observations support this model. Chloroplast ribosomes resemble bacterial ribosomes in their overall organization, whereas the major cytosolic ribosomes of plant cells are eukaryotic. Chloroplast genetic sequences can be compared with bacterial sequences, and many reveal cyanobacterial ancestry. The plastid divides by a constriction process involving bacterial-related division machinery, and the organization of oxygenic photosynthetic electron transport has close parallels with that of cyanobacteria. The envelope structure is consistent with a long history of internalization and remodeling. No single feature is sufficient by itself: two membranes can occur in organelles with different functions. The strength of the explanation comes from agreement between multiple kinds of evidence.

The endosymbiont did not retain its entire original gene complement. Genes were lost or transferred to the host nuclear genome, and protein import systems evolved to bring many nuclear-encoded products back into the plastid. This historical transformation explains an otherwise puzzling feature of present-day cells: chloroplasts possess DNA and ribosomes yet require extensive support from the nucleus. Their semi-autonomy means limited internal genetic and synthetic capacity, not independent reproduction outside the host. Inhibiting the synthesis of nuclear-encoded proteins eventually affects plastid function, even if plastid translation is initially unaffected.

The primary plastids of land plants have the familiar two-membrane envelope. Some algae contain plastids of secondary endosymbiotic origin, acquired through an ancestral host taking up another eukaryotic alga. These plastids may have additional surrounding membranes. Such cases warn against treating the membrane number of the textbook land-plant chloroplast as a universal criterion for all algal plastids. Cyanobacteria themselves do not contain chloroplasts: they are prokaryotic cells with their own photosynthetic membrane system rather than eukaryotic cells containing a separate photosynthetic organelle.

Endosymbiotic history also helps explain continuity during ordinary development. Plastids generally increase in number by division of existing plastids. A meristematic cell passes plastids to its daughter cells, and descendants of these organelles can later specialize. One need not invoke a separate evolutionary origin for the potato amyloplast and the leaf chloroplast. They share a common plastid ancestry within the plant, even though their mature contents look strikingly different.

3. Proplastids and the developmental decision

The starting point for many plastids in a developing plant is the proplastid. These small, relatively undifferentiated organelles are found particularly in meristematic and embryonic cells. Compared with a mature chloroplast, a proplastid has little internal membrane development and lacks the extensive photosynthetic apparatus required to support high rates of carbon assimilation. Nevertheless, it retains the organization needed to grow, divide, express plastid genes and import nuclear-encoded proteins. The absence of chlorophyll at this stage is therefore not evidence of an absent plastid.

Consider two descendants of the same meristem: a young leaf mesophyll cell exposed to daylight and an internal root cell growing in soil. Their nuclear genomes are largely the same, yet the regulatory signals and metabolic demands of the tissues differ. In the first, expression of photosynthesis-associated proteins, pigment synthesis and internal membrane development are coordinated. In the second, photosynthetic differentiation is usually suppressed and the plastid may instead specialize in starch metabolism or other biosynthetic tasks. Differentiation involves both nuclear gene expression and the changing state of the plastid itself. The resulting organelles have different proteomes, meaning different collections and relative amounts of proteins, not necessarily different sets of inherited plastid genes.

The term differentiation describes acquisition of a specialized state; interconversion describes a transition between recognizable plastid states. These are developmental processes rather than a simple relabeling of a coloured object. A plastid becoming a chloroplast must construct membranes, incorporate reaction-centre and antenna proteins, synthesize pigments and coordinate enzymes that use the newly available energy. A plastid becoming a chromoplast must often dismantle much of that photosynthetic apparatus while building structures that accumulate carotenoids. These transformations can involve changes in shape, protein turnover and metabolism as well as pigment content.

A lineage map is useful if interpreted carefully. Proplastids can give rise to different mature states, and certain mature forms can transform into others under appropriate conditions. There is no obligatory universal sequence in which all plastids become chloroplasts, then chromoplasts and finally leucoplasts. Tissue identity, species, age and environment affect which transitions are possible. The same developmental endpoint can be approached from different starting states, so one should not infer the previous identity of a chromoplast solely from the fact that it is red.

Plastid specialization is also not an all-or-none property. A photosynthetic chloroplast can store transient starch, and a non-green plastid may still synthesize fatty acids, amino acids or pigment precursors. The name identifies its dominant characteristic in the relevant tissue. This distinction prevents an especially common examination error: assuming that a plastid containing a starch grain must be an amyloplast, even if it also has a fully developed chlorophyll-containing thylakoid system.

4. Leucoplasts: storing carbon and supporting biosynthesis

A potato tuber is a sink tissue. It receives photosynthetic products made elsewhere, especially sucrose transported from leaves. It must store the carbon in a form that can be mobilized during sprouting without maintaining an extremely high concentration of soluble sugars in its cytoplasm. Amyloplasts meet this requirement by converting imported carbon into starch, which accumulates as poorly soluble granules within the plastid. The amyloplast envelope separates the reactions that produce starch from the surrounding cytosol; appropriate transport proteins allow suitable carbon precursors and other metabolites to enter and products to leave.

Starch consists primarily of glucose polymers, amylose and amylopectin. The detailed enzymatic synthesis of these polymers belongs to carbohydrate metabolism, but their physical state explains why amyloplasts are effective storage structures. A dissolved glucose molecule behaves as an independent osmotically active particle. Linking many residues into a large polymer reduces the number of independently dissolved particles; crystallization and granule formation reduce their osmotic contribution further. A cell can therefore accumulate substantial carbohydrate while minimizing the direct osmotic consequences that an equivalent amount of freely dissolved glucose would impose.

A simple calculation makes the distinction concrete. Imagine 0.10 mol of glucose residues dispersed as separate glucose molecules in a litre of water, and compare this with the hypothetical state in which exactly 1,000 residues form each soluble, non-interacting polymer. The monomer concentration is 0.10 mol L⁻¹. The polymer-particle concentration is 0.10/1,000 = 0.00010 mol L⁻¹. Under ideal dilute-solution assumptions, osmotic pressure is proportional to particle concentration, so the hypothetical polymer solution would contribute one-thousandth of the osmotic pressure. Actual starch granules are not an ideal solution of uniform polymers, and living cells contain many additional solutes; the calculation demonstrates the direction of the effect rather than predicting the turgor of a real tuber.

Amyloplasts may have a specialized sensory role. In certain gravity-sensing cells, such as the columella cells of root caps, dense starch-containing plastids can sediment when the orientation of the organ changes. Their displacement contributes to a signaling process that adjusts growth direction. These amyloplasts are called statoliths in this context. The gravity-sensing cell is the statocyte. The dense granule provides a physical means of relating the position of an organelle to gravity; conversion of displacement into differential growth requires signaling and is not accomplished by starch alone. Not all amyloplasts are statoliths, and gravity perception is not identical in all plant tissues.

Other leucoplast categories include elaioplasts, associated with lipid synthesis or accumulation, and proteinoplasts, sometimes called aleuroplasts, associated with protein-rich inclusions. These names should not be applied to every lipid droplet or every protein storage body in a plant cell. Many seed storage proteins accumulate in specialized vacuoles and many neutral lipids accumulate in cytosolic oil bodies derived from the ER. To identify a storage plastid, the location and boundary of the inclusion must be established, not merely its chemical composition. A plastid can also be relatively unspecialized in storage yet participate in essential metabolic reactions. Leucoplast is therefore a useful broad category, not a synonym for an inert container.

In a developing seed or tuber, the conversion of imported carbon into reserves demonstrates that plastid metabolism is integrated with whole-cell metabolism even without photosynthesis. An amyloplast requires energy and precursor supply. It cannot simply produce large amounts of starch because it has a double envelope or because it possesses DNA. The relevant enzymes, substrate transport and developmental regulation all have to be present. This is the same structure–function logic that will be applied to the more elaborate chloroplast.

5. Chromoplasts, etioplasts and senescence

The colour of a ripe tomato is a useful example of chromoplast differentiation. Many developing tomato fruits initially contain green chloroplasts. As ripening proceeds, chlorophyll abundance decreases, photosynthesis-associated proteins and thylakoid organization are remodeled, and carotenoids such as lycopene accumulate. Chromoplasts provide a suitable environment for synthesizing and sequestering these hydrophobic pigments. A carotenoid does not dissolve freely in the aqueous stroma at arbitrarily high concentration; specialized lipid-rich structures, membranes or crystals help accommodate it. The particular ultrastructure varies with species and pigment composition.

A carrot root illustrates why the tomato route must not be universalized. Its orange carotenoid-rich plastids do not necessarily pass through a mature green photosynthetic state. Likewise, yellow or orange petals may develop chromoplasts as part of flower differentiation rather than fruit ripening. The biological function of visible carotenoid accumulation may include attraction of pollinators or seed dispersers, while pigments can also have roles in plastid metabolism and protection. Not every red or purple plant tissue contains red chromoplasts. Anthocyanins, for example, are water-soluble pigments commonly accumulated in vacuoles. Pigment chemistry and subcellular localization, not colour alone, determine the appropriate organelle designation.

In green leaves, carotenoids already occur in the chloroplast, where they participate in light harvesting and photoprotection. The transition to a chromoplast is therefore not the first appearance of carotenoids in a previously carotenoid-free organelle. It involves a change in their abundance, composition, storage context and functional relationship to the photosynthetic apparatus. A green leaf can appear yellow as chlorophyll disappears and existing carotenoids become visually prominent; this does not necessarily imply the same developmental program as tomato ripening.

An etioplast is another instructive developmental state. A dark-grown seedling of a typical angiosperm can develop plastids with a characteristic internal prolamellar body, an ordered tubular membrane network containing protochlorophyllide and associated proteins. These plastids are adapted to subsequent greening. In darkness the usual light-dependent protochlorophyllide reduction step of angiosperms cannot proceed efficiently, limiting chlorophyll production. The plant can still build precursor-containing membranes and some proteins in preparation for exposure to light. An etioplast is thus an organized state, not simply a chloroplast from which the light has been switched off.

When the seedling emerges into light, the pigment precursor is converted toward chlorophyll, the prolamellar body reorganizes, thylakoids develop and photosynthetic proteins accumulate. Acquisition of pigment alone is insufficient for full function: chlorophyll must be associated correctly with proteins, membrane organization must permit electron transport and the soluble carbon-assimilation machinery must be present. The process takes time and involves coordinated nuclear and plastid gene expression. A green seedling with defective thylakoid assembly may still be poor at photosynthesis. Some plant lineages and other photosynthetic organisms can synthesize chlorophyll in darkness using a light-independent enzyme system, so the familiar angiosperm example should not be generalized to every chlorophyll-containing organism.

During leaf senescence, a chloroplast may become a gerontoplast. The photosynthetic apparatus is progressively dismantled, chlorophyll is degraded, lipid-rich plastoglobules often enlarge and nutrients such as nitrogen are remobilized for use elsewhere in the plant. This is an age-associated remodeling process, not the ordinary nocturnal disappearance of light. A healthy chloroplast remains a chloroplast during the night. Nor is a gerontoplast necessarily equivalent to a chromoplast; both may look yellowish and contain lipid-rich structures, but their developmental context and primary physiological roles differ.

Interconversions can occasionally proceed in the opposite direction. For instance, plastids of potato tissue exposed to light can acquire chloroplast characteristics, and re-greening has been observed in certain fruits under appropriate conditions. Such reversibility depends on developmental competence and environmental context. It does not mean that every terminally damaged or senescent plastid can become a fully functional chloroplast. The essential conclusion is that the internal composition of plastids is plastic, constrained by the tissue's regulatory state and the organelle's ability to rebuild its machinery.

6. Recognizing plastids without relying on colour alone

Microscopy allows us to test the classification developed above. In a green mesophyll cell, the pigment and lens-like organelle profiles provide a useful initial identification. Electron microscopy can reveal internal membrane stacks, while chlorophyll fluorescence supplies complementary evidence of pigment localization. In a potato cell, iodine staining identifies starch, but the existence of a surrounding plastid is inferred from the cellular context or confirmed by appropriate structural observation. A yellow pigment inclusion in a petal requires additional information because a vacuole and a plastid can both contribute to visible tissue coloration.

The most informative classification combines developmental origin, ultrastructure, composition and function. A specimen with abundant starch, no extensive thylakoid network and a recognizable plastid envelope is consistent with an amyloplast. A green organelle that also contains a starch inclusion is more likely a chloroplast if its light-harvesting structures are developed. A membrane-rich, chlorophyll-poor organelle with a prolamellar body in a dark-grown angiosperm seedling is consistent with an etioplast. A carotenoid-rich plastid in a ripening fruit may be a chromoplast, whereas an organelle in a senescing leaf must be interpreted in relation to the loss of photosynthetic structures and nutrient mobilization.

A single thin section is a two-dimensional sample of a three-dimensional object. A small section through a chloroplast can miss a granum or starch granule altogether. Conversely, a section through a compact membrane system can exaggerate the impression that membranes are separate disks. Examination of multiple profiles or complementary measurements is more reliable than counting one visible feature. Fixation and preparation can also change the appearance of lipid inclusions. In NET-style experimental questions, it is important to distinguish what the observed image establishes directly from the functional interpretation that depends on additional evidence.

7. Chloroplast ultrastructure: three membranes and three internal spaces

A mature chloroplast in the leaf of a typical flowering plant is often several micrometres long and flattened or lens-shaped. The exact size, number per cell and shape are variable. A large central vacuole pushes much of the cytoplasm towards the periphery in many mesophyll cells, and chloroplasts occupy that peripheral cytoplasm. Their location brings them close to the cell surface and to sites where carbon dioxide diffuses through the tissue. They are not embedded in the vacuolar lumen; the tonoplast separates that lumen from the cytoplasm containing chloroplasts.

Follow the boundaries from the cytoplasm inward. The first is the outer envelope membrane. Beneath it is a narrow aqueous intermembrane space, and then the inner envelope membrane. Together these membranes make the chloroplast envelope. The space enclosed by the inner envelope but lying outside the thylakoids is called the stroma. Suspended within this space is the extensive thylakoid membrane system, enclosing a further aqueous compartment, the thylakoid lumen. Thus there are three membrane systems in the familiar description: outer envelope, inner envelope and thylakoid. There are three corresponding internal aqueous compartments: envelope intermembrane space, stroma and lumen. Do not count the cytosol as an internal chloroplast compartment.

The outer and inner envelope membranes have different transport properties. The outer membrane contains channel proteins that permit many small solutes to pass, although it is not freely permeable to all molecules or macromolecules. The inner envelope provides more selective control of the stromal composition, with transporters for specific ions and metabolites. Maintaining appropriate stromal concentrations is necessary for enzyme activity, biosynthesis and exchange with the cytoplasm. Neither envelope membrane is the principal site of photosynthetic electron transport in a typical mature land-plant chloroplast.

A thylakoid is a flattened membrane-bounded region with an internal aqueous lumen. When portions of this membrane system are closely appressed in stacks, each stack is called a granum (plural grana). Unstacked stromal lamellae, also called stroma thylakoids or intergranal lamellae, connect regions of the system. The stacks and connecting lamellae should not be imagined as piles of freely floating, completely isolated coins. Three-dimensional organization in typical higher-plant chloroplasts permits connections within the thylakoid network, although the geometry of those connections is more complex than a simple drawing. The lumen is separated from the surrounding stroma by the thylakoid membrane, an essential distinction for energy conversion.

The stroma contains soluble enzymes, plastid nucleoids, ribosomes and metabolites. Transient starch grains and plastoglobules can also be observed. Plastoglobules are lipid-rich particles associated with thylakoid membranes and participate in lipid metabolism; they are not starch granules. Thylakoids contain pigments and protein complexes organized to absorb light, move electrons and create an ion gradient. The combination of a large membrane area and nearby aqueous enzyme-rich stroma allows the two functional systems to cooperate without mixing all their contents.

Three-dimensional topology is a stronger predictor of function than the visual appearance of a membrane. An enzyme located on the stromal surface of a thylakoid has immediate access to stromal substrates. An enzyme on the lumenal surface has access to a different ionic environment. A soluble protein located between the two envelope membranes is separated from the thylakoid lumen by at least the inner envelope and thylakoid membrane. These boundaries explain why a mutation in one transport protein can affect carbon assimilation without immediately abolishing the primary light reaction. They also explain why the description of a chloroplast as merely a double-membrane bag filled with chlorophyll is inadequate.

8. Grana, lamellae and the organization of photosynthetic membranes

Why make stacks of membranes rather than simply enlarge the chloroplast envelope? A chloroplast needs an extensive surface on which many pigment–protein complexes and energy-converting enzymes can be installed. Building internal membranes makes the available functional area much larger than the organelle's external surface, while allowing a separate lumen to be maintained. The flattened geometry brings large membrane surfaces into close proximity; stacking and unstacking also help organize different components laterally. Increased area is important, but stacking itself does not create new lipid molecules or automatically guarantee a higher rate of photosynthesis.

The lipid composition of thylakoids differs from that of many other cellular membranes. Galactolipids are prominent, together with other lipid classes including phosphatidylglycerol and sulfolipids. These lipids provide the matrix in which photosynthetic protein complexes operate. Chlorophyll has a polar region associated with its light-absorbing ring system and a hydrophobic tail that supports membrane-associated pigment–protein organization. Most functional chlorophyll is bound to proteins, not freely floating in the lumen. Carotenoids are also positioned in complexes and membranes, allowing absorption and protective functions to occur close to the vulnerable excited pigments.

Thylakoid stacking is a regulated physical organization. In typical higher-plant chloroplasts, photosystem II and much of its associated light-harvesting antenna are enriched in appressed granal membranes. Photosystem I and ATP synthase are enriched in unappressed regions such as stromal lamellae and grana end membranes. A bulky stromal projection would be difficult to fit within the narrow gaps between appressed membranes, helping explain why ATP synthase is relatively excluded from tightly stacked interiors. The distributions are enrichments rather than a claim that a granum contains only one photosystem or that unstacked membrane contains no other electron transport proteins. Mobile carriers and connected membrane domains enable electron flow between regions.

Granal organization is neither a necessary definition of oxygenic photosynthesis nor a universal feature of all chloroplasts. The degree of stacking varies with species, cell type and light conditions. Certain algal chloroplasts lack the typical land-plant granum architecture yet carry out photosynthesis. Even within a plant, chloroplasts of different cell types can exhibit different thylakoid arrangements. The valid generalization is that oxygenic chloroplasts use organized photosynthetic membranes; it is not that every chloroplast contains identical stacks of a fixed number of disks.

A geometric estimate illustrates why internal membranes matter. Approximate one flattened thylakoid region as a circular disk of radius r = 0.25 µm and ignore the curved rim. Each disk has two broad membrane faces, each with area πr². A stack of 20 such regions therefore has broad-face membrane area 2 × 20 × π × (0.25 µm)² = 7.85 µm². Here the factor of two counts two broad faces of the flattened sac, not the two molecular leaflets of each lipid bilayer. If four such stacks are present, their idealized broad-face area is 31.4 µm². Connections, rims and differences in radii are omitted. The calculation compares membrane inventory, not the surface area exposed directly to the stroma or the number of functioning reaction centres. Close appression restricts accessibility even though the membranes remain present.

An electron micrograph showing reduced stacking is therefore evidence of a structural change, but by itself it is not a measurement of ATP synthesis or carbon assimilation. A useful experiment would measure the organization and the amounts of functional complexes, then assess photosynthetic output under controlled conditions. If stacking decreases while membrane abundance and electron transport remain substantial, the initial structural observation need not imply loss of all photosynthetic capacity. Conversely, extensive grana can exist even when an enzyme needed for carbon assimilation is defective. Structure permits function, but functional measurements test whether that potential is realized.

9. From light capture to a chemically useful gradient

The arrangement of pigments on thylakoid membranes is meaningful because absorbed light can change the electronic state of a pigment molecule. Excitation energy collected by antenna pigments can be transferred to a reaction centre, where light-driven charge separation initiates electron transfer. Electron carriers then participate in reactions that convert the short-lived excitation into longer-lived chemical and electrochemical forms. A photosynthetic membrane does not simply collect heat from sunlight; it organizes directional transfer reactions so that energy can perform biochemical work.

In the standard oxygenic chloroplast, photosystem II supplies electrons ultimately obtained from water. Oxidation of water at the lumenal side of the photosystem releases molecular oxygen, protons and electrons. The oxygen that a green leaf evolves during photosynthesis therefore originates from water, not directly from the carbon dioxide that is incorporated into carbohydrates. Electrons pass through carriers in or associated with the thylakoid system and receive additional light energy at photosystem I. Reduction of NADP⁺ to NADPH occurs on the stromal side. This spatial arrangement allows the reductant to enter the compartment where carbon-assimilation enzymes need it.

Electron transfer also promotes proton accumulation inside the thylakoid lumen. Water oxidation contributes protons directly to the lumen; carrier-mediated reactions transfer additional protons from the stromal side to the lumenal side. Meanwhile, consumption of protons during stromal reductant formation contributes to the difference between the two aqueous compartments. As illumination continues, the lumen tends to become more acidic than the stroma. This is a chemical gradient across the thylakoid membrane, not a gradient between the outer envelope and inner envelope. Electrical potential can contribute as well, although ion movements often modify the relative contributions of voltage and pH.

Protons are driven back toward the stroma through ATP synthase. Its membrane-spanning portion provides a regulated path for proton movement, and its catalytic head projects into the stroma. Energy from downhill proton movement is coupled to ATP formation from ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP is consequently formed on the same side of the membrane where NADPH is produced and carbon assimilation occurs. The arrangement eliminates the need to transport the newly made ATP and NADPH across the thylakoid membrane to supply stromal enzymes.

A proton gradient is an energy store only while the two aqueous environments remain sufficiently separated. If a reagent makes the thylakoid membrane freely permeable to protons, the difference in proton electrochemical potential declines. ATP synthesis coupled to proton return then falls, even if some light-driven electron transfer and oxygen evolution continue. Such a reagent uncouples the energy-generating reactions from ATP formation rather than necessarily destroying every pigment or electron carrier. In an intact chloroplast the consequences can spread to carbon fixation and electron transport because substrate consumption and energy demand are interconnected.

The thylakoid system therefore has two indispensable structural properties: it provides a matrix for organized light-driven electron transfers and it preserves a barrier across which energy can be stored as an electrochemical difference. The inner envelope has a different role: it regulates exchange between the whole plastid and its surroundings. Confusing the two membranes reverses the explanation of chloroplast energy conversion. Detailed electron-carrier stoichiometry and the alternative routes of photophosphorylation are important in plant physiology; the central cell-biological lesson here is the localization and coupling of processes.

10. How membrane separation becomes measurable energy

The pH scale measures hydrogen ion activity logarithmically. For a dilute illustrative solution, we may approximate activity by concentration and write pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], where the numerical concentration is expressed relative to the standard concentration of 1 mol L⁻¹. Suppose the illuminated lumen is at pH 6 and the stroma at pH 8. These are hypothetical representative values, not fixed physiological constants. The corresponding concentrations are approximately 10⁻⁶ M and 10⁻⁸ M. The lumen-to-stroma concentration ratio is 10⁻⁶/10⁻⁸ = 100. A difference of two pH units is therefore a hundredfold concentration difference, not a twofold difference.

The chemical free-energy change for transferring one mole of protons from an initial compartment i to a final compartment f is RT ln(af/ai), with R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, temperature T in kelvin and a representing activity. This expression follows from the chemical potential of a dissolved species, μ = μ° + RT ln a: subtract the initial chemical potential from the final one and the standard-state term cancels for the same species. Because a proton carries one positive elementary charge, its electrical contribution per mole is F(ψf − ψi), where F ≈ 96,485 C mol⁻¹ and ψ denotes electrical potential in volts. The total electrochemical change is the sum of the two terms. A negative value indicates favourable movement in the stated direction.

For the hypothetical lumen-to-stroma movement above, take T = 298 K and assume the electrical potential difference is negligible. The ratio of final to initial activity is 0.01. Thus ΔG = 8.314 × 298 × ln(0.01) = −11,410 J mol⁻¹, or approximately −11.4 kJ mol⁻¹ of protons. The sign matters: it describes the spontaneous direction of proton return, not the energy needed to move protons into the lumen. If a positive electrical potential also existed in the lumen relative to the stroma, it would make lumen-to-stroma transfer more favourable for positive ions. An opposite electrical gradient could partly oppose the chemical driving force.

Suppose in a simplified model four protons return for each ATP formed. The available free energy from the chemical gradient alone would be approximately 4 × 11.4 = 45.6 kJ per mole of ATP formed. This is an illustrative coupling assumption, not a universal proton-to-ATP stoichiometry. Actual ATP synthase stoichiometry depends on its rotor architecture and the energetic costs considered. The free-energy requirement for ATP synthesis depends on the actual activities of ATP, ADP and phosphate rather than only the standard biochemical free energy. Thus the calculation shows how a pH difference can support ATP formation, but does not establish that four protons are always sufficient in a particular chloroplast.

This reasoning predicts an important distinction between making and using a gradient. Illumination is normally required to establish and sustain the gradient, but ATP synthase does not itself need to absorb a photon at each catalytic event. If the gradient were created artificially in the absence of light, and the catalytic machinery and substrates remained available, ATP formation could occur transiently. Once the imposed gradient dissipated, the driving force would disappear. The membrane's role as a barrier is the necessary link between the observations of proton accumulation and ATP formation.

11. The stroma: carbon assimilation and metabolic exchange

A plant cannot grow solely by accumulating ATP. ATP and NADPH are useful intermediate forms of chemical energy and reducing power, but a growing cell needs carbon skeletons for sugars, lipids, amino acids and many other compounds. Stromal enzymes allow the products of the thylakoid reactions to be used for carbon assimilation. Carbon dioxide entering the chloroplast can be incorporated into organic compounds by the Calvin–Benson cycle, whose principal carbon-fixing enzyme is ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, commonly called Rubisco.

Rubisco catalyzes the addition of carbon dioxide to a five-carbon acceptor, yielding products that enter a network of soluble enzyme reactions. Energy and reductant obtained from the thylakoid system allow this network to generate triose phosphates and regenerate the acceptor. Regeneration matters because a carbon-fixing reaction alone would consume the available acceptor and stop. The detailed reaction sequence is a subject of plant physiology, but the compartmental consequences are already clear: the enzymes require ATP and NADPH on the stromal side, and their continued consumption helps regenerate the ADP, phosphate and NADP⁺ needed by the energy-converting system.

The frequently used expression light-independent reactions means that the carbon-assimilation reactions do not directly absorb photons as the primary light reactions do. It does not mean that carbon fixation normally proceeds at the same rate throughout the night. ATP and NADPH supply, stromal pH and ion conditions, and the activation state of certain enzymes change with illumination. When the light is switched off, stored intermediates may support some reactions temporarily, but sustained activity requires appropriate energetic and regulatory conditions. The two reaction systems are chemically coupled despite their different immediate dependence on light.

The inner envelope mediates exchange of useful metabolites between the stroma and cytosol. Some triose phosphate can be exported in exchange for inorganic phosphate by a specific transporter, contributing to cytosolic sucrose synthesis. Other carbon can remain in the chloroplast and be incorporated into transient starch. This partitioning allows a leaf to allocate photosynthetic products between immediate export and storage for later use. At night, stored starch can be degraded and carbon exported in forms appropriate for metabolism. A chloroplast is therefore an active metabolic participant even during intervals when it does not carry out light-driven ATP production.

Consider a selective defect in triose phosphate export. If the thylakoid system is initially intact, light-driven reactions may still operate. But reduced export affects the allocation of newly fixed carbon and can alter phosphate availability within the stroma. Because free phosphate is a substrate for ATP synthesis and is exchanged through the transporter, carbon metabolism and photosynthetic energy conversion can eventually be affected together. It is not correct to conclude that the transporter itself is a photosystem, nor is it correct to assume that a defect in envelope exchange has no effect on thylakoid function. A primary lesion in one compartment may have secondary effects in others.

Plastids also contain pathways involved in fatty acid biosynthesis and the formation of selected amino acids and other metabolites. These functions are important in both green and non-green plastids; the exact complement of enzymes and substrate sources varies with tissue. This is why plastid integrity matters in roots as well as leaves. A non-photosynthetic cell still needs metabolic cooperation between its nucleus, mitochondria, cytoplasm and plastids. The existence of several organelles is not a duplication of identical jobs but a division of chemical labour.

12. Plastid genomes, ribosomes and protein targeting

The stroma contains discrete DNA–protein assemblies called nucleoids. The word nucleoid is appropriate because the DNA is not enclosed within a separate nuclear envelope. A plastid can contain several nucleoids and multiple genome copies. In many land plants the plastid genome is represented as a circular map of roughly 120–160 kilobase pairs, although sizes vary and physical DNA molecules can also take other forms. A circular genome map should not be mistaken for proof that every plastid contains precisely one small circular DNA molecule.

Plastid genes encode some RNAs and a subset of proteins required for photosynthesis and genetic expression. Plastid ribosomes are generally bacterial-type 70S ribosomes, made of small and large subunits; the numerical sedimentation coefficients do not add arithmetically. The main cytosolic ribosomes of the plant cell are 80S. This difference is useful in interpreting experiments that selectively inhibit translation in one compartment. It does not imply that every protein present in the plastid is synthesized by 70S ribosomes. In fact, most plastid proteins are nuclear-encoded and translated in the cytosol.

Rubisco offers a familiar example of coordinated synthesis. In typical land plants its large subunit is encoded by the plastid rbcL gene, while the small subunit is encoded by nuclear rbcS genes. The two subunits must ultimately assemble in the same stromal compartment. If cytosolic protein production or import is disrupted, small-subunit supply becomes limiting even if large-subunit synthesis continues. Conversely, a lesion in plastid gene expression can impair assembly despite normal nuclear transcription and translation. A functional multimeric enzyme thus depends on coordination of two genetic systems.

How does a protein made on a cytosolic ribosome enter the correct plastid? Many nuclear-encoded plastid proteins are synthesized as precursors with an amino-terminal transit peptide. Receptors and translocation machinery at the outer and inner envelope, commonly referred to as TOC and TIC complexes, recognize and transfer many such precursors toward the stroma. Import is coupled to energy-dependent molecular processes, and the transit peptide is generally removed after arrival. Protein folding and assembly can then occur with the assistance of stromal factors. This route differs from the usual ER–Golgi vesicular route described earlier: the protein commonly crosses the plastid envelope through translocation machinery rather than being delivered inside a secretory vesicle.

A protein destined for the thylakoid may need a further targeting signal. Reaching the stroma does not automatically insert a protein into the thylakoid membrane or transfer it to the lumen. Different classes of thylakoid proteins use distinct targeting and translocation mechanisms, but the important examination-level distinction is sequential destination selection. A mutation that removes the envelope transit peptide may prevent plastid entry entirely, whereas a mutation in a second targeting determinant may permit stromal arrival but prevent correct thylakoid localization. These two mutations could produce similar photosynthetic defects by different immediate mechanisms.

Plastid gene expression and nuclear gene expression influence each other. Nuclear control of plastid development is sometimes described as anterograde regulation, whereas signals reflecting the plastid's functional state can modify nuclear gene expression through retrograde regulation. This coordination helps avoid building large quantities of a photosynthetic protein when the plastid is unable to assemble its partners or synthesize its pigments. A white leaf sector might therefore result from a nuclear mutation affecting import, a plastid genome defect, or a defect in a developmental or pigment-synthesis process. Colour alone cannot distinguish these causal classes.

The organelle grows and divides rather than being assembled de novo from unrelated vesicles. During plastid division, bacterial-related FtsZ machinery contributes to constriction on the stromal side, while additional host-derived components participate at the envelope. Partitioning existing plastids during cell division and subsequent proliferation maintain the organelle population. This process can be coordinated with cell development without being identical to mitosis. A cell can increase its chloroplast number as it expands, and mature chloroplasts can be descendants of organelles that divided earlier as proplastids. Inheritance patterns of plastid genes, often uniparental but not universally maternal, are developed in genetics; the immediate point is the continuity of the organelle and dependence on nuclear support.

13. Experiments that separate membrane function from soluble reactions

The cellular localization of photosynthesis was established through more than observations of green particles. Researchers examined what isolated chloroplast preparations could do when removed from the rest of the cell. In the classical Hill reaction, illuminated chloroplast material evolves oxygen when a suitable artificial electron acceptor is supplied, even in the absence of carbon dioxide fixation. This separated the light-driven oxidation of water and electron transfer from the carbon-assimilation reactions. It did not demonstrate that the isolated material could make carbohydrates indefinitely without carbon dioxide; it showed that oxygen evolution is not obligatorily coupled to immediate carbon dioxide incorporation.

A complementary experiment by Jagendorf and Uribe in 1966 examined ATP formation after an acid-to-alkaline transition of spinach chloroplast preparations. The acid treatment loaded the internal thylakoid compartment with protons. Transfer to alkaline medium containing appropriate substrates created a temporary difference in proton concentration across the thylakoid membrane. ATP was formed even in darkness. The evidence supported the chemiosmotic explanation: the gradient itself could supply the energy for phosphorylation without simultaneous illumination. It did not show that sunlight was unnecessary for normal sustained photosynthesis; the experimental manipulation temporarily replaced the gradient-generating function of light.

Think through the controls. A preparation equilibrated at the same pH on both sides of the thylakoid should not produce the same gradient-driven ATP response. A preparation exposed to a proton-conducting uncoupler should lose the gradient more quickly. Removing ADP or phosphate should limit net ATP formation even if a gradient exists. These predictions help distinguish a chemical gradient from other changes caused by the acid treatment. The conceptual power of the experiment is that its independent variable is an imposed difference between two compartments, rather than the amount of chlorophyll or the intensity of light.

Subcellular fractionation provides a further line of evidence. If intact chloroplasts are opened in a controlled way, the soluble stromal fraction can be separated from membrane-rich thylakoid material by centrifugation. Pigments and photosynthetic electron-transfer complexes are enriched in the membrane fraction, while Rubisco and many carbon-assimilation enzymes are enriched in the soluble fraction. Isolated membranes can show certain light-driven activities when provided with appropriate substrates and acceptors. Isolated stromal material cannot generate a trans-thylakoid proton gradient because it lacks the necessary intact membrane system, although soluble enzymes may remain catalytically active when supplied with substrates and cofactors.

An important limitation is that separation can itself disrupt function. A thylakoid fragment may still transfer electrons but fail to maintain a sealed lumen; a stromal enzyme may adhere to membrane material and appear in both fractions; an impure chloroplast preparation may contain mitochondrial enzymes. Proper fractionation analysis therefore uses marker proteins, controls for recovery and independent assessments of membrane integrity. The aim at examination level is not to memorize centrifugation speeds but to ask whether an experimental preparation preserves the structure required for the measured reaction.

14. Chloroplasts and mitochondria: parallel principles, different topology

The preceding mitochondrial chapter and this chapter can now be connected through a common principle. Both organelles have an endosymbiotic ancestry, double envelope membranes, their own genetic systems, and energy-converting membranes containing electron-transfer machinery and ATP synthase. Both require imported nuclear-encoded proteins, and both couple an electrochemical proton gradient to ATP production. These similarities make comparative questions useful, but they can also produce mistakes if the organelle compartments are assumed to be identical.

In mitochondria, the inner membrane itself forms the cristae and contains the main respiratory electron transport machinery. The matrix is enclosed by that membrane, while protons are moved toward the intermembrane space and crista lumen. ATP synthase returns protons toward the matrix and produces ATP on the matrix side. In a chloroplast, the inner envelope is not the corresponding energy-converting membrane. It encloses the stroma, within which an additional internal thylakoid system provides the site of light-driven electron transport. Protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen and return to the stroma through ATP synthase.

The mitochondrion uses energy released during oxidation of metabolic substrates to drive its respiratory chain; oxygen commonly serves as the terminal electron acceptor during aerobic respiration. The oxygenic chloroplast uses light energy to drive electron transfer from water toward NADP⁺ and releases oxygen from water oxidation. Its newly formed ATP and NADPH primarily support reactions in the stroma. These differences are not merely labels: they predict the direction of proton movement, the location of catalytic sites, the response to inhibitors and the consequence of damage to a particular membrane.

The aqueous matrix of a mitochondrion and the stroma of a chloroplast are functionally comparable in the restricted sense that they receive ATP from an ATP synthase catalytic head and contain soluble metabolic enzymes. They are not identical in biochemical contents. Similarly, the mitochondrial intermembrane space and chloroplast thylakoid lumen are comparable as proton-receiving compartments during their respective energy-conversion processes, but their anatomical positions differ. The chloroplast's envelope intermembrane space has no direct equivalence to the lumen in this energy-conversion analogy. When asked to identify comparable membranes, use function and topology rather than matching names containing the word inner.

Plant cells link the two organelles through metabolites and cellular redox balance. Photosynthesis supplies organic carbon that can later be oxidized by mitochondrial respiration, while both organelles exchange metabolites with the cytosol. During photorespiration in typical C3 plants, chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria participate in a coordinated pathway. This is an example of organelle cooperation rather than evidence that the three compartments merge or lose their identities. The detailed reaction sequence belongs to plant physiology, but the example shows why studying each organelle in isolation is only the first step toward understanding the functioning cell.

15. Diagnosing organelle defects through cause and effect

A useful way to consolidate the chapter is to reason from an immediate defect to later consequences. Suppose a mutant seedling produces normal amounts of chlorophyll but forms few functional reaction centres. It may appear green despite inefficient photosynthetic electron transfer. Low ATP and NADPH availability can then restrict stromal carbon assimilation. The primary problem is not chlorophyll synthesis merely because photosynthesis is poor; a measurement of the reaction centres or electron-transfer activity would better test the proposed explanation.

Now consider a mutant with apparently normal thylakoid organization but greatly reduced Rubisco activity. In a brief assay with suitable external electron acceptors, some light-driven electron-transfer activity may remain. Whole-cell carbon assimilation will be impaired because the stromal carbon-fixing system cannot use the energy and reductant efficiently. Over longer periods, feedback from reduced carbon assimilation can affect the light reactions as well. This time dependence is essential: the first molecular defect is not necessarily the only abnormality measured in a chronically affected plant.

A third mutant contains structurally normal envelope membranes but lacks a nuclear-encoded protein required for plastid import. A range of chloroplast proteins may fail to accumulate despite their nuclear genes being transcribed. If a plastid-encoded reaction-centre subunit is still produced, it may not assemble properly with nuclear-encoded partners. A fourth mutant has defects in plastid ribosomes and shows the complementary problem: some plastid-encoded products cannot be supplied, even though cytosolic translation proceeds. Comparing the protein distribution between cytosol and organelle, rather than only measuring total cellular protein, helps distinguish the two causal possibilities.

A developmental change should also be distinguished from damage. The disappearance of grana and chlorophyll in a ripening tomato is compatible with a regulated transition toward carotenoid accumulation. The same observations in a young leaf exposed to a damaging treatment could represent injury and loss of function instead. To decide, consider tissue identity, developmental stage, carotenoid content, membrane organization and recovery after the treatment. A normal chromoplast is not simply a diseased chloroplast, nor is every chlorophyll-poor plastid a chromoplast.

Finally, remember that the plastid is spatially organized inside a larger cell. Chloroplasts can change position in response to light in many plants, partly through actin-associated mechanisms. Movement can alter their exposure to illumination and therefore influence light capture and protection. Their position also affects contacts and exchange with other cellular structures. This observation motivates the next chapter on the cytoskeleton: a functional organelle requires not only its own internal compartments and biochemical machinery but also controlled positioning within the surrounding cytoplasm.

16. Original CSIR-NET Part B practice

Choose the best answer before reading the explanation. These questions are original practice, not previous examination papers.

B1. Which statement best describes a leucoplast?

  1. A colourless plastid with biosynthetic or storage functions
  2. A plastid lacking an envelope
  3. A chloroplast lacking DNA
  4. A vacuole containing chlorophyll
Answer and explanation

A. A colourless plastid with biosynthetic or storage functions. Colourlessness does not remove the plastid envelope or genetic system. Vacuoles are a different organelle family.

B2. Where is the catalytic head of chloroplast ATP synthase exposed?

  1. Lumen
  2. Stroma
  3. Envelope intermembrane space
  4. Cytosol
Answer and explanation

B. Stroma. Protons return from lumen to stroma; ATP is produced where stromal enzymes can use it.

B3. The characteristic precursor-containing membrane network of an angiosperm etioplast is the

  1. Nucleoid
  2. Crista
  3. Prolamellar body
  4. Tonoplast
Answer and explanation

C. Prolamellar body. A nucleoid contains DNA; cristae are mitochondrial inner-membrane folds; tonoplast bounds a vacuole.

B4. A granum is best defined as

  1. A stack of inner envelope membranes
  2. A stromal starch granule
  3. A separate plastid
  4. A stack of appressed thylakoid regions
Answer and explanation

D. A stack of appressed thylakoid regions. Grana are part of the thylakoid system, not envelope folds or storage granules.

B5. Which combination supports plastid endosymbiotic origin?

  1. Bacterial-related genes, ribosomes and division machinery
  2. Chlorophyll colour alone
  3. Starch alone
  4. Presence in every eukaryotic cell
Answer and explanation

A. Bacterial-related genes, ribosomes and division machinery. The convergent genetic and cell-biological evidence is more specific than pigmentation or storage.

B6. A root cap statolith commonly corresponds to

  1. A carotenoid chromoplast
  2. A dense starch-containing amyloplast
  3. A lysosome
  4. A nucleus
Answer and explanation

B. A dense starch-containing amyloplast. Dense starch granules permit sedimentation; not every amyloplast is used in gravity sensing.

B7. In typical land plants, Rubisco large and small subunits are respectively encoded by

  1. Both nuclear
  2. Both plastid
  3. Plastid and nuclear genes
  4. Nuclear and mitochondrial
Answer and explanation

C. Plastid and nuclear genes. rbcL is plastid encoded and rbcS nuclear encoded; both products assemble in the stroma.

B8. Where does the main chloroplast photosynthetic electron transport occur?

  1. Inner envelope
  2. Outer envelope
  3. Soluble stroma
  4. Thylakoid membrane
Answer and explanation

D. Thylakoid membrane. The membrane houses light-driven complexes; stroma contains many carbon-assimilation enzymes.

B9. Which feature is particularly associated with chromoplast differentiation?

  1. Carotenoid accumulation and sequestration
  2. Formation of a new nucleus
  3. Mandatory loss of all DNA
  4. Expansion of mitochondrial cristae
Answer and explanation

A. Carotenoid accumulation and sequestration. Chromoplasts remodel plastid contents and may retain genetic machinery; they do not become nuclei or mitochondria.

B10. Which is not a valid generalization?

  1. Chloroplasts are plastids
  2. All chloroplasts possess identical grana
  3. Chloroplasts have photosynthetic membranes
  4. Most plastid proteins are nuclear encoded
Answer and explanation

B. All chloroplasts possess identical grana. Algal and specialized plant chloroplasts vary in thylakoid stacking.

B11. The plastid envelope import system generally recognizes

  1. A mannose-6-phosphate tag
  2. A nuclear export signal
  3. A plastid-targeting transit peptide
  4. A lysosomal lumen marker
Answer and explanation

C. A plastid-targeting transit peptide. TOC/TIC mediate import of many precursors; the alternatives address unrelated destinations.

B12. During normal illumination, the thylakoid lumen is generally

  1. Always identical in pH
  2. More alkaline than stroma
  3. In direct continuity with cytosol
  4. More acidic than the stroma
Answer and explanation

D. More acidic than the stroma. Protons accumulate in the lumen and return via ATP synthase. The exact gradient varies.

17. Original CSIR-NET Part C, calculations and assertion–reason

For each case, identify the immediate lesion, the evidence and the limitations of the inference. For assertion–reason items, decide the truth of each statement independently before testing the explanatory relationship.

C1. An isolated thylakoid preparation produces O₂ in light but ATP synthesis falls to 5% after addition of compound X. Which explanation fits best?

Worked answer and reasoning

X dissipates the proton gradient while permitting substantial electron transfer. If X completely blocked water oxidation, oxygen evolution would also collapse. Loss of carbon-fixing enzymes alone does not directly explain selective ATP loss. A proton leak accounts for both readouts; further controls would test the prediction.

C2. A lumen at pH 5.5 and stroma at pH 7.5 are compared at negligible voltage. Calculate the proton activity ratio and predict direction of favourable proton movement.

Worked answer and reasoning

100-fold higher in lumen; lumen to stroma. 10^(7.5−5.5) = 100. A positive proton moves down its chemical gradient into stroma. An opposing electrical gradient could modify the total driving force.

C3. A hypothetical thylakoid region has radius 0.30 µm. Estimate both broad faces of 15 circular regions, ignoring rims.

Worked answer and reasoning

8.48 µm². 2 × 15 × π × 0.30² = 8.4823 µm². A factor of one misses one broad face; counting bilayer leaflets would double-count membrane area.

C4. A dark-grown shoot contains prolamellar bodies. After exposure to light, chlorophyll increases but ATP output remains low. What should be investigated?

Worked answer and reasoning

Assembly and integrity of the thylakoid energy-converting machinery. Pigment acquisition is not sufficient for function. Check reaction centres, proton retention and ATP synthase activity. The observation alone cannot identify the exact molecular lesion.

C5. A chloroplast fraction contains 80 units of Rubisco and 5 units of chlorophyll; a second contains 10 units of Rubisco and 95 units of chlorophyll. Identify likely fractions.

Worked answer and reasoning

First is stroma-enriched; second is thylakoid-enriched. Relative enrichment supports assignments, but the nonzero minority signals suggest contamination, association or incomplete separation. The total Rubisco recovery is 90 units, so percentage recovery requires knowing the starting amount.

C6. An enzyme precursor enters the plastid but accumulates in the stroma instead of the lumen. What class of defect is consistent with this observation?

Worked answer and reasoning

A defect in subsequent thylakoid targeting or translocation. Successful stromal entry shows that at least the envelope import step occurred. Loss of the envelope transit peptide alone would more typically prevent entry. A lumen-targeting determinant or machinery could be defective.

C7. A white leaf sector retains plastid DNA but lacks mature thylakoids. Can one conclude that its mutation must be nuclear?

Worked answer and reasoning

No. Nuclear genes contribute most plastid proteins, but plastid genes also encode essential components. Retained DNA does not demonstrate functional expression. Pigment synthesis, import or developmental defects could cause the phenotype.

C8. Assertion: Plastid starch storage reduces the osmotic contribution of a given quantity of glucose residues. Reason: Polymerization and granule formation reduce the number of freely dissolved particles.

Worked answer and reasoning

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. Under dilute ideal assumptions osmotic pressure depends on particle concentration. Real starch granules are not ideal dissolved polymers, so this is a physical explanation rather than a direct turgor prediction.

C9. Assertion: ATP is synthesized on the stromal side of thylakoids. Reason: The chloroplast inner envelope contains the main photosynthetic ATP synthase.

Worked answer and reasoning

Assertion true; reason false. The ATP synthase is embedded in the thylakoid membrane with the catalytic head in stroma. Inner envelope transport is a separate function.

C10. Assertion: All chromoplasts arise from mature chloroplasts. Reason: Chromoplasts can accumulate carotenoids in specialized internal structures.

Worked answer and reasoning

Assertion false; reason true. Tomato illustrates chloroplast-to-chromoplast conversion, but chromoplasts can arise from other plastids. Carotenoid sequestration does not establish developmental ancestry.

C11. A root is rotated 90 degrees and its dense amyloplasts settle toward a new lower wall. Does this establish that starch directly controls differential cell elongation?

Worked answer and reasoning

No; it supports a physical gravity-sensing role. Sedimentation is the observation. To demonstrate causal signaling and differential growth, additional perturbations and growth readouts are required.

C12. A plant has an intact chloroplast envelope but a permeable thylakoid membrane. Which process is directly most vulnerable?

Worked answer and reasoning

Gradient-driven photophosphorylation. Envelope integrity does not preserve the barrier around the lumen. Some electron transfer may survive temporarily, but proton accumulation and coupled ATP formation are compromised.

C13. At 298 K, lumen pH 6 and stroma pH 7, assume no voltage. Estimate ΔG for proton movement from lumen to stroma.

Worked answer and reasoning

Approximately −5.70 kJ mol⁻¹. RT ln(10⁻⁷/10⁻⁶) = 8.314 × 298 × ln(0.1) J mol⁻¹ = −5.70 kJ mol⁻¹. Negative indicates favourable transfer; the reverse costs the same magnitude under the assumed conditions.

C14. A green leaf chloroplast contains a large starch grain in daylight. Does this require reclassification as an amyloplast?

Worked answer and reasoning

No. A photosynthetic chloroplast may accumulate transient starch. Classification relies on dominant internal organization and functional specialization, not a single inclusion.

C15. In a dark acid-base transition experiment, ATP is detected but oxygen is not. Which hypothesis is supported?

Worked answer and reasoning

An imposed gradient can drive ATP formation without simultaneous light-driven water oxidation. ATP synthesis uses the electrochemical gradient. This is a transient demonstration; it does not establish sustained dark photosynthesis or light-independent oxygen evolution.

C16. A preparation retains photosystem II activity but shows little CO₂ assimilation after removal of the stromal fraction. Explain the result.

Worked answer and reasoning

Membrane electron transfer is separable from soluble carbon-assimilation reactions. The thylakoid fraction may still carry out light-driven reactions with suitable acceptors; the removal of Rubisco and other stromal enzymes limits carbon fixation. Fraction purity and membrane integrity must be checked.

18. Consolidation: a structural map of plastid function

Begin revision by drawing the plastid family rather than memorizing isolated definitions. Place the proplastid at the developmental starting point, with arrows toward storage plastids, chloroplasts and pigment-rich forms. Include the dark-grown etioplast and the senescence-associated gerontoplast without making either an obligatory step in every lineage. For each form, explain why its internal organization is suited to its principal task and give a genuine example of the tissue in which that task occurs.

Next draw a chloroplast from the cytoplasm inward. Mark the outer envelope, narrow intermembrane space, inner envelope, stroma, thylakoid membrane and lumen. Put chlorophyll-associated complexes in the thylakoid membrane, oxygen evolution toward the lumenal side, ATP synthase catalytic activity and NADPH formation toward the stromal side, and the major carbon-assimilation enzymes in the stroma. Then mark protons moving into the lumen during light-driven reactions and returning to the stroma through ATP synthase. This exercise exposes incorrect membrane and compartment assignments immediately.

Use three independent forms of evidence to test your explanation: the Hill reaction separates light-driven oxygen evolution from carbon assimilation; the acid-base experiment separates gradient use from illumination; fractionation links membrane and soluble processes to different locations. Finally, explain why plastid DNA does not imply independence from the nuclear genome, using Rubisco assembly as an example. The next chapter develops the systems that support organelle positioning and cell shape, extending the structural view from the interior of a single organelle to the organization of the whole cell.
