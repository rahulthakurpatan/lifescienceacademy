1. Why circulating blood needs a local emergency seal

A small wound creates a difficult physiological problem. Blood is valuable because it moves through vessels, delivering oxygen and nutrients and distributing signals between organs. When a vessel is damaged, however, that same movement carries blood away from the body or into tissues. A useful response must stop the escape rapidly without blocking the rest of the circulation. Haemostasis is the coordinated set of processes that meets these opposing requirements. It depends on the vessel wall, circulating platelets and soluble plasma proteins, and on mechanisms that limit the response to the damaged region.

Consider a capillary network supplied by an arteriole. If the entire network were filled by an indiscriminate clot, the cells served by it would lose oxygen even though the wound stopped bleeding. In contrast, if blood remained fluid at every injured surface, loss of circulating volume and tissue bleeding would continue. The correct physiological outcome is a local barrier that restores vascular integrity while leaving neighbouring channels available for perfusion. The word haemostasis must therefore not be confused with homeostasis, the broader maintenance of internal physiological conditions, or with thrombosis, the pathological formation of an intravascular thrombus.

Primary haemostasis concerns the early vascular response and platelet-dependent closure of the breach. Secondary haemostasis concerns the enzymatic production of fibrin, which reinforces the platelet structure. Although the terminology is useful for learning, these processes do not wait for each other to finish. A platelet attached to exposed tissue may already encounter thrombin generated nearby. Activated platelets also provide a membrane surface that accelerates certain coagulation reactions. Thus platelet responses and coagulation support one another while the vessel repairs itself. The molecular clotting pathways are developed in the next chapter.

The ability of a platelet to remain relatively inactive in one location and respond strongly in another is more informative than a list of its contents. Its receptors encounter an endothelial surface under ordinary conditions and a very different set of ligands after vascular damage. Soluble signals can then reinforce the difference between the injured surface and its surroundings. This conversion from environmental information to altered cell behaviour is a direct application of cell adhesion and signal transduction. A haemostatic response is consequently an excellent model for understanding how local positive feedback can coexist with physiological negative regulation.

It is useful to distinguish a platelet plug from a blood clot formed outside the body. A plug originates at a lesion in a flowing vascular system, experiences mechanical forces and interacts with living endothelium. Blood allowed to clot in a tube lacks that spatial arrangement. Its behaviour can reveal biochemical capabilities but does not reproduce all of the signals and mechanical constraints present in a vessel. Experiments must be interpreted in the context in which they are performed.

2. The resting platelet: a small fragment with organised machinery

Human platelets are small anucleate cytoplasmic fragments derived from bone marrow megakaryocytes. They are commonly about two to three micrometres across and occur at approximately 150–450 × 10⁹ per litre in adult blood, although reference intervals vary among laboratories. The absence of a nucleus is not the absence of biological activity. Platelets contain mitochondria, metabolic enzymes, RNA, ribosomes, membrane receptors and regulated storage compartments. They can synthesise some proteins from inherited messenger RNA, but cannot carry out ordinary nuclear transcription. Their limited lifespan makes replacement by the marrow important.

A resting platelet is usually discoid rather than spherical. A circumferential band of microtubules helps maintain this flattened geometry. Actin filaments and their associated proteins provide a mechanically adaptable cortical framework. When the platelet is activated, the arrangement of the cytoskeleton changes; the disk becomes rounded, protrusions form and the platelet can spread across a suitable substrate. This increases contact with the damaged surface and other cells. Shape change does not by itself establish that all activation responses have occurred, because secretion, integrin activation and cytoskeletal changes are partly separable outputs.

The plasma membrane carries adhesion receptors and receptors for soluble agonists. Glycoprotein Ib within the GPIb-IX-V complex recognises von Willebrand factor (VWF); the collagen receptor GPVI provides a strong activation signal, and integrin α2β1 helps stabilise collagen contact. Integrin αIIbβ3 is abundant but normally has low affinity for soluble fibrinogen. An activated platelet changes the conformation and functional state of this integrin, making it competent to engage adhesive ligands. In this way the membrane distinguishes an adhesive environment from the mere presence of soluble proteins in circulating plasma.

Internal membrane organisation also matters. The open canalicular system comprises membrane channels connected with the exterior and contributes to surface membrane availability and secretion. The dense tubular system, derived from endoplasmic reticulum, participates in calcium storage and lipid mediator metabolism. These two membrane systems are not interchangeable. The former provides connections with the extracellular environment, whereas the latter is associated with intracellular biochemical regulation. An increase in cytosolic calcium can therefore result from internal release as well as from calcium entry across the plasma membrane.

Alpha granules store many proteins, including VWF, fibrinogen, platelet factor 4 and growth factors. P-selectin is located in alpha-granule membranes and becomes accessible on the cell surface during granule fusion. Dense granules contain small molecules and ions, notably ADP, ATP, serotonin and calcium. Their contents provide rapid extracellular communication. Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes. These compartments differ in cargo, origin and function; a normal number of platelets is not evidence that each granule population is normal.

Platelet activity consumes energy. ATP supports ion pumps, cytoskeletal remodelling, secretion and other active processes. Both glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism contribute to this demand. A metabolic perturbation may leave a cell with its receptors intact but unable to execute a downstream response. Similarly, measuring the abundance of a receptor by an antibody does not establish that it has adopted the high-affinity state required for a particular function. Function must be tested separately from presence.

Most non-mammalian vertebrates possess nucleated thrombocytes rather than mammalian-style anucleate platelets. They serve related haemostatic functions, but structural and regulatory details are not identical across taxa. Statements about the lack of a nucleus should therefore be restricted to mammalian platelets rather than generalised to every vertebrate blood element.

Organelle inheritance, membrane topology and experimental identification

The small size of a platelet creates a practical problem when it is examined by ordinary light microscopy. A stained blood film shows purple granular structures that are much smaller than erythrocytes, but it cannot reliably identify the individual intracellular membranes that give a platelet its secretory capacity. Electron microscopy reveals more detail. A dense granule is identified by its characteristic electron-dense core, whereas an alpha granule has a different internal structure. This distinction is based on physical organisation as well as chemical cargo. A platelet population can have apparently normal granularity under a routine stain while still displaying an abnormal dense-granule number under more appropriate imaging conditions.

The morphological appearance of an activated platelet depends on how it is observed. Platelets suspended in a sample can transform from disks into more rounded structures with projections. Platelets contacting a coated surface may first extend filopodia and then spread over the substrate by forming broader lamellipodia. The actin cytoskeleton supports both kinds of change, but a spread platelet encounters resistance from the surface and adhesive contacts transmit forces. For this reason, spreading area on a fibrinogen-coated surface and aggregation in a stirred tube are not equivalent measurements. One primarily reports behaviour against an immobilised ligand; the other involves bridging freely suspended cells.

The distinction between the surface-connected canalicular system and the calcium-storing dense tubular system provides a direct application of membrane topology. A tracer that cannot cross the plasma membrane may access a space connected with the extracellular fluid. Such access does not prove that the tracer has entered the cytosol. In contrast, calcium released from an internal membrane-bounded store can increase the concentration of the ion within the cytosol without the initial requirement for extracellular calcium entry. A cell image showing channels and a rise in calcium cannot by itself prove that the channels are calcium stores. The two claims require distinct functional tests.

Granule release provides an example of how a fusion event changes both the extracellular environment and the cell surface simultaneously. Soluble cargo initially inside the granule can enter the surrounding fluid, while membrane proteins originally facing the granule lumen may become exposed to extracellular probes. P-selectin detection exploits this latter consequence. If a manipulation interferes with fusion, one expects reduced surface appearance of granule membrane markers and reduced release of appropriate cargo. If fusion occurs but the granules were deficient in a specific stored protein, however, the membrane marker could still appear while release of that particular protein remains poor.

One can distinguish the problem of loading a granule during development from the problem of emptying it after activation. Consider two hypothetical samples. Sample A contains markedly fewer dense granules but releases an ordinary fraction of their remaining contents after stimulation. Sample B has a normal granule count but releases very little ATP after stimulation. Both could provide a weak extracellular nucleotide signal. In sample A, available storage capacity is impaired; in sample B, stimulus-dependent release may be impaired. A measurement of extracellular ATP alone would not identify which explanation applies. Combining imaging, total cellular nucleotide content and secretion measurements can separate them.

The plasma membrane is not chemically uniform. Phosphatidylserine is normally enriched in the cytoplasmic leaflet. ATP-dependent lipid transport helps maintain phospholipid asymmetry, and strong stimulation may activate calcium-sensitive lipid scrambling that redistributes phosphatidylserine toward the external leaflet. The external exposure is functionally important because it provides a negatively charged surface for certain coagulation complexes. A fluorescent annexin V probe can report accessible phosphatidylserine in a calcium-dependent manner. Annexin binding should not be interpreted as direct proof that a platelet is dead: stimulated viable platelets can display procoagulant phosphatidylserine exposure.

Platelet ageing introduces another distinction between function and abundance. As circulating fragments age, membrane proteins, metabolic reserves and responsiveness may change. At steady state a blood sample contains a mixture of newly released and older platelets. Immature platelets can contain more residual RNA and may be recognised by appropriate fluorescent dyes. A rise in the immature platelet fraction may be consistent with increased marrow output in response to peripheral loss, but its meaning also depends on absolute platelet count and the method used. A high percentage of a very small total pool does not necessarily imply a large absolute number of immature platelets. For example, 10% of 30 × 10⁹/L is 3 × 10⁹/L, while 3% of 200 × 10⁹/L is 6 × 10⁹/L. The sample with the smaller fraction in this example contains twice as many immature platelets per litre.

3. Thrombopoiesis and the regulation of platelet number

Platelet production begins with haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells in the marrow. Commitment to the megakaryocytic lineage involves successive changes in transcriptional organisation and growth-factor responsiveness. The resulting megakaryocyte becomes a very large cell with a highly developed cytoplasm. Its capacity to supply many platelets depends on accumulating membrane, granules, proteins and cytoskeletal components before the fragments enter blood. The earlier haematopoiesis chapter provides the general framework for lineage commitment; here the emphasis is on the specialisation needed to produce platelets.

Megakaryocytes increase their DNA content through endomitosis. DNA replication and aspects of mitosis occur, but ordinary complete cell division does not follow. A polyploid nucleus can therefore coexist with a single extensive cytoplasm. Polyploidisation is distinct from the multiplication of separate megakaryocytes by cell division. It is also distinct from the formation of a platelet, which contains cytoplasm rather than a small copy of the parent nucleus. Mature cells develop a demarcation membrane system that supplies a large membrane reserve during platelet production.

Megakaryocytes interact with marrow sinusoids, the vascular channels through which newly produced blood elements gain access to the circulation. Long cytoplasmic extensions called proplatelets can enter these channels. Microtubule rearrangement and cytoplasmic transport distribute granules and organelles along extensions, and terminal fragmentation contributes to the formation of circulating platelets. Final maturation may continue within the circulation, and pulmonary vessels can contribute to platelet production. The simple marrow model is useful but should not be treated as the only possible site or route of release.

Thrombopoietin, often abbreviated TPO, is the central hormonal regulator of megakaryocyte development and platelet production. The liver is its major source. TPO binds the receptor MPL on cells of the megakaryocytic lineage and on platelets. MPL has no intrinsic tyrosine kinase domain; ligand engagement signals through associated kinases including JAK2. Activation of STAT transcription factors in nucleated progenitors helps regulate survival, proliferation and differentiation. The presence of MPL on mature platelets also contributes to the removal of circulating TPO. This is an example in which the end product of a developmental pathway influences the availability of its regulatory hormone.

An elementary model assumes constant secretion of TPO by the liver and removal of hormone in proportion to the mass of receptor-bearing platelets and megakaryocytes. If the mass falls, fewer receptor-mediated uptake sites remain and free TPO may rise. More hormonal support can then favour platelet production. The relationship is not instantaneous because differentiation and release require time. It is also not perfectly inverse, because inflammation, receptor expression and the reason for the low count can influence the system.

Evidence from experiments on ageing platelets adds another regulatory route. Loss of terminal sialic acid from surface glycans can expose galactose residues recognised by the hepatic Ashwell–Morell receptor. Research by Grozovsky and colleagues demonstrated a link between clearance of desialylated platelets and stimulation of hepatic TPO production through JAK2–STAT3 signalling. This is not the same receptor as MPL: the hepatic receptor senses features of platelets being removed, whereas MPL recognises the hormonal product of the liver. Thus feedback can be carried by a changing rate of removal as well as by changing receptor-mediated hormone uptake.

Many circulating platelets survive for roughly a week to ten days under normal human conditions. Senescent or damaged platelets are cleared through hepatic and splenic mechanisms. A fraction of the total platelet pool is normally held in the spleen, and altered splenic sequestration can reduce the count in a peripheral blood sample without an equivalent decrease in total body platelet mass. A measured low platelet count consequently has several possible explanations: insufficient production, excessive destruction, consumption in thrombi, redistribution or even a measurement artefact.

A useful steady-state calculation starts with the total freely circulating pool N and a mean residence time τ. If removal is balanced by production at rate P, then P = N/τ under a simple constant-pool model. A concentration of 250 × 10⁹ platelets per litre in five litres of blood gives N = 1.25 × 10¹² platelets. With a mean residence time of eight days, the replacement estimate is 1.56 × 10¹¹ platelets per day. This is a model calculation rather than a universal production rate; it ignores the splenic reserve and assumes stable blood volume and residence time. If consumption suddenly doubles, a new equilibrium will not occur until regulatory and marrow responses have had sufficient time to develop.

Evidence from lineage, organ-specific release and feedback kinetics

A single megakaryocyte must make enough cytoplasmic material to supply many fragments without allowing the fragments to contain pieces of chromosomal DNA as their defining structural component. The developmental stages are therefore most easily understood as changes in a large parent cell: commitment, maturation, amplification of biosynthetic capacity, accumulation of specialised components and release of cytoplasm. Observing an increase in megakaryocyte size does not demonstrate that platelet production has increased. A defect in extension or fragmentation can permit large mature cells to accumulate while the release of circulating platelets remains impaired.

Transcription factors such as GATA1 and FLI1 contribute to the development of the megakaryocytic programme. Their roles provide a link to the general principles of lineage-specific gene expression. A transcriptional defect can affect numerous platelet proteins simultaneously, whereas a defect in a mature platelet's fibrinogen receptor can selectively impair cohesion. This difference matters for genetic reasoning. A mutation expressed early in megakaryocyte differentiation may produce abnormalities of platelet size, count and organelle composition in combination, making a single downstream assay insufficient to describe the phenotype.

Experiments on MPL and TPO can distinguish receptor-dependent hormone action from hormone concentration. Imagine progenitor cells cultured with identical nutrients but different TPO concentrations. Normal cells should respond within an appropriate concentration range, whereas cells lacking functional MPL cannot respond through that receptor even if the hormone concentration is high. Adding TPO to a receptor-deficient preparation does not replace the missing receptor. The control of viability is essential because a sample that fails to survive cannot be interpreted as specifically defective in differentiation. A separate control in which another appropriate survival signal is provided can help establish whether the cells remain generally responsive.

The relationship between free TPO and platelet mass can be represented with a deliberately simplified steady-state model. Let S be the rate of delivery of hormone into a compartment in amount per unit time, C its concentration, V the effective distribution volume, k the first-order non-receptor clearance constant, and M a dimensionless index of functional MPL-bearing cell mass. If receptor-dependent removal contributes a first-order term aM, where a has units of inverse time, the steady concentration is C = S/[V(k + aM)]. The expression follows by equating the rate of input to the rate of removal. It predicts a higher concentration when receptor mass is decreased, provided synthesis, distribution and all remaining clearance terms are held constant.

For a numerical illustration take k = 0.1 per hour, a = 0.4 per hour at M = 1 and a constant S/V of 5 concentration units per hour. The predicted baseline concentration is 5/(0.1 + 0.4) = 10 units. If receptor mass is halved, the new theoretical steady concentration is 5/(0.1 + 0.2), or approximately 16.7 units. Halving receptor mass does not double the concentration in this example because a separate clearance pathway remains. This is not a clinical equation for predicting an individual's TPO concentration. The receptor mass index does not directly equal the laboratory platelet count, and actual production and clearance are not necessarily linear.

To appreciate the significance of this limitation, compare two cases with the same circulating platelet count. In the first, marrow megakaryocytes are greatly depleted; in the second, marrow megakaryocytes are preserved or increased while circulating platelets are destroyed. Total receptor-bearing mass may differ even at equal counts. Hormonal feedback and platelet release may consequently differ as well. A useful examination question may provide TPO concentrations and marrow observations alongside platelet counts precisely to avoid the mistaken assumption that all low-count conditions share the same mechanism.

Evidence for clearance-linked feedback extends beyond this receptor-mass model. In the study by Grozovsky and colleagues, the hepatic recognition of altered platelet surface glycans was connected experimentally to increased expression of TPO. Conceptually, comparing the hepatic response to platelets with different degrees of desialylation tests whether the composition of removed platelets matters, rather than simply the number removed. A receptor-deficient or signalling-inhibited comparison helps test whether the response depends on the proposed recognition and signalling route. The important conclusion is that ageing and clearance can supply regulatory information to the liver. It does not imply that every form of platelet removal activates the same pathway to the same degree.

The anatomical site of platelet release also illustrates the difference between a plausible model and directly observed evidence. Marrow megakaryocytes and vascular extensions are well-established participants in platelet production. In 2017 Lefrançais and colleagues used imaging of the mouse pulmonary microcirculation to observe megakaryocytes releasing platelets in the lung. Such an observation provides stronger evidence for local production than simply finding a higher count in a blood sample after passage through an organ, because concentration differences can arise through other processes. The study supports pulmonary contribution in the experimental animal, but its quantitative estimates should not be transferred without qualification to every human physiological or pathological condition.

A time-dependent balance clarifies the response to sudden loss. If N is the circulating pool and P and R are the production and removal rates, the change in pool is dN/dt = P − R. In a simplified case with first-order removal R = N/τ and constant production, the pool approaches Pτ. Immediately after a large loss, however, production may initially remain near its earlier value because new maturation takes time. A later increase in release can drive recovery. A sequence of daily counts, compared with an immature platelet measure and marrow findings, is more informative about dynamics than a single count.

A different form of reasoning concerns dilution. Suppose five litres of blood contains 250 × 10⁹ platelets per litre, giving 1.25 × 10¹² circulating platelets. If intravascular volume expands hypothetically to 6.25 litres with no addition, removal or redistribution of platelets, the concentration falls to 200 × 10⁹/L despite an unchanged pool. A numerical decrease in concentration can thus occur without loss of platelets. Actual fluid administration and haemorrhage involve more complex exchanges, but the mass-balance principle explains why concentration measurements must not automatically be equated with total cell number.

4. Vascular injury and the first response

The endothelium is a living interface between blood and underlying connective tissue. In an undamaged vessel its luminal surface normally discourages inappropriate platelet adhesion. Endothelial nitric oxide diffuses to platelets and stimulates soluble guanylyl cyclase, increasing cyclic GMP. Endothelial prostacyclin activates its receptor and favours cyclic AMP generation. These intracellular signals make activation less likely, helping circulating platelets remain responsive without becoming continuously adhesive.

Endothelial cells also express ectonucleotidases such as CD39, which break down extracellular ATP and ADP. This helps remove a signal that could otherwise recruit platelets outside the site of injury. ADP is not intrinsically harmful or beneficial; its local persistence determines whether it reinforces the response. An intact surface additionally separates circulating platelets from collagen-rich extracellular matrix. This physical separation is important even when plasma contains soluble adhesive proteins.

Injury interrupts the continuity of the vessel wall. A rapid reduction in local vessel calibre can reduce the amount of blood escaping and alter the mechanical conditions at the wound. Smooth muscle contraction may follow direct injury, neural responses or locally acting substances. Endothelin and mediators released during haemostasis can contribute in appropriate vascular beds. Capillaries lack a continuous smooth muscle layer, so the textbook description of vasoconstriction should be understood in the context of vessels capable of active contraction and the upstream vascular network.

The influence of vessel radius can be demonstrated with an idealised tube. For steady laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid in a rigid cylindrical tube, the volume flow rate Q is πΔPr⁴/(8ηL), where ΔP is the pressure difference, r the radius, η dynamic viscosity and L tube length. If radius decreases by 20% and the other variables remain fixed, the relative flow is 0.8⁴ = 0.4096, or about 41% of the initial value. This fourth-power relationship helps explain why small radius changes can have large consequences. A real bleeding wound, however, is not a rigid tube with constant pressure, and blood is not a simple Newtonian fluid in every vascular setting. The estimate illustrates sensitivity rather than predicting the amount of blood lost.

A reduction in bulk flow is not equivalent to eliminating local shear. Shear describes the spatial variation of fluid velocity. In an ideal tube with mean velocity U, wall shear rate is 4U/r. At a fixed flow rate, narrowing the radius increases mean velocity and wall shear rate; at a fixed pressure gradient under ideal Poiseuille conditions, both flow and shear change differently. An examination problem must specify the maintained variable before a prediction is made. This distinction becomes especially important when considering the capture of platelets by VWF under arterial flow.

Red blood cells influence transport of platelets toward the vessel wall. Their interactions and migration within flowing blood can enrich smaller platelets in the near-wall region, a phenomenon termed margination. A lesion can therefore encounter platelets even though the majority of blood volume consists of other components. Severe changes in haematocrit may modify platelet delivery independently of the platelet concentration itself. The biological response reflects the interaction of flow, blood composition and adhesive chemistry rather than any single variable.

Hydrodynamic constraints on capture

The fact that blood flows introduces an asymmetry between receptor binding in solution and receptor binding at a vessel wall. A soluble molecule can encounter a platelet anywhere in the lumen. A wall ligand can be encountered only if the platelet approaches the wall closely enough for binding to occur. This encounter depends on transport, not only the molecular binding affinity. Platelets located far from the wall do not contribute equally to early adhesion even if every receptor is fully functional. Increasing the near-wall concentration can enhance the rate at which the damaged surface encounters platelets without changing their receptor composition.

The idealised wall shear rate in a cylindrical tube can be derived from the velocity profile for laminar Poiseuille flow. If x is the distance from the tube axis and U the mean velocity, the local axial velocity is u(x) = 2U[1 − (x/r)²]. The magnitude of the slope at the wall x = r is 4U/r. If U is expressed in metres per second and r in metres, shear rate is expressed in inverse seconds. Shear stress is the force per unit area associated with this velocity gradient, and in a Newtonian fluid it equals dynamic viscosity multiplied by shear rate. The terms rate and stress must not be confused: increasing viscosity at the same shear rate increases the stress.

Consider an illustrative tube of radius 0.0005 m with a mean velocity of 0.1 m/s. The calculated wall shear rate is 4 × 0.1/0.0005 = 800 s⁻¹. If the same mean velocity is maintained after the radius is halved, the shear rate becomes 1600 s⁻¹. If instead volume flow rate is held constant, halving radius increases mean velocity fourfold, leading to an eightfold shear-rate increase. These predictions depend on what is controlled experimentally. The real vascular network has branching, compliance, pulsatile flow and cellular suspension effects; the model is a conceptual baseline rather than an exact vessel simulation.

A high shear rate can shorten the time available for an unbound platelet to establish contact with a surface. VWF can help overcome this limitation because its adhesive behaviour is adapted to mechanical conditions. The effect is not captured by stating that stronger flow always removes platelets. Flow can increase the forces promoting molecular extension and surface encounter while also increasing detachment forces. The measured effect may therefore depend on ligand density and receptor function. Students should avoid an unconditional prediction from flow speed alone.

An assay designed to test the specific contribution of red cells to platelet delivery could compare reconstituted blood samples with equal platelet counts and different haematocrits. The experiment would need comparable flow conditions, a defined adhesive substrate and control of plasma composition. If fewer platelets reach the surface when erythrocytes are depleted, this supports a transport contribution. However, erythrocyte depletion also changes viscosity and the mechanical environment. A further experiment that monitors near-wall platelet concentration directly would help distinguish a delivery effect from altered detachment or receptor signalling.

Flow is also important after attachment. A growing aggregate changes the shape of the lumen and creates local regions in which fluid moves differently. A surface protrusion can increase forces on the aggregate, while gaps between tightly packed platelets can limit solute exchange. Consequently, platelet activation and the mechanical environment are coupled: aggregation modifies the flow that subsequently influences aggregation. The biological seal is a dynamic structure, not a fixed object constructed in an unchanged tube.

5. Capture, adhesion and the role of von Willebrand factor

At a damaged surface the extracellular matrix is exposed to blood. Platelets must first establish contact despite movement of the surrounding fluid. Von Willebrand factor is a large multimeric adhesive glycoprotein synthesised by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes. Endothelial cells can store it within Weibel–Palade bodies, while platelets store some VWF within alpha granules. In plasma it also carries and stabilises coagulation factor VIII. The two functions explain why a deficiency of VWF can interfere both with platelet adhesion and with the availability of a coagulation factor.

VWF can bind exposed matrix components and interact with the platelet GPIb-IX-V receptor complex, particularly through the GPIbα subunit. At sufficiently high shear, mechanical forces favour exposure of binding sites on immobilised or stretched VWF. Transient receptor–ligand interactions slow platelets relative to the moving blood and allow additional adhesive and signalling interactions to develop. This is especially important in arterial conditions. Under lower shear, direct contacts with matrix receptors can contribute more effectively. The relative roles of VWF and collagen receptors depend on the substrate and flow conditions.

The initial capture of a platelet must not be confused with the formation of a mechanically stable attachment. A platelet temporarily interacting with VWF may move along the injured surface or detach. GPVI engagement by collagen initiates intracellular signalling, while integrin α2β1 contributes to stable attachment to collagen. These events can overlap and reinforce one another. A platelet receptor mutation may therefore have a greater effect on one measured outcome than another: fewer transient contacts, reduced stable surface coverage, impaired secretion or failure to recruit additional cells.

VWF multimer length matters because larger multimers offer multiple binding opportunities and can be particularly effective under flow. ADAMTS13, a plasma metalloprotease, cleaves unfolded VWF at a susceptible site. This limits the persistence of exceptionally adhesive multimers. Severe reduction of ADAMTS13 activity can permit platelet-rich microvascular thrombi, as in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. This illustrates that a protein needed for haemostasis must itself be regulated to prevent uncontrolled vascular obstruction.

Two inherited disorders illustrate the distinction between ligand and receptor. In von Willebrand disease the amount or function of VWF is impaired; in Bernard–Soulier syndrome, the GPIb-IX-V complex is defective and platelets are often unusually large and reduced in number. Both conditions can impair VWF-dependent platelet interactions, but supplying normal plasma VWF can help an assay in certain VWF deficiencies, whereas it cannot replace a defective platelet receptor. An experiment based on mixing plasma and platelet sources must specify what is deficient and which component is supplied.

Ristocetin-induced platelet agglutination is a useful laboratory probe of the VWF–GPIb interaction. Ristocetin is an assay reagent, not a physiological agonist required at a wound. It promotes an interaction that leads to platelet clumping through VWF and GPIb. A response can be impaired by abnormalities of either binding partner. Ordinary agonist-induced platelet aggregation, by contrast, is typically assessed through signalling-dependent integrin activation and platelet cross-linking. The distinction prevents a common error in interpreting platelet function tests.

From molecular affinity to a stable surface contact

To understand why von Willebrand factor is useful under flow, distinguish binding affinity measured in a stationary system from the probability of making contact while an object moves. Affinity describes the tendency of a ligand and receptor to associate under specified conditions. A flowing platelet may encounter a wall-bound ligand only briefly; the association must occur within that contact window. Once the bond forms, the motion of blood applies forces to the attachment. A short-lived contact can still be valuable if it slows the platelet long enough for additional bonds to form. The correct sequence of reasoning is encounter, initial tether, stabilisation and then recruitment. Each of these stages can be measured independently.

A multimer contains several copies of a molecular subunit. In the case of VWF, repeated binding regions mean that a single extended multimer can interact at multiple sites. The collective strength of multiple interactions is often called avidity. Even if one contact breaks, other contacts may remain, allowing the structure to stay attached. A large multimer can thus support effective adhesive interactions without requiring each individual molecular bond to be permanent. An experimental difference between small and large VWF multimers should not be described simply as a change in concentration unless the way concentration was defined is known. Equal protein mass does not mean equal number of multimers, and equal multimer number does not mean equal total number of binding sites.

The VWF A1 domain interacts with GPIbα, whereas other regions participate in binding collagen or factor VIII. The A2 domain contains a site susceptible to ADAMTS13 cleavage when exposed by unfolding. The separation of molecular functions helps explain why mutations may selectively affect binding, multimer distribution or factor VIII stabilisation. It also clarifies why measurement of total VWF antigen may not fully describe its functional activity. A protein could be present at a near-normal concentration but defective in a relevant interaction. Measurements of amount and function answer different questions.

ADAMTS13 illustrates a regulatory process that depends on the mechanical state of the substrate. Under conditions that promote extension of the molecule, a normally less accessible region can become available for cleavage. This mechanism does not imply that ordinary circulating VWF is destroyed indiscriminately as soon as it enters plasma. The relative exposure of the susceptible region is important. The consequence of deficient cleavage is an increased persistence of very large adhesive forms of VWF, which can favour platelet recruitment in small vessels. It is also possible to have a low circulating platelet count because platelets have been recruited into pathological microvascular aggregates rather than because they are unable to adhere. Therefore a reduced platelet count is compatible with excessive adhesion in an appropriate disease mechanism.

Now consider how collagen recognition differs from the role of VWF. Collagen is an insoluble structural protein organised into the extracellular matrix. GPVI recognises appropriate collagen structures and stimulates a signalling response that can transform a transient contact into a stronger functional interaction. Integrin α2β1 contributes to adhesion to collagen, especially after activation. Neither receptor should be treated as an exact substitute for the other. A receptor whose principal role is signalling can alter the behaviour of other adhesion molecules; a receptor that contributes mainly to physical attachment can prolong the contact required for signalling. Distinguishing these roles does not require assuming that each has only one possible function.

One experimental approach compares an immobilised collagen surface with an immobilised VWF surface. A selective inhibition of collagen-induced signalling may have a large effect on activation following contact with collagen but need not abolish every VWF-dependent transient interaction. A selective inhibition of GPIb may severely impair initial capture under the conditions in which VWF is the dominant tether. In mixed physiological matrices the separation will not be as clean as on a single purified substrate. This is why using both purified components and more complex surfaces can be informative: simplified assays identify a contribution, and complex assays test whether that contribution remains important in a richer environment.

A static adhesion assay can also be misleading if it is interpreted as a flow assay. Equal numbers of platelets allowed to settle onto two surfaces may produce similar eventual coverage even though their initial capture probabilities under rapid flow are very different. In the static experiment gravity and time can allow cells to contact a surface, whereas flow imposes a brief interaction window and mechanical detachment forces. An appropriate experimental comparison measures outcomes under defined conditions rather than assigning a receptor a universal importance from a single experiment.

An elementary way to interpret time-resolved surface data is to write the number of attached platelets as a balance between arrival and detachment. If A is the number adhering in a defined field, J is the arrival rate of successfully captured platelets per minute, and k is a first-order detachment constant per minute, then dA/dt = J − kA in a dilute, unsaturated model. The steady value is J/k. In a teaching example, a preparation captures 60 platelets per minute and k is 0.2 per minute, yielding a steady attached number of 300. A second preparation captures 30 per minute with the same detachment rate, yielding 150. A third retains the initial capture rate but doubles k to 0.4 per minute, also yielding 150. The same final count conceals different mechanisms. Early tracking of new contacts and residence times would distinguish the second from the third preparation. Real platelet growth is cooperative, surface sites can saturate, and aggregate formation changes the environment, so the equation is an intentionally simplified model of early adhesion.

6. Signal transduction, secretion and amplification

Adhesion provides a means of detecting a damaged surface, but a platelet needs more than a mechanical contact to become an efficient participant in plug formation. Signals from collagen, released nucleotides, thrombin and thromboxane change intracellular enzyme activity, calcium concentration, lipid metabolism and the cytoskeleton. Their receptors belong to different molecular families, yet their downstream pathways converge on several common functional outputs. This makes platelet activation both flexible and resistant to the loss of a single weak signal.

GPVI associates with the Fc receptor γ-chain, which contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif. Receptor engagement promotes phosphorylation of this motif by Src-family kinases, recruitment of Syk and downstream activation of phospholipase Cγ2. The enzyme hydrolyses phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate into inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol. Inositol trisphosphate promotes calcium release from intracellular stores, while diacylglycerol participates in protein kinase C activation. Calcium entry and other regulatory pathways can sustain the response. This is an example of a tyrosine-kinase-associated adhesion receptor communicating with lipid-derived second messengers.

ADP released from damaged cells or activated platelets activates two major platelet purinergic receptors. P2Y1 couples to Gq and contributes to phospholipase Cβ signalling, calcium mobilisation and shape change. P2Y12 couples to Gi, suppresses adenylyl cyclase and supports signalling pathways that stabilise integrin activation. P2Y1 and P2Y12 are complementary rather than redundant. Blocking one may leave responses mediated by the other visible, yet reduce the ability to sustain aggregation. Interpreting experimental inhibition requires distinguishing an initial response from a persistent one.

A second amplification route depends on arachidonic acid. Activation can release arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids through phospholipase A2. Cyclooxygenase-1 converts this substrate through prostaglandin intermediates, and thromboxane synthase produces thromboxane A2. Thromboxane acts through TP receptors on platelets and can contribute to vascular smooth muscle contraction. It is a short-lived mediator generated on demand, not a stable substance stored in dense granules. Its short lifetime helps concentrate its action near activated cells, although it is not the only determinant of localisation.

Thrombin also activates human platelets through protease-activated receptors PAR1 and PAR4. Proteolytic cleavage of a receptor exposes a tethered ligand that can activate the receptor itself. This differs from ordinary reversible binding of a freely diffusible ligand. The mechanism allows an enzyme generated during coagulation to function as a potent platelet agonist. It is a clear reason why the division between primary and secondary haemostasis is a conceptual distinction rather than a rigid temporal boundary. The generation of thrombin is treated in the next node.

During secretion, granules fuse with membrane systems and their cargo becomes accessible to the extracellular environment. Dense-granule ADP recruits or strengthens the response of nearby platelets. Alpha-granule proteins contribute adhesive material, local protein interactions and repair-associated signals. Surface P-selectin can interact with PSGL-1 on leukocytes, connecting haemostasis with inflammatory communication. Measurement of surface P-selectin is therefore useful as one indicator of alpha-granule release, but it does not measure dense-granule secretion directly.

An activated platelet can exhibit several states rather than a single all-or-none phenotype. Some platelets excel at spreading and aggregation, whereas a subset exposed to strong combined agonists undergoes substantial phosphatidylserine externalisation and becomes highly procoagulant. Negatively charged phosphatidylserine supports the assembly of calcium-dependent clotting complexes. The degree of integrin-mediated aggregation and the degree of procoagulant membrane exposure need not be identical in every platelet. This distinction is important when interpreting an assay in which one output changes but another remains comparatively intact.

A positive feedback mechanism exists because the products of activated platelets stimulate additional platelets, which can release more mediators. A response of this kind can convert a modest local stimulus into a substantial change in plug size. Yet unchecked amplification would be dangerous. Soluble mediators are diluted by flow, metabolised or otherwise removed, and nearby intact endothelial cells provide inhibitory signals. The spatial organisation of the wound, rather than a molecular switch alone, confines a useful haemostatic response.

Causal dissection of the converging signalling network

Platelet signalling becomes easier to reason about when the type of receptor is identified before memorising the pathway. A collagen receptor associated with a phosphorylatable signalling chain does not use the same immediate activation mechanism as a G-protein-coupled receptor. Yet both can activate phospholipases and change cytosolic calcium. This convergence explains why blocking a component shared by several pathways can have broader effects than blocking an upstream receptor specific to one stimulus. It also explains why inhibition of a single receptor does not necessarily eliminate activation by all other agonists.

Phospholipase C provides a useful bridge between membrane biochemistry and intracellular signalling. Its substrate PIP2 is a minor but functionally important membrane phospholipid. Hydrolysis creates IP3, which can diffuse through the cytosol, and diacylglycerol, which remains associated with the membrane. The products therefore carry information to different locations. IP3 binding to intracellular calcium-release channels helps raise cytosolic calcium. Diacylglycerol supports activation of appropriate protein kinase C isoforms, with calcium dependence varying among isoforms. The two products can influence overlapping functions, but one cannot be substituted automatically for the other.

A calcium transient is not a single event with a fixed magnitude and duration. Calcium may increase rapidly and then decline, remain elevated or show a complex temporal pattern. Pumps and exchangers help restore low cytosolic concentration, and depletion of internal stores can promote store-operated entry across the membrane. The magnitude and duration of calcium elevation influence downstream responses. In particular, strong and sustained calcium signals are important for procoagulant phospholipid exposure, whereas a shorter transient can support shape change and other activation outputs. A graph of average calcium concentration across many platelets can hide a subpopulation with much higher signals. Single-cell observations can therefore be useful alongside population averages.

The ADP response illustrates cooperation between signalling routes that at first appear contradictory. Gq signalling can increase calcium and promote rapid changes, while Gi signalling suppresses adenylyl cyclase. Reducing cyclic AMP removes part of an inhibitory influence. This is not the same as directly generating calcium through an identical pathway. Instead, two coordinated inputs help shift the platelet toward a stable adhesive state. P2Y12 signalling also involves phosphoinositide 3-kinase-dependent mechanisms that support sustained activation. A response that begins normally but reverses prematurely is thus compatible with loss of one reinforcing branch of the network.

Another nucleotide-sensitive receptor, P2X1, is an ATP-gated ion channel rather than a metabotropic P2Y receptor. It can contribute to rapid ion entry in response to ATP. The existence of these receptor types is a useful examination distinction because ATP and ADP are related nucleotides but do not have interchangeable signalling functions. A channel changes ion permeability directly upon activation; a G-protein-coupled receptor acts through intracellular intermediates. A response to extracellular ATP is not sufficient evidence for P2Y12 activation, particularly when the kinetics and antagonist sensitivity are not known.

Thromboxane generation requires access to an appropriate membrane-derived fatty acid. Cyclooxygenase activity is upstream of thromboxane synthase, and thromboxane itself is unstable. A direct TP-receptor agonist can be useful in a conceptual rescue experiment. If a manipulation blocks synthesis of thromboxane but leaves TP receptor signalling intact, a suitable receptor agonist can bypass the blocked production step. If the receptor itself is absent, supplying more ligand does not restore that missing target. Rescue therefore helps identify the order of pathway components, provided that the downstream machinery remains functional and the agonist is selective under the tested conditions.

Thrombin provides a further example of different mechanisms converging on similar outputs. PAR activation involves receptor cleavage, but the newly exposed tethered ligand initiates signalling through the receptor rather than merely destroying it. A synthetic agonist peptide corresponding to a suitable tethered ligand can activate the relevant receptor without reproducing all the enzymatic actions of thrombin. This distinction permits researchers to examine platelet receptor signalling separately from thrombin-dependent conversion of fibrinogen. Human and mouse platelets do not use identical combinations of protease-activated receptors, so species must be specified when interpreting a receptor-specific experiment.

Students may be tempted to assume that platelets have a universal activation hierarchy in which collagen must always act first, ADP must always act second and thrombin must always act last. That is a pedagogical simplification rather than a general experimental rule. At a lesion, spatial proximity to collagen, local thrombin production, the availability of secreted mediators and the movement of fluid all vary. A platelet initially distant from the exposed collagen may be recruited by mediators released by other platelets. It can consequently acquire activated integrins without itself directly touching the collagen-rich surface.

The molecular switch Rap1 helps connect signals to integrin activation. Like many small GTPases, Rap1 cycles between GDP-bound and GTP-bound states. Regulators influence nucleotide exchange or hydrolysis and therefore the duration of activity. Calcium-responsive exchange mechanisms can promote activation, and reinforcing agonist pathways can support the activated state. Talin and kindlin associate with integrin cytoplasmic regions and contribute to changes in extracellular ligand binding. The useful conceptual link is not that calcium directly turns fibrinogen into an adhesive polymer; it is that intracellular signals alter the competence of a pre-existing membrane receptor.

A perturbation experiment could distinguish calcium signalling from integrin activation. Suppose a preparation produces a near-normal cytosolic calcium response to a defined agonist yet fails to bind a conformation-sensitive integrin probe. A lesion downstream of calcium, or in a parallel pathway needed for integrin regulation, is plausible. Alternatively, the receptor itself or the probe epitope may be abnormal. To discriminate these possibilities, measure total receptor expression with a conformation-independent antibody, examine other activation responses and test ligand binding by a second method. One readout alone cannot localise every possible lesion.

Platelet responses also need to be studied in time. A sample can show a normal early peak of calcium yet have a reduced sustained phase. If the secretion event occurs early, a single late measurement may underestimate initial release. If the question concerns stable aggregation, an early reading may overestimate functional competence. The appropriate analysis is a matched time series with well-defined measurement points. A time-resolved experiment enables a distinction between the ability to initiate activation and the ability to maintain it.

7. Integrin-mediated aggregation and consolidation

Adhesion links a platelet to a surface. Aggregation links platelets to one another. The central receptor involved in classical platelet aggregation is integrin αIIbβ3, historically called GPIIb/IIIa. In the resting state its binding properties prevent circulating fibrinogen from spontaneously cross-linking every platelet. Agonist signalling changes the integrin through intracellular control involving the small GTPase Rap1 and adaptor proteins such as talin and kindlin. These interactions favour an integrin conformation capable of binding soluble adhesive ligands. The direction of information transfer is described as inside-out signalling because the intracellular signal changes extracellular binding behaviour.

Fibrinogen is a soluble plasma protein with binding sites that permit it to connect activated integrins on neighbouring platelets. Repeated bridges build a multicellular aggregate. VWF can also contribute to integrin-dependent cohesion, especially under particular flow conditions. This is distinct from fibrin formation: fibrinogen is a ligand during platelet aggregation before it is converted by thrombin into the polymerising fibrin components of a clot. A platelet can thus participate in aggregation even in an experimental system in which fibrin production is prevented.

Ligand binding to αIIbβ3 can in turn generate signals inside the platelet. Such outside-in signalling supports spreading, cytoskeletal organisation and mechanical stabilisation. Actin and myosin generate contractile force that can be transmitted to fibrin through integrin-linked contacts as the clot matures. Clot retraction is not simply a reduction in platelet count, nor is it clot dissolution. It reflects active mechanical rearrangement that compacts the clot. Fibrinolysis, by contrast, involves enzymatic degradation of fibrin.

Glanzmann thrombasthenia provides evidence that αIIbβ3 is essential for conventional aggregation. Platelets can retain important upstream responses but fail to aggregate normally when the integrin is absent or dysfunctional. A ristocetin-dependent VWF–GPIb assay can be relatively preserved because it does not depend on the same integrin bridge. Comparing this pattern with Bernard–Soulier syndrome allows students to localise defects to different molecular steps rather than relying solely on the observation that both disorders cause bleeding.

The growing plug is not necessarily uniform. Near the injured wall, intense activation and coagulation signals can produce a densely packed core; more peripheral platelets can be less strongly activated and rely more on soluble feedback. The exact architecture depends on vessel size, injury model, flow and time. A core–shell description is useful for interpreting experiments, but not every thrombus has an identical arrangement. In an idealised model, limiting diffusion of an agonist from the core may increase local concentration while exposing peripheral cells to a weaker stimulus.

Mechanical stability requires a balance between adhesive strength and forces tending to detach material. Even a platelet with high-affinity integrins may not remain in a plug if the relevant ligand is missing or flow forces exceed adhesion. Conversely, a strong adhesion reaction does not by itself guarantee that an adequate seal has formed over the full injury. The appropriate outcome is functional closure of the lesion, not simply maximum aggregate size in a laboratory tube.

Mechanical reinforcement and heterogeneous plug architecture

An aggregate forms when several platelets make sufficiently persistent adhesive contacts. The geometry of those contacts is consequential. A platelet with a large spread area against a solid surface can transmit forces through many attachments. A platelet at the edge of a growing plug may contact only one or a few other platelets and may detach more easily when flow changes. As more cells accumulate, internal gaps and diffusion paths are reorganised. The plug itself becomes a microenvironment that influences the fate of subsequently recruited platelets.

The fibrinogen bridge is a molecular connection, not simply glue coating a platelet indiscriminately. Ligand binding depends on the appropriate receptor conformation and available binding sites. When an integrin engages its ligand, association with intracellular cytoskeletal machinery can help transmit force. A cell deficient in a component required for outside-in signalling may bind ligand after stimulation yet spread poorly or fail to develop normal mechanical reinforcement. An assay that measures initial fibrinogen binding could therefore look relatively preserved while later structural stability is compromised. The distinction between activation and force transmission is useful beyond platelets because integrins mediate mechanochemical connections in many cell types.

Myosin-dependent contraction requires energy and an appropriate actin framework. A clot containing platelets and fibrin can become more compact as contractile forces act through adhesive interactions. The reduction in clot volume may expel fluid from the fibrin-rich structure. It is not evidence that all the fibrin has been chemically degraded. A useful experimental distinction is to compare clot volume, intact fibrin structure and soluble degradation products. Reduced volume with relatively preserved fibrin material supports compaction; release of fibrin degradation products supports enzymatic breakdown. The two processes may occur at different stages during the lifetime of a clot.

The heterogeneous organisation of a platelet plug has experimental support from animal injury models. Stalker and colleagues reported that regions near the injury could differ from regions at the periphery in activation state and packing. Fluorescent markers of platelet accumulation and selected activation outputs help distinguish a dense region from a region that is merely populated by platelets. Further studies of transport between cells found that solute movement can be restricted in densely packed regions. The findings establish that aggregate architecture and agonist distribution influence one another in the investigated systems; they do not impose a fixed geometry on every haemostatic lesion.

A model illustrates how agonist transport and removal could create spatial differences. Consider a small region of the plug in which a soluble agonist enters at rate S and is removed with an effective first-order constant k. The amount X obeys dX/dt = S − kX if the region and rate constants are held fixed. At steady state X = S/k. If two equally sized regions receive the same input of ten arbitrary amount units per minute but the first has k = 0.5 per minute and the second k = 2 per minute, the steady amounts are 20 and 5 units respectively. A fourfold difference arises even without changing the rate of synthesis. In a real plug, diffusion, binding, flow and degradation are not adequately represented by one constant, but the model illustrates why retained agonist can enhance local signalling.

It follows that loss of peripheral platelets in an experimental injury model need not mean that initial adhesion at the lesion has disappeared. A manipulation of ADP-dependent amplification may preferentially affect the recruitment or retention of less strongly stimulated platelets. Conversely, a perturbation of a collagen-recognition mechanism may compromise early contact and activation near the exposed matrix. Measurements of total accumulated platelet mass without spatial information can miss these distinctions. Separate measurements of core and peripheral regions can give more mechanistic information, provided the regions are defined objectively rather than chosen after observing the expected result.

Finally, an adequate seal is not necessarily the largest possible thrombus. A plug that stops blood cell escape may still permit some plasma leakage, while a compact plug can further reduce movement of plasma solutes. The functional endpoint must be specified. A research study assessing leakage of a fluorescent plasma tracer does not measure exactly the same event as one tracking escape of erythrocytes. For physiological reasoning, distinguish cessation of visible bleeding, retention of plasma constituents, restoration of wall integrity and preservation of downstream flow.

8. Keeping the response local and interpreting dysfunction

Inhibitory mechanisms can be understood as control points acting on platelet signalling, ligand availability and the environment. Nitric oxide increases cyclic GMP; prostacyclin increases cyclic AMP. Their downstream protein kinases can restrain calcium-dependent activation and other functional responses. CD39 lowers extracellular nucleotide signals. Flow dilutes secreted agonists, and intact endothelium conceals the matrix. These factors together establish a boundary between the wound and adjacent patent vessel.

The loss of one inhibitory pathway may not spontaneously produce a thrombus in every vessel because other inhibitory influences remain. However, the probability of an inappropriate response can rise in the presence of other stimuli. The same reasoning applies to amplification pathways: blocking thromboxane production may reduce aggregation induced by a weak stimulus but need not eliminate a strong response driven by thrombin. Physiological networks rarely behave as simple one-step reactions with only one possible input.

Aspirin is a classical experimental and pharmacological example because it irreversibly acetylates platelet cyclooxygenase-1, decreasing thromboxane production. Platelets cannot readily replace the inhibited enzyme by new nuclear transcription. Newly produced platelets restore activity over time. P2Y12 antagonists instead interfere with ADP-mediated signalling, and αIIbβ3 antagonism interferes with the final integrin-dependent cohesion step. These are distinct intervention points; the expected experimental signatures differ. This discussion concerns mechanism, not drug selection or dosing.

Thrombocytopenia denotes a reduced platelet count; thrombocytosis denotes an increased count. Either may arise from a variety of mechanisms. A qualitative platelet disorder may occur despite a count within the reference interval. Defects in storage granules, receptors, enzymes or signal transduction can compromise the contribution of each platelet. Conversely, an apparently low automated count may reflect platelet clumping in the specimen rather than genuine depletion of circulating platelets. A blood film or an appropriately recollected sample can help distinguish the possibilities.

Bleeding from defects of primary haemostasis often involves superficial mucocutaneous sites, including petechiae and epistaxis, whereas severe coagulation factor deficiencies often produce deeper bleeding. These are tendencies, not absolute diagnostic rules, because the two haemostatic systems interact and some diseases affect both. In particular VWF deficiency may reduce factor VIII activity as well as impair platelet adhesion. A normal plasma clotting time does not exclude a platelet defect because routine clotting-time assays are designed mainly around soluble coagulation reactions rather than adhesion under flow.

Excessive platelet activation and aggregation can contribute to arterial thrombosis when a vascular lesion exposes thrombogenic material. Atherosclerotic plaque disruption is one important setting. The same receptor–ligand interactions that help seal a wound may now contribute to pathological occlusion of an artery. The difference lies in the biological context and extent of the response, not in the invention of an entirely separate platelet mechanism. Understanding this continuity links animal physiology with disease without requiring specialist clinical protocols.

Inhibitory signalling, endothelial integrity and the threshold for activation

The presence of circulating agonists does not necessarily produce a haemostatic response. The concentration at the receptor, the duration of exposure, the activity of inhibitory pathways and the availability of adhesive surfaces all contribute to the outcome. A platelet moving through an intact vessel repeatedly encounters signals from the endothelium that favour a resting state. At a wound, an activation signal can become sufficiently strong or persistent to overcome those restraints. This organisation is more informative than regarding resting and activated platelets as two completely separate cell types.

Nitric oxide is a diffusible messenger rather than a conventional secretory protein that must bind a membrane receptor. Endothelial nitric oxide synthase produces it from L-arginine in an appropriate cellular environment. Once nitric oxide reaches a platelet, it stimulates soluble guanylyl cyclase, promoting synthesis of cyclic GMP. Cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinase can influence calcium handling and other components of activation. The physiological effect is that a given weak agonist stimulus becomes less effective. This provides an example of an extracellular signal that acts on an intracellular enzyme after crossing a membrane.

Prostacyclin is generated from lipid-derived intermediates by endothelial cells. It binds the IP receptor, which can signal through Gs to stimulate adenylyl cyclase and raise cyclic AMP. Cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase can restrain calcium mobilisation and adhesive responses. This signalling pathway is opposed in part by Gi-mediated P2Y12 activation. It is not necessary for prostacyclin to remove ADP from solution in order to oppose an ADP-dependent response; the two inputs can influence intracellular signalling in different directions. CD39, by contrast, acts directly on the extracellular availability of nucleotide agonists.

The balance between prostacyclin and thromboxane provides a useful illustration of opposing effects of related lipid mediators. Both arise from arachidonic acid metabolism through cyclooxygenase-derived intermediates, but the terminal synthetic enzymes and biological targets differ. Thromboxane A2 favours platelet recruitment and can support vasoconstriction, whereas prostacyclin restrains activation and supports vasodilation. The physiological effects depend on the cells producing the mediators and on their receptor distribution. It is a mistake to predict the effect of every prostanoid solely from the fact that it originated from arachidonic acid.

The local degradation of extracellular ADP by endothelial CD39 illustrates the principle of termination of a signal. A secreted mediator can remain biologically important even if its average concentration in circulating blood is very low, provided that it reaches a sufficient local concentration near receptors before being removed. Conversely, blocking a degradation enzyme can enhance an agonist response without increasing the amount released per platelet. A direct measurement of secretion and a measurement of local agonist persistence are therefore distinct experiments. In an illustrative system, identical initial amounts of extracellular ADP could decay at different rates after adding endothelial ectonucleotidase activity. The time for the concentration to fall below an activation threshold would then differ.

A simple first-order model helps make this argument quantitative. If extracellular mediator concentration C decays according to dC/dt = −kC, the solution is C(t) = C₀e⁻ᵏᵗ, where k is a rate constant in inverse time. The half-life is ln(2)/k. With an illustrative k of 0.693 per minute, the half-life is about one minute; after three minutes, one eighth of the starting concentration remains. If k is halved, the half-life doubles to approximately two minutes. For an initial concentration of eight arbitrary units and a threshold of two units, the threshold is reached after two half-lives: about two minutes in the first model and four minutes in the second. No claim is made that physiological ADP always follows this exponential decay; secretion, flow and enzyme kinetics can change throughout a haemostatic response.

Cyclic nucleotide turnover provides an analogous intracellular example. A measured cyclic AMP concentration reflects both production by adenylyl cyclase and removal by phosphodiesterases. A high level could result from enhanced synthesis or reduced degradation. If a receptor antagonist lowers the concentration of a messenger, it does not establish which enzyme has changed without further evidence. Measuring the synthesis rate under defined conditions or examining the response to a phosphodiesterase inhibitor can help distinguish mechanisms. The general lesson is that concentrations represent a balance of fluxes rather than a direct reading of a single enzymatic activity.

The architecture of an intact endothelial monolayer also limits exposure of tissue factor and extracellular matrix. A preparation of isolated platelets lacks this boundary, so it can reveal platelet-intrinsic responses while omitting endothelial restraint. Addition of an endothelial-derived messenger to such a preparation restores one regulatory influence but does not recreate the entire vascular wall. Experiments involving intact vessels or endothelial co-culture can help examine regulation in context. They introduce additional variables, however, because the endothelium responds dynamically to the same changes in flow and chemical conditions.

A useful comparison is between two hypothetical platelet preparations. One has a normal response to a strong agonist but responds excessively to weak stimulation. Another fails to respond to either weak or strong agonists. The first could reflect a defect in inhibition or amplification threshold, while the second suggests an important failure of activation machinery or viability. Neither conclusion follows from the count alone. Constructing concentration–response curves rather than testing one arbitrary concentration can help identify which aspect of responsiveness is altered.

The threshold of an activation response should not be confused with the maximal response. A treatment might shift the agonist concentration needed to achieve half the maximal response while leaving that maximum relatively unchanged. Another treatment might reduce the maximum even at high agonist concentrations. The first pattern suggests altered sensitivity under the tested conditions, whereas the second suggests a limited capacity or an incompletely bypassable pathway. The interpretation depends on assay design, receptor occupancy, network feedback and the definition of the response. A maximal aggregation value is not a direct measurement of a single receptor's affinity.

Inherited and acquired defects as physiological experiments

A platelet disorder can be understood as a perturbation of an otherwise organised sequence. Bernard–Soulier syndrome illustrates impaired initial recognition of VWF, Glanzmann thrombasthenia illustrates impaired integrin-dependent cohesion, and dense-granule storage pool disorders illustrate impaired amplification. These are useful idealised categories because each points toward a different functional component. In reality, a genetic abnormality may have secondary effects, and disease severity can vary with the residual function of the affected protein and other biological factors. The examination problem usually provides a simplified pattern that allows the major defect to be identified.

A basic distinction in a mixing experiment is between the liquid component and the platelet component. Suppose a patient's plasma contains deficient functional VWF but their platelets express an intact GPIb complex. Mixing an appropriately prepared sample of those platelets with a normal source of VWF can improve the relevant agglutination response. If GPIb itself is defective, adding a normal ligand source cannot restore the missing binding interface. A converse test combining normal platelets with patient plasma can provide complementary information about the plasma component. The reasoning is based on complementation of a missing function. Actual clinical assays require specialised sample handling, validated controls and interpretation of VWF subtypes; a simple mixing exercise is not a complete diagnostic method.

Glanzmann thrombasthenia may be caused by alterations in the αIIb or β3 integrin subunit. The heterodimer is needed for the conventional platelet aggregation response to multiple agonists. A defect can reduce expression of the receptor or impair its functional behaviour despite detectable expression. These alternatives demonstrate why receptor abundance and receptor activation are separate measurements. An immunological assay that uses an antibody to a constitutive receptor epitope may show reduced expression in one case but not in another. An activation-sensitive assay, ligand-binding measurement and functional aggregation experiment can together resolve some of this uncertainty.

Dense-granule deficiency differs mechanistically from failure of the receptor that detects released ADP. In one case the amplifier is not supplied adequately; in the other the platelet cannot respond normally to the supplied amplifier. Consider an experiment in which exogenous ADP is added to an isolated preparation. If the receptor and downstream machinery are intact, providing extracellular ADP can compensate for some consequences of insufficient release. A P2Y receptor defect would not be restored simply by replacing missing secreted nucleotide, although the presence of more than one receptor and the strength of stimulation complicate the observed response. A rescue experiment needs to measure a specific endpoint rather than assume complete restoration of every platelet function.

Some platelet defects arise from medication or systemic illness rather than inherited variants. The irreversible effect of aspirin on platelet cyclooxygenase is useful for thinking about enzyme replacement in an anucleate cell. The inhibited platelet population does not regain normal enzyme activity simply because the inhibitor disappears from plasma. Restoration depends importantly on new platelets entering the circulation. In contrast, a reversibly bound inhibitor can lose its effect as occupancy falls if receptor function is otherwise preserved. The distinction between drug clearance and biological recovery is a general pharmacological principle and does not require assuming that all antiplatelet drugs behave like aspirin.

If the platelet pool is replaced uniformly over an illustrative ten-day period, approximately one tenth of the pool would be replaced per day under a simple fixed-lifespan model at steady state. After three days, about 30% of the original inhibited population could have been replaced, assuming constant production and removal according to age. A different model with random removal and an exponential residence-time distribution would yield a different time course. The calculation illustrates why platelet turnover matters for persistent enzyme inhibition; it does not provide a medically valid schedule for stopping or restarting medicines.

A low platelet count can reflect consumption in a process characterised by excessive activation. This is particularly important when comparing a simple marrow production defect with a microvascular thrombotic disorder. In the first, fewer platelets enter the circulating pool. In the second, platelets may be removed into aggregates faster than the marrow can replace them. Both conditions can produce thrombocytopenia, yet their causal physiology differs. The combination of count, marrow response, evidence of vascular injury and other findings is more informative than the count alone. Thrombocytopenia should therefore never be treated as a synonym for inability to form thrombi.

An additional laboratory pitfall is pseudothrombocytopenia due to in vitro platelet clumping. Automated instruments may classify clumps incorrectly or fail to count individual platelets within them. The resulting number may be lower than the true circulating concentration. Examination of a blood film and comparison with a properly collected alternative specimen can help reveal this artefact. This example belongs to a general lesson in scientific measurement: before interpreting a numerical abnormality physiologically, confirm that the measured value represents the intended biological variable.

Routine tests of soluble coagulation have different endpoints from platelet function assays. Prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time are commonly performed in systems designed to investigate plasma coagulation reactions. They are not direct measures of the ability of platelets to capture VWF under high shear, release dense-granule nucleotides or activate αIIbβ3. A normal result cannot exclude such abnormalities. Conversely, a deficiency affecting factor VIII through reduced VWF stabilisation can influence a plasma coagulation test in some settings. The two haemostatic systems overlap, but each assay has a specific experimental scope.

9. Experimental evidence and quantitative interpretation

A platelet count measures the concentration of particles classified as platelets. Microscopy provides complementary evidence about size, morphology and clumping. Neither directly measures the capacity for adhesion or secretion. The choice of an experimental endpoint should therefore be determined by the question rather than by the convenience of the instrument.

In 1962 Gustav Born reported a photometric approach for studying aggregation induced by ADP. Platelet-rich plasma is relatively turbid because suspended platelets scatter light. When platelets form sufficiently large aggregates, light transmission generally increases. A platelet-poor plasma sample supplies a high-transmission reference and the initial platelet-rich plasma supplies a low-transmission reference. The experiment makes aggregation an observable time-dependent quantity and allows investigators to compare the effects of different agonists or inhibitors. Its value lies in measuring a dynamic response rather than simply examining a static blood film.

Suppose an illustrative light-transmission experiment uses a platelet-rich baseline of 20 arbitrary units and a platelet-poor reference of 80 units. After stimulation the signal reaches 62 units. Relative transmission-based aggregation is estimated by 100 × (62 − 20)/(80 − 20) = 70%. If the signal in a treated sample reaches 38 units using the same references, the value is 30%. The difference is 40 percentage points. Expressed relative to the untreated response, the decrease is 40/70 × 100 = approximately 57%. These numbers are a teaching dataset, not published measurements. A change in optical transmission does not directly count every aggregated platelet and can be affected by the quality of the sample, platelet concentration and assay conditions.

To test whether ADP receptors contribute to the maintenance of an aggregate, one could compare agonist responses in paired samples from the same donor with vehicle or a selective receptor antagonist. Equal platelet counts, comparable sample handling, identical agonist concentration and controlled timing would be important. An early rise followed by a loss of transmission response would suggest that initiation occurred but stability was impaired. A second agonist can help distinguish an ADP-specific defect from a broader inability to activate integrins. Such an experiment supports a pathway interpretation but does not alone identify the receptor genotype.

Flow cytometry can measure the exposure of P-selectin after secretion and detect activated αIIbβ3 using conformation-sensitive probes such as PAC-1 in human platelet assays. In an illustrative comparison, a sample showing normal agonist-induced P-selectin exposure but little PAC-1 binding suggests separation of secretion from integrin activation. This inference assumes that antibodies bind normally, the reagents are appropriate and gating identifies comparable platelet populations. Loss of an antibody epitope could otherwise imitate loss of the biological response. Unstimulated controls and controls for antibody binding are therefore essential.

A flow chamber containing collagen or immobilised VWF allows researchers to expose a surface to blood under controlled flow. By microscopy one can distinguish initial contacts, contact duration, surface coverage and later aggregate growth. Selective blockade of GPIb might markedly reduce capture on VWF at high shear, whereas blockade of αIIbβ3 may allow some initial capture but prevent efficient platelet–platelet accumulation. The conditions must be described because a result obtained on one substrate at one shear rate cannot be extrapolated to every vessel.

A factorial experiment can compare intact and inhibited collagen signalling at low and high shear. If receptor inhibition has a disproportionately large effect at one flow condition, this suggests an interaction between biochemical recognition and mechanical transport. An interaction cannot be inferred merely because one condition has a significant difference and another does not; the contrast between effects must itself be considered. Biological replicates, matched samples and independent quantification help distinguish a reproducible interaction from noise.

The safest strategy for solving a platelet experiment is to identify the readout first. A count reports abundance, an adhesion assay reports contact, an activation marker reports a selected signalling output and an aggregation assay reports multicellular cohesion. A change in one readout may influence the others but does not logically establish that all changed. This principle is transferable to many biological systems in which an observed phenotype is several causal steps downstream from the intervention.

Designing discriminating tests and analysing imperfect evidence

The goal of a Part C question is often to select an experiment that can distinguish two reasonable explanations for the same observation. Platelet physiology provides many such examples because the final formation of an aggregate depends on several processes. A reduced maximal aggregation response could arise from fewer platelets, poor initial activation, a secretion defect, deficient fibrinogen availability or a failure of integrin function. A single endpoint at the end of an assay usually cannot identify which stage is responsible. The first step is to build a causal map, then choose additional measurements located before and after the suspected stage.

In a hypothetical experiment the following values are obtained after ADP stimulation of matched platelet samples. The control has high P-selectin exposure, high activated-integrin probe binding and substantial aggregation. Sample X retains high P-selectin exposure but has low probe binding and low aggregation. Sample Y has low P-selectin exposure but retains considerable activated-integrin binding and moderate aggregation. The data suggest that secretion and integrin activation are separable outputs. They do not establish that sample X necessarily lacks the integrin or that sample Y necessarily lacks alpha granules. Total receptor abundance, resting fluorescence, granule imaging and direct secretion measurements would help test those alternatives.

A useful complement to optical aggregation is measurement of extracellular ATP by a luciferin–luciferase-based system. Luciferase-dependent light production can be calibrated against known ATP standards within an appropriate range. In a platelet secretion experiment, the signal reports extracellular ATP as a proxy for release from nucleotide-containing granules. It is not a direct measurement of the concentration of every secreted compound, and extracellular ATP can originate from other sources if the sample contains damaged cells. A low ATP signal could reflect low cellular content, poor release, altered nucleotide degradation or a problem with the detection system. Calibration, blank samples and assessment of sample quality are necessary before assigning a mechanism.

A blood film can reveal platelet morphology and clumping but cannot reveal the amount of soluble VWF circulating in plasma. A VWF antigen assay can estimate protein abundance but does not itself establish binding performance. Ristocetin-dependent methods probe a particular adhesive interaction but do not reproduce every aspect of physiological wall capture. Each method removes some of the complexity of the living vessel in exchange for clearer interpretation of a selected feature. Understanding those trade-offs is more important than memorising a rigid diagnostic table.

In an assay comparing plasma and platelet contributions, experimental design determines whether a negative result is informative. If normal platelets are added to a patient's plasma and the response is restored, the result may suggest that the platelet component of the original system was limiting, provided that the plasma and platelet concentrations are appropriately controlled. If normal plasma restores the response of patient platelets, a soluble component may be limiting. If neither manipulation restores the response, there may be multiple defects, inadequate experimental conditions or an incorrect assumption about the measured mechanism. One should not automatically infer an entirely new biological pathway whenever a rescue experiment fails.

The same principles apply to an experiment using specific agonists. A researcher studying cyclooxygenase might compare stimulation by arachidonic acid with stimulation by a direct TP agonist. The first depends on downstream lipid mediator synthesis, whereas the second can bypass the requirement for synthesis of thromboxane. A response lost to arachidonic acid but retained with a direct TP agonist supports a defect in a synthesis-related step, assuming the assays were properly matched and alternative effects were excluded. This experiment identifies pathway order without requiring a complete catalogue of every intermediate enzyme.

Flow cytometric data require care with denominators. If the analysis reports that 70% of gated platelets bind an activation-sensitive antibody, this does not state the absolute number of positive platelets in the blood. If the total concentration is 100 × 10⁹/L, the corresponding concentration of positive platelets is approximately 70 × 10⁹/L. A second preparation with 50% positivity but 200 × 10⁹/L would contain about 100 × 10⁹/L positive platelets. The sample with lower percentage can contain more positive cells per litre. Both percentages and counts may be relevant, but they answer different questions.

There is also a difference between the percentage of cells exceeding a fluorescence threshold and the average fluorescence intensity among those cells. A treatment could reduce the intensity of activated-integrin binding on most platelets without moving many below the positive threshold. Another treatment could leave the response of a subpopulation unchanged while eliminating the response in the remainder. Histograms or single-cell distributions can help distinguish the patterns. Mean values alone can obscure heterogeneity, especially if procoagulant and aggregating platelet states are represented differently in the population.

A simple hypothetical concentration–response dataset can illustrate this. At an agonist concentration of one arbitrary unit the control reaches 30% aggregation, at four units 65%, and at sixteen units 90%. A treated sample reaches 8%, 32% and 86% at the same concentrations. The near-recovery at high concentration suggests a reduction of responsiveness to low and intermediate stimulation under the test conditions, rather than complete loss of the capacity to aggregate. It would be premature to conclude that the antagonist competes directly at a receptor; a change in downstream signalling or amplification can produce a similar shape. Direct binding or a more complete dose–response analysis would be needed to support a specific pharmacological mechanism.

Replicates are important when evaluating these comparisons. Repeating a measurement several times on aliquots from one donor can estimate technical variation, but it does not show whether the finding applies to a wider population. Samples from different donors provide biological replication. Pairing treated and untreated aliquots from the same donor controls some donor-specific differences. A robust conclusion should consider the size and consistency of the effect across biological samples, not merely the visual difference between two representative traces. These are standard principles of experimental design that can be applied directly to a CSIR-NET reasoning question.

Finally, a mechanistic conclusion should be stated at the level supported by the evidence. If a selective blocker reduces surface coverage, the safest inference is that the blocked process contributes to surface coverage under the tested conditions. A stronger conclusion that the receptor is absolutely essential for all haemostasis would require much broader evidence. If an effect is absent in a static assay but present under flow, the interaction with mechanical conditions is scientifically interesting rather than contradictory. The aim of integrating multiple approaches is to identify the circumstances under which each mechanism becomes important, while respecting the limits of the experimental system.

Integrated reasoning: following a lesion from detection to sealing

Imagine that a small arteriole is injured while the blood remains under pressure. The first prediction concerns the accessibility of the wall, rather than platelet activation in isolation. Loss of endothelial continuity removes a physical barrier and creates a local region in which platelets can encounter extracellular matrix. Vasoconstriction may reduce the rate of escape, but it cannot substitute for molecular adhesion when a breach persists. Initial platelet capture occurs in a changing flow field. The relative importance of VWF depends partly on that field and on the exposed surface. If the capture mechanism is disabled, a platelet that can aggregate perfectly in a stirred laboratory tube may nevertheless fail to reach the place where aggregation is required.

Once a platelet is retained, collagen and locally available agonists can generate intracellular signals. An early calcium increase is evidence for one part of this process, not a measure of final plug size. Secretion changes the extracellular environment, supplying signals that affect other platelets. Meanwhile activated integrins allow platelets to adhere to one another through appropriate ligands. A platelet's capacity to aggregate can thus be understood at two levels: the intracellular state of each participating platelet and the availability of molecular bridges between them. If soluble fibrinogen is absent from an otherwise suitable experimental suspension, activation-sensitive probes may still recognise activated integrins even though conventional fibrinogen-dependent cohesion is compromised.

The process has an important spatial consequence. The first adherent platelets are close to the exposed matrix, while later recruits may lie several cell diameters away. They do not necessarily receive identical collagen signals. Instead, the later recruits can be influenced by released ADP, locally produced thromboxane and thrombin. A manipulation that prevents one form of secretory feedback may thus have a relatively modest effect on early retention at a collagen surface but a much larger effect on the growth of an outer region of a plug. The predicted outcome is not necessarily complete loss of all platelet accumulation. This is the sort of graded, context-dependent prediction that distinguishes mechanistic understanding from an all-or-none memorised pathway.

The developing platelet structure also helps support coagulation. The negative charge supplied by exposed phosphatidylserine can facilitate assembly of particular enzyme and cofactor complexes in the presence of calcium. Thrombin generated in the developing haemostatic environment contributes both to platelet activation and to fibrin formation. Fibrin can then reinforce the local structure and provide a substrate through which platelet contractile forces act. It is possible to explain why a platelet plug and fibrin reinforcement cooperate without learning the full clotting cascade at this stage. That cascade is the subject of the next chapter. For the current node, the important boundary is that platelet aggregation and the enzymatic conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin are related but distinct mechanisms.

Now compare three hypothetical disturbances at the same injury. The first removes functional VWF while preserving normal counts and platelet responses to direct soluble agonists. The second preserves initial VWF-dependent attachment but prevents fibrinogen binding to αIIbβ3. The third preserves attachment and integrin activation during intense stimulation but reduces extracellular nucleotide release. The first is predicted to compromise capture particularly under suitable high-shear conditions. The second compromises platelet cohesion even when early activation occurs. The third compromises the amplification of relatively weak signals and may alter the stability of the expanding structure. The precise effects depend on injury and assay conditions; the value of the comparison is that the three lesions affect different causal links.

A fourth disturbance could leave platelet intrinsic functions normal but reduce endothelial production of inhibitory mediators. A conventional aggregation test performed without endothelium might not reveal the primary lesion. To investigate this explanation, one must study the vessel interface or reconstruct the relevant inhibitory influence. This highlights the danger of assuming that every altered haemostatic phenotype resides inside the platelet. Haemostasis is a system property involving circulating formed elements, soluble factors, tissue surfaces and mechanical conditions.

The most useful final prediction concerns what should happen beyond the lesion. Healthy surrounding endothelium should continue to restrain unnecessary activation, and flowing blood should remove many soluble mediators that are not retained at the wound. If the plug grows until it completely blocks an artery that could have remained open, the response has not achieved an optimal physiological balance even if bleeding has stopped. Conversely, preserving perfect patency at the price of persistent haemorrhage is also not success. A student's explanation should therefore identify both the mechanism that generates a local adhesive structure and the mechanisms that prevent the response from expanding indefinitely.

This integrated sequence also connects the present chapter to the rest of animal physiology. The quantity of circulating platelets depends on haematopoietic regulation and blood volume; their delivery to a wound depends on circulation and the mechanical properties of blood; their activation depends on receptors, signalling intermediates, secretion and energy metabolism. The consequences of failure can be recognised by comparing different experimental readouts. A coherent account therefore connects cellular organisation, developmental regulation and whole-organ function without confusing any one measurement with the entire haemostatic process.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Platelets are inert because they lack nuclei. They retain organelles, receptors and metabolic pathways. Nuclear transcription and biological activity are different properties.
  2. Thrombopoietin is secreted exclusively by megakaryocytes. The liver is the major source; megakaryocytes and platelets bear MPL.
  3. Megakaryocyte polyploidy creates many separate nuclei in separate daughter cells. Endomitosis increases DNA content without ordinary completed cell division.
  4. Thrombocytopenia proves reduced production. Destruction, consumption, sequestration and sampling artefacts can lower a count.
  5. Adhesion and aggregation are identical. Adhesion is surface binding; aggregation is platelet–platelet cohesion.
  6. VWF is itself the GPIb receptor. VWF is an adhesive ligand; GPIb-IX-V is a platelet receptor complex.
  7. All collagen responses use GPIb alone. GPVI and α2β1 have distinct collagen-dependent functions.
  8. Thromboxane A2 is stored in dense granules. It is synthesised upon activation; dense granules store ADP and other small substances.
  9. P2Y1 and P2Y12 have the same G protein. P2Y1 primarily couples to Gq, P2Y12 to Gi.
  10. αIIbβ3 is absent from resting platelets. It is present but generally has low affinity for soluble fibrinogen until activated.
  11. Fibrinogen and fibrin are interchangeable. Fibrinogen bridges activated platelets; fibrin is produced by coagulation.
  12. Ristocetin is a natural wound agonist. It is a laboratory reagent used to probe VWF–GPIb interactions.
  13. Normal platelet count excludes platelet disease. Qualitative receptor and secretion defects can occur with a normal count.
  14. Primary haemostasis finishes before any thrombin appears. The platelet and coagulation responses overlap in space and time.
  15. Clot retraction is fibrinolysis. Retraction is mechanical compaction; fibrinolysis is enzymatic degradation.

  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Count alone does not establish function; qualitative abnormalities are distinct from platelet abundance.

51. State SET — Which statement about VWF is correct?

  1. Only a platelet cytoskeletal component
  2. Adhesive glycoprotein that also carries factor VIII
  3. A fibrinolytic enzyme
  4. An ATP-gated ion channel
Answer and explanation

B. VWF serves adhesion and factor VIII stabilisation; it is neither an enzyme of fibrinolysis nor an ion channel.

52. State SET — Which comparison is correct?

  1. Haemostasis is local bleeding control; thrombosis is pathological intravascular thrombus formation
  2. Haemostasis and homeostasis are exact synonyms
  3. Platelet aggregation means erythrocyte oxygen loading
  4. Fibrinolysis is megakaryocyte maturation
Answer and explanation

A. The first distinction describes the physiological context; the remaining options confuse different biological processes.

53. State SET — Which statements are correct? I: Dense granules contain ADP. II: Thromboxane is synthesised during activation. III: P-selectin surface expression can report alpha-granule fusion.

  1. I only
  2. II only
  3. I and III only
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

D. All three correctly distinguish stored nucleotide, newly formed lipid mediator and a membrane-associated secretion marker.

54. State SET — Which is a correct species comparison?

  1. All vertebrate thrombocytes are anucleate
  2. Mammalian platelets are anucleate; many non-mammalian vertebrates have nucleated thrombocytes
  3. All vertebrates lack formed elements
  4. Only mammals can undergo haemostasis
Answer and explanation

B. Mammalian platelets are cytoplasmic fragments; many other vertebrates use nucleated thrombocytes with related functions.
