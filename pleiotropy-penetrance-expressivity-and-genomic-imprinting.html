From genotype to phenotype

A genetic cross tells us which alleles offspring may inherit. It does not, by itself, establish every observable property of those offspring. This distinction is central to interpreting the extensions of Mendelian genetics. Imagine two plants carrying the same allele that reduces the function of an enzyme involved in pigment synthesis. One grows in bright light and the other in shade. Their pigment concentrations may differ despite identical genotypes at the enzyme locus. Conversely, a plant carrying a normal allele might become pale for nutritional reasons. The observable colour is therefore evidence about the underlying biological system, not an infallible label for a particular genotype.

A locus is a position in the genome; an allele is a sequence variant at that locus. A genotype specifies the alleles under consideration, whereas a phenotype specifies a measurable characteristic of the organism. A phenotype can be molecular, such as enzyme activity; cellular, such as the shape of a red blood cell; or organismal, such as a developmental feature. A meaningful genetic statement must specify the level at which the phenotype was measured. A variant that changes enzyme activity in every carrier may produce a visible abnormality in only a fraction of those carriers.

Between the DNA sequence and the organismal phenotype lies a chain of events: transcription, RNA processing, translation, protein function, cellular metabolism or signalling, tissue development and physiological regulation. Environmental conditions and alleles at other loci can alter this chain. Because the links can be nonlinear, reducing enzyme function by half need not reduce an organismal trait by half. A tissue may contain excess enzyme capacity, so that a moderate reduction has little effect until the activity crosses a threshold.

Mendelian segregation remains a model of allele transmission even when the final phenotype is uncertain. For an autosomal heterozygote A/a crossed with a/a, half of the zygotes are expected to carry A under unbiased segregation and equal survival. A second question is whether those A-bearing offspring show the defined characteristic. A third question is how severely or in what form the characteristic appears. Keeping these questions separate prevents a common mistake: replacing a genotype probability with a phenotype probability without checking the assumptions about expression.

The present chapter deals with several different relationships. Pleiotropy concerns how many traits a gene influences. Penetrance concerns the frequency with which a defined phenotype occurs among individuals with a specified genotype. Expressivity concerns the nature or degree of manifestations. Phenocopy concerns a similar appearance produced by a different, environmental route. Genomic imprinting adds another dimension: the functional contribution of an allele may depend on which parent transmitted it. None of these concepts should be treated as interchangeable explanations for an unusual ratio.

Pleiotropy: one gene, multiple consequences

A gene is pleiotropic when variation at that gene influences multiple distinct phenotypic characteristics. This is biologically unsurprising: a gene product may be required in several tissues, may participate in several reactions or may initiate a change that propagates through a physiological network. Pleiotropy does not require a protein to perform an entirely different molecular reaction for each affected organ. The same molecular activity can be necessary in several contexts.

Consider a structural component of extracellular connective tissue. Fibrillin-1 contributes to extracellular microfibrils; disturbances in its function can compromise tissues that differ greatly in anatomy. The mechanical demands of the aortic wall differ from those of lens-supporting structures and the skeleton, yet all can depend on a common component of the extracellular matrix. FBN1-related Marfan syndrome illustrates how an alteration at one locus can influence skeletal, ocular and cardiovascular traits. The combination and degree of manifestations are not identical in every affected person. Pleiotropy explains why several systems can be involved; variable expressivity explains why the manifestations differ among individuals.

A second route to pleiotropy is disruption of metabolism. Phenylalanine hydroxylase normally converts phenylalanine to tyrosine with the help of tetrahydrobiopterin. Severe loss of enzyme function can increase phenylalanine concentrations and disturb normal brain development if the metabolic abnormality remains unmanaged. Changes in pigmentation may also occur through altered substrate availability and effects on melanogenesis. These consequences are not independent mutations in genes for cognition and pigmentation. They illustrate how one metabolic perturbation reaches several biological endpoints.

A gene may also influence several features through developmental timing. A regulator that controls the proliferation of a precursor population can affect all the structures derived from that population. An alteration early in development may change the number of cells available, the signals those cells produce, and the physical organisation of the mature tissue. To reason causally, identify the first changed biological function and trace each downstream endpoint. Merely listing symptoms does not establish the mechanism.

Pleiotropy should not be confused with polygenic inheritance. Polygenic inheritance describes a trait influenced by multiple loci, for example a quantitative measurement of body size. These are different directions in a network: one locus can point to many traits, and one trait can receive contributions from many loci. Both patterns can coexist. A locus involved in body size may also influence reproductive timing, while both traits respond to other loci.

A statistical association with two traits is not automatically proof of pleiotropy. Two causal variants in neighbouring genes may tend to be inherited together. Their linkage can make a marker correlate with both traits even if each causal variant influences a separate phenotype. Recombinant individuals can help separate the effects. A controlled comparison of organisms differing at a candidate locus, and restoration of the relevant function, can strengthen the inference. The aim is to discriminate a genuinely shared genetic cause from correlated inheritance or correlated environmental exposures.

A further distinction is between independent downstream effects and a mediated chain. If a variant causes tissue dysfunction which then changes activity level and thus alters muscle mass, its influence on muscle mass is mediated by behaviour. If the same variant directly alters a structural protein in both tissues, there are two more immediate routes. Both situations can produce multiple associated traits, but the causal network is different. The distinction matters when designing experiments: correcting the intermediate dysfunction may remove a mediated consequence without correcting other direct effects.

Penetrance: measuring whether a trait appears

Penetrance is conditional probability. Define G as a particular genotype and T as a phenotype assessed by a stated rule in a defined population at a specified time. Penetrance is P(T given G). For a sample of known genotype carriers, an estimate is the number satisfying the phenotype criterion divided by the number of carriers evaluated. It is not the fraction of affected people who carry the allele, which reverses the conditioning. It is also not the fraction of all individuals who exhibit the phenotype.

Suppose 80 of 100 genotyped carriers display a feature at age 40. The estimated penetrance for that definition and observation age is 80 percent. The remaining 20 individuals still carry the genotype. If one of them is a heterozygote, the absence of the visible feature does not prevent segregation of the allele into gametes. A pedigree may therefore appear to skip a generation even though an autosomal dominant susceptibility allele has been transmitted continuously.

Complete penetrance means that all individuals in the defined genotype category exhibit the specified phenotype under the conditions of the model. Incomplete penetrance means that some do not. A finite sample with all 20 carriers affected is compatible with very high penetrance but cannot establish that every carrier in the wider population will necessarily be affected. The sampling frame also matters. Recruiting families through clinics may overrepresent people with severe manifestations, whereas screening a population by genotype can reveal carriers who never sought medical attention.

Age-dependent penetrance is best understood by introducing time explicitly. If T means onset by age t, the probability P(T by t given G) may increase as carriers become older. An unaffected child and an unaffected older adult provide different evidence about an allele that typically manifests later in life. A young carrier observed only until age ten cannot simply be counted as permanently unaffected. Longitudinal follow-up, with careful treatment of individuals not yet observed through the relevant risk period, is needed to distinguish delayed onset from lifelong absence.

For a worked hypothetical cross, let a heterozygous parent transmit A with probability 0.5, and let penetrance among A/a offspring be 0.8. Assume noncarriers never exhibit the trait and offspring survival is independent of genotype. The probability of an affected child equals the probability of inheriting A multiplied by the conditional probability of manifestation: 0.5 times 0.8 equals 0.4. Forty percent is the predicted phenotype frequency, not the transmission frequency. The remaining 60 percent include 50 percent noncarriers and 10 percent nonpenetrant carriers.

The same model can be inverted. What is the probability that an unaffected offspring carries A? The joint probability of carrying A and being unaffected is 0.5 times 0.2, or 0.1. The probability of being unaffected is 0.1 plus 0.5, or 0.6. Dividing gives 1/6. This conditional calculation shows why a normal phenotype reduces but does not eliminate the probability of carrier status when penetrance is incomplete. The result depends on the stated parental cross, age and absence of phenocopies; it must not be generalised to every pedigree.

If a noncarrier has an independent probability q of showing the scored phenotype, the total probability of an affected offspring becomes 0.5p plus 0.5q, where p denotes penetrance among heterozygotes. For p equal to 0.8 and q equal to 0.04, the predicted phenotype frequency is 0.42. Two routes contribute to the visible outcome. Consequently, even an apparently precise phenotype ratio can be explained by several different underlying transmission and manifestation models unless genotype information is also collected.

Variable expressivity and biological thresholds

Penetrance asks whether an observation crosses a defined boundary. Expressivity asks how the characteristic is manifested, such as its intensity, distribution or combination of features. A genotype might be fully penetrant for the presence of an extra digit while showing variable expressivity in the number, size or anatomical location of extra digits. A single genotype can therefore have complete penetrance for one definition of a trait and variable expression of its details.

An illustrative dataset contains 100 carriers: 20 with no scored manifestations, 40 with mild manifestations, 30 with moderate manifestations and 10 with severe manifestations. The penetrance of any scored manifestation is 80 percent. Among the 80 manifesting carriers, 10/80 or 12.5 percent show severe manifestations. If the phenotype criterion is changed to severe manifestations only, the penetrance for that stricter criterion is 10 percent. No biological data have changed; the definition of the outcome has changed.

A threshold model connects these observations. Let a quantitative liability represent the combined consequences of gene function, modifier alleles, environmental conditions and developmental variation. Individuals with liability above a specified threshold are classified as expressing a trait. Those below it are not. The distribution of liability above the threshold can still be broad. This is a conceptual model, not a claim that every disorder can be reduced to a single measurable number. Some disorders have qualitatively different combinations of features that cannot be ranked meaningfully on one severity axis.

A modifier locus can alter the phenotype produced by another locus. For instance, a second allele may increase the capacity of an alternative pathway and reduce the consequences of a deficient enzyme. When comparing two carriers of a primary variant, the relevant question is not merely whether the primary locus is identical but whether their wider genetic backgrounds and environments are comparable. A modifier can alter severity or the probability of exceeding a manifestation threshold, so expressivity and penetrance can both change.

Developmental variability can persist even in experimentally controlled populations. Random differences in the expression of regulatory genes or in the timing of developmental events may be amplified by feedback. Therefore variation among individuals with matching genotype and environment is not necessarily evidence of an overlooked Mendelian locus. A careful experiment uses enough biological replicates, standardises scoring and measures potential covariates rather than assuming that every unexplained difference is measurement error.

Phenocopies and environmental modification

A phenocopy is an environmentally produced phenotype resembling one associated with a genetic variant. The resemblance does not imply that the organism has acquired the corresponding inherited variant. Environmental disruption of a developmental pathway may reproduce the anatomical outcome of a mutation in a component of that pathway. The similarity is informative about shared biological processes, but morphology alone cannot identify the cause.

In a hypothetical insect experiment, a mutation reduces the activity of a developmental regulator and causes an altered appendage. Wild-type individuals exposed to an environmental stress during a sensitive developmental interval display a similar appendage. To distinguish the two routes, compare genotypes, use appropriately matched untreated controls, and examine descendants reared without the stress. If the wild-type environmentally exposed group does not transmit the phenotype according to the mutant segregation pattern, this supports an environmental explanation. The developmental defect can nevertheless be permanent within the exposed organism.

An environmental influence on carriers of a variant is not necessarily a phenocopy. If the variant creates susceptibility and environmental exposure determines whether the characteristic appears, the appropriate model may be gene–environment interaction. Consider two genotypes grown at two temperatures. If the trait changes by the same amount in both, temperature has a main effect under an additive model. If the genotype difference increases or reverses across temperatures, the response to environment differs by genotype on that measurement scale.

Suppose a normal genotype produces an average trait value of 10 at the lower temperature and 14 at the higher temperature. A variant genotype produces values of 9 and 5 respectively. The effect of temperature is plus four units in the normal genotype but minus four units in the variant. The genotype difference is minus one unit at the lower temperature and minus nine at the higher temperature. This hypothetical factorial example illustrates a nonparallel reaction norm. Replicate measurements and uncertainty estimates would be needed to assess statistical evidence in a real experiment.

Environmental effects can alter phenotype without changing inherited allele sequence. However, it would be too strong to say that environmental exposures can never cause mutations; some exposures are mutagenic. The term phenocopy is reserved for the environmental production of a phenotype resembling a genetic one, rather than for an exposure that directly produces the same causal inherited sequence variant. The classification depends on mechanism and evidence.

Genomic imprinting: why parental origin matters

Ordinary autosomal inheritance begins with one homologous chromosome from each parent. In many cases both alleles of a gene are available for expression, although their sequence and regulatory activity can differ. At an imprinted locus, the maternal and paternal copies have systematically different expression states because of parent-of-origin-dependent regulation. The important distinction is the origin of the allele in the present individual, not whether that individual is male or female.

In classical mouse experiments, McGrath and Solter compared embryos reconstructed with maternal and paternal nuclear contributions to embryos containing two contributions from the same parental sex. The same-sex diploid nuclear combinations did not support complete development. The normal parental combination was essential under those experimental conditions. This result challenged the assumption that two sets of mammalian chromosomes were functionally interchangeable merely because the embryos were diploid. It did not identify every imprinted gene, but it established the developmental significance of parental origin.

A germline imprint is established during the development of egg or sperm cells. A major mechanism involves DNA methylation at particular regulatory regions. A methylated cytosine has a chemical modification without a change in its base identity. Methylation can affect binding of regulatory proteins and local chromatin organisation. Not all methylation silences transcription: the consequence depends on which regulatory element is modified and how that element normally functions.

After fertilisation, many epigenetic marks are remodelled in the developing mammalian embryo, yet protected imprinting control regions can retain their parental differences. Maintenance mechanisms copy methylation patterns through somatic divisions. Consequently, descendants of a cell can preserve a regulatory distinction between the two homologous chromosomes. Tissue-specific transcriptional factors still matter, so a gene with parent-specific expression in one tissue may have a different expression pattern elsewhere.

During the development of the next generation's germ cells, old parental imprints are erased and new sex-appropriate germline imprints are established. A chromosome that entered a woman through her father can be transmitted through her egg with the maternal imprint appropriate to that locus. The DNA sequence can remain unchanged while the parent-specific regulatory state changes. Without this reset, the expression pattern would reflect the sex of a remote ancestor rather than the parent from whom the allele was most recently inherited.

The mouse Igf2–H19 region illustrates the relationship between methylation and enhancer access. In the canonical model, the maternal imprinting control region is unmethylated and binds CTCF, which restricts communication between shared enhancers and the Igf2 promoter. The paternal control region is methylated, preventing the corresponding CTCF-dependent insulator configuration. The enhancers can activate paternal Igf2 in the relevant developmental context. H19 shows the complementary parent-specific pattern in the classical model. The methylated region is regulatory; the methylation does not simply mean that every nearby gene is inactive.

This model provides an experimental prediction. If the maternal insulator loses its capacity to bind CTCF, maternal Igf2 expression may become possible, depending on the exact perturbation and cell type. If a paternal allele acquires an inappropriate maternal-like regulatory state, normal paternal Igf2 expression can be compromised. Such predictions should be tested by measuring transcripts separately from each parental homolog, not by total RNA abundance alone. A change in total transcript might reflect a change in cellular composition or transcription from an already active allele rather than loss of imprinting.

Reciprocal crosses offer a powerful way to examine parental origin. Suppose a variant v is present at an autosomal gene expressed exclusively from the paternal allele in a particular tissue. A v/+ father crossed to a +/+ mother transmits v to half the offspring, and those offspring have v on the active paternal allele. A v/+ mother crossed to a +/+ father also transmits v to half the offspring, but the inherited v is on the silent maternal allele in that tissue. If v is a fully penetrant loss-of-function variant when active and one active normal copy suffices, the first cross produces affected offspring with probability one half and the second with probability zero.

These outcomes must not be confused with sex linkage. Both sons and daughters can inherit the autosomal variant from either parent; the asymmetry arises from the expression state of the allele. Nor does the outcome establish maternal effect inheritance, in which the mother's genotype affects offspring through products or conditions she supplies. In an imprinting model, the child's own inherited allele and its immediate parental origin determine the relevant expression state.

Chromosome 15: complementary imprinting disorders

The human 15q11.2–q13 region contains genes whose expression depends on parental origin. Loss of the functional paternal contribution of genes in the Prader–Willi critical region causes Prader–Willi syndrome. Loss of maternal UBE3A function in relevant neuronal populations is the basis of Angelman syndrome. These disorders demonstrate that deletion of similar chromosomal intervals can have different consequences depending on the parental homolog affected.

A deletion is not the only route to a loss of functional expression. Uniparental disomy describes a situation in which both copies of a chromosome or relevant chromosomal segment originate from one parent. If both copies of chromosome 15 are maternal, the dosage of the normally paternally expressed genes can be deficient even when two DNA copies are present. Conversely, two paternal copies lack the ordinary maternal neuronal UBE3A contribution. An imprinting defect can also produce inappropriate parent-specific expression without a corresponding deletion or uniparental disomy.

The educational significance of these examples is the separation between DNA copy number and functional dosage. A copy-number assay can detect many deletions, but a normal copy number does not exclude uniparental disomy or an imprinting defect. Parent-informative genetic markers can help establish chromosomal origin. Methylation-sensitive assays can assess parent-specific regulatory patterns at selected regions. Different mechanisms can produce similar methylation findings, so the assay result must be interpreted in relation to its actual target and complementary evidence.

UBE3A expression illustrates tissue specificity. The paternal allele is normally silenced in many neuronal contexts through the action of a long antisense transcript, whereas expression in other tissues may be biallelic. It is therefore misleading to describe the paternal UBE3A sequence as deleted or permanently silent in every cell. The molecular distinction between sequence absence, transcriptional suppression and tissue-dependent expression is a recurring theme throughout the chapter.

A final contrast concerns ordinary epigenetic regulation. A muscle-specific gene can be expressed in muscle and silent in neurons without being imprinted; both homologs may respond similarly to tissue-specific regulators. Random monoallelic expression can differ between cells without tracking parental origin. To establish genomic imprinting, the essential observation is reproducible parental-origin-dependent expression with appropriate controls for allele sequence, tissue and developmental context.

Testing the causal basis of pleiotropy

Geneticists can distinguish a description of a phenotype from an explanation of its cause by choosing experiments that change the suspected causal factor while preserving other conditions. Consider a breeding population in which both a short stem and an unusual leaf shape are associated with the same chromosome segment. At this stage the observation establishes cosegregation, not that one gene controls two traits. The same segment may contain two tightly linked genes. Testing this alternative requires a comparison in which the two effects can be uncoupled, or in which only the function of one proposed gene changes.

A recombinant chromosome is particularly informative because crossing over between two relevant loci can separate alleles that were previously inherited together. Imagine an original haplotype carrying a recessive short-stem allele s and an independent recessive altered-leaf allele l, whereas the other parental chromosome carries the corresponding normal alleles S and L. If the genes are close enough that most gametes carry the parental combinations, short stems and altered leaves will frequently appear together in an appropriately designed cross. Nevertheless, rare recombinant gametes can carry s L or S l. When tested against a double-recessive parent, a recombinant combination could produce only one abnormal trait. Such a result is inconsistent with the simplest model in which one indivisible causal variant is necessary and sufficient for both characteristics.

Failure to recover separated phenotypes is less conclusive. If only a small number of offspring have been examined, tight linkage is difficult to distinguish from pleiotropy. For instance, if a hypothetical recombination fraction between two loci is 0.01, the probability that 50 independent informative gametes contain no recombinant is 0.99 raised to the power 50, approximately 0.605. Even with 50 observations, the absence of recombinants is quite plausible. Conversely, the chance of observing at least one recombinant rises to approximately 0.951 with 300 gametes. These calculations assume that all recombinant gametes are detected and that viability does not differ. The logic prepares us for linkage analysis in the next chapter without attempting a detailed map here.

An additional route is to compare genotypes in a nearly identical background. If a validated change in a candidate gene produces two measurable features in organisms with otherwise matched backgrounds, both changes become candidates for pleiotropic effects. A functional restoration experiment can further strengthen causal inference: reintroducing the normal function should restore the relevant phenotypes under a model where the missing function causes both. The experiment needs an appropriate comparison in which the restoration procedure itself is performed without restoring the gene, because otherwise a procedural or background effect could be mistaken for biological rescue.

Tissue-specific restoration can help distinguish branches in the causal network. Suppose an enzyme acts in the liver and another tissue develops an abnormal phenotype because a metabolite accumulates in circulation. Restoration of the enzyme in liver could normalise the metabolite and consequently improve both tissues, even without restoration in the second tissue. By contrast, if the gene product acts independently within the two tissues, liver-specific restoration might correct only one feature. These alternative outcomes illustrate direct and indirect pleiotropic effects. The inference is not that each endpoint must correspond to a different biochemical function, but that the causal connections can be analysed.

A useful evidence hierarchy is association, inheritance, perturbation and restoration. Association identifies a candidate connection; segregation tests whether the connection follows inheritance; a targeted perturbation tests whether altering the candidate changes the trait; restoration tests whether recovering function reverses the result. Each method has limitations. For example, a restoration construct may be expressed at an inappropriate level or developmental stage. A failed restoration therefore does not automatically refute the gene's involvement. The interpretation should always name the biological assumption necessary for the inference.

For examination purposes, consider the difference between multiple molecular products and multiple phenotypic consequences. Alternative splicing of a gene may generate isoforms used in different tissues, offering one mechanism by which a variant could have several effects. But splicing is not required for pleiotropy, and alternative splicing alone does not establish pleiotropy unless distinct outcomes are affected. Similarly, several symptoms downstream of a single physiological change need not represent multiple independent gene functions. A correct answer explains the direction of causation rather than simply counting products or observations.

Reading pedigrees with incomplete penetrance

A pedigree is a record of relationships and phenotypes across generations. When a genotype has complete penetrance and a clear phenotype, familiar Mendelian patterns can provide strong clues about mode of inheritance. With incomplete penetrance, the interpretation becomes conditional. An unaffected individual may be a noncarrier, a carrier who will develop the phenotype later, or a carrier who never develops it according to the selected definition. A phenotype symbol alone does not distinguish these possibilities.

Consider a hypothetical three-generation family. An affected heterozygous grandfather transmits a variant to his daughter, who remains unaffected at the age of assessment. She in turn transmits it to an affected son. Without genotyping, the appearance of unaffected parents producing affected offspring might suggest recessive inheritance. With genotyping, however, continuous transmission of a dominant susceptibility allele is directly demonstrated. The apparently skipped generation is a skipped manifestation, not a skipped genetic transmission.

This distinction can be tested by assigning probabilities to a family. Assume a particular autosomal variant is transmitted with probability one half from a known heterozygous parent, the other parent has two normal copies, and no noncarrier shows the phenotype. Among carriers, penetrance by the relevant age is 0.6. An offspring is unaffected with probability 0.7: the combined probability of being a noncarrier (0.5) or a carrier without manifestation (0.5 times 0.4). The conditional carrier probability among unaffected offspring is 0.2 divided by 0.7, or 2/7. If the unaffected offspring subsequently has a child with a known noncarrier, and no other information changes the estimate, the probability of that child carrying the variant is 2/7 times 1/2, or 1/7. With the same age-specific penetrance of 0.6 in the child, the probability of the defined manifestation by that age is 3/35, about 8.57 percent.

The calculation changes if the parent has been molecularly confirmed as a carrier. Once the genotype is known, it is no longer appropriate to multiply transmission by 2/7. The carrier probability is then one, and the variant transmission probability is one half, irrespective of the parent's phenotype. This simple contrast explains why pedigree questions often state explicitly that a person carries a variant or that the person is merely an unaffected relative.

Multiple siblings provide additional information about an uncertain parental genotype, but the observations are not automatically independent after averaging over uncertainty. Suppose a parent has probability one half of carrying A, and if the parent is a carrier each child's affected probability is 0.4. If the parent is not a carrier, no child will be affected. The marginal probability for the first child is 0.2. The probability that two children are both affected is 0.5 times 0.4 times 0.4, or 0.08, not 0.2 squared, because the same uncertain genotype influences both outcomes. Conditional on a known parental genotype, the transmissions may be independent; after marginalising over that shared uncertainty, the outcomes are associated.

Now suppose that one child is unaffected. Under the same model, the probability of observing this result is 0.5 times 0.6 plus 0.5 times 1, which equals 0.8. Bayes' rule updates the parent's carrier probability to 0.3/0.8, or 0.375. The probability that a subsequent child will be affected is then 0.375 times 0.4, or 0.15. This calculation is not an invitation to infer a real family's clinical risks without genetic evidence; it is a demonstration of conditional probability under explicitly specified assumptions.

Pedigree analysis is also influenced by ascertainment. If a family is recruited because one individual has a severe manifestation, counting that individual together with all relatives can bias a naive estimate of penetrance. The recruitment strategy selects families with at least one observable case and can make the sampled families differ from the population of all variant carriers. Similar difficulties occur when records preferentially include older relatives or when mild features were never assessed systematically. A convincing estimate describes the genotype ascertainment strategy, the phenotype definition, the age structure and whether the individuals were related.

A carrier without symptoms is not necessarily biologically unaffected at every level. A sensitive biochemical assay may detect a change while a clinical examination does not. In an experimental organism, a fluorescent reporter may reveal altered pathway activity even though the visible anatomical trait is normal. It is therefore important to say which outcome is being scored. A question asking about the penetrance of a molecular phenotype is not equivalent to one asking about a visible developmental phenotype, even when the same genotype and individuals are being evaluated.

Quantitative interpretation of penetrance studies

An observed fraction in a group of carriers is an estimate, not an immutable property of a sequence variant. Two studies with different sample sizes can report the same proportion while providing different levels of statistical precision. For example, eight manifesting carriers out of ten and eighty manifesting carriers out of one hundred both give an estimate of 0.8. The smaller study is more sensitive to an additional observation, and its uncertainty is generally larger.

Under a simple binomial model in which each independently sampled carrier has an identical probability p of showing the defined trait, the number X of manifesting carriers among n individuals has mean np and variance np(1 − p). The estimated fraction X/n has variance p(1 − p)/n. Dividing by n in the variance expression arises because the count is scaled by n, so its variance is divided by n squared. The standard deviation of the estimated fraction is consequently the square root of p(1 − p)/n. Substituting the observed proportion for the unknown p gives a simple approximate standard error.

For the 100-person illustrative study with 80 manifesting carriers, the estimated standard error is the square root of 0.8 times 0.2 divided by 100, which is 0.04. A rough normal-approximation interval constructed as the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors would run from approximately 0.722 to 0.878. This is only an approximation; the normal interval performs poorly with small samples or probabilities near zero or one. The central teaching point is not to memorise a particular confidence interval procedure but to recognise that the reliability of a proportion depends on its denominator and sampling assumptions.

The independent binomial model may be inadequate when carriers belong to a few families. Their genetic backgrounds and environmental conditions may be correlated. If many relatives share a protective modifier allele, observations from those relatives need not represent independent samples from the wider population. Similarly, comparing carriers with heterogeneous ages using a single binomial probability ignores changes in cumulative penetrance over time. It is possible to calculate a fraction accurately from the available data while drawing an inaccurate conclusion about what that fraction means.

Age-stratified data help make the problem visible. In a hypothetical cohort, 15 of 50 carriers aged 15–29 manifest a trait, 30 of 50 aged 30–49 manifest it, and 40 of 50 aged 50–69 manifest it. The pooled proportion is 85/150, approximately 56.7 percent. Yet the age-stratified proportions are 30, 60 and 80 percent. The pooled result is useful only as a description of this mixture of ages. It should not be interpreted as the probability of manifestation by age 69. Cross-sectional strata contain different individuals, and differences across strata may also reflect birth-cohort or sampling effects.

A longitudinal design observes individuals through time. If a carrier has not manifested the trait at the last assessment, the event time is not known; it is known only to exceed that observation time. This is called right censoring. An elementary understanding of censoring helps explain why simply classifying every person lost to follow-up as permanently unaffected can underestimate cumulative penetrance. Detailed survival modelling is beyond the present scope, but an exam question may ask why age-specific estimates are preferable to a single pooled estimate.

A further distinction is between penetrance and predictive value. Suppose a population contains 1,000 people, 100 of whom carry a variant. Sixty of the carriers manifest the phenotype, while 18 of the 900 noncarriers manifest an indistinguishable phenotype. The variant penetrance is 60/100 or 60 percent. The fraction of manifesting individuals who carry the variant is instead 60/78, approximately 76.9 percent. The second fraction depends on the carrier frequency and the occurrence of the phenotype among noncarriers. Two populations can have the same penetrance but very different predictive values if their carrier frequencies differ.

This example also clarifies why an observed phenotypic ratio cannot identify the genotype ratio without additional assumptions. In a simple monohybrid F2, the genotype proportions AA, Aa and aa are one quarter, one half and one quarter. If penetrances for a defined trait are 0.9, 0.6 and 0.1 respectively, the phenotype probability is (1/4 times 0.9) plus (1/2 times 0.6) plus (1/4 times 0.1), which equals 0.55. The expected phenotypic ratio is therefore 55:45, or 11:9, despite normal Mendelian segregation. This is an illustration of the law of total probability, not a new law of segregation.

The model should be read from left to right: first establish genotype frequencies, then assign the relevant expression probability to each genotype, then sum the weighted contributions. If the question gives offspring genotype counts rather than expected proportions, an analogous calculation can be used to predict expected phenotypic counts conditional on those observed genotype counts. When examining real data, keep the uncertainty of genotype frequencies separate from the uncertainty in the penetrance estimates.

Separating genotype, environment and their interaction

Experimental genetics has a particular advantage over observations of naturally varying populations: environmental conditions can sometimes be assigned systematically. Suppose a researcher measures a pigment trait in two strains of a plant. The strains differ at a candidate locus, but the researcher initially grows one strain in a greenhouse and the other outdoors. A difference in colour is observed. This design cannot separate genetic effects from environmental effects because every genotype is paired with only one environment.

A more informative design includes both genotypes in both environments. Random assignment of individual plants to growth positions reduces the chance that a local difference in light or moisture becomes systematically associated with genotype. Replicate individuals are needed within each combination to estimate biological variability. The researcher must use a measurement protocol that scores all groups consistently, preferably without knowledge of their genotype if the measurement involves subjective judgement. Such features of the design are as important as the choice of the candidate locus.

A reaction norm is a description of how the phenotype of a genotype varies across environments. On a graph, the environmental condition is placed on the horizontal axis and the measured phenotype on the vertical axis. Different genotypes have separate lines or curves. Parallel lines in a simple two-environment example suggest no interaction on the chosen additive scale; nonparallel lines show that the environmental difference is not identical for both genotypes. Crossing lines indicate that the relative ranking of genotypes reverses between the environments.

For an illustrative numerical example, genotype A has means of 12 and 20 units in environments E1 and E2. Genotype B has means of 8 and 16. The environmental effect is eight units in each genotype; genotype A exceeds B by four units in both environments. In a second experiment, the four means are 12, 20, 8 and 10. Now the environmental change is eight units for A but only two for B. The difference between environmental effects is six units. The same interaction contrast can be obtained by subtracting the genotype difference in E1 from that in E2: the difference grows from four to ten units.

The choice of scale matters. A treatment that doubles pigment concentration in both genotypes can create different absolute changes when the initial concentrations differ. On a logarithmic scale, doubling represents the same additive change for both. Thus the phrase gene–environment interaction should be tied to the response variable and model used. In a CSIR-NET analytical problem, inspect whether the supplied data are absolute quantities, proportions or fold changes before deciding which comparison is intended.

The observation of an interaction does not identify a molecular mechanism. A temperature-dependent protein-folding defect could make a variant more sensitive to high temperature, whereas another variant might alter the regulation of a protective stress response. Both could produce nonparallel reaction norms. Measuring protein abundance, activity and downstream outcomes under matched conditions can help distinguish the models. The question to ask is whether the environment changes the amount of protein, the activity of the existing protein, the demand on the pathway or another factor that affects the measured trait.

Phenocopies can also be understood through this framework. A genetically normal individual subjected to an environmental perturbation may cross a developmental threshold normally crossed by a mutant. In such a case genotype A under environment E2 may have the same visible phenotype as genotype B under E1. Identical outcomes do not make the two genotypes identical. When offspring are reared under standardised conditions, the mutant allele can continue to segregate, whereas the environmentally induced phenotype may not recur unless the relevant exposure or another causal condition is present.

An important nuance is that environmental effects may persist across time or even influence descendants in some contexts. Persistence alone is not proof of a DNA sequence change, and absence of a phenotype in one generation is not proof that a variant has been lost. Experimental comparisons need to separate the inheritance of a DNA sequence, the transmission of an epigenetic state and the recurrence of an environmental exposure. This conceptual discipline prepares the reader for genomic imprinting, where an inherited allele can carry a characteristic regulatory state but that state is normally reset during germline development.

Experimental reasoning: identifying parent-of-origin expression

The distinction between inheritance and expression can be investigated with reciprocal crosses in which the parental strains differ at informative sequence markers. Imagine a mouse strain A carrying a harmless distinguishable nucleotide at a transcribed position in a gene, and strain B carrying another nucleotide at the same position. Both strains contain functional copies of the gene. Crossing an A female with a B male produces offspring that carry one A and one B allele. The reciprocal cross produces offspring with the same unordered genotype but reverses which allele is maternally derived.

A DNA measurement confirms that the offspring contain both markers. An RNA measurement can then ask which marker is represented in the expressed transcripts. If the A allele is expressed preferentially in both reciprocal crosses, the observation supports an allele-specific regulatory difference associated with the A sequence or its surrounding genetic background. If the expressed allele changes from A to B when the maternal strain changes, expression follows parental origin rather than strain identity. For a maternally expressed gene, the reciprocal pattern would be A transcripts in offspring of A mothers and B transcripts in offspring of B mothers.

The experiment needs controls at several levels. DNA genotyping establishes that the two alleles are present and excludes a simple deletion as the explanation for missing RNA. A mixture containing known amounts of both allele sequences can test whether the measurement method detects the two markers equally efficiently. Tissue samples should be collected at comparable developmental stages, and several offspring should be analysed from each cross rather than relying on one animal. If the measurements differ across tissues, the tissue identity becomes part of the expression model instead of a nuisance to be ignored.

A measured RNA ratio need not be exactly zero or one to be biologically meaningful. Some imprinted genes show preferential rather than absolute parent-specific expression, and a sample composed of several cell types can contain both monoallelic and biallelic populations. Suppose an illustrative bulk sample contains 60 percent cells expressing only the maternal allele and 40 percent cells expressing both alleles equally. Assume total gene expression per cell is identical in the two populations. The maternal contribution is 0.6 plus 0.4 times 0.5, or 0.8; the paternal contribution is 0.2. The measured ratio is therefore four to one even though no individual cell need express the two alleles in that ratio.

Conversely, a one-to-one ratio in a bulk sample does not always prove that every cell expresses both copies. A mixture of cells with maternal-only and paternal-only expression in equal proportions could yield a similar overall ratio. To distinguish these possibilities, allele-resolved measurements of relevant cell populations may be necessary. The inference concerns the resolution of the data: a measurement of total tissue RNA tells us about average contribution, not necessarily the transcriptional state in each cell.

The classical nuclear-transfer experiments addressed a different question. They tested whether two parental sets of nuclear chromosomes were functionally interchangeable during mammalian development. A reconstructed diploid embryo with two maternal or two paternal nuclear contributions has two chromosome sets but lacks the normal combination of parental-origin states. Comparing such embryos with reconstructed controls containing one contribution from each parent helps separate the effect of parental origin from an effect of manipulation itself. The observation of failed complete development in same-parent-origin combinations motivated the search for individual loci with parent-dependent expression.

The outcome of those experiments does not demonstrate that every maternal gene is needed in the embryo and every paternal gene is needed in the placenta. Different imprinted genes act in different biological contexts, and developmental defects can reflect multiple altered gene dosages simultaneously. It would also be incorrect to infer that mammals are unable to survive any instance of uniparental disomy. The consequences of inheriting two copies from one parent depend on the chromosome or segment involved, the imprinted genes it contains and other genetic factors such as homozygosity for recessive variants.

A more local experiment examines a particular regulatory element. Consider an imprinted locus with a differentially methylated control region. Comparing methylation patterns on parental homologs identifies a candidate mark associated with expression differences. Yet an association is not sufficient to establish that the mark causes the expression difference. It could arise as a consequence of transcription or reflect a broader regulatory state. Alterations that specifically disrupt the relevant control element, combined with allele-resolved expression measurements, help reveal whether the element is required for maintaining the parent-dependent expression pattern.

The interpretation is especially powerful when a regulatory perturbation is inherited through each parent in turn. Suppose deleting a control element causes expression of a normally silent maternal copy only when the deletion is maternally inherited. The result is more informative than a general reduction of expression following any deletion, because it connects the control element to a parental regulatory state. Appropriate controls include the intact homolog, comparable tissue and developmental stage, and confirmation of the precise genotype. A deletion that also removes a neighbouring promoter may alter several mechanisms at once, limiting the ability to assign the effect to a single regulatory interaction.

A student can organise these experiments into four progressively stronger questions. First, are both alleles present in DNA? Second, does their RNA output differ according to parent of origin in reciprocal crosses? Third, is a regulatory mark or chromatin state associated with that difference? Fourth, does perturbing the regulatory element change the predicted expression outcome? The answer to each question narrows the set of alternative explanations. This sequence is useful for interpreting unfamiliar genetic data in Part C questions because it emphasises what each measurement actually establishes.

The molecular distinction between a mark and its consequence

The chemical structure of DNA offers a way to carry regulatory information in addition to nucleotide sequence. In mammals, methylation commonly occurs at cytosines followed by guanine in the same strand, known as CpG sites. Addition of a methyl group to cytosine does not convert the sequence into a different base-pairing instruction in the ordinary genetic sense. The sequence remains recognisable as containing cytosine, while the chemical modification can influence interactions with proteins and the surrounding chromatin.

A differentially methylated region is a stretch of DNA whose methylation patterns differ between biological conditions or alleles. In genomic imprinting, one important class is the germline differentially methylated region, at which parent-specific methylation is established before fertilisation. Not every differentially methylated region in the mature tissue is necessarily a primary germline imprint. Some acquire their differences later as a consequence of the initial regulatory asymmetry. Therefore, determining when a mark appears is relevant to deciding whether it could initiate the parent-specific expression pattern.

During DNA replication, the two daughter DNA molecules contain newly synthesised strands paired with parental strands. If a symmetric CpG was methylated on both parental strands, replication initially generates daughter molecules with methylation on the old strand but not yet on the new strand. Maintenance methylation machinery can restore the methylation on the new strands. This provides a mechanism for preserving a regulatory state through cell division without changing the inherited base sequence.

Establishing a new methylation pattern and maintaining an existing one are distinct processes. In the germline, de novo methyltransferase activity contributes to setting methylation patterns appropriate to the developing gamete. In somatic lineages, maintenance mechanisms preserve many existing patterns through replication. These are functional distinctions rather than an assertion that each enzyme can perform only one task under every condition. For the NET/SET level, the central point is that a mark must be installed, protected or maintained where appropriate, and erased in the germline for normal intergenerational resetting.

Early mammalian development involves extensive reorganisation of DNA methylation and chromatin. If all parent-specific marks were erased indiscriminately immediately after fertilisation, the embryo would lose the regulatory information that distinguishes the parental homologs at imprinted regions. The protection and maintenance of important imprinting control regions during this period is therefore an essential part of the imprinting cycle. This statement does not mean that all methylated sites on the inherited genomes are protected or that imprinting remains uniform in every differentiated cell.

The germline presents the converse requirement. An individual must produce gametes with marks appropriate to the kind of gamete being formed, regardless of whether an individual chromosome originally arrived through a sperm or an egg. The resetting process solves a problem that would otherwise accumulate across generations. For example, a woman carries a chromosome inherited from her father. If that chromosome retained its paternal imprint in all of her eggs, an offspring receiving it from her would carry an inappropriate parental expression state. Normally, erasure and establishment of maternal germline imprints prevent that error.

Methylation has context-dependent effects. When methylation prevents a transcription factor from binding to an activating regulatory element, it can reduce transcription. When it prevents binding of a protein that normally blocks enhancer access, it may permit expression of a nearby gene. A methylated regulatory region can also affect a noncoding transcript that in turn influences another gene. A general statement that methylation is associated with repression is useful in many promoter contexts but does not replace analysis of the relevant element.

The distinction can be formalised with a two-step causal diagram. Suppose a control element is unmethylated and binds an inhibitory protein. Binding prevents activation of gene X. Methylation prevents binding, thereby relieving inhibition of X. The net effect of methylation on X is positive even though the immediate effect on protein binding is negative. Many examination traps arise from collapsing a two-step regulatory relationship into the slogan that methylation always turns genes off. Correct interpretation requires identifying the molecular target of the modification and tracing the sign of every intervening relationship.

Measurements of methylation and transcription answer different questions. Methylation assays report the state of selected cytosines in the DNA sampled; RNA assays report transcripts present under the measurement conditions. If both copies of a gene are present but transcription originates mainly from one, methylation data may help explain that asymmetry. They cannot, by themselves, establish that a particular protein is present at an enhancer or that a particular gene is expressed in a specified cell type. An appropriate combination of data is required to connect the chemical mark with the functional consequence.

The Igf2–H19 locus as a model of enhancer regulation

A gene promoter is a region associated with transcription initiation. An enhancer is a regulatory DNA element that can increase transcription from a promoter, often at some distance along the chromosome. Whether an enhancer can act on a promoter depends on the arrangement of the surrounding regulatory elements, the binding of transcriptional regulators and the organisation of chromatin. An insulator can restrict communication across a boundary between regulatory elements.

At the classical mouse Igf2–H19 locus, shared downstream enhancers participate in the regulation of neighbouring genes. On the maternal homolog, an unmethylated imprinting control region can bind CTCF. The resulting regulatory configuration restricts enhancer communication with Igf2, and the maternal H19 transcript is expressed in the canonical model. On the paternal homolog, methylation at the relevant sites prevents the same CTCF-dependent boundary from operating. The enhancers can contribute to paternal Igf2 expression, while H19 is silenced in the corresponding paternal context.

A useful way to reason through this locus is to avoid placing the labels active and inactive on the entire chromosome. The maternal chromosome does not become globally inactive. Rather, particular genes have distinct expression states depending on the local regulatory arrangement. The paternal chromosome likewise carries functional genes even though H19 expression is repressed in the canonical model. This matters because the same parent-specific methylation difference can have opposite consequences for neighbouring genes.

Consider an illustrative perturbation in which the maternal imprinting control element remains present but its CTCF-binding capacity is specifically lost. If the shared enhancer is active and the other components of the classical regulatory model remain functional, enhancer access to maternal Igf2 may increase. In that simplified scenario the ordinary restriction on maternal Igf2 expression is relaxed. The outcome is different from deleting the Igf2 coding sequence: the coding gene can remain intact in a regulatory perturbation even though the pattern of expression is abnormal.

Now consider a perturbation of the enhancer itself. If both promoters depend on that enhancer in the tissue being studied, removing enhancer activity can reduce expression from either homolog according to the local regulatory state. This differs from removing the boundary: removing a boundary can permit a new regulatory interaction, whereas removing an enhancer eliminates a source of activation. In an examination problem it is therefore important to mark the position of each changed element on a diagram before predicting the output.

A further distinction is between loss of imprinting and loss of expression. Loss of imprinting refers to disturbance of the ordinary parent-specific expression pattern. It may include abnormal expression from a normally silent copy, or other changes in allele-specific regulation. Loss of expression from the normally active copy is a reduction of functional output. These are not necessarily the same event. A perturbation that makes both alleles active can increase expression dosage; one that prevents the active allele from functioning can decrease it.

To make dosage reasoning concrete, assume the normal paternal allele produces 100 arbitrary units of Igf2 RNA and the maternal allele produces none in a simplified tissue. If the maternal allele becomes active and produces an additional 80 units without changing paternal expression, total output is 180 units. This is an 80 percent increase relative to the original total. It is not exactly a doubling because the two allele outputs in this illustrative case are not equal. If, instead, the paternal contribution falls to 30 units and the maternal allele remains silent, output is reduced by 70 percent.

The biological effect of altered growth-factor expression depends on tissue responsiveness, timing and downstream regulatory feedback. It is not valid to infer that an 80 percent increase in RNA automatically produces an 80 percent increase in tissue mass. Transcript abundance, protein abundance, receptor occupancy and downstream responses need not be linearly related. The quantitative example teaches dosage accounting, not a measured growth-response law. This distinction keeps a useful mathematical exercise within defensible biological limits.

In the canonical model, the imprinted locus coordinates two nearby expression outcomes through a shared regulatory architecture. Thus, even when the two genes have different products, one change to the control region may affect several outputs. This creates a connection with the earlier discussion of pleiotropy, although a regulatory element controlling multiple genes should not automatically be described as a single protein-coding gene with multiple molecular functions. The precise genetic unit being perturbed determines the interpretation.

Transmission probabilities across generations of an imprinted allele

Parent-of-origin effects can look unusual in a family because the expression state of a variant may change between generations even though its sequence does not. To follow the logic, denote a variant allele by v and a normal allele by N. Write the maternal allele first and the paternal allele second. The ordered genotypes v/N and N/v are identical in their unordered allele content but differ in parental origin. At a locus expressed only from the paternal copy in a relevant tissue, v/N and N/v can therefore have different functional consequences.

Suppose v is a loss-of-function allele and the paternal copy must function for the defined phenotype to remain normal. Under a simplified fully penetrant model, v/N is unaffected because the active paternal allele is normal, whereas N/v is affected because the active paternal allele is defective. N/N is unaffected, and v/v is affected in the model because neither copy supplies normal function. The important assumption is that the maternal allele does not provide sufficient expression in the tissue being considered. At a tissue where both copies are expressed, the same prediction may not hold.

A heterozygous man carrying v on his maternal homolog can be unaffected under this model. When he produces sperm, however, the relevant imprints are reset to the paternal pattern. Half his gametes are expected to carry v. If his partner is N/N, half of their offspring will inherit v on the paternal homolog and show the defined trait. The man's unaffected phenotype does not establish that the allele is harmless; its expression state changes when it passes through the male germline.

Now follow an affected heterozygous woman who originally inherited v from her father. When she forms eggs, a v-bearing egg has the maternal imprint appropriate to the locus. If her partner is N/N, half the offspring inherit v but none manifest the defined trait under the simple paternal-expression model. Among those offspring, a son who carries v may later transmit it through sperm, restoring its potential to be active in the following generation. This is not a change of dominance or mutation reversal. It reflects the normal reset of the parent-specific expression state.

Consider a randomly selected offspring of the affected heterozygous woman and a normal partner. The probability of inheriting v is one half. If this offspring is known to be male and later has a child with a normal partner, the probability that the grandchild inherits v through the male is one half times one half, or one quarter. Under the stated fully penetrant model, one quarter is also the probability of manifestation in that grandchild. If the intervening offspring is a female, the transmission probability remains one quarter but the phenotype probability is zero because transmission from her is maternal.

If penetrance is 0.7 even when v is on the active paternal homolog, the phenotype probability through the intervening male becomes one quarter times 0.7, or 0.175. This example combines imprinting with incomplete penetrance and illustrates why the two phenomena are not mutually exclusive. The first factor accounts for genetic transmission, the second for the probability that the transmitted allele is on the relevant active homolog in the proposed cross, and the final factor for manifestation given the active variant. Avoid multiplying by a factor twice if the stated penetrance already incorporates parental-origin effects.

A model in which expression is exclusively maternal produces the mirror-image reasoning. A variant transmitted through the egg may affect the phenotype, while the same variant transmitted through sperm may not. Both sexes can still carry the autosomal allele. The sex of the transmitting parent determines the regulatory origin of the allele; the sex of the offspring need not determine whether the allele is expressed. This distinguishes parent-of-origin inheritance from a sex-limited trait, where the phenotype depends on features of the offspring such as hormonal or anatomical context.

Reciprocal-cross differences alone do not prove imprinting. In a cross between a normal female and mutant male compared with a mutant female and normal male, the cytoplasm, maternal developmental environment and parent-specific genotype all change. An alternative mechanism could involve maternal effect, organelle transmission or a sex-chromosome locus. To distinguish them, ask whether the relevant trait tracks the allele in the offspring, the genotype of the mother, the cytoplasmic inheritance pattern or the sex chromosome inherited. Allele-specific expression data provide direct support for the imprinting hypothesis when expression tracks the immediate parental origin of an autosomal copy.
