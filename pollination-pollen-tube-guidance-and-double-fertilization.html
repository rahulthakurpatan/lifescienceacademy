1. Two journeys inside one reproductive cycle

A flowering plant faces an unusual problem when it reproduces sexually. Its male and female gametes are produced in different structures, and the egg lies deep inside an ovule enclosed by the ovary. Unlike a typical motile animal sperm, an angiosperm sperm cell cannot swim independently through a surrounding liquid to reach the egg. The plant therefore separates the task of long-distance transport from the task of gamete fusion. A pollen grain travels between floral structures, and a living extension of the pollen grain subsequently grows through maternal tissues. Only at the end of this journey are the two sperm cells delivered to the female gametophyte. Understanding this division of labour explains why successful pollination is necessary in conventional sexual reproduction but is not, by itself, proof that fertilization has occurred.

The conspicuous parts of a flower should not obscure the less visible reproductive compartments. The anther produces pollen, while the pistil contains the stigma, style and ovary in a generalized flower. The stigma presents the surface on which compatible pollen can establish contact. The style commonly separates the stigma from the ovary and provides a route through which pollen tubes grow; some species have a very short style or a different arrangement. The ovary contains ovules attached through their funiculi. Each ovule encloses a female gametophyte, also called the embryo sac in flowering plants. The ovule is therefore not an egg, and the ovary is not a single embryo sac. A flower may contain many ovules, each with its own potential reproductive outcome.

Consider the route from the perspective of a pollen grain. Its first obstacle is reaching a suitable stigma. Its next obstacle is remaining viable and receiving sufficient water to resume growth. After a tube emerges, it must advance through a route containing walls, extracellular material and other cells. At the ovary, the tube must find an individual receptive ovule rather than simply stop in the general vicinity of female tissue. It then needs to enter a suitable region of the ovule and communicate with the embryo sac so that sperm delivery occurs at the appropriate time. Finally, the two sperm cells must establish productive interactions with two different female cells. Each obstacle is a distinct biological checkpoint, so the failure of any checkpoint may lead to infertility even though the previous steps appear normal.

The final two fusion events are responsible for the name double fertilization. One sperm fuses with the egg to establish the zygote, which initiates the embryonic lineage. The other fuses with the central cell to establish the primary endosperm nucleus and endosperm lineage. In the common Polygonum-type female gametophyte of a diploid flowering plant, the zygotic nucleus receives one maternal and one paternal chromosome set, whereas the primary endosperm nucleus receives two maternal sets and one paternal set. This is a developmental distinction, not merely a calculation of chromosome number. The two products have different identities and tasks even when an unusual species changes their chromosome-set proportions. The embryo becomes the new plant sporophyte; the endosperm generally participates in its nutritional and developmental support.

An ovule that has undergone the two fusions is not instantly a mature seed. It has entered a new developmental state in which the embryo and endosperm begin to develop and maternal integuments participate in seed coat formation. Similarly, the fruit usually develops from ovarian tissue, sometimes with additional floral contributions. The genetic identity of a tissue must be traced to its cellular origin, not inferred from its location in the seed or fruit. An embryo, endosperm and seed coat may be physically adjacent but possess different parental contributions and developmental histories. The present chapter ends at the establishment of these lineages; the patterning of the embryo and the later interaction of seed compartments belong to the following chapters.

The order of events also reveals why plant and animal reproductive terminology must be used carefully. A pollen tube is a cellular growth structure, not a sperm flagellum. The central cell is an additional fertilization target, not a second egg. The embryo sac is a gametophyte, not a mature embryo. When the student can locate each object in the appropriate generation and identify its function, the rest of the mechanism becomes a connected explanation rather than a collection of names.

2. Discovering the second fertilization

The observation that pollen is necessary for seed formation does not reveal what happens to the pollen inside the pistil. Pollen is visible at the floral surface, but its relationship to the deeply enclosed female gametophyte is difficult to establish without examining internal structures. Early studies of plant sexuality established the importance of pollen and the formation of offspring from crosses. Yet an explanation of inheritance through sexual reproduction still required evidence that the contents of pollen actually reached and interacted with the female reproductive cells. The discovery of a pollen tube provided an anatomical solution to the problem of distance.

The pollen tube was initially significant because it linked two locations that could not communicate by independent sperm swimming in flowering plants. Once the tube was recognized as an extension of the pollen grain, the next question was what its contents did after reaching the ovule. Improved microscopy and the study of stained serial sections permitted investigators to follow nuclei at successive stages. Such sections can reveal the spatial positions of cells and nuclei, although an individual fixed preparation cannot establish the full temporal order by itself. By comparing preparations from different developmental stages, investigators reconstructed a plausible sequence. This distinction between observing a structure and inferring a process remains important when interpreting histological evidence.

In 1898 Sergius Nawaschin reported the two fertilization events in flowering plants, studying lilies and fritillaries. Léon Guignard independently documented double fertilization in the following period. Their observations challenged the idea that only one male nucleus had an essential reproductive role while the remaining male nucleus merely degenerated. Instead, the second male nucleus participated in a distinct fusion involving the central region of the female gametophyte. The studies supplied a developmental origin for the endosperm that differed from the origin of the embryo. Subsequent examination of other angiosperms showed that the phenomenon was not restricted to these first examples. The precise organization of the female gametophyte and the resulting endosperm can nevertheless vary among lineages.

Classical cytology provides a powerful way to test the two-fusion model. If only the egg were fertilized, one would expect a paternal nuclear contribution in the zygote but not in the fertilization-derived endosperm nucleus. If the second sperm instead entered the central cell, the central cell should acquire paternal chromosomes as well. This prediction can be investigated using chromosome counts and distinguishable parental genetic markers. The important comparison is between the state of the egg and central cell before the pollen tube reaches them and their state after fertilization. The presence of the paternal genome in both resulting lineages supports the two-fusion interpretation. It does not imply that every gene contributed by the two parents is expressed equally in each lineage.

Modern imaging allows scientists to distinguish intermediate events that were difficult to resolve in fixed sections. Fluorescent reporters can mark pollen tubes, sperm nuclei or female cell boundaries. A record of the same ovule at successive times can establish whether tube entry, discharge, sperm relocation and fusion occur in the predicted sequence. A fluorescent sperm nucleus close to the egg is not necessarily inside the egg; that inference requires spatial resolution and, preferably, independent indicators of membrane or nuclear fusion. Imaging therefore complements genetic evidence rather than replacing it. A mutation that prevents membrane fusion despite apparently normal delivery provides a particularly useful way to separate transport from fertilization itself.

The history is best understood as a progression from dependence to location, from location to nuclear contributions, and then from nuclear contributions to causal cell biology. Classical observations showed that the two fusions happen. Experimental analysis now asks why the tube reaches a particular ovule, how it knows when to stop growing, and which proteins make the gametes competent to fuse. These are separate questions. A molecule required for attracting pollen tubes does not necessarily participate in membrane fusion; an egg protein required for sperm activation need not guide the tube. The distinction is central to interpreting modern developmental mutants.

3. The cells that must meet

Before examining pollen recognition, it is useful to establish the genetic identity of the cells involved. The conspicuous flowering plant is the sporophyte. In an ordinary diploid species, its somatic cells possess two sets of chromosomes. Meiosis in reproductive tissues produces haploid spores. The spores subsequently divide by mitosis to form reduced gametophytes. The male gametophyte is the pollen grain and its tube, whereas the female gametophyte develops inside the ovule. These gametophytes are not produced by the direct fusion of gametes, and the pollen grain is not itself a single gamete.

A mature pollen grain has a vegetative component and a generative lineage. The vegetative cell supplies the cytoplasm that grows into the tube and maintains its polarized growth. The generative cell divides mitotically to produce two sperm cells. Depending on the species, this division is completed before pollen is released from the anther or after germination within the growing tube. Thus a two-celled pollen grain can still accomplish double fertilization if its generative cell divides at the appropriate later time. A three-celled pollen grain contains the vegetative cell and the two sperm cells already present. The occurrence of mitosis within a haploid lineage explains why sperm are haploid without requiring any additional reduction division at the point of fertilization.

The two sperm cells of a pollen grain are normally mitotic descendants of a single generative cell. They therefore usually possess the same haploid nuclear genotype, barring mutation or other unusual changes. Nevertheless, they can participate in two developmentally different outcomes. The recipient cytoplasms, nuclear configurations and regulatory states differ between the egg and central cell. Genetic similarity between the sperm does not demand that their products acquire identical developmental fates. The fate of a fertilization product is established through the interaction of the genomes and the cellular context into which the sperm enters.

In the common Polygonum-type embryo sac, an egg cell and two synergid cells are located at the micropylar end. The micropyle is the passage through the ovule integuments toward the female gametophyte; it is not a membrane pore in the egg itself. The egg apparatus consists of the egg and the two synergids. Toward the opposite, chalazal end there are typically three antipodal cells, which may persist or degenerate depending on the species. The large central cell occupies much of the embryo sac and contains the two maternal polar nuclei or their fused derivative. A standard seven-celled, eight-nucleate description applies before fusion of the polar nuclei and before species-specific degeneration changes the counts.

The synergids are especially important because their anatomical location allows them to connect the external pollen-tube route with the internal gametes. Their micropylar region contains a specialized wall structure known as the filiform apparatus, which includes wall ingrowths that increase the area of interaction at the cell surface. This structure is associated with secretion and exchange and provides an interface for the approaching tube. The synergid is not fertilized in conventional double fertilization. Instead, its role as an accessory cell helps deliver the sperm toward the egg and central cell.

The pre-fertilization central cell deserves special attention. A cell containing two haploid nuclei contains two maternal chromosome-set equivalents even when the nuclei are physically separate. It is imprecise to describe it as necessarily containing one diploid nucleus at every developmental moment. In species where the polar nuclei fuse before fertilization, a secondary nucleus with two maternal sets is present; in other developmental contexts the fusion may be completed in association with fertilization. A chromosome bookkeeping exercise can group the two maternal sets together, but a cytological drawing should show their actual nuclear organization in the example under consideration.

These distinctions clarify the roles of both generations in the same flower. The stigma, style, ovule integuments and nucellus are maternal sporophytic tissues. The egg, synergids, central cell and pollen tube belong to their respective gametophytes. After fertilization, the embryo and endosperm have new nuclear contributions, but the integuments do not acquire paternal chromosomes simply because they surround the products. When interpreting a gene-expression defect or a genetic cross, the identity of the tissue in which the gene acts is often as important as its allele. A mutation expressed in a stigma can affect acceptance of pollen irrespective of the genotype inside the sperm, whereas a mutation expressed only in the sperm can permit the entire route to succeed and still prevent fertilization.

4. Pollination and the decision to accept pollen

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma. In self-pollination, pollen originates from the same flower or another flower on the same plant, depending on the terminology used for the particular case. Autogamy denotes transfer within the same flower, geitonogamy denotes transfer between flowers of the same individual, and xenogamy denotes transfer between different individuals. Geitonogamy can involve the same physical vectors as cross-pollination between plants, but genetically it is selfing. These distinctions concern the origin and route of pollen. They do not establish whether sperm and egg have actually fused.

A grain landing on the stigma faces a change from dispersal conditions to a physiological environment appropriate for growth. The surface must support attachment and, in the case of dry stigmas, regulated water transfer. Wet stigmas have extracellular secretions that can support pollen hydration and interactions with the surface. These categories describe surface characteristics rather than a universal molecular mechanism. Different flowering plants show different degrees of selectivity, hydration control and extracellular secretion. It is therefore misleading to extrapolate the behavior of a single experimental species to all flowering plants.

Compatibility is a property of an interaction between pollen and pistil. A grain may be perfectly viable in a suitable medium yet fail to hydrate or grow on a particular stigma. Conversely, a compatible genetic combination does not guarantee success if the grain is damaged or the pistil is unreceptive. An experimental distinction between incompatibility and general loss of viability requires a positive control. If a pollen sample fails on one pistil but grows normally on another suitable pistil, general pollen death becomes a less plausible explanation. If it fails everywhere, incompatibility is not established by that evidence.

Self-incompatibility is a genetically controlled mechanism that can limit self-fertilization or mating between plants sharing relevant recognition specificities. It should be distinguished from separation of male and female maturation times, separation of sexual organs, physical separation of sexes and genetic male sterility. Those conditions may reduce selfing without requiring an allele-specific rejection response. Self-incompatible pollen can be alive, hydrated in other conditions and capable of fertilizing a suitable partner. The incompatibility response acts before successful fertilization and does not imply that a resulting embryo was rejected after formation.

A well studied example occurs in Brassica, where a pollen-coat ligand called SCR or SP11 participates in specificity and a stigma-localized receptor kinase called SRK participates in recognition. When their relevant S specificities match, the interaction initiates a rejection response in the stigma papilla. This receptor-ligand example is unusual to a student accustomed to thinking of recognition as a mechanism that always promotes adhesion or fusion. Here, specific recognition triggers rejection. A mismatch may permit the normal compatible pathway, provided that other requirements for hydration and growth are satisfied. The binding reaction is a molecular event, whereas the failure of hydration or penetration is a later physiological consequence.

In sporophytic self-incompatibility, the specificity expressed by a pollen grain is determined by the diploid pollen-producing plant. The surrounding sporophytic anther tissues contribute to the pollen coat, helping explain why the specificity displayed at the pollen surface need not be predictable from the haploid sperm genotype alone. Dominance relationships among S alleles can further affect the phenotype. In gametophytic self-incompatibility, by contrast, the pollen's own haploid S genotype determines its specificity. One well-known gametophytic system involves S-RNases in the pistil and selective rejection of incompatible pollen tubes. Different families employ distinct molecular systems, so S-RNase should not be assumed to mediate the Brassica receptor response.

Consider a simplified gametophytic incompatibility cross in which an S1S2 pistil receives equal numbers of viable S1 and S3 pollen grains from an S1S3 donor. If both pistil alleles are expressed and sharing either specificity causes rejection, the S1 grains are rejected while the S3 grains are accepted. Under equal segregation and viability, half of the grains would be genetically compatible. This prediction concerns the acceptance step only. It does not predict exactly half as many seeds as a fully compatible cross, because many pollen grains may compete for a limited number of ovules and additional failures may occur downstream. A change in pollen density or ovule number could alter the seed outcome without changing the incompatibility rule.

In a sporophytic system the preceding calculation cannot simply be reused. If both S1 and S3 specificities are displayed by each pollen grain and either shared specificity is sufficient to cause rejection, the S1 match could lead to rejection of the entire cohort on an S1S2 pistil. In systems with dominance, a different displayed specificity could produce a different outcome. The question must specify which system operates, which allele products are expressed and the relevant recognition rule. Many examination distractors are constructed by substituting the allele segregation of the haploid gametes for the phenotype of the pollen coat.

Once accepted, the pollen grain absorbs water, resumes metabolic activity and establishes a site from which polarized extension can occur. The plant has not yet achieved fertilization; it has selected a living male gametophyte that is permitted to attempt the next stage. The transition from surface acceptance to tube initiation is a natural bridge between genetics, cell signaling and the mechanics of cellular growth.

5. Hydration, germination and the establishment of polarity

A mature pollen grain can survive a period of transport during which growth is reduced or suspended. Many species disperse pollen in a relatively dehydrated state, although the degree of dehydration varies widely. When a compatible grain contacts a receptive stigma, water enters the pollen, allowing its cytoplasm to regain a condition that supports active metabolism. The transition is more complex than swelling a dry particle. The grain must maintain membrane integrity, regulate the movement of solutes and water, activate enzyme systems, and begin the synthesis and trafficking of wall material. A viable grain that fails to establish this coordination cannot develop a productive tube.

The pollen wall offers both protection and an opportunity for emergence. The outer exine contains resistant sporopollenin and the inner intine contains polysaccharide-rich material. Apertures in many pollen grains are regions where the exine is reduced or modified. They provide plausible sites for tube emergence because extension through the wall is facilitated there. Aperture pattern varies across taxa, and some pollen grains can germinate from regions that do not conform to a simple aperture rule. The important principle is that the mechanical organization of a wall influences where a living cell can extend, but it does not by itself determine the entire developmental program.

Germination begins when a restricted portion of the vegetative cell becomes an active site of extension. A growing protrusion receives new membrane and wall materials, and the rest of the grain remains comparatively stable. This is an example of cell polarity: the distribution of activities and structures differs between regions of the same cell. The growing surface will become the tube apex, while the region behind the apex becomes the tube shank. The pollen tube is therefore not a chain of cells formed by repeated cell divisions. It is a greatly elongated part of a single vegetative cell, containing cytoplasm and a lumen continuous with the pollen grain until regions are partitioned by callose plugs.

Pollen germination can be examined outside the pistil in a suitable medium, but a tube growing in such a medium has not necessarily acquired all properties required for fertilization. Sugars provide substrates for metabolism and influence the water potential of the environment. Calcium and other ions participate in the regulation of growth and the properties of the cell wall. Boron is important in many pollen germination media, in part because borate-mediated crosslinking contributes to the organization of wall polysaccharides. Nutritional requirements and optimal concentrations differ among species. It would be incorrect to infer that a single published medium reproduces the exact conditions of every stigma or style.

Water movement illustrates the need to balance environmental support and mechanical stability. For a simplified plant cell, water potential can be expressed as the sum of solute potential and pressure potential when gravitational and matric terms are negligible. A higher concentration of dissolved substances generally makes solute potential more negative, whereas positive turgor raises pressure potential. Water tends to move from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential across a sufficiently permeable boundary. As water enters and the cell expands, pressure may rise. This pressure can supply a physical driving force for extension, provided that the wall yields in the intended region.

An excessively dilute external medium may allow rapid water uptake before the growing cell can adequately manage its wall and membrane. A highly concentrated external medium can reduce water uptake or even cause water loss. The resulting inhibition of growth is not evidence of an incompatibility response unless the experimental design independently establishes the specific recognition event. A chemical treatment can prevent germination by altering physical conditions that all pollen needs, without interacting with an S-locus receptor. Such a result helps explain why germination experiments should distinguish the osmotic properties of the medium from the effects of individual nutrients or signaling molecules.

The location of the protrusion can be studied by observing the same grain during germination. An aperture may exist before hydration, while the concentration of growth-related activity at a particular location appears later. A researcher can ask whether the location was predetermined by the wall, induced by the local environment, or stabilized through a combination of these influences. In an examination setting, the presence of a protrusion is evidence of local expansion, not automatically evidence that a particular signaling protein caused the polarization. Perturbing the protein and testing whether the polarity can be restored provides a stronger causal argument than its localization alone.

The vegetative nucleus and the generative lineage occupy the expanding cytoplasm as tube growth begins. A generative cell that has not yet divided can subsequently produce two sperm. These internal cells must remain viable during transport. Their movement should not be confused with an independent migration through the maternal style. The vegetative cell is the component in direct contact with the external environment, and its growing wall and membrane respond to the cues encountered along the route. The sperm are carried within this living vehicle. A defect in vegetative cell function can therefore prevent successful delivery even when the two sperm nuclei and their genetic contents are normal.

6. The mechanics and molecular organization of pollen-tube growth

Most plant cells enlarge over a substantial part of their surface, constrained by their walls and neighbouring cells. A pollen tube instead extends mainly at its rounded apex. The sides of the tube generally retain their established shape, while the apex is continuously renewed. This form of localized expansion is called tip growth. The result is an elongated cell that can advance through restricted spaces without making the entire tube wider at the same rate. Tip growth is also encountered in structures such as root hairs, but a pollen tube has the distinctive responsibility of transporting the male gametes through a reproductive pathway.

The mechanical problem of tip growth can be understood by considering a cell whose internal fluid exerts pressure on its wall. Turgor applies a force broadly rather than exclusively at one point. If all regions of the wall were equally deformable, pressure could cause expansion in unwanted regions. A pollen tube controls the spatial distribution of wall compliance so that the tip can yield while the shank resists excessive deformation. Turgor is necessary as a driving force, but a local difference in wall properties is essential for converting that force into a narrow, directed growth pattern. Raising pressure alone does not explain the location of extension.

Pectin is particularly important in this mechanical arrangement. It comprises a family of wall polysaccharides, including homogalacturonan with variable degrees of methyl esterification. Newly deposited pectin at the growing apex is commonly relatively methyl esterified. Pectin methylesterases remove methyl groups from suitable pectin chains. The resulting carboxyl groups can participate in calcium-mediated crosslinking when the distribution of de-esterified residues and local ionic conditions permit it. Such interactions can increase wall stiffness in appropriate regions. Pectin modification can also have other consequences, including altered susceptibility to pectin-degrading enzymes, so the statement that all de-esterification always stiffens every wall is too simple.

As the tip advances, material initially located at the apex becomes part of the subapical and then lateral wall. The distribution of wall polysaccharides and modifying enzymes changes along this axis. The wall behind the growing apex often contains more callose and cellulose than the softest apical region. Callose is a glucan containing predominantly beta-1,3 linkages, whereas cellulose is composed of beta-1,4-linked glucose chains. These polymers have different structures and properties. Callose deposition contributes to the organization of the tube shank and to the formation of plugs that partition older regions of the tube from the actively growing region.

Callose plugs do not form additional sperm cells or divide the vegetative nucleus. They create transverse barriers that help restrict the active cytoplasmic compartment as the tube extends. If the tube maintained the entire cytoplasm of an increasingly long cylinder in a fully active state, its transport and maintenance demands would increase considerably. Restricting the active region offers a way to maintain an efficient cytoplasmic domain near the growth front. The spacing and presence of plugs vary, and their disruption can alter growth and fertility without necessarily producing one identical phenotype in every species.

The increasing surface area of a growing tube also requires delivery of new plasma membrane. Secretory vesicles carry wall precursors and membrane components toward the growing region, where exocytosis contributes material to the surface. Actin filaments and myosin-dependent transport help distribute vesicles and other organelles through the cytoplasm. The actin cytoskeleton is not a rigid tube-shaped scaffold that simply pushes the apex forward. Its dynamic organization coordinates movement of material, secretion and the establishment of a polarized cytoplasmic domain. The effect of disturbing actin may therefore include failures of vesicle delivery, organelle positioning and directional growth.

Membrane addition must be balanced with retrieval and recycling. If exocytosis adds more membrane than is needed to accommodate surface expansion and no compensating retrieval occurs, membrane organization can be disturbed. Endocytosis recovers membrane and membrane-associated proteins and helps maintain appropriate surface composition. These trafficking processes are not merely housekeeping operations. They contribute to the distribution of receptors and signaling components that the tube uses to respond to its environment. A pollen tube that can produce wall polymers but cannot deliver them to the appropriate location will not achieve normal polarized extension.

A calcium-rich cytoplasmic domain is commonly observed near the growing tip. Calcium ions act as signaling molecules, influencing secretion, the cytoskeleton and other processes. Their concentration in the cytoplasm is maintained by regulated transport across membranes and by intracellular buffering and sequestration. An influx of calcium near the tip can help sustain a spatially restricted signal. The fact that calcium also contributes to extracellular pectin interactions does not mean that its intracellular signaling role and extracellular structural role are interchangeable. One concerns regulation of cellular machinery; the other concerns properties of the wall material.

Local pH also influences processes that determine growth. Proton transport can establish differences in hydrogen ion concentration between regions of the cytoplasm and external environment. Enzymes and cytoskeletal proteins respond to chemical conditions, so a change in proton distribution can alter the behavior of growth-related machinery. Reactive oxygen species can participate in signaling and wall-related processes at regulated levels but can damage the cell at excessive levels. The growth apex is thus a dynamic region where ionic signals, chemical reactions and material trafficking operate together. None of these signals is adequately described by a single universal rule such as more calcium always produces faster growth.

Members of the plant ROP family of small GTPases help organize polarized growth. A small GTPase can cycle between states associated with binding GTP and GDP, thereby regulating interactions with downstream proteins. Spatial restriction of active signaling allows growth-related processes to be concentrated near the apex. If the active region becomes abnormally broad, secretion and wall expansion can become less focused, potentially generating a swollen or misshapen tip rather than a narrow tube. This provides a useful conceptual example of why the spatial arrangement of signaling is important in addition to the total amount of signaling activity.

The shape of a pollen tube emerges from a balance that must be maintained continuously. New material arrives, the wall stretches, and the surface is remodelled as the cell advances. Too little deposition relative to expansion can compromise wall integrity. Too much rigidification at the apex can impede extension. Loss of the localized control of growth can produce a broad tip, abnormal branching or an arrested cell. A tube that ruptures before reaching an ovule loses its ability to deliver intact sperm to the correct target. The controlled rupture at the female gametophyte, discussed later, must therefore be distinguished from uncontrolled failure during transit.

Experiments on pollen-tube growth frequently use time-lapse imaging to measure tip displacement. Such observations can reveal periods of faster and slower extension rather than a perfectly constant rate. Oscillations in growth and changes in calcium or pH have been observed in experimental systems. Temporal associations can suggest coordinated regulation, but a peak in one variable followed by a peak in another does not, alone, prove a simple direct causal relationship. Several variables may be affected by the same upstream process, and the measured phase relationship may depend on the conditions of observation. A strong mechanistic interpretation combines temporal measurements with perturbation and appropriate controls.

Energy is another requirement that cannot be separated from cell mechanics. The synthesis of polysaccharides and proteins, vesicle movement, ion transport and cytoplasmic maintenance all depend directly or indirectly on metabolic resources. Sugars available from the pollen grain and pistil-associated environment provide substrates for energy metabolism and biosynthesis. ATP-dependent pumps maintain gradients that would otherwise dissipate, and cytoskeletal motors require energy to carry out transport. Turgor does not replace these metabolic requirements. A tube could experience sufficient physical pressure yet still cease growth if it could not produce and distribute new material.

This coordinated mechanism explains different classes of loss-of-function phenotypes. A defect in a wall-modifying enzyme might alter the stiffness distribution and cause a change in shape. A severe trafficking defect could reduce delivery of material at the apex. A disruption of ion regulation could influence signaling and the wall environment. A defect in a receptor for an ovular attractant, on the other hand, might leave basic tip growth relatively intact while impairing the ability to orient toward a reproductive target. Measuring tube length alone would not distinguish all these possibilities. Combining morphology, growth rate, spatial trajectory and sperm delivery is much more informative.

7. Passage through the pistil: growth support and directional routes

Once a tube has emerged from a compatible pollen grain, it must enter the tissues of the pistil. The details differ with the anatomy of the stigma and style. In many flowering plants, tubes advance through a transmitting tract containing specialized maternal cells and extracellular material. Some styles contain a canal, whereas others contain a relatively solid tract through which tubes grow. This diversity is an important reminder that pollen tubes do not travel through an identical hollow pipe in every flower. The organization of the route influences how the tube encounters nutrients, physical constraints and signals.

Maternal tissue can contribute to growth through its extracellular environment. Wall-associated molecules and secreted substances may provide suitable conditions for adhesion, extension and nutrition. A pollen tube moving through the style does not become a maternal sporophytic cell. It retains its male gametophytic identity and its haploid vegetative genome. The tube can respond to maternal conditions without receiving maternal chromosomes. Interactions between a male gametophyte and a diploid maternal tissue represent communication across generations within the same reproductive organ.

Growth through a transmitting tract and accurate targeting of a particular ovule are not identical processes. A tract can channel growth over a long distance, reducing the number of directions that a tube must explore. At the ovary, a tube must leave the general route and navigate toward an ovule. In Arabidopsis, for example, tubes may emerge from the transmitting tract onto the septum, travel toward the funiculus of an ovule and then enter its micropylar region. The exact path varies with floral and ovular architecture. The sequential distinction between tract passage, ovule targeting and micropylar entry is more general than the geometry of one model species.

The terms funicular guidance and micropylar guidance describe two useful portions of this trajectory. Funicular guidance concerns navigation toward and along the structure that attaches the ovule to surrounding ovarian tissue. Micropylar guidance concerns the final directed approach to the opening leading toward the female gametophyte. A tube reaching the base of an ovule has not necessarily completed micropylar guidance. Likewise, entry into the micropyle does not guarantee sperm release. A defect at each stage produces a different spatial pattern, which can be identified by tracing the location of tube tips in appropriately stained or imaged pistils.

One way to visualize pollen-tube trajectories in fixed plant tissues is to stain callose with an appropriate fluorescent stain such as aniline blue. The method is useful because callose occurs in pollen-tube walls and plugs, allowing routes to be identified against the surrounding tissue. However, a bright fluorescent line indicates stained material, not automatically a currently living or successfully fertilizing tube. Other callose-containing structures can contribute fluorescence. A researcher should distinguish tube identity and actual fusion outcomes using additional anatomical or genetic evidence. Fixed preparations can establish the distribution of tubes at a sampled time but cannot show subsequent movement of the same tube.

The maternal pistil does more than supply a passive route. Passage through its tissues can affect the physiological state of the male gametophyte. Some experimentally studied pollen tubes acquire responsiveness to ovular cues after exposure to particular female tissues or signals. This is a form of competence in which a cell's ability to respond depends on its previous developmental experiences. A tube may be perfectly capable of elongation on an artificial medium while failing to respond strongly to an isolated attractant if it has not acquired the necessary receptive state. Such a result does not necessarily mean that the attractant is irrelevant in the intact flower.

Consider a hypothetical reciprocal cross experiment. Pollen from a mutant plant grows normally through a wild-type pistil and produces seeds, while wild-type pollen placed on a mutant pistil arrests in the style. The evidence suggests that the relevant defect lies in the female environment rather than being intrinsic to the pollen. A reciprocal cross alone does not specify the exact tissue or molecule, because the mutant pistil includes several interacting structures. Additional evidence, such as tissue-specific restoration of the affected gene or observation of the first abnormal stage, can narrow the explanation. If the same mutant also shows abnormal ovule development, that possibility must be considered before assigning its entire fertility phenotype to pollen-tube guidance.

Pollen tubes also compete in pistils that receive more grains than there are receptive ovules. Differences in the timing of germination, rate of extension or responsiveness to guidance can affect which grains ultimately deliver sperm. This is one reason haploid gene expression in the male gametophyte can influence reproductive success. However, competition is not reducible to speed alone. A very fast tube that cannot perceive an ovular cue may fail while a slower but correctly guided tube succeeds. Equal representation of two pollen genotypes at the stigma does not guarantee equal representation among resulting embryos. Such departures can reflect pre-fertilization selection rather than a failure of Mendelian chromosome segregation during meiosis.

The route can now be divided into local tasks. Near the stigma, the important question is whether a viable compatible grain can initiate growth. Within the style, the question is whether the tube can maintain integrity and reach the ovary. Near individual ovules, the question becomes whether it responds to the appropriate spatial information. A final interaction at the embryo sac determines whether growth stops and sperm are released. Keeping these tasks distinct prevents a general observation such as reduced seed set from being misinterpreted as direct proof of a particular guidance mechanism.

8. How an ovule directs the final approach

The location of an ovule in the ovary creates a problem that cannot be solved solely by continued growth through the style. A tube leaving the transmitting tract may encounter several possible paths, and an ovule may be receptive only during a particular period of development. A mechanism that preferentially directs tubes toward suitable targets increases the likelihood that the sperm will arrive at a female gametophyte capable of fertilization. The developing ovule and the female gametophyte therefore contribute actively to the reproductive process rather than behaving merely as passive destinations.

A directional chemical cue must be distinguished from a substance that simply improves growth. Suppose an experimental medium contains a substance uniformly distributed throughout the region around a pollen tube. If the tube extends more rapidly, the result supports a growth-promoting effect under those conditions. It does not establish that the substance can guide growth toward an ovule. Directional guidance requires spatial information: a tube must respond differently to different locations or directions. A diffusible signal can provide this information if its concentration varies in space and the tube has a mechanism for detecting that variation.

For a nonmotile tube, the relevant response is a change in the orientation of future growth. The established portion of the tube generally does not rotate as a rigid object. Instead, the expanding apical region changes direction, and subsequently deposited wall follows the new trajectory. Chemical guidance therefore operates through machinery that is already responsible for polarized extension. A local signal detected near one side of the apex may bias signaling and secretion so that extension becomes asymmetric. Over successive moments, a small directional bias can produce a substantial change in the path of the tube. The exact intracellular events coupling receptor activation to turning are not identical across all species or signaling systems.

The spatial distribution of a diffusible cue depends on release from its source, movement through the surrounding environment and loss through degradation, binding or other processes. An attractant released near the micropyle may become less concentrated at increasing distances from the source. Tissue architecture can modify this distribution, and a biological gradient should not be assumed to be a simple linear function of distance. The gradient also changes with time if secretion changes or the source cell degenerates. The tube responds to the actual signal available to its receptors, which may differ from the total amount of protein present in the ovule.

The synergid cells at the micropylar end of the embryo sac occupy a particularly advantageous position for short-range communication with the tube. Studies of flowering plants have shown that they can release molecules that attract pollen tubes. The wall ingrowths of the filiform apparatus create an extensive interface for exchange. A healthy synergid can therefore influence the local environment around the approaching tube while remaining physically distinct from the egg and central cell. The signal's site of production is not necessarily the cell that later receives the sperm nucleus.

Experiments using Torenia fournieri made an especially important contribution because its embryo sac is accessible for observation outside the ovule in an unusual manner. This anatomical feature enables investigators to manipulate individual cells and observe the behaviour of pollen tubes in relation to the embryo sac. Removing or damaging specific cells can help identify which cell types are required for attraction. If the loss of synergids eliminates attraction while comparable manipulation of another cell type does not, the result supports a role for synergids. It remains necessary to control for nonspecific injury, because damaging one cell might indirectly disrupt neighboring cells or their secretion.

In 2009 Okuda and colleagues identified cysteine-rich defensin-like peptides called LUREs in Torenia. Their work combined the identification of candidate molecules produced by synergids with functional attraction assays and interference with the candidates. A peptide capable of directing tubes in an appropriate assay supplies evidence that a molecular cue can influence direction. Reduction of normal attraction when its production is impaired supplies a complementary form of evidence. The conjunction of these observations makes a stronger case than either expression analysis or a single attraction assay alone.

LURE peptides provide a useful molecular example of short-range reproductive communication. Their cysteine-rich nature is associated with disulfide bonds that help stabilize the folded peptide. The term defensin-like refers to structural similarity; it does not imply that the only function of these peptides is defense against microorganisms. In this context the relevant function is attraction of compatible or preferentially responsive pollen tubes. The ability of a molecule to act as a signal depends on the appropriate receptor system and physiological state of the responding cell, not solely on its chemical structure.

In Arabidopsis, attractant peptides and receptor-like kinases on pollen tubes provide further examples of interaction between the female and male gametophytes. The extracellular portion of a receptor participates in recognizing a signal, while its intracellular machinery can transmit information to growth-related processes. Receptors such as PRK6 have been studied in relation to AtLURE-mediated attraction. Multiple signals and receptors can contribute to ovule targeting, and related ligands may differ in the extent of species preference. At the examination level, the central causal idea is that an ovular peptide can be detected by the growing male cell and thereby influence directional growth. It is not necessary to memorize every receptor family member or assume that a single receptor accounts for all guidance.

A directional response is easiest to interpret when basal growth remains intact. Imagine a receptor mutant whose pollen germinates at the same frequency as wild type and whose tubes reach the ovary at a similar rate, but whose tips frequently pass the micropyle without entering. That pattern localizes the likely defect to late guidance more strongly than a reduction in seed set alone. The same mutant could also have a general defect in sensory competence, so it is useful to test attraction to a defined cue and to restore the normal receptor genetically. If the receptor is expressed in pollen, a normal maternal plant may not rescue the mutant pollen's directional response; the defect is intrinsic to the responding cell.

Loss of the attractant and loss of its receptor can produce superficially similar trajectories because both disrupt communication. Reciprocal crosses can distinguish the sending and responding sides. Wild-type pollen may fail to target mutant ovules that cannot produce an important attractant, while receptor-deficient pollen may fail to target wild-type ovules that produce the normal signal. Restoring the attractant in the female gametophyte addresses a sending defect, whereas restoring the receptor in pollen addresses a responding defect. Neither rescue necessarily corrects a loss of general tip-growth machinery. This framework links the present chapter to the principles of ligand–receptor signaling studied in cellular communication.

Specificity is another important feature of these interactions. If a peptide from one species preferentially attracts pollen tubes of that species, the result suggests that divergence of ligand–receptor communication can influence reproductive isolation. Preferential attraction is not the same as an absolute species barrier. Other signals may support cross-species attraction, and failures may occur at earlier compatibility or later fusion stages. To test a specific preference, the experiment should compare pollen from the candidate species under conditions where both samples can grow and are developmentally competent. A tube that does not grow at all in the assay cannot provide an informative negative result about directional specificity.

Even a strong response to a purified attractant does not prove that the same molecule alone determines the route in an intact ovary. The source geometry, competing signals, tissue barriers and developmental state of the tube may alter the outcome. The most useful conclusion is therefore appropriately restricted: a specified signal contributes to attraction under particular biological conditions. At the level of an intact plant, accurate targeting emerges from a sequence of interactions involving maternal sporophytic structures, male gametophytic growth machinery and female gametophytic communication. The last portion of this route ends when the tube arrives at the synergid-associated reception interface.

9. Separating attraction from growth in experimental evidence

A guidance experiment should be designed around the distinction between elongation and direction. The investigator can establish a reference trajectory of a growing pollen tube, place a localized source of candidate signal to one side of the expected path and observe whether the growth apex changes orientation toward the source. A control source made of the same carrier material but without the candidate signal is needed because the carrier itself may influence hydration or growth. A second control can confirm that the tube is capable of growing and turning under suitable conditions. Multiple independent tubes and preparations reduce the risk of treating an unusual trajectory as a general effect.

One useful response variable is the turning angle. The initial direction of growth defines a reference axis, and the later direction can be compared with this axis. A convention must be established so that a positive angle represents turning toward the source rather than simply any change in direction. An alternative response is the fraction of tubes that reach a specified target region within a fixed observation period. Each measure answers a different question. The mean angle can change even when the number of successful arrivals remains low, while arrival frequency also depends on the distance of the source and the time allowed for growth.

A common interpretive mistake is to count a group of tubes close to a signal source and conclude that the tubes were attracted. The same pattern could arise if tubes that happened to grow in that direction elongated faster, survived longer or were more likely to be included in the measurement. Time-lapse trajectories allow the experimenter to distinguish an actual change in direction from selective survival or faster extension. If initial orientations and starting distances are comparable, an increased tendency to turn toward the signal provides stronger evidence for directional sensing. Measuring growth rates independently helps establish that a guidance defect is not simply a consequence of reduced cell viability.

Consider a teaching dataset in which 80 out of 100 wild-type tubes reach a peptide-associated target, while only 20 out of 100 reach a carrier-only target. If both groups have similar elongation rates and the target geometry is identical, the difference is consistent with a directional effect of the peptide. Suppose a receptor mutant reaches the peptide-associated target in only 25 cases out of 100. If its basal growth is normal, the result supports a contribution of the receptor to the response. It does not prove that the receptor binds the peptide directly. A mutation could instead disrupt a downstream process needed to respond to several unrelated signals. Direct binding evidence and a suitable restoration experiment would make the molecular interpretation more specific.

The difference between the peptide and carrier groups is sixty percentage points in the wild type. This is distinct from saying there is a sixty percent relative increase. Relative to a baseline of twenty percent, the response of eighty percent is fourfold as large, corresponding to a three hundred percent relative increase. Choosing the appropriate expression matters because examinations may ask for an absolute change, a ratio or a relative change. These descriptive calculations do not establish statistical significance. Independent replicate experiments and the distribution of measurements across those replicates are needed to evaluate variability and experimental reliability.

The choice of denominator also matters. If a treatment causes half the pollen grains to die before observation, a high arrival fraction among the survivors could mask severe damage at the earlier stage. The probability of success per grain deposited on a stigma is not the same as the probability of success per viable germinated tube. When reporting a specific guidance assay, it is reasonable to condition on the tubes that began growth, provided that their viability and baseline growth are also assessed. A complete fertility study should additionally account for the earlier losses.

Another useful approach is to manipulate the female source rather than the male responder. A female gametophyte lacking a candidate signal might show reduced attraction, and restoration of the signal in the appropriate cell type might recover the phenotype. If restoration outside the normal source cell also attracts tubes, the result can help distinguish the importance of the molecule itself from the importance of its spatial release. Mislocalized expression may even guide tubes toward an abnormal site, demonstrating that the position of the signal contributes information. However, the effects of an artificial source do not automatically establish the normal physiological concentration or the exact range of action in the ovule.

These principles illustrate how an experimental question can be progressively sharpened. First, does a healthy embryo sac affect tube direction? Second, which cell contributes the effect? Third, which molecule accounts for some of the effect? Fourth, does a defined receptor participate in the response? Fifth, does the direction change without loss of ordinary growth? Each question requires different evidence. The process of scientific inference moves from a biological phenomenon to progressively more precise mechanistic statements rather than jumping directly from a mutant phenotype to a complete pathway.

10. Pollen-tube reception and the controlled release of sperm

A tube that reaches the micropyle must change from a continuously growing transport cell into a structure that releases its contents. This transition presents a problem of timing. If the tube releases its contents too early, the sperm are stranded away from the female gametes. If it never releases them, even perfectly guided sperm remain enclosed within the transport cell. Reproductive success therefore requires a mechanism that maintains tube integrity during transit but permits discharge at the appropriate destination. The relevant process is known as pollen-tube reception.

At the embryo sac, the tube typically interacts with one of the two synergids. The synergid that receives the tube is often referred to as the receptive synergid. Degeneration of this cell is associated with tube arrival and sperm release, although the exact timing and relationships among these events can vary with the species and experimental context. The other synergid may remain functional for a time. This asymmetry is not a distinction between two eggs. It is a division of roles between two accessory cells that occupy comparable locations beside the egg.

Reception includes communication that changes the behaviour of both interacting structures. A normally growing tube has mechanisms that maintain its apex and prevent uncontrolled bursting. When the tube reaches a receptive female interface, the balance of growth and integrity changes, allowing growth arrest and rupture. The two sperm are released from the tube and become available for interaction with the egg and central cell. They do not need to construct new flagella or undertake long-distance independent swimming. Their release close to the gametes places them in an environment in which cell contacts, local movements and fusion can occur.

The receptor-like kinase FERONIA and the glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein LORELEI provide a well-established Arabidopsis example of female-side reception functions. In mutants affecting these components, pollen tubes can sometimes enter or approach the female gametophyte but fail to undergo normal reception; instead, continued tube growth can be observed within the ovule. This phenotype is particularly instructive because the tube demonstrates the ability to grow and navigate while failing to terminate its journey appropriately. Fertility is reduced even though the previous targeting step was successful.

A receptor-like kinase contains an extracellular region that can participate in signal perception and an intracellular region that participates in transmission of signals. A membrane-associated accessory protein may influence the receptor's function or the signaling environment at the cell surface. The precise biochemical relationships in reception are more complex than a universal single-ligand switch. For syllabus-level interpretation, the important distinction is that normal reception depends on communication at the pollen-tube and synergid interface. FERONIA and LORELEI are not pollen-tube attractant peptides, and a reception defect is not necessarily a defect in the sperm fusion protein.

Calcium signaling provides one way to relate reception to the earlier discussion of polarized growth. Calcium dynamics have been observed in the synergid and tube during their interaction. These changes can be associated with communication, changes in growth and the eventual release of the tube contents. Nevertheless, a calcium signal is not by itself equivalent to membrane fusion of the sperm with a female gamete. The location of the signal and its timing relative to other events determine how it should be interpreted. A change in calcium within the growing tube during transit may regulate extension, while a signal at the reception interface may be associated with a different physiological transition.

The tube's own integrity-maintaining signaling systems also matter. In Arabidopsis, members of the ANXUR and BUPS receptor-like kinase groups and associated RALF peptide signaling have been implicated in the regulation of tube integrity. Mutations can lead to premature rupture or abnormal growth behavior. Such defects illustrate the opposite failure from continued growth within a receptive ovule. In one case, the vehicle breaks before its destination; in the other, the vehicle reaches the destination but fails to release its contents normally. Both can produce low fertility, yet their cell-biological causes and likely rescue strategies differ.

The interpretation of a reception mutant should begin with direct observation of the location and state of the tube. If a tube never reaches the ovule, it is premature to diagnose a reception defect. If a tube reaches and enters the micropylar region but its tip continues to elongate without releasing its contents, a defect in reception becomes plausible. If a tube discharges normally but neither female gamete acquires a paternal genome, the defect lies later than discharge unless the released sperm were already damaged. Combining tube labeling with sperm-specific and female-cell markers can resolve these alternatives.

The receiving synergid can be viewed as an interface between two different cellular tasks. Before arrival, the synergid-associated environment helps direct the tube; after arrival, communication with the receptive female structure contributes to a transition that permits sperm release. These tasks can involve overlapping anatomical regions without being functionally identical. An attractant-producing cell can participate in both phases, but the molecules involved need not be the same. A strong examination answer should therefore identify the precise missing event, rather than assign every synergid-related phenotype to failed attraction.

Entry through the micropyle is called porogamy. Alternative routes have been described in some flowering plants, including chalazogamy, in which the approach occurs through the chalazal region, and mesogamy, involving another route through ovular tissues. These terms describe anatomical paths of entry, not different definitions of fertilization. Porogamy is the standard model for interpreting an ordinary Polygonum-type embryo sac diagram, but it is not an absolute requirement across angiosperm diversity. The central question remains whether sperm are delivered to appropriate female targets in a way that supports the two necessary fusions.

After reception, the male gametes are no longer simply internal cargo in a growing vegetative cell. The next developmental checkpoint requires them to interact with two distinct female cells. The transport system has brought the genomes into the correct local environment; it has not itself joined the membranes or nuclei. The change from delivery to fusion is the transition from spatial navigation to the formation of new reproductive lineages.

11. Two distinct gamete fusions

When the two sperm cells have been released from a pollen tube, successful reproduction requires more than their physical proximity to the egg and central cell. Each sperm must make an appropriate contact, achieve fusion of the plasma membranes and contribute its nucleus to the relevant female cell. The joining of cellular contents through membrane fusion is often termed plasmogamy. The fusion of the parental nuclei is termed karyogamy. Although these processes are closely connected in normal fertilization, they are conceptually distinct. A genetic defect could interfere with one stage even if the previous stage had occurred.

Cell membranes normally maintain boundaries between cytoplasms. Bringing two lipid bilayers close together does not ordinarily cause them to merge efficiently, because membrane hydration, molecular organization and the physical energy barriers to bilayer rearrangement oppose spontaneous fusion. Specialized cellular machinery can promote the transition from separate membranes to a shared boundary. In sexual reproduction the machinery must also be regulated so that a cell does not fuse indiscriminately with every neighboring cell. The sperm cells encounter several different female structures after release, but the normal reproductive outcome requires fusion with the two gametic cells.

Evidence from Arabidopsis has established an important role for the sperm-associated protein HAP2, also known as GCS1, in gamete fusion. The name HAP2 reflects a hapless mutant identified through genetic studies, while GCS1 refers to expression in the male generative lineage. A sperm lacking functional HAP2 can be transported to an ovule and released without completing the usual fusion events. Thus HAP2 function can be distinguished experimentally from the growth of the vegetative tube. HAP2-related fusion proteins occur across diverse eukaryotic lineages, although the details of their roles and molecular partners differ. They should not be assumed to be the universal fusion machinery of every animal or plant gamete.

The egg also participates actively in activating sperm for fusion. In Arabidopsis, work by Sprunck and colleagues identified small cysteine-rich proteins of the EC1 family stored in the egg. Release of these proteins during reproductive interactions promotes redistribution of HAP2/GCS1 toward the sperm surface. The mechanistic significance is that the sperm's capacity to fuse can be regulated by localization of a protein rather than by its mere presence in the cell. A sperm may synthesize a relevant protein during gametophyte development but require a subsequent signal to position it appropriately for action.

This example connects fertilization with the general cell-biological principle that the location of a protein can govern its function. A membrane fusogen sequestered in an intracellular compartment is not positioned to act on the interface between two gametes. Redistribution to the plasma membrane can permit participation in fusion at the appropriate stage. Experimental detection of HAP2 protein in a mutant or treated sperm does not necessarily demonstrate that the protein is functional or located correctly. A researcher may need to examine both protein abundance and spatial distribution, as well as the actual fusion outcome.

The two male gametes are generally similar in genetic origin, yet one participates in egg fertilization and the other in central-cell fertilization. In Arabidopsis there is no simple obligatory assignment in which the first sperm formed during development must always fertilize a particular female cell. Other flowering plants exhibit interesting differences between sperm cells and preferences in their interactions with the female gametes. The examination-level principle is to avoid assuming a fixed correspondence unless the example species and experimental evidence establish one. The standard two-fusion outcome does not require a universal genetically determined distinction between an egg-fertilizing sperm and an endosperm-fertilizing sperm.

The fusion with the egg is called syngamy in the standard botanical account of double fertilization. Once the sperm and egg have contributed their nuclear genomes, the resulting zygote possesses a new genetic combination. It may subsequently undergo changes in polarity, cell cycle activity and gene expression. Those processes will establish the initial embryo. Their details are not required to identify the fusion itself. An egg that has received a paternal genome is not yet an organized multicellular embryo, just as a fertilized animal egg is not yet a gastrula.

The second fusion involves the central cell. In the common female gametophyte organization, the central cell contains two maternal polar nuclei or a nucleus derived from their fusion. The sperm contributes a third haploid chromosome set to the primary endosperm nucleus. The participation of three nuclear contributions is often called triple fusion. If the two maternal polar nuclei have already fused, the sperm may fuse with a single diploid secondary nucleus rather than with three physically separate nuclei simultaneously. The name triple fusion therefore refers to the three haploid contributions in the standard model, not an invariant observation of three nuclei joining at exactly the same moment.

The term double fertilization refers to the pair of gametic fusions, not to the number of nuclear contributions in the second event. Syngamy and triple fusion are commonly described as the two components of double fertilization in the Polygonum-type embryo sac. The distinction between events and chromosome sets is useful in examinations because statements that confuse them can sound plausible. Three chromosome sets in the primary endosperm nucleus do not imply three fertilization events. Two sperm are normally delivered by one tube, and each fertilizes a different female gametic cell.

The two fusions do not have to be understood as a universally rigid sequence in which one always precedes the other by a prescribed interval. Their relative timing can vary, and experimental measurements depend on the definitions used for successful fusion. A fluorescent sperm marker might reveal entry into a female cell before nuclear fusion is complete. Another marker might report the onset of development rather than the membrane event. When a question supplies specific time points, it is important to identify which stage each marker measures. The defining feature of double fertilization is the successful participation of the two sperm in the two female-cell fusions, rather than a universal timing schedule.

The developmental roles of the products are different because the recipient cells were different before fertilization. An egg is organized to initiate an embryo, whereas the central cell is organized to initiate the endosperm. Nuclear contributions affect the subsequent program, but the initial cellular environment is also significant. This relationship illustrates a principle encountered in developmental biology: identical or closely related genomes can participate in different cellular outcomes depending on cytoplasmic organization and gene regulation. Double fertilization is thus an example of two coordinated transitions in two pre-existing cell types, not merely the production of nuclei with different ploidies.

12. Chromosome bookkeeping and parental genome dosage

Ploidy describes the number of complete chromosome sets in a nucleus. It should not be confused with the amount of DNA present, which can change as chromosomes replicate. For example, a haploid sperm with a replicated genome would still be haploid even though its DNA content was greater than before replication. The notation n is commonly used for the gametic chromosome number and 2n for the chromosome number of an ordinary diploid sporophyte. In numerical exercises involving polyploids, the basic chromosome number x may be needed to distinguish the gametic chromosome count from the number of basic sets.

Begin with a diploid species whose somatic cells contain twenty-four chromosomes. Under the assumption of ordinary reductional meiosis and normal gametophyte development, a sperm nucleus contains twelve chromosomes. An egg nucleus also contains twelve chromosomes. Fusion of these nuclei establishes a zygotic nucleus with twenty-four chromosomes. The two haploid maternal polar nuclei each contain twelve chromosomes, so the central cell contains a total of twenty-four maternal chromosomes before fertilization. Addition of twelve paternal chromosomes produces a primary endosperm nucleus with thirty-six chromosomes. The embryo and endosperm can therefore be distinguished by nuclear chromosome counts in this standard case.

The arithmetic follows directly from the cellular origins. Let m denote the chromosome number of a normally reduced gamete in the maternal plant and p denote the chromosome number of a normally reduced sperm in the paternal plant. If the central cell contains two maternal nuclear contributions, each with m chromosomes, the zygote has m plus p chromosomes and the primary endosperm nucleus has twice m plus p chromosomes. For a conventional within-species diploid cross with equal gametic chromosome numbers, m equals p equals n. The equations reduce to 2n and 3n. A numerical result obtained from these expressions is conditional on the assumed origin and number of the nuclei.

The embryo contains one maternal and one paternal set, so the usual parental genome dosage ratio is one to one. The endosperm contains two maternal sets and one paternal set, giving a dosage ratio of two to one. In terms of sets, the maternal fraction is two thirds in the primary endosperm nucleus and one half in the zygote. These ratios describe genomic contributions, not the fractions of transcripts or proteins derived from each parent. Gene expression can depend on allele-specific regulation, dosage compensation or genomic imprinting. A maternal fraction of two thirds of nuclear chromosome sets does not require that two thirds of every transcript in the endosperm be maternal.

The distinction between genome dosage and cell identity can be investigated using a genetic cross in which the maternal plant and paternal plant are homozygous for different alleles of a nuclear marker. Denote the maternal allele as A and the paternal allele as a. Under normal sexual reproduction in the standard embryo sac, the embryo carries A and a, while the primary endosperm nucleus carries A, A and a. A maternal seed coat cell carries A and A without a paternal contribution. These expected nuclear genotypes are useful when interpreting allele dosage data from separately sampled seed tissues. They do not predict the dominant visible phenotype without additional information about allele expression and developmental context.

The two maternal polar nuclei in the common monosporic embryo sac descend from the same functional megaspore through mitosis. They therefore normally share its haploid nuclear genotype. Consider a heterozygous maternal plant carrying A and a. In one ordinary embryo sac derived from an A-bearing megaspore, the egg and both polar nuclear contributions carry A at the marker. In another sac derived from an a-bearing megaspore, the corresponding nuclei carry a. The alleles of the two polar nuclei are not independently sampled from the maternal plant's diploid genotype during the final fertilization event. Their common mitotic ancestry accounts for their correlation.

Suppose that the paternal plant is homozygous a and the maternal plant is heterozygous A/a at this marker. Assume equal production and survival of the two maternal megaspore genotypes and ordinary monosporic development. Half the embryo sacs are expected to contribute A and half a. The embryos would then occur in two expected classes, A/a and a/a, in equal proportions. The endosperm classes would be A/A/a and a/a/a, also in equal proportions. A class A/a/a, with only one maternal A, is not expected under these assumptions because the maternal contributions come from the same megaspore. This deduction concerns the marker and does not imply that every maternal chromosome segment is genetically unchanged through the earlier meiotic recombination process.

Ploidy calculations also expose why the seed coat is different from the other seed tissues. A seed coat originates from integuments of the maternal sporophyte. It therefore carries the nuclear genotype of the maternal plant, whether that plant is homozygous or heterozygous at the marker. Fertilization can trigger its development without adding paternal nuclear chromosomes to its cells. A tissue may respond to a signal generated after fertilization without itself being a product of gamete fusion. This distinction will be important when studying how embryo, endosperm and maternal tissues coordinate later seed development.

Polyploid crosses provide useful conditional examples of altered parental dosage. Suppose a tetraploid maternal sporophyte has four basic chromosome sets and produces reduced gametes with two basic sets. A diploid pollen parent produces sperm with one basic set. If the maternal embryo sac has two polar contributions of two sets each, the zygote has three basic sets and the primary endosperm nucleus has five. The parental ratios are two maternal to one paternal in the embryo and four maternal to one paternal in the endosperm. The reverse cross, with a diploid female and tetraploid male, also produces a three-set embryo but gives a four-set primary endosperm nucleus with two maternal and two paternal sets. The two reciprocal crosses produce embryos of the same total ploidy under these assumptions, yet their endosperm dosage patterns differ.

This reasoning is relevant because altered parental genome proportions may affect endosperm development and seed viability. The magnitude and direction of effects depend on species, genotypes and developmental regulation. The arithmetic does not demonstrate that a particular cross will necessarily fail. It identifies a genomic difference that could help explain an observed difference in seed development. A student should not convert a dosage calculation into an unconditional prediction of lethality. The effects of imprinting and endosperm regulation are developed more fully in the appropriate neighboring chapters.

Endosperm ploidy is not universally triploid. The common Polygonum type is an important default when a question explicitly specifies an ordinary diploid angiosperm with two maternal polar nuclei, but a different embryo-sac type can alter the outcome. In Nuphar polysepala, for example, a female gametophyte with a single haploid central-cell nucleus has been associated with diploid biparental endosperm. Fertilization adds one paternal set to the single maternal set. The egg still receives its own sperm and establishes a diploid zygote. The two resulting nuclei can thus have the same total number of chromosome sets while belonging to developmentally distinct lineages.

The Nuphar example shows why developmental origin is a more general definition of endosperm than an assumed chromosome count. A diploid endosperm is still an endosperm when its origin and developmental role establish that identity. Similarly, a nucleus containing three sets of chromosomes is not necessarily an endosperm if it occurs in some unrelated polyploid tissue. The appropriate order of reasoning is to identify the cell and the fusion event, determine the contributing chromosome complements, and then calculate the product. Applying the number three before examining the underlying organization risks a correct-looking answer for the wrong biological reason.

13. Preventing redundant fertilization while retaining a recovery route

When a pistil receives a large number of pollen grains, many tubes may approach the ovules. A normal ovule usually requires only one successfully discharging tube, because one male gametophyte supplies both sperm. Unrestricted delivery of additional sperm could waste reproductive resources and create opportunities for abnormal extra fusion. However, excluding every further tube immediately after the first arrives would be disadvantageous if that first tube failed to release functional sperm. The reproductive system must therefore reconcile avoidance of unnecessary multiple arrivals with the possibility of recovering from an unsuccessful first attempt.

Polytubey means entry or targeting of an ovule by multiple pollen tubes, according to the experimental definition being used. Polyspermy refers to the fusion of more than one sperm with a particular female gamete. The terms describe events at different levels. Two tubes may enter an ovule without any female gamete undergoing polyspermy, for example when the first tube delivers defective sperm that do not fuse. Conversely, abnormal fusion of both sperm from a single tube with one female target would constitute a form of polyspermy without requiring multiple tubes. Distinguishing these cases is essential when interpreting an image showing several tube tracks.

Normal fertilization can change the signaling state of the ovule. The receptive synergid associated with the first tube is no longer available in its previous condition. The remaining synergid may initially retain the capacity to attract another tube, particularly if fertilization has failed. Successful fertilization can promote changes that diminish further attraction, including elimination of the persistent synergid and alterations in the local communication environment. These processes help prevent redundant targeting while allowing a limited recovery opportunity in appropriate circumstances.

Experiments in Arabidopsis have provided direct evidence for this principle. Studies reported in 2012 by Kasahara and colleagues and by Beale and colleagues examined pollen tubes carrying defects that compromise sperm fertility. They observed that an ovule receiving an unsuccessful first tube can accept an additional tube, and that successful fertilization by functional sperm can restore reproductive success in at least some cases. The findings argue against an explanation in which the arrival of the first tube alone is always sufficient to permanently shut down attraction. The status of gamete fusion contributes to the transition from a receptive ovule to one that resists additional targeting.

A follow-up experimental question is whether the egg and central cell contribute separately to that transition. If fertilization of either cell fails while the other succeeds, the ovule may not fully suppress further attraction. Evidence from Arabidopsis supports contributions from both female gametic cells to the regulation of additional tube targeting. This does not mean that every instance of partial fertilization necessarily results in successful recovery. The remaining synergid must be functional long enough, additional pollen tubes must be available, and the particular unfertilized gamete must still be able to complete fertilization. The recovery mechanism has constraints and is not equivalent to unlimited repeated attempts.

A useful experimental design is mixed pollination using pollen types carrying distinguishable fluorescent markers. A first population may contain a defined fusion-defective genotype, while a second population supplies normal sperm. Imaging can determine whether the second population targets ovules previously visited by the first. To demonstrate actual recovery, investigators must also establish that a previously unfertilized female cell subsequently acquires the appropriate paternal contribution. Counting two fluorescent tube traces is evidence of multiple targeting but not sufficient evidence of successful rescue.

Timing and pollen availability can complicate interpretation. If the second pollen population is applied too late, the female gametophyte may lose competence or the remaining synergid may degenerate. If too few viable grains are present, the absence of an additional tube does not prove a complete block to attraction. A comparison should therefore use equivalent observation intervals, suitable pollen numbers and a reference population of normally fertilized ovules. Differences in the rates of pollen germination and tube extension can also affect which population reaches a particular ovule first. A well-designed study establishes the order of arrivals rather than inferring it solely from the order of pollen application.

Blocks to additional targeting and mechanisms limiting extra sperm fusion should not be treated as an exact plant equivalent of the classical fast electrical and slow cortical blocks described in some animals. Animal and flowering-plant reproductive architectures differ. Angiosperm sperm travel within a tube, and the ovule regulates access partly by modifying the attraction and reception environment. In addition, the individual female gametes have mechanisms that influence their capacity to fuse again, and the efficiency of those mechanisms can differ between egg and central cell in particular experimental contexts. The shared functional principle is that normal reproduction must regulate the number and identity of gametic contributions; the detailed solutions are not interchangeable across organisms.

Consider an egg that is normally fertilized but a central cell that remains unfertilized because the second sperm is defective. The embryo lineage has received its paternal genome, but the usual fertilization-derived endosperm lineage has not been established. If the ovule remains receptive to a second functional tube, central-cell fertilization might be rescued under favorable conditions. This hypothetical outcome illustrates the developmental advantage of retaining some responsiveness after incomplete fertilization. It does not imply that the already fertilized egg must accept another sperm, or that every incomplete fertilization will be repaired.

The reverse situation is also instructive. A central cell may receive its paternal contribution while the egg fails to fuse with a sperm. The endosperm lineage may initiate some developmental changes, yet the ordinary sexual embryo lineage is missing. Observing a change in the central cell or enlargement of surrounding maternal tissue is not sufficient evidence that the egg was fertilized. To classify a reproductive outcome, each female target must be assessed separately. The two events are coordinated but experimentally separable, and the phenotypes of their selective failure reveal the necessity of the two components of double fertilization.

14. Evolutionary and developmental significance

Flowering plants belong to the seed plants, but seed plants do not all produce the same type of fertilization-derived nutritive tissue. In many gymnosperms, the developing embryo receives support from the maternal female gametophyte, which is generally haploid in a conventional diploid life cycle. That tissue is established before fertilization and does not require the same biparental origin as the typical angiosperm endosperm. Although some gymnosperms possess more complex nuclear fusion events and some have motile sperm, the ordinary angiosperm combination of embryo formation and fertilization-derived endosperm is a distinctive developmental arrangement.

Cycads and Ginkgo retain motile sperm, although pollen-related structures are still involved in reproduction. Other seed plants use pollen tubes in ways that avoid long-distance independent sperm motility. These comparisons demonstrate that the presence of a pollen tube and the occurrence of conventional angiosperm double fertilization are separate evolutionary characters. One should not infer double fertilization solely from the presence of pollen or a tube, or assume that all seed plants lack motile sperm. Similarly, the absence of long-distance swimming in ordinary angiosperm sperm does not mean that sperm cells are incapable of any local repositioning after release.

Double fertilization is often interpreted as a mechanism that helps coordinate initiation of the embryo and its support tissue. By linking development of the endosperm to a reproductive event, the plant can associate investment in this tissue with the formation of a new sexual generation. This is a reasonable functional interpretation but must be distinguished from a fully demonstrated historical explanation of the origin of the mechanism. Evolutionary scenarios concerning the ancestry of the endosperm and the sequence of changes that produced it have been proposed. The established observation is that the two fertilization products are coordinated in flowering-plant reproduction; the precise historical pathway is not directly established simply by identifying their present functions.

The dosage of maternal and paternal genomes also differs between the two products in the common triploid-endosperm arrangement. This difference offers a biological context for studying regulation of gene expression and parental contributions to seed development. Genomic imprinting, for example, can cause the activity of particular alleles to depend on parental origin. The occurrence of imprinting does not follow automatically for every gene merely because the tissue has a two-to-one genomic ratio. Its identification requires evidence that the same allele behaves differently according to its maternal or paternal transmission. Thus ploidy creates a genomic context, whereas imprinting concerns a particular regulatory phenomenon.

The evolution of an enclosed ovule and directional delivery of sperm also introduces opportunities for reproductive selection before fertilization. Pollen may be rejected at the stigma, tubes may be restricted by the transmitting tissues, or recognition of local signals may favor particular compatible male gametophytes. These interactions can affect reproductive isolation and the genetic composition of offspring. They are relevant to breeding because failure of a cross can originate from a barrier well before embryo development. At the same time, successful pollen-tube guidance and fertilization do not guarantee that an interspecific or interploidy seed will develop normally. Later incompatibilities may be independent of the earlier transport route.

Comparison with animals highlights both shared and different solutions. In some animal species, sperm move toward eggs using their own motility, and molecular interactions with egg-associated structures regulate recognition and fusion. In flowering plants, pollen has an additional vegetative growth apparatus that performs delivery and responds to female-associated cues. Both systems require regulation of cellular contact, membrane fusion and parental nuclear contribution. Both can show species-related recognition preferences and mechanisms that reduce abnormal additional fertilization. But the pollen tube is not simply an elongated animal sperm, and the endosperm is not equivalent to an animal placenta or a second germ layer.

A central theme across this unit is the division of complex development into experimentally distinguishable transitions. The animal chapters followed gamete production, recognition, fertilization, cleavage and spatial organization of early embryos. The plant chapters follow male and female gametophyte development, pollen delivery, fertilization and the establishment of embryo and endosperm lineages. These are parallel developmental tracks shaped by distinct organismal architectures. The useful connections are mechanistic principles such as cell polarity, competence, signaling, fusion and developmental state, rather than forced equivalences between structures that have different origins.

A practical implication of this framework is that the absence of seeds should be investigated as a multistage phenotype. If no pollen reaches the stigma, a delivery problem precedes every intracellular fertilization mechanism. If compatible pollen germinates but tubes rupture in the style, the problem is likely to involve growth or integrity. If tubes reach the micropyle but do not discharge, reception is a candidate. If sperm are discharged but paternal nuclear contributions are absent, the relevant failure is later. If the two fusions occur but seeds abort, attention shifts toward embryo, endosperm and maternal tissue development. Each transition defines a different class of experiments.

At the end of double fertilization, the zygote and primary endosperm nucleus represent the starting points of two connected developmental programs. The zygote must establish polarity and generate the cell populations that form the embryo. The endosperm must undergo its own development and interact with the embryo and surrounding maternal tissues. The chapter that follows examines the first of these programs: how a newly formed plant zygote divides, acquires an apical–basal organization and begins to establish the tissue pattern of a new sporophyte.
