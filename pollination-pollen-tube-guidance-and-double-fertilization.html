1. Two journeys inside one reproductive cycle

A flowering plant faces an unusual problem when it reproduces sexually. Its male and female gametes are produced in different structures, and the egg lies deep inside an ovule enclosed by the ovary. Unlike a typical motile animal sperm, an angiosperm sperm cell cannot swim independently through a surrounding liquid to reach the egg. The plant therefore separates the task of long-distance transport from the task of gamete fusion. A pollen grain travels between floral structures, and a living extension of the pollen grain subsequently grows through maternal tissues. Only at the end of this journey are the two sperm cells delivered to the female gametophyte. Understanding this division of labour explains why successful pollination is necessary in conventional sexual reproduction but is not, by itself, proof that fertilization has occurred.

The conspicuous parts of a flower should not obscure the less visible reproductive compartments. The anther produces pollen, while the pistil contains the stigma, style and ovary in a generalized flower. The stigma presents the surface on which compatible pollen can establish contact. The style commonly separates the stigma from the ovary and provides a route through which pollen tubes grow; some species have a very short style or a different arrangement. The ovary contains ovules attached through their funiculi. Each ovule encloses a female gametophyte, also called the embryo sac in flowering plants. The ovule is therefore not an egg, and the ovary is not a single embryo sac. A flower may contain many ovules, each with its own potential reproductive outcome.

Consider the route from the perspective of a pollen grain. Its first obstacle is reaching a suitable stigma. Its next obstacle is remaining viable and receiving sufficient water to resume growth. After a tube emerges, it must advance through a route containing walls, extracellular material and other cells. At the ovary, the tube must find an individual receptive ovule rather than simply stop in the general vicinity of female tissue. It then needs to enter a suitable region of the ovule and communicate with the embryo sac so that sperm delivery occurs at the appropriate time. Finally, the two sperm cells must establish productive interactions with two different female cells. Each obstacle is a distinct biological checkpoint, so the failure of any checkpoint may lead to infertility even though the previous steps appear normal.

The final two fusion events are responsible for the name double fertilization. One sperm fuses with the egg to establish the zygote, which initiates the embryonic lineage. The other fuses with the central cell to establish the primary endosperm nucleus and endosperm lineage. In the common Polygonum-type female gametophyte of a diploid flowering plant, the zygotic nucleus receives one maternal and one paternal chromosome set, whereas the primary endosperm nucleus receives two maternal sets and one paternal set. This is a developmental distinction, not merely a calculation of chromosome number. The two products have different identities and tasks even when an unusual species changes their chromosome-set proportions. The embryo becomes the new plant sporophyte; the endosperm generally participates in its nutritional and developmental support.

An ovule that has undergone the two fusions is not instantly a mature seed. It has entered a new developmental state in which the embryo and endosperm begin to develop and maternal integuments participate in seed coat formation. Similarly, the fruit usually develops from ovarian tissue, sometimes with additional floral contributions. The genetic identity of a tissue must be traced to its cellular origin, not inferred from its location in the seed or fruit. An embryo, endosperm and seed coat may be physically adjacent but possess different parental contributions and developmental histories. The present chapter ends at the establishment of these lineages; the patterning of the embryo and the later interaction of seed compartments belong to the following chapters.

The order of events also reveals why plant and animal reproductive terminology must be used carefully. A pollen tube is a cellular growth structure, not a sperm flagellum. The central cell is an additional fertilization target, not a second egg. The embryo sac is a gametophyte, not a mature embryo. When the student can locate each object in the appropriate generation and identify its function, the rest of the mechanism becomes a connected explanation rather than a collection of names.

2. Discovering the second fertilization

The observation that pollen is necessary for seed formation does not reveal what happens to the pollen inside the pistil. Pollen is visible at the floral surface, but its relationship to the deeply enclosed female gametophyte is difficult to establish without examining internal structures. Early studies of plant sexuality established the importance of pollen and the formation of offspring from crosses. Yet an explanation of inheritance through sexual reproduction still required evidence that the contents of pollen actually reached and interacted with the female reproductive cells. The discovery of a pollen tube provided an anatomical solution to the problem of distance.

The pollen tube was initially significant because it linked two locations that could not communicate by independent sperm swimming in flowering plants. Once the tube was recognized as an extension of the pollen grain, the next question was what its contents did after reaching the ovule. Improved microscopy and the study of stained serial sections permitted investigators to follow nuclei at successive stages. Such sections can reveal the spatial positions of cells and nuclei, although an individual fixed preparation cannot establish the full temporal order by itself. By comparing preparations from different developmental stages, investigators reconstructed a plausible sequence. This distinction between observing a structure and inferring a process remains important when interpreting histological evidence.

In 1898 Sergius Nawaschin reported the two fertilization events in flowering plants, studying lilies and fritillaries. Léon Guignard independently documented double fertilization in the following period. Their observations challenged the idea that only one male nucleus had an essential reproductive role while the remaining male nucleus merely degenerated. Instead, the second male nucleus participated in a distinct fusion involving the central region of the female gametophyte. The studies supplied a developmental origin for the endosperm that differed from the origin of the embryo. Subsequent examination of other angiosperms showed that the phenomenon was not restricted to these first examples. The precise organization of the female gametophyte and the resulting endosperm can nevertheless vary among lineages.

Classical cytology provides a powerful way to test the two-fusion model. If only the egg were fertilized, one would expect a paternal nuclear contribution in the zygote but not in the fertilization-derived endosperm nucleus. If the second sperm instead entered the central cell, the central cell should acquire paternal chromosomes as well. This prediction can be investigated using chromosome counts and distinguishable parental genetic markers. The important comparison is between the state of the egg and central cell before the pollen tube reaches them and their state after fertilization. The presence of the paternal genome in both resulting lineages supports the two-fusion interpretation. It does not imply that every gene contributed by the two parents is expressed equally in each lineage.

Modern imaging allows scientists to distinguish intermediate events that were difficult to resolve in fixed sections. Fluorescent reporters can mark pollen tubes, sperm nuclei or female cell boundaries. A record of the same ovule at successive times can establish whether tube entry, discharge, sperm relocation and fusion occur in the predicted sequence. A fluorescent sperm nucleus close to the egg is not necessarily inside the egg; that inference requires spatial resolution and, preferably, independent indicators of membrane or nuclear fusion. Imaging therefore complements genetic evidence rather than replacing it. A mutation that prevents membrane fusion despite apparently normal delivery provides a particularly useful way to separate transport from fertilization itself.

The history is best understood as a progression from dependence to location, from location to nuclear contributions, and then from nuclear contributions to causal cell biology. Classical observations showed that the two fusions happen. Experimental analysis now asks why the tube reaches a particular ovule, how it knows when to stop growing, and which proteins make the gametes competent to fuse. These are separate questions. A molecule required for attracting pollen tubes does not necessarily participate in membrane fusion; an egg protein required for sperm activation need not guide the tube. The distinction is central to interpreting modern developmental mutants.

3. The cells that must meet

Before examining pollen recognition, it is useful to establish the genetic identity of the cells involved. The conspicuous flowering plant is the sporophyte. In an ordinary diploid species, its somatic cells possess two sets of chromosomes. Meiosis in reproductive tissues produces haploid spores. The spores subsequently divide by mitosis to form reduced gametophytes. The male gametophyte is the pollen grain and its tube, whereas the female gametophyte develops inside the ovule. These gametophytes are not produced by the direct fusion of gametes, and the pollen grain is not itself a single gamete.

A mature pollen grain has a vegetative component and a generative lineage. The vegetative cell supplies the cytoplasm that grows into the tube and maintains its polarized growth. The generative cell divides mitotically to produce two sperm cells. Depending on the species, this division is completed before pollen is released from the anther or after germination within the growing tube. Thus a two-celled pollen grain can still accomplish double fertilization if its generative cell divides at the appropriate later time. A three-celled pollen grain contains the vegetative cell and the two sperm cells already present. The occurrence of mitosis within a haploid lineage explains why sperm are haploid without requiring any additional reduction division at the point of fertilization.

The two sperm cells of a pollen grain are normally mitotic descendants of a single generative cell. They therefore usually possess the same haploid nuclear genotype, barring mutation or other unusual changes. Nevertheless, they can participate in two developmentally different outcomes. The recipient cytoplasms, nuclear configurations and regulatory states differ between the egg and central cell. Genetic similarity between the sperm does not demand that their products acquire identical developmental fates. The fate of a fertilization product is established through the interaction of the genomes and the cellular context into which the sperm enters.

In the common Polygonum-type embryo sac, an egg cell and two synergid cells are located at the micropylar end. The micropyle is the passage through the ovule integuments toward the female gametophyte; it is not a membrane pore in the egg itself. The egg apparatus consists of the egg and the two synergids. Toward the opposite, chalazal end there are typically three antipodal cells, which may persist or degenerate depending on the species. The large central cell occupies much of the embryo sac and contains the two maternal polar nuclei or their fused derivative. A standard seven-celled, eight-nucleate description applies before fusion of the polar nuclei and before species-specific degeneration changes the counts.

The synergids are especially important because their anatomical location allows them to connect the external pollen-tube route with the internal gametes. Their micropylar region contains a specialized wall structure known as the filiform apparatus, which includes wall ingrowths that increase the area of interaction at the cell surface. This structure is associated with secretion and exchange and provides an interface for the approaching tube. The synergid is not fertilized in conventional double fertilization. Instead, its role as an accessory cell helps deliver the sperm toward the egg and central cell.

The pre-fertilization central cell deserves special attention. A cell containing two haploid nuclei contains two maternal chromosome-set equivalents even when the nuclei are physically separate. It is imprecise to describe it as necessarily containing one diploid nucleus at every developmental moment. In species where the polar nuclei fuse before fertilization, a secondary nucleus with two maternal sets is present; in other developmental contexts the fusion may be completed in association with fertilization. A chromosome bookkeeping exercise can group the two maternal sets together, but a cytological drawing should show their actual nuclear organization in the example under consideration.

These distinctions clarify the roles of both generations in the same flower. The stigma, style, ovule integuments and nucellus are maternal sporophytic tissues. The egg, synergids, central cell and pollen tube belong to their respective gametophytes. After fertilization, the embryo and endosperm have new nuclear contributions, but the integuments do not acquire paternal chromosomes simply because they surround the products. When interpreting a gene-expression defect or a genetic cross, the identity of the tissue in which the gene acts is often as important as its allele. A mutation expressed in a stigma can affect acceptance of pollen irrespective of the genotype inside the sperm, whereas a mutation expressed only in the sperm can permit the entire route to succeed and still prevent fertilization.

4. Pollination and the decision to accept pollen

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to a stigma. In self-pollination, pollen originates from the same flower or another flower on the same plant, depending on the terminology used for the particular case. Autogamy denotes transfer within the same flower, geitonogamy denotes transfer between flowers of the same individual, and xenogamy denotes transfer between different individuals. Geitonogamy can involve the same physical vectors as cross-pollination between plants, but genetically it is selfing. These distinctions concern the origin and route of pollen. They do not establish whether sperm and egg have actually fused.

A grain landing on the stigma faces a change from dispersal conditions to a physiological environment appropriate for growth. The surface must support attachment and, in the case of dry stigmas, regulated water transfer. Wet stigmas have extracellular secretions that can support pollen hydration and interactions with the surface. These categories describe surface characteristics rather than a universal molecular mechanism. Different flowering plants show different degrees of selectivity, hydration control and extracellular secretion. It is therefore misleading to extrapolate the behavior of a single experimental species to all flowering plants.

Compatibility is a property of an interaction between pollen and pistil. A grain may be perfectly viable in a suitable medium yet fail to hydrate or grow on a particular stigma. Conversely, a compatible genetic combination does not guarantee success if the grain is damaged or the pistil is unreceptive. An experimental distinction between incompatibility and general loss of viability requires a positive control. If a pollen sample fails on one pistil but grows normally on another suitable pistil, general pollen death becomes a less plausible explanation. If it fails everywhere, incompatibility is not established by that evidence.

Self-incompatibility is a genetically controlled mechanism that can limit self-fertilization or mating between plants sharing relevant recognition specificities. It should be distinguished from separation of male and female maturation times, separation of sexual organs, physical separation of sexes and genetic male sterility. Those conditions may reduce selfing without requiring an allele-specific rejection response. Self-incompatible pollen can be alive, hydrated in other conditions and capable of fertilizing a suitable partner. The incompatibility response acts before successful fertilization and does not imply that a resulting embryo was rejected after formation.

A well studied example occurs in Brassica, where a pollen-coat ligand called SCR or SP11 participates in specificity and a stigma-localized receptor kinase called SRK participates in recognition. When their relevant S specificities match, the interaction initiates a rejection response in the stigma papilla. This receptor-ligand example is unusual to a student accustomed to thinking of recognition as a mechanism that always promotes adhesion or fusion. Here, specific recognition triggers rejection. A mismatch may permit the normal compatible pathway, provided that other requirements for hydration and growth are satisfied. The binding reaction is a molecular event, whereas the failure of hydration or penetration is a later physiological consequence.

In sporophytic self-incompatibility, the specificity expressed by a pollen grain is determined by the diploid pollen-producing plant. The surrounding sporophytic anther tissues contribute to the pollen coat, helping explain why the specificity displayed at the pollen surface need not be predictable from the haploid sperm genotype alone. Dominance relationships among S alleles can further affect the phenotype. In gametophytic self-incompatibility, by contrast, the pollen's own haploid S genotype determines its specificity. One well-known gametophytic system involves S-RNases in the pistil and selective rejection of incompatible pollen tubes. Different families employ distinct molecular systems, so S-RNase should not be assumed to mediate the Brassica receptor response.

Consider a simplified gametophytic incompatibility cross in which an S1S2 pistil receives equal numbers of viable S1 and S3 pollen grains from an S1S3 donor. If both pistil alleles are expressed and sharing either specificity causes rejection, the S1 grains are rejected while the S3 grains are accepted. Under equal segregation and viability, half of the grains would be genetically compatible. This prediction concerns the acceptance step only. It does not predict exactly half as many seeds as a fully compatible cross, because many pollen grains may compete for a limited number of ovules and additional failures may occur downstream. A change in pollen density or ovule number could alter the seed outcome without changing the incompatibility rule.

In a sporophytic system the preceding calculation cannot simply be reused. If both S1 and S3 specificities are displayed by each pollen grain and either shared specificity is sufficient to cause rejection, the S1 match could lead to rejection of the entire cohort on an S1S2 pistil. In systems with dominance, a different displayed specificity could produce a different outcome. The question must specify which system operates, which allele products are expressed and the relevant recognition rule. Many examination distractors are constructed by substituting the allele segregation of the haploid gametes for the phenotype of the pollen coat.

Once accepted, the pollen grain absorbs water, resumes metabolic activity and establishes a site from which polarized extension can occur. The plant has not yet achieved fertilization; it has selected a living male gametophyte that is permitted to attempt the next stage. The transition from surface acceptance to tube initiation is a natural bridge between genetics, cell signaling and the mechanics of cellular growth.

5. Hydration, germination and the establishment of polarity

A mature pollen grain can survive a period of transport during which growth is reduced or suspended. Many species disperse pollen in a relatively dehydrated state, although the degree of dehydration varies widely. When a compatible grain contacts a receptive stigma, water enters the pollen, allowing its cytoplasm to regain a condition that supports active metabolism. The transition is more complex than swelling a dry particle. The grain must maintain membrane integrity, regulate the movement of solutes and water, activate enzyme systems, and begin the synthesis and trafficking of wall material. A viable grain that fails to establish this coordination cannot develop a productive tube.

The pollen wall offers both protection and an opportunity for emergence. The outer exine contains resistant sporopollenin and the inner intine contains polysaccharide-rich material. Apertures in many pollen grains are regions where the exine is reduced or modified. They provide plausible sites for tube emergence because extension through the wall is facilitated there. Aperture pattern varies across taxa, and some pollen grains can germinate from regions that do not conform to a simple aperture rule. The important principle is that the mechanical organization of a wall influences where a living cell can extend, but it does not by itself determine the entire developmental program.

Germination begins when a restricted portion of the vegetative cell becomes an active site of extension. A growing protrusion receives new membrane and wall materials, and the rest of the grain remains comparatively stable. This is an example of cell polarity: the distribution of activities and structures differs between regions of the same cell. The growing surface will become the tube apex, while the region behind the apex becomes the tube shank. The pollen tube is therefore not a chain of cells formed by repeated cell divisions. It is a greatly elongated part of a single vegetative cell, containing cytoplasm and a lumen continuous with the pollen grain until regions are partitioned by callose plugs.

Pollen germination can be examined outside the pistil in a suitable medium, but a tube growing in such a medium has not necessarily acquired all properties required for fertilization. Sugars provide substrates for metabolism and influence the water potential of the environment. Calcium and other ions participate in the regulation of growth and the properties of the cell wall. Boron is important in many pollen germination media, in part because borate-mediated crosslinking contributes to the organization of wall polysaccharides. Nutritional requirements and optimal concentrations differ among species. It would be incorrect to infer that a single published medium reproduces the exact conditions of every stigma or style.

Water movement illustrates the need to balance environmental support and mechanical stability. For a simplified plant cell, water potential can be expressed as the sum of solute potential and pressure potential when gravitational and matric terms are negligible. A higher concentration of dissolved substances generally makes solute potential more negative, whereas positive turgor raises pressure potential. Water tends to move from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential across a sufficiently permeable boundary. As water enters and the cell expands, pressure may rise. This pressure can supply a physical driving force for extension, provided that the wall yields in the intended region.

An excessively dilute external medium may allow rapid water uptake before the growing cell can adequately manage its wall and membrane. A highly concentrated external medium can reduce water uptake or even cause water loss. The resulting inhibition of growth is not evidence of an incompatibility response unless the experimental design independently establishes the specific recognition event. A chemical treatment can prevent germination by altering physical conditions that all pollen needs, without interacting with an S-locus receptor. Such a result helps explain why germination experiments should distinguish the osmotic properties of the medium from the effects of individual nutrients or signaling molecules.

The location of the protrusion can be studied by observing the same grain during germination. An aperture may exist before hydration, while the concentration of growth-related activity at a particular location appears later. A researcher can ask whether the location was predetermined by the wall, induced by the local environment, or stabilized through a combination of these influences. In an examination setting, the presence of a protrusion is evidence of local expansion, not automatically evidence that a particular signaling protein caused the polarization. Perturbing the protein and testing whether the polarity can be restored provides a stronger causal argument than its localization alone.

The vegetative nucleus and the generative lineage occupy the expanding cytoplasm as tube growth begins. A generative cell that has not yet divided can subsequently produce two sperm. These internal cells must remain viable during transport. Their movement should not be confused with an independent migration through the maternal style. The vegetative cell is the component in direct contact with the external environment, and its growing wall and membrane respond to the cues encountered along the route. The sperm are carried within this living vehicle. A defect in vegetative cell function can therefore prevent successful delivery even when the two sperm nuclei and their genetic contents are normal.

6. The mechanics and molecular organization of pollen-tube growth

Most plant cells enlarge over a substantial part of their surface, constrained by their walls and neighbouring cells. A pollen tube instead extends mainly at its rounded apex. The sides of the tube generally retain their established shape, while the apex is continuously renewed. This form of localized expansion is called tip growth. The result is an elongated cell that can advance through restricted spaces without making the entire tube wider at the same rate. Tip growth is also encountered in structures such as root hairs, but a pollen tube has the distinctive responsibility of transporting the male gametes through a reproductive pathway.

The mechanical problem of tip growth can be understood by considering a cell whose internal fluid exerts pressure on its wall. Turgor applies a force broadly rather than exclusively at one point. If all regions of the wall were equally deformable, pressure could cause expansion in unwanted regions. A pollen tube controls the spatial distribution of wall compliance so that the tip can yield while the shank resists excessive deformation. Turgor is necessary as a driving force, but a local difference in wall properties is essential for converting that force into a narrow, directed growth pattern. Raising pressure alone does not explain the location of extension.

Pectin is particularly important in this mechanical arrangement. It comprises a family of wall polysaccharides, including homogalacturonan with variable degrees of methyl esterification. Newly deposited pectin at the growing apex is commonly relatively methyl esterified. Pectin methylesterases remove methyl groups from suitable pectin chains. The resulting carboxyl groups can participate in calcium-mediated crosslinking when the distribution of de-esterified residues and local ionic conditions permit it. Such interactions can increase wall stiffness in appropriate regions. Pectin modification can also have other consequences, including altered susceptibility to pectin-degrading enzymes, so the statement that all de-esterification always stiffens every wall is too simple.

As the tip advances, material initially located at the apex becomes part of the subapical and then lateral wall. The distribution of wall polysaccharides and modifying enzymes changes along this axis. The wall behind the growing apex often contains more callose and cellulose than the softest apical region. Callose is a glucan containing predominantly beta-1,3 linkages, whereas cellulose is composed of beta-1,4-linked glucose chains. These polymers have different structures and properties. Callose deposition contributes to the organization of the tube shank and to the formation of plugs that partition older regions of the tube from the actively growing region.

Callose plugs do not form additional sperm cells or divide the vegetative nucleus. They create transverse barriers that help restrict the active cytoplasmic compartment as the tube extends. If the tube maintained the entire cytoplasm of an increasingly long cylinder in a fully active state, its transport and maintenance demands would increase considerably. Restricting the active region offers a way to maintain an efficient cytoplasmic domain near the growth front. The spacing and presence of plugs vary, and their disruption can alter growth and fertility without necessarily producing one identical phenotype in every species.

The increasing surface area of a growing tube also requires delivery of new plasma membrane. Secretory vesicles carry wall precursors and membrane components toward the growing region, where exocytosis contributes material to the surface. Actin filaments and myosin-dependent transport help distribute vesicles and other organelles through the cytoplasm. The actin cytoskeleton is not a rigid tube-shaped scaffold that simply pushes the apex forward. Its dynamic organization coordinates movement of material, secretion and the establishment of a polarized cytoplasmic domain. The effect of disturbing actin may therefore include failures of vesicle delivery, organelle positioning and directional growth.

Membrane addition must be balanced with retrieval and recycling. If exocytosis adds more membrane than is needed to accommodate surface expansion and no compensating retrieval occurs, membrane organization can be disturbed. Endocytosis recovers membrane and membrane-associated proteins and helps maintain appropriate surface composition. These trafficking processes are not merely housekeeping operations. They contribute to the distribution of receptors and signaling components that the tube uses to respond to its environment. A pollen tube that can produce wall polymers but cannot deliver them to the appropriate location will not achieve normal polarized extension.

A calcium-rich cytoplasmic domain is commonly observed near the growing tip. Calcium ions act as signaling molecules, influencing secretion, the cytoskeleton and other processes. Their concentration in the cytoplasm is maintained by regulated transport across membranes and by intracellular buffering and sequestration. An influx of calcium near the tip can help sustain a spatially restricted signal. The fact that calcium also contributes to extracellular pectin interactions does not mean that its intracellular signaling role and extracellular structural role are interchangeable. One concerns regulation of cellular machinery; the other concerns properties of the wall material.

Local pH also influences processes that determine growth. Proton transport can establish differences in hydrogen ion concentration between regions of the cytoplasm and external environment. Enzymes and cytoskeletal proteins respond to chemical conditions, so a change in proton distribution can alter the behavior of growth-related machinery. Reactive oxygen species can participate in signaling and wall-related processes at regulated levels but can damage the cell at excessive levels. The growth apex is thus a dynamic region where ionic signals, chemical reactions and material trafficking operate together. None of these signals is adequately described by a single universal rule such as more calcium always produces faster growth.

Members of the plant ROP family of small GTPases help organize polarized growth. A small GTPase can cycle between states associated with binding GTP and GDP, thereby regulating interactions with downstream proteins. Spatial restriction of active signaling allows growth-related processes to be concentrated near the apex. If the active region becomes abnormally broad, secretion and wall expansion can become less focused, potentially generating a swollen or misshapen tip rather than a narrow tube. This provides a useful conceptual example of why the spatial arrangement of signaling is important in addition to the total amount of signaling activity.

The shape of a pollen tube emerges from a balance that must be maintained continuously. New material arrives, the wall stretches, and the surface is remodelled as the cell advances. Too little deposition relative to expansion can compromise wall integrity. Too much rigidification at the apex can impede extension. Loss of the localized control of growth can produce a broad tip, abnormal branching or an arrested cell. A tube that ruptures before reaching an ovule loses its ability to deliver intact sperm to the correct target. The controlled rupture at the female gametophyte, discussed later, must therefore be distinguished from uncontrolled failure during transit.

Experiments on pollen-tube growth frequently use time-lapse imaging to measure tip displacement. Such observations can reveal periods of faster and slower extension rather than a perfectly constant rate. Oscillations in growth and changes in calcium or pH have been observed in experimental systems. Temporal associations can suggest coordinated regulation, but a peak in one variable followed by a peak in another does not, alone, prove a simple direct causal relationship. Several variables may be affected by the same upstream process, and the measured phase relationship may depend on the conditions of observation. A strong mechanistic interpretation combines temporal measurements with perturbation and appropriate controls.

Energy is another requirement that cannot be separated from cell mechanics. The synthesis of polysaccharides and proteins, vesicle movement, ion transport and cytoplasmic maintenance all depend directly or indirectly on metabolic resources. Sugars available from the pollen grain and pistil-associated environment provide substrates for energy metabolism and biosynthesis. ATP-dependent pumps maintain gradients that would otherwise dissipate, and cytoskeletal motors require energy to carry out transport. Turgor does not replace these metabolic requirements. A tube could experience sufficient physical pressure yet still cease growth if it could not produce and distribute new material.

This coordinated mechanism explains different classes of loss-of-function phenotypes. A defect in a wall-modifying enzyme might alter the stiffness distribution and cause a change in shape. A severe trafficking defect could reduce delivery of material at the apex. A disruption of ion regulation could influence signaling and the wall environment. A defect in a receptor for an ovular attractant, on the other hand, might leave basic tip growth relatively intact while impairing the ability to orient toward a reproductive target. Measuring tube length alone would not distinguish all these possibilities. Combining morphology, growth rate, spatial trajectory and sperm delivery is much more informative.

7. Passage through the pistil: growth support and directional routes

Once a tube has emerged from a compatible pollen grain, it must enter the tissues of the pistil. The details differ with the anatomy of the stigma and style. In many flowering plants, tubes advance through a transmitting tract containing specialized maternal cells and extracellular material. Some styles contain a canal, whereas others contain a relatively solid tract through which tubes grow. This diversity is an important reminder that pollen tubes do not travel through an identical hollow pipe in every flower. The organization of the route influences how the tube encounters nutrients, physical constraints and signals.

Maternal tissue can contribute to growth through its extracellular environment. Wall-associated molecules and secreted substances may provide suitable conditions for adhesion, extension and nutrition. A pollen tube moving through the style does not become a maternal sporophytic cell. It retains its male gametophytic identity and its haploid vegetative genome. The tube can respond to maternal conditions without receiving maternal chromosomes. Interactions between a male gametophyte and a diploid maternal tissue represent communication across generations within the same reproductive organ.

Growth through a transmitting tract and accurate targeting of a particular ovule are not identical processes. A tract can channel growth over a long distance, reducing the number of directions that a tube must explore. At the ovary, a tube must leave the general route and navigate toward an ovule. In Arabidopsis, for example, tubes may emerge from the transmitting tract onto the septum, travel toward the funiculus of an ovule and then enter its micropylar region. The exact path varies with floral and ovular architecture. The sequential distinction between tract passage, ovule targeting and micropylar entry is more general than the geometry of one model species.

The terms funicular guidance and micropylar guidance describe two useful portions of this trajectory. Funicular guidance concerns navigation toward and along the structure that attaches the ovule to surrounding ovarian tissue. Micropylar guidance concerns the final directed approach to the opening leading toward the female gametophyte. A tube reaching the base of an ovule has not necessarily completed micropylar guidance. Likewise, entry into the micropyle does not guarantee sperm release. A defect at each stage produces a different spatial pattern, which can be identified by tracing the location of tube tips in appropriately stained or imaged pistils.

One way to visualize pollen-tube trajectories in fixed plant tissues is to stain callose with an appropriate fluorescent stain such as aniline blue. The method is useful because callose occurs in pollen-tube walls and plugs, allowing routes to be identified against the surrounding tissue. However, a bright fluorescent line indicates stained material, not automatically a currently living or successfully fertilizing tube. Other callose-containing structures can contribute fluorescence. A researcher should distinguish tube identity and actual fusion outcomes using additional anatomical or genetic evidence. Fixed preparations can establish the distribution of tubes at a sampled time but cannot show subsequent movement of the same tube.

The maternal pistil does more than supply a passive route. Passage through its tissues can affect the physiological state of the male gametophyte. Some experimentally studied pollen tubes acquire responsiveness to ovular cues after exposure to particular female tissues or signals. This is a form of competence in which a cell's ability to respond depends on its previous developmental experiences. A tube may be perfectly capable of elongation on an artificial medium while failing to respond strongly to an isolated attractant if it has not acquired the necessary receptive state. Such a result does not necessarily mean that the attractant is irrelevant in the intact flower.

Consider a hypothetical reciprocal cross experiment. Pollen from a mutant plant grows normally through a wild-type pistil and produces seeds, while wild-type pollen placed on a mutant pistil arrests in the style. The evidence suggests that the relevant defect lies in the female environment rather than being intrinsic to the pollen. A reciprocal cross alone does not specify the exact tissue or molecule, because the mutant pistil includes several interacting structures. Additional evidence, such as tissue-specific restoration of the affected gene or observation of the first abnormal stage, can narrow the explanation. If the same mutant also shows abnormal ovule development, that possibility must be considered before assigning its entire fertility phenotype to pollen-tube guidance.

Pollen tubes also compete in pistils that receive more grains than there are receptive ovules. Differences in the timing of germination, rate of extension or responsiveness to guidance can affect which grains ultimately deliver sperm. This is one reason haploid gene expression in the male gametophyte can influence reproductive success. However, competition is not reducible to speed alone. A very fast tube that cannot perceive an ovular cue may fail while a slower but correctly guided tube succeeds. Equal representation of two pollen genotypes at the stigma does not guarantee equal representation among resulting embryos. Such departures can reflect pre-fertilization selection rather than a failure of Mendelian chromosome segregation during meiosis.

The route can now be divided into local tasks. Near the stigma, the important question is whether a viable compatible grain can initiate growth. Within the style, the question is whether the tube can maintain integrity and reach the ovary. Near individual ovules, the question becomes whether it responds to the appropriate spatial information. A final interaction at the embryo sac determines whether growth stops and sperm are released. Keeping these tasks distinct prevents a general observation such as reduced seed set from being misinterpreted as direct proof of a particular guidance mechanism.
