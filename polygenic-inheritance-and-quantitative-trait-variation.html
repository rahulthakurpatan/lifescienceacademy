1. From Mendelian characters to measurable variation

A field of wheat provides a useful starting point for understanding quantitative inheritance. Some plants reach the ear-bearing stage earlier than others; some produce taller stems, heavier grains or more tillers. The investigator can count these structures or measure their lengths, weights and times. Measurements collected from many plants seldom form just two neat groups. A short plant may differ from a slightly taller plant by a few centimetres, while another differs by several more. Yet each plant still acquired its chromosomes through the same fundamental processes of meiosis and fertilization that operate in a Mendelian cross. The apparent puzzle is how the inheritance of discrete alleles can produce a graded biological outcome.

To distinguish what is inherited from what is measured, consider three levels of description. A locus is a genetic position; an allele is one version of the inherited sequence at that locus. The genotype describes the allelic constitution of an organism at the positions under consideration. A phenotype is an observed characteristic produced during development. Stem height, for example, is not an allele. It is a measured consequence of growth, including cell proliferation and elongation. Inheritance changes the potential activities of biological systems; the phenotype records their realized outcome under the prevailing conditions.

A qualitative phenotype is assigned to categories, such as one of two clearly distinguishable flower colours. A quantitative phenotype is measured on a numerical scale, for example grain mass in milligrams. Quantitative traits can be continuous, at least in principle, like length, or meristic, like number of seeds per pod. A count remains an integer no matter how accurately it is measured. Both continuous and count traits can have complex inheritance. Consequently, calling a character quantitative does not mean that it has infinitely many possible values, nor does calling it categorical establish that only one gene influences it.

The number of contributing loci and the type of phenotype are separate aspects of the problem. A single locus can have a measurable graded effect, as when the heterozygote has an enzyme activity intermediate between the two homozygotes. Conversely, several loci can influence whether a categorical developmental outcome occurs. Quantitative genetics therefore addresses how the distribution of measurable variation arises; polygenic inheritance refers specifically to the contribution of multiple loci to a trait. The term multifactorial is broader in a different direction: it emphasizes that inherited and noninherited influences both contribute. Many familiar phenotypes are simultaneously polygenic, multifactorial and continuous, but these words are not interchangeable.

The distinction has practical consequences for the design of experiments. If an investigator scores a plant as tall or short using a cutoff, a gradual distribution may appear to split into categories. Changing the cutoff changes the number of plants in each class without changing any inherited allele. A second investigator who records exact heights can detect differences among plants that would have been lost in the first analysis. Before interpreting an inheritance ratio, we must identify exactly what was measured, how it was classified, and which biological process the measurement reflects.

Polygenic inheritance must also be distinguished from multiple allelism and pleiotropy. A population can contain three or more alleles of a single locus; an ordinary diploid individual still carries at most two alleles at that autosomal locus. Pleiotropy occurs when one locus influences more than one characteristic. Polygeny reverses the direction of the description: several loci influence one characteristic. These arrangements can coexist. For example, a gene affecting hormone biosynthesis may contribute to height alongside several other loci, while also influencing flowering time. Its influence on multiple traits does not make the height phenotype nonpolygenic.

The continuous appearance of a distribution is a clue rather than a demonstration of its genetic architecture. A broad distribution could arise when environmental differences act on genetically identical organisms. A narrow distribution could arise when a genetically diverse population is measured under conditions that suppress differences in growth. Different combinations of genetic and environmental causes can yield the same mean, range and shape of a histogram. This is why crosses, controlled environments and repeat measurements are necessary to establish an explanatory model.

2. Classical wheat crosses and the multiple-factor explanation

Early genetics faced a conceptual tension between the particulate inheritance demonstrated by Mendelian crosses and the smooth phenotypic variation observed in natural populations. If a gene exists in distinct alternative forms, one might expect the phenotypes it influences to form equally distinct groups. This conclusion silently assumes that each observed characteristic is controlled by one locus and that the environment does not blur the phenotypic differences. Neither assumption is generally justified.

The work of Herman Nilsson-Ehle on the colour of wheat kernels provided a classical experimental route through this difficulty. Segregating progeny could show several degrees of pigmentation rather than a single contrast between strongly coloured and uncoloured kernels. The multiple-factor interpretation assigned portions of the colour difference to more than one inherited factor. Each contributing factor could segregate according to Mendelian rules even though the combined colour phenotype appeared as a series of intensities. This was a bridge between Mendelian analysis and the study of quantitative variation. The Iowa State University crop genetics teaching text describes both the historical observation and the relationship between environmental variation and the underlying segregation model.

A useful instructional reconstruction starts with two independently assorting diploid loci, A/a and B/b. Treat uppercase alleles as pigment-increasing alleles, not as symbols of complete dominance. Suppose each A or B copy increases the measured pigment score by one unit; lowercase copies contribute zero units on this chosen scale. Then AABB carries four increasing copies and aabb carries none. Their offspring AaBb carry two copies and are predicted to have an intermediate score. The intermediate appearance does not imply permanent blending of hereditary material. AaBb retains distinguishable inherited alleles and can transmit gametes containing any of the independently assorted combinations AB, Ab, aB or ab.

When F1 individuals interbreed, some F2 offspring inherit more increasing copies than either F1 parent, some inherit fewer, and others inherit the same count. The genetic information responsible for the extreme parental scores has therefore not disappeared in the intermediate generation. It can be reconstructed by segregation and fertilization. This is a powerful conceptual contrast between temporary phenotypic intermediacy and a model in which the actual inherited determinants become irreversibly averaged.

The equal-effect reconstruction is deliberately simplified. It is not a claim that every experimental wheat cultivar possesses exactly two segregating loci, or that all wheat pigmentation genes have identical effects. Bread wheat has a polyploid genomic history; a particular cross can nevertheless be represented by disomically segregating factors under appropriate assumptions. Real colour depends on tissue, developmental timing, pigment biochemistry and the method used to score intensity. The educational example isolates the mathematical consequences of equal additive contributions rather than pretending that those consequences provide a complete account of wheat biology.

Why should several genes influence pigmentation? Producing a pigment can require precursor availability, catalytic reactions, transport, cellular storage and transcriptional regulation. Variation at multiple steps may change the final concentration. Some variants can alter the quantity of an active protein; others can change catalytic efficiency or the timing of expression. If the contributions are approximately independent and measured over a range without saturation, their effects may sum approximately on the selected scale. If a necessary precursor is absent, or if transport is already limiting, another enzyme's activity may have little additional effect. Thus biochemical architecture explains both why polygeny is common and why exact additivity cannot be presumed.

The historical significance of this example is not the memorization of a single F2 ratio. It shows how a distribution can be explained from a transmission mechanism and a genotype-to-phenotype map. The transmission mechanism determines how frequently allelic combinations occur. The phenotype map determines how each combination is expressed. Once these two components are separated, a student can modify one assumption at a time and predict what happens to the distribution. This approach is more general than recalling the particular numerical pattern of a textbook wheat cross.

3. Deriving the equal-effect additive model

Consider n independently segregating diploid loci at which the two parental lines carry opposite homozygous alleles. Assume that all increasing copies add the same amount c to a baseline phenotype P0, that there is no dominance or epistasis on the measured scale, and that neither survival nor measurement biases the observed progeny. Assume for the moment that the environment is constant. The F1 is heterozygous at every locus. In the F2, a locus contributes zero increasing copies with probability 1/4, one copy with probability 1/2 and two copies with probability 1/4. These probabilities follow directly from the two independent gametic transmissions at that locus.

Let K be the total number of increasing copies inherited across all loci. Every offspring has 2n allelic positions in the simplified diploid model. Each position is supplied by a gamete of an F1 individual, and each increasing allele has probability 1/2. With independent assortment and independent fertilization, these positions provide 2n independent Bernoulli trials with equal success probability. Their sum is a binomial random variable: Pr(K=k) = C(2n,k)/2^(2n), where k is an integer from 0 through 2n. Here C(m,k) denotes the number of ways to select k positions from m. The denominator counts equally probable transmission patterns, not the number of distinct multilocus genotypes.

The phenotype in this idealized system is P = P0 + cK. If c is nonzero, each of the 2n+1 possible values of K corresponds to one distinct phenotypic class. Hence the familiar expression 2n+1 is not a universal property of polygenic inheritance. It is a result derived from these particular assumptions. In a cross in which some contributing loci are not segregating, they contribute a constant to the baseline instead of increasing the number of variable classes. Unequal allele effects can split a class, and dominance can merge or displace classes. Environmental variation can obscure them completely.

For two loci the polynomial for the number of increasing copies at one locus is (1+2x+x²)/4. Multiplying the independent probability-generating polynomials gives (1+2x+x²)²/16 = (1+4x+6x²+4x³+x⁴)/16. The coefficient of x² is six: four of the sixteen equally probable gamete-pair combinations make AaBb, one makes AAbb, and one makes aaBB. These are distinct genotypes with the same idealized score. This is why five phenotype classes arise from nine possible two-locus genotypes, and why the middle class is not synonymous with the double heterozygote.

For three loci the same derivation gives seven classes in the ratio 1:6:15:20:15:6:1. Both extremes have expected frequency 1/64. Under the stated model, an F2 sample of 640 independently sampled offspring has expected counts 10, 60, 150, 200, 150, 60 and 10. Expected counts are not obligatory outcomes. If no extreme plants occur in a small sample, the possibility of a three-locus model cannot be dismissed solely from their absence. The sampling distribution is especially important for rare combinations.

For a single specified extreme the probability is (1/4)^n because the required homozygous combination must arise independently at every locus. If a question explicitly asks for either of two reciprocal extreme parental classes, the two mutually exclusive probabilities must be added, giving 2(1/4)^n. In a two-locus cross the probability of the low extreme is 1/16, the high extreme is 1/16, and either is 1/8. These denominators refer to an F2 generated by intercrossing the fully heterozygous F1; they cannot be carried uncritically into a backcross.

If AaBb is backcrossed to aabb, every offspring receives a lowercase allele at each locus from the tester. The F1 gamete contributes the variable copy. The distribution of K is now binomial with two trials, giving 1:2:1 across scores zero, one and two. The same two loci are present, but only two positions vary among the offspring. In general, the corresponding backcross to the low homozygous parent gives n+1 possible equally spaced classes when effects are equal and additive. Backcross to the high homozygous parent shifts the range from n to 2n increasing copies without changing this class frequency pattern.

The additive model predicts more than counts. If each increasing copy adds c units, the F2 mean is P0 + nc because the expected number of increasing copies is n. The variance of K is 2n(1/2)(1/2)=n/2, so the genetic variance on this particular phenotypic scale is c²n/2 in the ideal segregating F2. The square on c is essential: variance has squared units. If height is recorded in centimetres, variance is in square centimetres, while standard deviation is again in centimetres. These expressions are properties of this particular cross, not formulas for heritability in an arbitrary population.

For example, let three segregating loci each contribute two centimetres per increasing copy to a baseline of 30 cm. The F2 range is 30 to 42 cm, and its expected mean is 36 cm. The expected genetic variance is 2² × 3/2 = 6 cm², with standard deviation about 2.45 cm. A genotype carrying exactly four increasing copies has predicted height 38 cm. Its expected class frequency is 15/64. These calculations show how a probability distribution for transmission becomes a numerical prediction about measurements, provided the genotype-to-phenotype assumptions hold.

A frequently tested inverse problem uses the frequency of an extreme class to estimate the number of contributing factors. If 1/256 of the progeny are expected to be in one extreme class and every assumption is explicitly satisfied, then 4^n = 256, so n=4. However, real observed fractions are subject to sampling variation. An inferred effective number of factors is not proof that there are precisely four causal genes. Unequal effects, linkage, viability differences and environmental overlap can all invalidate that inference. The correct approach to an examination problem is to state whether the calculation assumes ideal segregation or attempts to estimate unknown biological architecture from actual data.

4. Unequal effects, dominance and epistasis

The first complication to the equal-effect model is unequal additive contribution. Suppose an A copy adds two units while a B copy adds five. The scores of AAbb and aaBB are then four and ten, although both carry two increasing copies. AaBb has score seven. The three genotypes that were indistinguishable in the equal-effect model can now be distinguished by their predicted mean phenotypes. With two loci and effects two and five, the nine possible genotypes give nine distinct scores: 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12 and 14. Additivity remains intact because each additional copy has the same effect within its own locus and regardless of the other locus. Equal effects across loci are not part of the definition of additivity itself.

At one locus, denote the mean phenotypes of aa, Aa and AA by g0, g1 and g2. The midpoint of the homozygotes is m = (g0+g2)/2. The additive half-difference is a = (g2−g0)/2, and the dominance deviation is d = g1−m. This parameterization describes genotypic values on a specified measurement scale. If g0=10, g1=13 and g2=16, then m=13, a=3 and d=0. If the heterozygote instead has value 15, d=2 and the genotype is partially dominant toward the high-value homozygote. The allele's biological effect has not changed its Mendelian probability of transmission.

Complete dominance toward the high-value homozygote occurs when g1=g2; the heterozygote and one homozygote share the same mean. Overdominance on a quantitative scale occurs when the heterozygote's value exceeds both homozygotes, and a heterozygote value below both can be described as underdominance on that scale. These terms describe the relationship between genotypic values, not necessarily organismal fitness. An intermediate enzyme concentration may produce a phenotype close to a maximum because flux saturates. Therefore a molecularly intermediate heterozygote can appear phenotypically dominant.

Epistasis concerns dependence of the effect of one locus on the genotype at another. Imagine that at a pigment-producing locus B, an increasing copy has no measurable effect when the plant is aa because a required precursor is not produced. In an AA background the same B copy can increase pigment. The genetic effect depends on the background. In the simplest additive model, changing bb to BB must have the same predicted increment in any A genotype. If that increment differs between backgrounds, the joint effects cannot be represented by a sum of fixed locus contributions on the observed scale.

A two-by-two comparison gives a direct way to detect such departure in a controlled example. Suppose homozygous combinations aabb, AAbb, aaBB and AABB have means 10, 14, 15 and 23 units, respectively. Relative to aabb, the A change adds four and the B change adds five when each is made alone. The additive prediction for AABB is 10+4+5=19. The observed value is four units greater. The interaction contrast is 23−14−15+10=4 units. This contrast is a comparison of conditional effects, not a measure of the probability that the loci assort independently. Even independently assorting genes may interact in their physiological effects.

One must also specify the scale of analysis. Multiplication of independent relative effects on the original scale becomes addition after taking logarithms. For example, if an A genotype doubles a positive trait and a B genotype triples it, their combined multiplicative value is six times the baseline. On the raw scale a two-factor additive contrast can be nonzero; on the log scale these idealized relative effects sum. A statistical interaction therefore motivates investigation of mechanisms but does not automatically establish that the encoded proteins physically interact. Experimental evidence about molecular functions is needed to make that stronger claim.

Linkage acts at a different stage. In a cross between AABB and aabb with tight coupling-phase linkage, the F1 produces more AB and ab gametes than Ab and aB. At the limit of complete linkage, the two loci travel together as a unit. An additive genotype-to-phenotype model still can apply, but F2 phenotype class frequencies no longer follow the independent two-locus binomial pattern. Specifically, with no recombination, the F2 yields AABB, AaBb and aabb in a 1:2:1 ratio; scores corresponding to one and three increasing copies are absent. In repulsion phase, Ab and aB can be the predominant gametes, and complete linkage can yield only intermediate total scores in this equal-effect model. Physical association in transmission and functional interaction in expression must not be confused.

5. Environmental variation and reaction norms

An organism's measured phenotype is generated over time. Plant height depends on meristem activity, cell elongation, water supply, nutrition, temperature and developmental stage. A genetically specified difference in a growth regulator need not be expressed equally under all conditions. An environmental influence does not have to alter a DNA sequence in order to alter the phenotype. Changing the supply of a limiting nutrient can alter enzyme activity and growth even when the genotype remains unchanged.

A simple model represents phenotype as P = G + E, with the baseline included in G and with E representing environmental deviation. G is the expected phenotype associated with a genotype in the stated reference conditions. E can be positive or negative relative to that expectation. The same genotype can yield a range of phenotypes if individuals encounter different environments or developmental perturbations. Conversely, different genotypes may yield similar observations when environmental deviations offset the underlying genotypic differences. A phenotype alone is then an imperfect indicator of genotype.

To illustrate this overlap, consider two genotypes with mean heights 35 and 39 cm. If environmental variation is small, their observed distributions may be clearly separated. If within-genotype standard deviations are several centimetres, the distributions may overlap substantially. A plant measured at 37 cm might belong to either genotype. Increasing the precision of the ruler does not necessarily resolve the ambiguity because much of the spread may be genuine biological variation rather than instrument error.

At a more detailed level the simple additive model may be inadequate because genotypes differ in how they respond to environments. A reaction norm is the function or pattern of phenotypic values associated with a genotype across a set of environments. If genotype A is four centimetres taller than genotype B at every studied nutrient level, the reaction norms are parallel on the centimetre scale. Both respond to the environment, but the difference is constant. If A gains ten centimetres across the nutrient treatment while B gains only two, the lines are not parallel and there is a genotype-by-environment interaction. If A is taller under one condition and shorter under another, the rankings reverse, providing a particularly clear form of interaction.

Suppose genotype X averages 20 cm in a low-nutrient treatment and 32 cm in a high-nutrient treatment, whereas genotype Y averages 24 and 28 cm. Both genotypes increase height in the richer treatment, but X increases by twelve and Y by four centimetres. The difference of responses is eight centimetres. The ranking also reverses: Y is taller under low nutrients and X is taller under high nutrients. No claim about the exact molecular basis follows from these four means alone. Differences in resource acquisition, sensitivity to growth signals or allocation are candidate explanations that would require further tests.

A carefully designed common-garden experiment grows genetically distinct individuals under matched conditions. Random placement helps prevent one genotype from occupying systematically better soil or brighter locations. Replication allows estimates of variation among individuals sharing a genotype and treatment. If genotype identity can be replicated through clones or inbred lines, its mean phenotype can be compared across treatments. A factorial arrangement in which every genotype is represented in every environment makes the interaction interpretable. A design with genotype X grown only in rich soil and Y only in poor soil cannot separate the two causes, no matter how accurate the measurements are.

Environmental effects and genotype–environment correlation should be distinguished from interaction. Correlation means that genetic backgrounds are not randomly distributed among environments, perhaps because different lines are cultivated in different regions. Interaction means the phenotypic response to a change in environment depends on genetic background. The first can confound a comparison even when all reaction norms are parallel. The second may be observed in a balanced controlled design even if no genotype–environment correlation exists. The formal consequences for variance partitioning are reserved for the following node.

Environmental measurements must be made at biologically appropriate times. Nutrient supply at the start of vegetative growth can have a different effect from nutrient supply after flowering. Two plants measured on the same calendar date may be at different developmental stages. A researcher must decide whether the trait of interest is height at a fixed age, height at flowering, final height or growth rate. Each definition is legitimate, but it can yield a different genetic and environmental pattern. This is one reason quantitative trait descriptions must include the measurement protocol rather than relying on a trait name alone.

6. Distributions, environmental smoothing and transgressive segregation

A phenotype distribution describes how observations are spread across possible values. A histogram divides the measurement range into bins and counts observations in each. The location of the distribution can be summarized by a mean, while dispersion can be described by variance or standard deviation. Neither quantity alone uniquely specifies shape. Two populations with the same mean and variance can have different tails or numbers of peaks. It is useful to inspect the distribution before assigning a particular statistical model.

In the equal-effect F2 model, the total increasing-copy count has a binomial distribution. As the number of independently segregating loci increases, there are more possible intermediate totals and more combinations that yield values near the centre. The relative frequency of extreme values decreases. If the effects are sufficiently small, measurement intervals and environmental variation can make adjacent genotypic classes indistinguishable. A smooth phenotype distribution can consequently arise from many particulate contributions. The normal distribution is often a useful approximation when numerous independent contributions have no overwhelmingly large individual effect, but it is not a necessary signature of polygeny.

One can understand environmental smoothing as a mixture. Suppose two-locus genotypic classes have means 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, with relative weights 1:4:6:4:1. If a common environmental deviation distribution applies to every class, the overall phenotype distribution is obtained by adding the class-specific distributions with these weights. When environmental spread is narrow, the separate classes may remain visible. As it becomes wider, neighbouring distributions overlap and a single broad central region may result. Environmental spread does not create additional Mendelian segregation classes; it creates variation in the observed phenotype within each class.

Not every quantitative distribution is symmetric. Counts such as offspring number cannot be negative and may have a long upper tail. A trait measured across two developmental stages may show separate modes. A mixture of populations with different mean values can also produce bimodality without a single large-effect locus. A large-effect segregating locus on top of a polygenic background may generate overlapping groups. These possibilities explain why a graph's appearance should suggest hypotheses to test, not decide the inheritance mechanism by itself.

A particularly informative outcome is transgressive segregation, in which segregants have phenotypes beyond those of either parent. Consider AAbb and aaBB when each increasing allele contributes one unit. Both parents have an additive score of two, as does their F1. The F2 includes AABB with score four and aabb with score zero, each at probability 1/16 under independent assortment. The parental lines had opposing favourable and unfavourable alleles; recombination and segregation can assemble increasing alleles from both parents or combine their decreasing alternatives. Parental similarity can therefore conceal substantial genetic differences.

Transgressive phenotypes are not proof of a new mutation. They may result from new combinations of existing alleles, epistasis, environmental conditions or measurement error. An experimenter should compare progeny and parents in the same environment and verify replication of the extreme phenotype. Conversely, in the cross between AABB and aabb under the strictly additive model, no segregant can have a genetic score beyond four or below zero. If a plant exceeds the high parental measurement, environmental deviations or a violated model assumption must be considered before inferring transgression of genetic value.

A controlled cross with genetically uniform parents and F1 offspring can provide a comparison between nonsegregating and segregating generations. If the parents and F1 are genuinely homogeneous at the relevant loci, variation within each of these groups reflects environmental and other nonsegregating sources under the simplified assumptions. Greater variation in the F2 is compatible with genetic segregation. However, environmental variance may differ among groups, and maternal effects or differences in developmental stability can complicate interpretation. The increase is evidence to evaluate, not a direct, assumption-free measure of genetic variance.

The observed distribution must also be interpreted at the appropriate sampling level. A plant measured repeatedly contributes several observations but is still one genetic individual. Counting repeated observations as if they came from independent plants underestimates uncertainty. Similarly, plants from the same family share alleles and may share microenvironmental conditions. Randomization, independent biological replication and explicit recording of family structure are essential to interpreting quantitative trait differences rather than mistaking correlated measurements for independent evidence.

7. Threshold phenotypes and latent liability

A categorical outcome can arise from a continuously varying underlying process. Consider a plant that initiates flowering only after a developmental signal exceeds some effective threshold. The quantity of the signal may be influenced by multiple loci and environmental exposure, while the observer records only whether a specified event has occurred by a fixed time. Individuals immediately below and immediately above the threshold have similar underlying values but different recorded categories. This illustrates how a trait can have a complex quantitative basis despite binary scoring.

The liability-threshold model formalizes this relationship. Liability is an unobserved or incompletely observed continuous variable representing a combined propensity toward an outcome. A binary outcome is recorded when liability exceeds a specified threshold. In one simplified population model, liability is normally distributed with mean zero and variance one after standardization. If the threshold is z=1, the fraction above it is approximately 0.1587 under that normal model. This fraction is a probability across the modeled population, not an inevitable outcome for any individual with a particular genotype.

The threshold need not be a literal molecular switch with a measured numerical concentration. It can be a statistical representation of several processes acting together. A change in environment can shift the distribution of liability so that more individuals exceed a fixed threshold. Alternatively, the biological or diagnostic criterion for recording the outcome may change. These two changes can both alter the observed frequency, although the underlying mechanisms differ. The model is most useful when its assumptions and the definition of the recorded category are stated explicitly.

For a hypothetical standardized normal liability with mean zero and standard deviation one, let the threshold be 1.645, so approximately five percent of the population is above it. If a particular environment shifts the mean to 0.5 while leaving the standard deviation unchanged and the threshold fixed, the standardized distance from the new mean is 1.145. The upper-tail fraction increases to roughly 12.6 percent. A modest shift of a continuous distribution can therefore produce a substantial relative change in the number of individuals classified above threshold. This numerical example is an illustration, not an estimate of risk for any real disease.

Relatives may have more similar liabilities than unrelated individuals because they share genetic factors and sometimes environments. An affected relative can provide information about familial susceptibility, but does not identify the precise genes involved. A binary familial outcome is not automatically evidence for a recessive or dominant major locus. Conversely, a major gene with incomplete penetrance can also produce a binary phenotype with complex familial patterns. Alternative hypotheses should be distinguished by genetic data, appropriately designed family or population studies, and knowledge of the developmental mechanism.

Threshold reasoning has a useful connection to the earlier phenotype distribution. Recording exact growth or signal values preserves quantitative information; recording only a classification compresses them. An observer cannot recover the complete liability distribution from a single proportion above a threshold without further assumptions. Two populations may have the same fraction above threshold while differing substantially in their mean and variance. This limitation is especially relevant when interpreting examination problems that present only the prevalence of a multifactorial condition.

The term penetrance describes the probability that a specified genotype expresses a defined phenotype under specified conditions. It is not identical to population prevalence, which combines outcomes across genotypes and environments. A liability-threshold model can help explain variable penetrance if multiple genetic backgrounds and environmental influences change whether the threshold is crossed. The term expressivity instead describes the degree or manner of phenotype expression among individuals. These distinctions connect quantitative inheritance to earlier Mendelian extensions without replacing the specific definitions of those concepts.

8. Experimental reasoning and limits of inference

The starting question in quantitative genetic analysis is not how many genes are involved, but what observations can distinguish competing explanations. A continuous distribution observed in a single unstructured population is compatible with many genetic architectures. To obtain stronger evidence, researchers choose parents with contrasting trait values, examine F1 and F2 progeny, compare environmental responses and use genetic markers when appropriate. Each stage reduces some ambiguity while introducing assumptions of its own.

In a classical plant crossing experiment, parents should be characterized in the same environment and scored repeatedly or in replicated plots. A cross between stable homozygous lines can produce a relatively uniform F1 at segregating nuclear loci, though the F1 is not necessarily phenotypically uniform. Reciprocal crosses can reveal differences attributable to maternal environment or cytoplasmic inheritance. F2 plants then reveal newly segregating combinations. A backcross can be used to test transmission predictions with a defined parent. Independent replication improves the reliability of observed frequencies.

Suppose an illustrative two-locus cross gives 160 F2 offspring with scores distributed as 10, 40, 60, 40 and 10. The equal-effect model predicts exactly these expected counts because the probabilities are 1, 4, 6, 4 and 1 divided by 16. This exact match is compatible with the model but cannot establish it as the unique cause. Different biological systems could generate similar counts. If a second dataset gives 14, 34, 63, 38 and 11, the expected numbers remain 10, 40, 60, 40 and 10. The deviations must be compared to sampling variability before concluding that the assumptions are violated.

The Pearson goodness-of-fit statistic for that illustrative second dataset is the sum of (observed−expected)²/expected across the five classes. It equals 16/10+36/40+9/60+4/40+1/10=2.85. If the probabilities were specified independently of these observations and observations are independent, the usual reference distribution has four degrees of freedom. This result is below the approximate 5 percent critical value of 9.49. The experiment does not reject the ideal class model at that significance level. A failure to reject is not proof of independent assortment or equal effects; it means that the stated discrepancy is not large enough under the test assumptions.

An investigator may discover that counts differ because a pigment-scoring instrument assigns neighbouring intensities to the same category. Another explanation is reduced viability among extreme genotypes. Genotyping seeds before germination and plants after establishment can help distinguish altered transmission from differential survival, provided sampling is comparable. Controlled replicated measurement can distinguish a systematic classification error from genuine overlapping genotypic distributions. A marker that predicts phenotype may further support a genetic contribution, but detailed linkage-based mapping is the subject of node 8.E.05.

Population-specific allele frequencies also influence the observed genetic variation. A strongly acting allele may be fixed in one population and segregate in another. A fixed allele can contribute greatly to the population's mean phenotype while contributing no within-population segregation variance. Thus biological importance and contribution to observed variation are different questions. When the following chapter partitions phenotypic variance into genetic and environmental components, it will build on this distinction. A variance component is a statistical property of a defined population under specified conditions, not a universal property of the gene.

The central analytical habit is to separate assumptions about transmission, expression, environmental effects and measurement. For each proposed ratio, ask which cross generated the gametes. For each proposed phenotypic class, ask how genetic contributions combine and on which scale. For each distribution, ask whether environmental variation or population structure could produce the observed shape. For each claim about causation, ask what independent experiment would discriminate the candidate mechanisms. These questions connect classical crosses to quantitative genetics without turning a convenient textbook model into a rigid description of every biological trait.

9. The biological routes from allelic variation to a quantitative phenotype

A phenotype such as grain mass is an endpoint of a developmental process rather than a direct readout of a single enzyme. Grain mass depends on the number and size of cells, the movement of photosynthetic products, the duration of grain filling, and the conversion of transported substances into stored compounds. Each of these processes can be influenced by allelic differences. In some cases the effect of a variant is mediated by the amount of protein synthesized. In others the catalytic or regulatory properties of the protein differ. A quantitative genetic model does not need to describe every molecular step before it can predict segregation. Nevertheless, understanding these steps explains why one can observe either additive contributions or departures from additivity.

Consider a hypothetical metabolic pathway in which an intermediate is converted through two consecutive reactions into a stored product. Suppose enzyme A determines the flux into the pathway and enzyme B is present in substantial excess. An allele that increases enzyme A activity might increase the amount of final product accumulated, whereas a comparable proportional change in enzyme B might have little measurable effect because the second reaction already handles the available intermediate. This does not mean that gene B is unimportant: removing all B activity would stop the pathway. It means that the particular B variants segregating in the population have small effects within the range being investigated. The question of which genes are essential is different from the question of which genetic variants explain differences among individuals.

If enzyme B becomes limiting in a second genetic background, the effect of its alleles can become larger. The background dependence arises because the relevant physiological state has changed. This mechanism is an example of why epistasis is a property of combinations of alleles under particular circumstances rather than a permanent label attached to two gene names. Genetic backgrounds can change substrate abundance, regulatory feedback, tissue organization or developmental timing. The same alleles can therefore appear approximately additive in one experiment and display substantial interaction in another.

An additive approximation is often biologically informative when variants induce small changes around a reference state. For instance, suppose several variants each alter the duration of a particular phase of growth by a small amount. If extending this phase by one day has nearly the same effect on final height regardless of the other small changes, the combined height differences may be approximated by their sum. Such a local approximation does not imply that final height increases linearly without limit. At extremes of growth duration, resource exhaustion, mechanical support, or changes in developmental regulation can alter the relationship. The model is valid over the measured range, not necessarily across all imaginable phenotypes.

Regulatory variants provide an important route to polygenic variation. A difference in a regulatory sequence may alter when, where or how strongly a gene is expressed rather than changing the protein's amino acid sequence. Such alleles can affect one tissue differently from another. If several genes independently contribute to the rate at which a tissue grows, small differences in expression can translate into an aggregate difference in its final size. Protein-coding variation may produce a similar phenotype through a different mechanism. Observing a quantitative difference alone cannot establish whether the causal sequence changes protein function or expression.

The measured endpoint can also integrate events occurring at different times. A plant might be tall because its early growth rate is high, because it grows for a longer interval, or because growth slows less sharply as resources become limiting. Two plants of identical final height could have different growth trajectories and carry distinct alleles. If growth is measured over time, these differences may become detectable. A genetic analysis of final height and a genetic analysis of the rate of height increase need not identify exactly the same set of contributors. The definition of a quantitative trait includes its temporal and biological context.

Quantitative variation is not restricted to morphological dimensions. Enzyme activity, pigment concentration, protein abundance, the timing of a developmental transition and the number of offspring are all measurable traits. Some are much closer to the immediate consequences of allelic changes than others. A protein abundance measurement may reflect a relatively direct effect of a regulatory variant, whereas adult body size integrates many upstream physiological processes. The latter type of trait creates more opportunities for multiple genes and environmental factors to influence the observed variation. However, proximity to a molecular process does not guarantee a simple genetic architecture, because even expression of one gene can be influenced by several regulatory loci.

The scale of measurement also affects how biological effects are interpreted. Two kernels with 4 and 8 milligrams of a substance per gram of tissue differ by 4 mg/g and have a twofold ratio. If kernel masses differ, the total amount of substance per kernel may not differ in the same way as the concentration. An allele that increases tissue mass without changing the total amount of a compound can reduce its concentration. Whether this is a favourable or unfavourable quantitative effect depends on the biological question. Before explaining the contribution of an allele, determine whether the phenotype is a concentration, an amount, a rate, a proportion or a size.

One should not equate a pigment-increasing allele with a gain-of-function mutation in the molecular genetic sense. A reduction in the function of a repressor can increase downstream pigment formation; an allele that reduces degradation can similarly increase product abundance. Conversely, a molecular gain of activity could reduce a measured trait if the active protein inhibits growth. Symbols such as A and a encode alternatives for an explanatory cross, not a universal hierarchy of molecular functionality. The sign of a quantitative effect is defined relative to the reference genotype and measured trait.

The quantitative approach therefore links three questions. How are the alternative alleles transmitted? What molecular and developmental processes connect those alternatives to measured phenotypes? Under what environmental and measurement conditions are the effects approximately additive? A student who separates these questions can explain why different crosses yield different ratios even when they involve similar biological pathways, and why one mathematical model may describe a restricted range of observations without being an exhaustive mechanism.

10. Predicting distributions when loci contribute different amounts

The preceding equal-effect derivation gives a convenient distribution of allele counts. Real genetic crosses often involve alleles whose contributions differ, so it is useful to retain the probability calculation while changing the mapping from genotypes to phenotype values. Consider two loci that assort independently. At the A locus each increasing allele adds one unit to the phenotype, while at the B locus each increasing allele adds two. No dominance or epistasis is assumed. The parental cross AABB × aabb produces an F1 of score three. In the F2 the A contribution can be zero, one or two, and the B contribution can be zero, two or four. Each locus has the familiar genotype probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4.

To obtain the total distribution, first calculate every pair of contributions and their joint probability. A=0 and B=0 give score zero with probability 1/16. A=1 and B=0 give score one with probability 2/16. Score two can arise from A=2,B=0 or A=0,B=2; these have probabilities 1/16 and 2/16 respectively. Their mutually exclusive probabilities sum to 3/16. Score three arises from A=1,B=2 with probability 4/16. Symmetry around the mean gives scores four, five and six with probabilities 3/16, 2/16 and 1/16. There are seven phenotypic values although there are only two segregating loci.

This example is especially important in examination questions because an observed seven-class pattern does not uniquely support a three-locus equal-effect model. Under that model the predicted relative frequencies would be 1:6:15:20:15:6:1 out of 64. Under the two-locus unequal-effect model just described the frequencies are 1:2:3:4:3:2:1 out of 16. The frequency pattern provides more information than the class count alone. In addition, a well-designed experiment can directly genotype the loci to discover whether plants in two classes carry different allelic combinations. Classification without genetic data is often insufficient to establish how many loci are segregating.

The same calculation can be written compactly through probability-generating polynomials when contributions are integer multiples of a common measurement unit. The A-locus polynomial is (1+2x+x²)/4 and the B-locus polynomial is (1+2x²+x⁴)/4. Each exponent records the phenotype increment; each coefficient records its probability. The product gives (1+2x+3x²+4x³+3x⁴+2x⁵+x⁶)/16. This is not the same generating polynomial as that for the total number of increasing alleles because each B allele contributes two score units. The algebra is simply a concise representation of the same exhaustive enumeration.

If contributions are incommensurable or expressed as real-valued quantities, constructing a polynomial with integer exponents may be inconvenient. Enumeration of the genotypic values and multiplication of independent probabilities still works. The maximum number of distinct genotypic means for n biallelic diploid loci with three possible genotypes at each locus is 3^n, when all multilocus genotypes are possible and each happens to have a different value. The observed number may be much smaller because different genotypes have equal means or cannot be distinguished experimentally. Neither 3^n nor 2n+1 is an unconditional prediction of the number of visible phenotypic classes.

For arbitrary locus-specific per-copy effects c1, c2 and so on, the ideal additive F2 mean relative to the low parent equals the sum of the effects because the expected increasing-copy count at each locus is one. The variance is one half of the sum of squared effects if the loci segregate independently. For two effects one and two, this gives mean three and variance (1²+2²)/2 = 2.5 score units squared. We can verify the variance directly from the class distribution. Around mean three, the squared deviations for scores zero through six are 9,4,1,0,1,4,9. The weighted sum divided by sixteen is (9+8+3+0+3+8+9)/16=40/16=2.5. This is a useful check because two independent methods arrive at the same result.

Why do squared effects appear in variance? If a random allelic dosage deviates from its expectation by one copy, a locus with contribution two changes the phenotype by two units. When deviations are squared to calculate variance, the contribution is four times as large as that of a locus with effect one, assuming the dosage variances are the same. The mean sums the effects themselves; the dispersion depends on squared effects. Consequently, two crosses can have identical differences between parental means while having different F2 variances. A larger share of the parental difference concentrated at one locus yields more dispersed segregation than the same total difference divided among many small independent contributors.

The independence assumption enters the variance calculation through covariance. If the contributions of loci A and B tend to be above their respective means together, the covariance is positive and the variance of the sum increases. If one tends to be above its mean when the other is below, the covariance is negative and the variance decreases. Coupling and repulsion linkage phases supply intuitive genetic examples. In the absence of recombination, an increasing allele at A can travel with an increasing allele at B or with a decreasing allele at B. The possible total scores depend on which arrangement was inherited from the original parents.

A broad phenotypic distribution therefore need not result from a larger number of contributing loci than a narrow distribution. It can instead reflect larger effects, positive covariance between genetic contributions, greater environmental spread, or some combination of these influences. The correct quantitative reasoning begins by stating the model and calculating its predictions, then interpreting observations relative to those predictions. It does not begin by assigning a gene count from the width of a histogram.

11. Rare phenotypic classes, sampling and the effective number of factors

The extreme classes of a classical equal-effect F2 are often used to illustrate why a quantitative trait can show very few recognizable parental types. When n=5, one specified extreme has expected probability 1/1024. A greenhouse containing 100 F2 plants will typically provide little information about that rare extreme. Even when the model is exactly correct, the expected number of extreme plants is only 100/1024, approximately 0.098. This is a mean over repeated greenhouse populations, not a prediction that one tenth of a plant can be observed.

The probability of missing an extreme is particularly informative. If each sampled plant independently has probability p of being in a specified extreme class, the probability that none of N plants belongs to the class is (1−p)^N. For n=3 the value of p is 1/64. With 64 plants, the expected count is one, but the probability of observing none is (63/64)^64, approximately 0.365. Thus even a sample with an expected extreme count of one has more than a one-third chance of containing no such plant. The absence of a rare class provides weak evidence when the sample is small.

The same reasoning can help decide how large a sample is needed to have a high probability of seeing at least one extreme. For a three-locus example, the probability of seeing at least one is 1−(63/64)^N. To make this at least 0.95, solve (63/64)^N ≤ 0.05. Taking natural logarithms, N ln(63/64) ≤ ln(0.05). Since ln(63/64) is negative, dividing reverses the inequality. N must be at least ln(0.05)/ln(63/64), about 190.2; the smallest whole number meeting the condition is 191. This calculation assumes independent samples and a phenotype that identifies the extreme genotype without error. It provides a model-based sampling requirement, not a universal recommendation for real genetic experiments.

When both reciprocal extremes are of interest, their total probability is 2p. For the three-locus model this is 1/32, so the chance of seeing at least one of either extreme is 1−(31/32)^N. This is not the probability of seeing at least one individual from each extreme class. The latter event requires two types to occur. By inclusion and exclusion, its probability is 1−2(1−p)^N+(1−2p)^N. The final term restores the probability of samples missing both types, which was subtracted twice. The distinction is an example of how exact question wording affects the mathematical expression.

Phenotypic extremes can be difficult to classify when environmental variability overlaps the genotype-specific means. A plant with a measured value close to the high parent's mean might carry one fewer increasing allele, offset by favourable environmental conditions. A genetically extreme plant may also be displaced from the parental range by unfavourable conditions. Counting individuals that resemble a parent in appearance is then an imperfect way of counting extreme genotypes. The expression (1/4)^n still describes the genetic probability in the ideal cross; the probability of being classified as a parent-like phenotype may differ.

A second classical approach estimates an effective number of contributing factors from a difference between parental means and the increase in variance among segregating progeny. Its logic can be derived from the equal-effect model without memorizing an isolated formula. Let the parental phenotypic difference be D=2nc, with c the contribution of one increasing copy and n the number of independent segregating loci. The genetic variance of the ideal F2 is c²n/2. Squaring D gives 4n²c². Dividing by eight times the genetic variance gives 4n²c²/(4nc²)=n. Hence under the model the number of effective factors equals D²/(8Vg), where Vg is the F2 genetic segregation variance.

The variance attributed to segregation can be estimated from the difference between F2 phenotypic variance and an estimate of nonsegregating environmental variance, assuming that environmental variance is comparable across generations and there is no relevant covariance. If the observed F2 variance is 13 units squared, the estimated environmental variance is 5 units squared, and the parental mean difference is 16 units, the estimated segregation variance is 8 units squared. The resulting effective number is 16²/(8×8)=4. The squared units cancel, as required for an estimate expressed as a count. This teaching calculation is an instance of the Castle–Wright approach under simple assumptions, not a direct count of molecular loci.

The assumptions matter especially when the parental means do not reflect opposite fixed alleles at every segregating locus. With unequal additive effects, the expression becomes (sum of c_i)² divided by sum of c_i². When all effects are positive and equal, this equals n. When the effects vary in magnitude, the ratio is no greater than n, by the relationship between the square of a sum and the sum of squares. For example, two effects of one and three give (1+3)²/(1²+3²)=16/10=1.6, even though two loci contribute. Fractional estimates are therefore not fractional genes. They indicate how the observed effect distribution compares with a simplified equal-effect reference model.

If some increasing and decreasing alleles are distributed across both parents, their total parental difference can be small even when many loci segregate in the F2. The effective number estimate can then be misleadingly small. With linkage, dominance or environmental differences between generations, the assumed relationship between parental difference and F2 variance fails. If the estimated F2 genetic variance is zero or negative, the formula cannot supply a meaningful positive estimate. Such a result signals limitations of the data or assumptions; it must not be repaired by arbitrarily taking an absolute value or forcing the estimate to be at least one.

These two classical methods teach complementary lessons. Rare extremes reveal the multiplicative nature of independent segregation probabilities; parental differences and segregation variances reveal the relationship between effect size and dispersion. Both depend on a clearly defined cross and an explicit genetic model. They can be useful for conceptual and quantitative examination problems but are not substitutes for molecular mapping or experimental verification of a proposed causal locus.

12. Biological evidence: from early statistical genetics to crop and flowering traits

The wheat example addressed the apparent contradiction between particulate inheritance and graded phenotypes. A related historical question concerned resemblance among relatives. If individuals in a family resemble each other in a quantitative characteristic, how can that resemblance be understood using discrete hereditary factors? R. A. Fisher's work of 1918 provided a mathematical synthesis connecting Mendelian inheritance with statistical variation and correlations among relatives. Its significance for the present chapter is the conceptual result that many inherited factors can produce continuous variation and predictable patterns of resemblance. A formal account of genetic variance and resemblance is developed in the next chapter, where the necessary assumptions can be examined in greater detail.

The transition from classical crosses to selection studies provides another source of evidence. In the Illinois long-term maize experiment, initiated in 1896, researchers selected populations for divergent grain oil and protein concentrations. A quantitative characteristic was measured and individuals with preferred values were chosen as parents in successive generations. A continued change in the population mean is consistent with transmissible genetic differences affecting the phenotype. The experiment has provided valuable material for subsequent analysis of the genetic architecture of kernel composition. A study published in Genetics in 2004 examined the genetic basis of oil concentration in material derived from these divergent lines and supported a complex polygenic architecture.

The interpretation of long-term selection is different from the interpretation of a single F2 cross. A selection experiment follows changes in a population across generations; a cross creates a specific segregation distribution from specified parents. In a selected population, allele frequencies can change, associations between alleles can arise, and genetic variants may become fixed. Therefore a change in population mean does not establish that all contributing effects are equal, nor does it yield the number of causal loci directly. A replicated comparison of selected and unselected lines in shared environments provides stronger evidence than a simple comparison of measurements made many generations apart under different conditions.

The same reasoning is useful for distinguishing selection from environmental improvement. Imagine that average grain oil concentration rises over five growing seasons. If all plants experienced changes in fertilizer application or climate, the measured trend might occur without any change in inherited differences. If high-oil and control lines are grown together in later common environments and retain a systematic difference, an inherited contribution becomes more plausible. A reciprocal environmental comparison helps test whether the inherited difference is stable across conditions or depends on the growth environment. These comparisons are not substitutes for genetic analysis, but they establish the kind of evidence needed to interpret a phenotypic trend.

Flowering time in Arabidopsis illustrates how genetic effects can depend on environmental history. Flowering is a developmental transition regulated by signals involving day length, temperature, developmental age and internal physiological conditions. Natural accessions can differ in the amount of prolonged cold exposure needed for rapid flowering under favourable subsequent conditions. The process by which prolonged cold promotes flowering in responsive plants is called vernalization. Studies of natural Arabidopsis accessions, including work published in 2005 on diverse flowering responses, have identified important roles for genes such as FRIGIDA (FRI) and FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC), while demonstrating that flowering behaviour cannot be reduced to a single genetic difference across all accessions.

In an appropriate genetic background, functional FRI promotes high expression of FLC, a floral repressor. A period of prolonged cold can reduce FLC-mediated repression, allowing later flowering-promoting signals to have their effects. Consequently, the phenotypic difference associated with FRI or FLC may be large in one environmental treatment and smaller after sufficient vernalization. This is a biological route by which an environmental history changes the expression of genetic differences. It provides a mechanistic example of genotype-by-environment interaction without suggesting that every flowering-time allele acts through this particular pathway.

A student can predict an experimental comparison from this explanation. Grow representatives of two accessions in the same photoperiod, with each accession divided into a cold-treated and an untreated group. Record days to flowering or leaf number at flowering, keeping the trait definition fixed. If one accession flowers substantially earlier after cold exposure while the other changes little, a response difference has been observed. If the accessions differ at FRI and FLC as well as numerous other positions, the interaction cannot be attributed solely to those genes on the basis of the whole-accession comparison. Genetic crosses or lines differing more specifically at a candidate locus would strengthen causal inference.

The appropriate controls follow from the biological question. Untreated plants provide a baseline for each genotype. Matched growth conditions after treatment reduce unrelated differences in developmental rate. Replicate plants allow a researcher to distinguish a systematic response from variation among individuals. Measuring developmental timing carefully avoids comparing plants simply because they were harvested at different stages. A genetic control that changes one locus in an otherwise similar background can help distinguish the effect of the candidate gene from other allelic differences. None of these controls requires the assumption that the whole trait is monogenic.

It is useful to compare the kinds of evidence from the three examples. The wheat crosses connect frequency classes to segregating factors. The maize experiment and subsequent studies demonstrate the relevance of inherited variation in a selected quantitative trait. Arabidopsis flowering experiments illustrate a specific biological connection between environmental response and genetic regulation. Together they show that quantitative genetics is not merely a mathematical description of a bell-shaped curve. It is a framework for asking how inherited differences act through development, how they are expressed under environmental conditions, and how their joint effects can be tested against observations.

13. Measurement, variation and the interpretation of experimental data

The quality of a quantitative genetic inference begins with the quality of the phenotype. In a classical categorical experiment, an investigator may distinguish yellow and green seeds with little ambiguity. In quantitative analysis, differences between measurements may be only slightly larger than the uncertainty of the measuring instrument. A height difference of one millimetre has a different interpretation if the instrument resolves one tenth of a millimetre than if readings are rounded to the nearest centimetre. When environmental deviations are also substantial, the precision of the instrument and the biological variability must be considered separately.

Suppose a researcher weighs ten kernels from each plant. If the scale is calibrated, variation in repeated readings of the same kernel provides information about measurement error. Variation among different kernels of the same plant includes additional biological influences, such as their developmental position and nutrient allocation. Variation among plants of the same inbred line includes further developmental and microenvironmental effects. Variation among lines adds genetic differences to these sources. The identity of the experimental unit is important: ten kernels from a plant do not constitute ten independently segregating plants. A line comparison that ignores this hierarchy can overstate the amount of independent evidence.

An appropriate way to investigate grain mass may be to compute the average mass of a defined number of mature grains from each plant and then compare plant means. This defines the phenotype as the average grain mass of a plant rather than the mass of any randomly chosen kernel. Such an approach reduces the contribution of kernel-to-kernel variation to the measured average, but it does not remove environmental effects shared by the entire plant. More measurements within each plant may improve measurement precision; more independent plants improve the ability to estimate variation among plants. These are related but distinct objectives.

The definition of the measured phenotype becomes even more consequential for proportions. Grain oil percentage is a ratio of oil mass to total grain mass. If an allele changes grain mass while oil mass remains unchanged, the ratio changes. If both oil mass and total mass increase proportionally, the ratio does not change even though the plant produces more oil per grain. Therefore a genetic influence on a percentage trait can originate from variation in either the numerator or denominator. Measuring both components allows an investigator to distinguish alternative explanations for a difference in the ratio.

A similar issue arises when comparing growth rates. A plant growing from 10 to 20 cm has increased by 10 cm and doubled its initial height; a plant growing from 20 to 30 cm has also increased by 10 cm but its relative increase is only 50 percent. Depending on the question, the appropriate phenotype could be absolute growth, percentage growth or the rate of change over time. Genetic contributions that are additive on one scale need not remain additive on another. The model must be fitted to a scale justified by the biological measurement, rather than chosen solely because it produces a familiar ratio.

To summarize a small set of observations, calculate the arithmetic mean by dividing their sum by the number of observations. For five plant heights of 16,18,20,22 and 24 cm, the mean is 20 cm. The deviations from the mean are −4,−2,0,2 and 4 cm. Their sum is zero by construction, so merely adding signed deviations does not quantify spread. Squaring them gives 16,4,0,4 and 16 cm², with sum 40 cm². If these five measurements are the entire defined population, its variance is 40/5=8 cm². If they are treated as an independent sample used to estimate an underlying population variance, the usual sample variance is 40/(5−1)=10 cm². The distinction between the two denominators should be explicit in numerical questions.

The sample denominator of N−1 compensates for estimating the population mean from the same sample, under the usual independent identically distributed sampling assumptions. The five deviations are constrained to sum to zero: once four deviations and the mean are known, the fifth is not independent. This is one way to understand the loss of one degree of freedom. The standard deviation is the square root of the relevant variance, so the sample standard deviation in the example is about 3.16 cm. It describes the dispersion of observations and should not be confused with the standard error of their mean.

If independent measurements have variance σ², the mean of N such measurements has variance σ²/N. The standard error of the mean is therefore σ/√N when σ is known. In practice the sample standard deviation can provide an estimate. If a set of sixteen independent plants has a sample standard deviation of 4 cm, the estimated standard error of its mean is 1 cm. It would be incorrect to say that most individual plants vary by only 1 cm around the mean. The standard error addresses uncertainty in the estimate of the mean; the standard deviation addresses variation among individual plants.

This distinction also helps interpret an experimental graph. Error bars may denote standard deviation, standard error or a confidence interval. They are not interchangeable, and their apparent overlap cannot be interpreted correctly without knowing which quantity they represent and how the measurements were sampled. A bar chart presenting means with no description of the error bars is insufficient to evaluate the strength of a difference. A scatter plot showing individual observations may expose heterogeneous spread or an unusual observation that is obscured by summary statistics alone.

A large sample can improve precision, but it cannot repair a systematically biased design. Suppose all plants carrying a high-score genotype are measured after an extra week of growth relative to plants carrying a low-score genotype. Measuring thousands of plants with extreme instrument precision would not separate genotype effects from the effect of age. Randomization, matched developmental stages and replication address different problems from sample size. A student evaluating an experiment should first ask whether the comparison is biologically meaningful, then whether the variation and uncertainty have been quantified appropriately.

14. Combining environmental and genetic distributions

A useful way to move from a genetic class model to an observed quantitative distribution is to examine the expected phenotype within each genotype. Suppose a cross generates three genotypes with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. In the absence of environmental spread, their phenotypes are fixed at 8,10 and 12 units. The overall mean is obtained by weighting each class mean by its probability: 8/4+10/2+12/4=10. The genetic class distribution has variance 1/4(8−10)²+1/2(10−10)²+1/4(12−10)²=2 units squared. If every genotype also experiences independent environmental deviations with mean zero and variance 3 units squared, the overall mean remains ten, and the overall variance becomes five units squared.

The reason the variance increases can be seen by writing an observation as the sum of its genotype-specific mean and a deviation from that mean. Across the entire population there is variation between genotype means, as well as variation among individuals within a genotype. When the deviations average to zero within each genotype, the total variance is the weighted average of within-genotype variances plus the variance of the genotype-specific means. The expression captures two distinct sources of spread. This observation prepares for genetic variance partitioning in the next node; it does not by itself tell us how to separate additive, dominance and interaction components in a population.

Importantly, the within-genotype variances need not all be identical. Imagine that the three genotype-specific means remain 8,10 and 12, but the within-genotype variances are now 1,4 and 9 units squared. The weighted within-genotype variance is 1/4×1+1/2×4+1/4×9=4.5. Adding the between-genotype component of two gives a total variance of 6.5 units squared. The third genotype produces a more dispersed phenotype than the first, even though their mean values remain in the same additive relationship. This is an example of genotype-dependent variation around a mean.

Biologically, some genotypes may be more sensitive to small environmental fluctuations than others. A buffering mechanism might maintain a relatively constant developmental output over a range of environmental conditions. An allele that alters this buffering could increase the variation among genetically similar individuals without necessarily shifting their mean by a large amount. Developmental stability is thus a relevant distinction from mean trait value. Nevertheless, observing unequal within-group variances does not automatically identify a buffering gene. Unequal measurement quality, variation in developmental stage or different ranges of exposure can also produce unequal spread.

Consider another hypothetical example in which genetically identical plants are divided between two environments. In environment one their mean is 10 and in environment two their mean is 20, with variance one within each group. If equal numbers of plants are sampled from both environments, the combined population has mean 15. Its variance is the within-group variance of one plus the variance of the two means, which is 25. The overall variance is therefore 26 despite the absence of segregating variation among plants. A researcher who pools the observations without recording the environments could incorrectly interpret the broad, perhaps bimodal, distribution as evidence for two genetic classes.

Conversely, a mixture of genotypes can produce a unimodal distribution if their means are close relative to the spread within each. These two examples explain why modality cannot reliably count genetic groups. A mode is a region with high observed density, not a directly observed genotype. The number of peaks can also change with histogram bin width or sample size. Too narrow a bin width in a small dataset produces a jagged plot, while too wide a bin width merges distinct clusters. Visual examination is helpful for generating hypotheses but must be followed by comparisons that account for genotypes, environment and measurement.

The normal distribution is particularly useful when interpreting continuous variation near the centre of a distribution. Its standardized form has mean zero and standard deviation one. Transforming a measured value P into z=(P−μ)/σ expresses its distance from the mean in standard-deviation units. The transformation preserves rank when σ is positive, while changing the numerical units. A plant 6 cm above a mean of 30 cm in a population with standard deviation 3 cm has z=2. A second plant 6 cm above the mean in a population with standard deviation 6 cm has z=1. Their absolute differences are equal, but their relative positions within their respective distributions differ.

If the distribution is approximately normal, a z-score of two corresponds to an upper-tail fraction of about 2.3 percent. This interpretation is not valid merely because a z-score was calculated. Standardization can be performed for skewed or multimodal distributions as well, and it does not transform them into normal distributions. The appropriate probability calculation depends on the actual distribution or the stated model. Examination questions may explicitly assume normality; in biological data analysis, it should be examined rather than taken for granted.

For threshold traits, the mean and dispersion both influence the proportion above a fixed threshold. If the threshold lies above the mean, widening a symmetric normal distribution with the same mean increases the upper-tail fraction. If the threshold lies below the mean, the same widening reduces the above-threshold fraction toward one half. At a threshold equal to the mean, any symmetric continuous distribution has half its observations above and half below, irrespective of its spread. Thus even the direction of a change in prevalence due to variance depends on the position of the threshold. A student should locate the threshold relative to the distribution before predicting how a shift or widening changes the outcome.

15. Designing and interpreting a factorial genotype–environment experiment

A factorial experiment compares more than one factor simultaneously, with a set of combinations chosen so that their separate and joint influences can be examined. For the present chapter the two factors may be genotype and environment. Suppose two inbred plant lines are tested at two nutrient levels. Each line is represented by several independent plants at each level. The four experimental groups are X-low, X-high, Y-low and Y-high. Randomizing pot positions within the greenhouse and using the same trait measurement protocol reduces systematic differences that are unrelated to genotype or treatment.

To understand the comparison, imagine the following explicitly hypothetical group means for final biomass in grams: X-low=10, X-high=18, Y-low=14 and Y-high=20. Within the low-nutrient environment, the Y line exceeds X by four grams. Within the high-nutrient environment, Y exceeds X by two grams. The response of X to high nutrients is eight grams, whereas that of Y is six grams. The interaction contrast is eight minus six, or two grams, when the X response is used as the reference direction. Equivalently the difference between Y and X narrows by two grams at the higher nutrient level.

The two reaction norms are nonparallel, but they do not cross because Y remains larger than X in both environments. This example is especially helpful because students sometimes identify interaction only when the preferred genotype changes. A difference in the magnitude of a genotype effect is sufficient for interaction on the observed scale. A change in rank is one particularly consequential type of interaction, not its definition. Whether the two-gram interaction is statistically reliable depends on replication and variability; a difference between sample means alone is not proof that the corresponding population means differ.

Suppose the group means instead are X-low=10, X-high=18, Y-low=14 and Y-high=22. The nutrient response is eight grams for both lines, and the Y−X difference is four grams at both nutrient levels. The mean structure is additive with respect to genotype and treatment. Both genotype and environment influence the trait, even though their interaction contrast is zero. This pattern clarifies that the description “multifactorial” is compatible with the absence of a genotype–environment interaction in a specified experimental range.

In a balanced two-by-two experiment, the marginal genotype means are the averages of the two environmental means for each genotype. For the first dataset, X has marginal mean 14 g and Y has marginal mean 17 g. The marginal difference is three grams. The environment means, averaging over the two genotypes, are 12 g at low nutrients and 19 g at high nutrients. The average treatment response is seven grams. These marginal comparisons summarize the data, but they do not replace the conditional comparisons because the response differs between lines. If the experimental objective is to identify the line most suited to a specific environment, the conditional means may be more relevant than the marginal means.

An unbalanced design changes the interpretation of a marginal mean calculated from pooled individuals. If X is represented mainly at low nutrients while Y is represented mainly at high nutrients, the pooled mean difference includes differences in the representation of environments. The comparison is confounded by the design. One can calculate means at each environment when both genotypes are represented, but a missing group prevents direct estimation of the complete two-by-two interaction. This is why a question presenting a table of group means and group sample sizes should be read carefully before a pooled comparison is accepted as a genetic effect.

Replication enables a second distinction: variation among independent plants in the same group versus differences between group means. Suppose each of the four groups has one plant. The four observations can still be used to calculate an interaction contrast, but the design does not provide an independent estimate of plant-to-plant variability within groups. If the groups each contain twelve independent plants, the spread of their measurements supplies more information about the uncertainty of group means. The experiment is then capable of distinguishing a consistent treatment response from fluctuations caused by uncontrolled individual differences, assuming the replication is biologically independent.

A greenhouse may nevertheless contain environmental gradients. Pots near a window might receive more light than those at the centre. If all X plants are placed near the window and all Y plants farther away, light is confounded with genotype. Randomization helps distribute such influences among treatment groups. Blocking can be used when environmental gradients are known: a block may contain representative plants of every genotype and nutrient combination, allowing comparisons within relatively similar locations. Blocking is not a method for increasing genetic variation; it is a design method for reducing unwanted environmental variation in comparisons.

When a phenotype is measured in several environments, reaction norms connect only the environments actually observed. Two points determine a straight line segment for illustration, but they do not establish that the response is linear at intermediate or more extreme nutrient levels. A genotype may exhibit saturation, an optimum or a decline at high exposure. To infer the shape of a reaction norm, several treatment levels are needed. To generalize across seasons or sites, the relevant range of environments should be represented. The same line may perform well in a controlled greenhouse yet respond differently in a field because the environment contains multiple changing factors.

These principles also apply beyond crop experiments. Genetically distinct laboratory model organisms can be compared under different diets, temperatures or developmental conditions. Genetically uniform cell lines may be used to test how a regulatory variant alters response to a signal. The essential logic remains the same: observe both genetic backgrounds under matched environmental treatments, define a meaningful phenotype, use suitable independent replication, and interpret conditional differences. A quantitative genetic conclusion is strongest when the experimental design can distinguish the alternative explanations it claims to separate.

16. Integrating polygenic models: a worked comparison of alternative hypotheses

Consider a hypothetical set of plant crosses in which two inbred lines differ in mature fruit length. The low parent averages 20 mm and the high parent averages 32 mm. Both parent lines have a within-line standard deviation near 1 mm, and the F1 averages 26 mm with a similar spread. In the F2, plants are observed across a wider range, including several near both parental means. The F2 shows a broad distribution whose centre is also near 26 mm. These observations are consistent with an additive explanation, but the precise inheritance model remains to be tested.

An initial hypothesis is that three independently segregating loci contribute two millimetres per increasing copy. The predicted parental difference is 2×3×2=12 mm, and the F1 score is three increasing copies, giving 26 mm. The genotype-specific F2 means are 20,22,24,26,28,30 and 32 mm, with expected frequencies 1:6:15:20:15:6:1. Under this model the F2 genetic variance is c²n/2=6 mm². If environmental variance is one square millimetre in every generation and independent of genotype, the expected F2 phenotypic variance is seven square millimetres. This is a definite quantitative prediction that can be compared with new observations.

A second hypothesis proposes two independently assorting loci, with per-copy effects of two and four millimetres. The parental difference and F1 mean are unchanged: two increasing copies of each effect total 12 mm at the high extreme, and one of each gives a six-millimetre F1 increase. The possible F2 mean scores are again 20,22,24,26,28,30 and 32 mm because the effect of the second locus is twice that of the first. However, their frequencies differ: 1:2:3:4:3:2:1. The genetic variance is (2²+4²)/2=10 mm², and under the same environmental assumption the total variance is eleven square millimetres. Both models have seven ideal classes and identical parental means, yet they make different predictions about dispersion.

A third hypothesis proposes a single additive locus with a per-copy effect of six millimetres. The F2 contains three genotypic means, 20,26 and 32 mm, with relative frequencies 1:2:1. The genetic variance is 6²/2=18 mm²; adding the assumed environmental variance gives nineteen square millimetres. If the measured environmental spread is indeed near one millimetre, separate groups around the three means are much more likely to be visible than under either multilocus explanation. Nevertheless, an environment with greater spread, genotype-dependent responses or classification errors could blur these distinctions. The observations should be compared with explicit models rather than a single expectation about the appearance of a histogram.

A controlled F2 sample with phenotypic variance near eleven square millimetres would be more compatible with the second model than the first or third under the common assumptions. But sampling variation affects every estimated variance. Moreover, equal within-parent variances do not guarantee equal within-genotype variances in the F2. Some segregants may express greater environmental sensitivity. Linkage could change the distribution of genetic scores. The second model should therefore be treated as a promising hypothesis, not as established fact based solely on a variance comparison.

The experiment can be strengthened by measuring large replicated groups, characterizing the distribution of parental and F1 phenotypes in the same environment, and comparing genetic markers with observed length. If the two-locus hypothesis predicts that plants of a particular marker combination will have a mean fruit length near 26 mm, the prediction should be examined in an independent sample or progeny from a suitable cross. Marker associations may reflect linkage to causal variants rather than the effect of the marker itself. Detailed mapping is reserved for node 8.E.05, but the distinction between an association and a mechanism is already essential to evaluating these competing models.

There is a further conceptual possibility. The two parental lines could differ in fruit length because one has a longer period of cell proliferation and the other has a different rate of expansion. Their F1 mean could be intermediate on the final-length scale while its developmental components are not intermediate. Measuring cell number and average cell length at matched developmental stages could reveal how the difference arises. The number of loci estimated from final length would not identify these physiological pathways without additional evidence. The strength of an integrative experiment is that genetic prediction can guide the choice of biological measurements, and biological measurements can suggest alternative genetic models.

Consider now the effect of classifying the fruits as short or long using a cutoff of 28 mm. Each of the three hypothetical inheritance models could yield a different proportion above that cutoff. Environmental deviations around a class mean of 28 mm will send some observations into either category. An exact categorical ratio therefore depends on whether the cutoff is inclusive, how the phenotype is measured, and the distribution of environmental deviations. This is a simple illustration of how information is lost when quantitative measurements are reduced to categories. It also provides a direct conceptual bridge to liability-threshold models.

The combined reasoning in this example involves no new inheritance principle beyond Mendelian segregation, probability and a specified genotype-to-phenotype map. Its difficulty lies in comparing several plausible explanations for the same partial observation. In examination problems, look for the condition that discriminates the models: expected extremes, different relative class frequencies, genetic variance, reaction norms or molecular evidence. When none of these is supplied, a conclusion about exact locus number may not be justified. Recognizing insufficient evidence is an important form of scientific mastery, not a failure to solve the problem.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Continuous means non-Mendelian

Each contributing locus can segregate normally even when combined effects form a smooth distribution.

Uppercase means dominant

In the additive example uppercase indicates a score-increasing allele. A heterozygote can be intermediate.

Polygenic means multiple alleles at one locus

Polygenic describes several loci; multiple allelism describes alternatives at one locus.

Five classes always indicate two genes

The 2n+1 formula requires equal additive effects and the specified F2 cross.

The central class is exclusively double heterozygous

AAbb and aaBB share the same equal-effect score as AaBb.

Both extreme classes have probability 1/4^n together

One specified extreme has that probability; both together have twice that probability.

Backcross ratios equal F2 ratios

Only the heterozygous parent contributes variable alleles in the low-parent backcross.

Dominance alters allele transmission

Dominance describes expression, not segregation probability.

Epistasis is physical linkage

Epistasis is a dependence of effects; linkage concerns transmission.

Unequal additive effects imply epistasis

Locus effects can differ in size yet remain additive across genetic backgrounds.

A normal distribution proves polygeny

Environmental variation and other processes may generate similar shapes.

All environmental effects are G×E

Parallel reaction norms show environmental response without an interaction on that scale.

G×E requires crossing reaction norms

Nonparallel lines suffice; crossing is a special case.

Transgressive segregation requires a mutation

Existing alleles from complementary parents can generate extreme combinations.

Binary outcomes must be monogenic

A continuous liability may be converted to a binary phenotype by a threshold.

An exact observed ratio proves the genetic model

Agreement establishes compatibility, not unique causal identification.

Original examination practice

1. Part B: Which property defines polygenic inheritance?

A. Multiple phenotypes per gene; B. Multiple loci influence one trait; C. More than two alleles per diploid locus; D. No Mendelian segregation

Answer and explanation

B. The defining feature is several contributing loci. A describes pleiotropy; C confuses polygeny with multiple allelism; D is unnecessary.

2. Part B: Under the ideal equal-effect F2 model, four loci yield how many score classes?

A. 4; B. 8; C. 9; D. 16

Answer and explanation

C. K runs from zero through eight increasing copies, giving nine classes. The number of allelic positions is eight, not the number of classes.

3. Part B: What is the expected proportion of a specified extreme F2 phenotype for three independent loci?

A. 1/8; B. 1/16; C. 1/32; D. 1/64

Answer and explanation

D. Each required homozygous state has probability 1/4. Their joint probability is (1/4)³=1/64.

4. Part B: Which pair is compatible with zero dominance deviation?

A. aa=2,Aa=4,AA=6; B. aa=2,Aa=6,AA=6; C. aa=2,Aa=7,AA=6; D. aa=2,Aa=2,AA=6

Answer and explanation

A. The midpoint is four. Options B and D describe complete dominance toward opposite homozygotes; C is above both.

5. Part B: Which observation demonstrates a genotype-by-environment interaction on the measured scale?

A. Both genotypes increase by 3 units; B. One increases by 8 and another by 2; C. Both have the same mean in every environment; D. An environmental factor changes both equally

Answer and explanation

B. Different response increments imply nonparallel reaction norms. A and D describe an equal environmental response.

6. Part B: What is liability in a threshold model?

A. A single necessarily recessive allele; B. An underlying continuous propensity; C. The observed binary category itself; D. A population allele frequency

Answer and explanation

B. Liability is the latent quantitative variable. The threshold converts it into a recorded category.

7. Part C: An AaBb F2 has equal additive effects. Among individuals with score two, what fraction are AaBb?

A. 1/6; B. 1/3; C. 2/3; D. 1

Answer and explanation

C. AaBb has probability 4/16. The total score-two class has probability 6/16, giving conditional probability 4/6=2/3. AAbb and aaBB account for the rest.

8. Part C: An F1 heterozygous at four equal-effect independent loci is crossed to the low parent. What fraction of progeny receive exactly one increasing copy?

A. 1/16; B. 1/4; C. 1/2; D. 3/4

Answer and explanation

B. Four independently variable gametic positions give C(4,1)/2⁴=4/16. Applying the eight-position F2 distribution would be incorrect.

9. Part C: Homozygous two-locus means are 5 for aabb, 9 for AAbb, 8 for aaBB and 15 for AABB. What is the interaction contrast?

A. 0; B. 3; C. 7; D. 10

Answer and explanation

B. 15−9−8+5=3. The additive prediction is 5+4+3=12; the excess is three units.

10. Part C: In an F2 from AABB × aabb, complete coupling-phase linkage and equal additive effects are assumed. Which classes occur?

A. 0,1,2,3,4 equally; B. 0,2,4 in 1:2:1; C. 1,2,3 in 1:2:1; D. All at score two

Answer and explanation

B. Only AB and ab gametes occur. Their unions yield aabb, AaBb and AABB with probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4.

11. Part C: Two populations have identical normal liability variance and threshold. Population Y has a higher mean liability. What follows?

A. Lower above-threshold fraction; B. Higher above-threshold fraction; C. Identical fraction; D. Necessarily more loci

Answer and explanation

B. With fixed threshold and variance, a higher mean shifts the distribution closer to and beyond the threshold. It does not identify the number of loci.

12. Part C: Genotype A is grown only in rich soil and B only in poor soil; A is taller. Which conclusion is warranted?

A. A has greater genetic height; B. Rich soil alone caused the difference; C. G×E is proven; D. Genetic and environmental contributions are confounded

Answer and explanation

D. Neither genotype is evaluated in both environments, so the main effects and interaction cannot be separated. A balanced factorial comparison is necessary.

13. Part B: Which is a meristic trait?

A. Number of seeds per pod; B. Exact stem length; C. Blood group; D. Nucleotide identity at one locus

Answer and explanation

A. A meristic trait is a count with integer values. Length is continuous; the other options are categorical characteristics.

14. Part B: Which statement about an essential enzyme locus is correct?

A. It must explain most variation; B. It can be essential yet contribute little segregating variation; C. Its alleles are necessarily dominant; D. Its effects cannot depend on background

Answer and explanation

B. If all individuals carry functionally similar alleles, the locus can be essential but account for little or no within-population variation.

15. Part B: What is the dominance deviation if aa=4, Aa=9 and AA=10?

A. 2; B. 3; C. 5; D. 7

Answer and explanation

A. The homozygote midpoint is 7; 9−7=2. The half-difference is 3, which is a distinct parameter.

16. Part B: For n independent biallelic diploid loci, what is the maximum number of distinguishable multilocus genotypes in an F2?

A. n²; B. 2n+1; C. 3^n; D. 4^n

Answer and explanation

C. Each locus has three possible genotypes. Multiplying independent possibilities gives 3^n; many genotypes can share phenotypic scores.

17. Part B: If an additive model describes a positive quantity after logarithmic transformation, what may be true on the original scale?

A. All alleles have no effect; B. Independent relative effects multiply; C. Segregation must cease; D. Genotype frequencies must be equal

Answer and explanation

B. Logarithms convert products into sums. The phenotypic scale does not change Mendelian transmission.

18. Part B: What causes an increase in expected phenotypic variance when genotype contributions are positively correlated?

A. A negative covariance term; B. A positive covariance term; C. A change in measurement units only; D. Necessarily an increased number of loci

Answer and explanation

B. Variance of a sum includes twice the covariance; positive covariance raises it relative to independent contributions.

19. Part B: Which is a correct interpretation of the Castle–Wright estimate under unequal effects?

A. It counts causal genes precisely; B. It may be fractional as an effective factor number; C. Negative estimates identify recessive genes; D. It is independent of parental differences

Answer and explanation

B. It describes the observed segregation variance and parental contrast relative to an ideal equal-effect model.

20. Part B: A fixed allele contributes what to within-population segregation variance in a simple additive model?

A. Zero; B. One-half of its effect; C. Its squared effect always; D. Twice its effect

Answer and explanation

A. A fixed allele does not vary among individuals. It can affect the mean while contributing no segregation variance.

21. Part C: Two independent loci have per-copy effects of one and two units. In the F2, what is the probability of score two?

A. 1/16; B. 2/16; C. 3/16; D. 6/16

Answer and explanation

C. A=2,B=0 gives 1/16 and A=0,B=2 gives 2/16. Their total is 3/16.

22. Part C: Four independent loci each add 1.5 cm per increasing copy. What is the ideal F2 genetic variance?

A. 3 cm²; B. 4.5 cm²; C. 6 cm²; D. 9 cm²

Answer and explanation

B. The count variance is n/2=2. Multiplying by the squared contribution 2.25 gives 4.5 cm².

23. Part C: In an F2 with one specified extreme probability 1/64, what is the chance of no such extreme in 64 independent plants?

A. Exactly zero; B. 1/64; C. (63/64)^64; D. 63/64

Answer and explanation

C. Every individual must independently miss the extreme. Expected count one does not guarantee observing one.

24. Part C: The two reciprocal F2 extreme classes each have probability p. Which expression gives the chance that both occur at least once in a sample of N?

A. 1−(1−2p)^N; B. 2p; C. 1−2(1−p)^N+(1−2p)^N; D. p^N

Answer and explanation

C. Inclusion and exclusion subtracts samples missing each class and adds samples missing both. A describes at least one of either extreme.

25. Part C: Two homozygous lines differ by 12 units. F2 variance is 9 and estimated environmental variance is 3. Under the Castle–Wright assumptions, estimate n.

A. 2; B. 3; C. 4; D. 6

Answer and explanation

B. Segregation variance is 6; 12²/(8×6)=144/48=3.

26. Part C: Effects are c1=2 and c2=6 in an independent additive cross. What effective factor estimate follows from the ideal unequal-effect derivation?

A. 1; B. 1.6; C. 2; D. 4

Answer and explanation

B. (2+6)²/(2²+6²)=64/40=1.6, less than the true two segregating loci because effects are unequal.

27. Part C: A gene increases product accumulation only when a precursor-producing locus is functional. What evidence best distinguishes this from linkage?

A. Two loci are on the same chromosome; B. The product effect differs in matched genotypes differing at the precursor locus; C. One locus shows a 1:2:1 ratio; D. Parents differ in total product

Answer and explanation

B. Comparing conditional effects in defined backgrounds tests epistasis. Linkage concerns gamete transmission rather than biochemical dependence.

28. Part C: In a cold-treatment flowering experiment genotype X flowers 30 days earlier after cold, while Y changes by 2 days. What follows from the means alone?

A. The trait must be monogenic; B. An interaction on the days scale is suggested; C. FLC is necessarily the only causal locus; D. There is no environment effect

Answer and explanation

B. Different treatment responses suggest G×E; whole-genotype comparisons do not isolate the causal variants.

29. Part C: Selected and control maize populations differ in oil concentration when grown in the same replicated environment. What inference is most appropriate?

A. All effects are equal; B. The difference is compatible with inherited differences, but the number and effects of loci require further study; C. Environmental effects never occur; D. The difference proves a single locus

Answer and explanation

B. The common environment supports an inherited contribution without establishing its precise architecture.

30. Part B: What is the standard error of a mean primarily a measure of?

A. Variation among observations; B. Uncertainty of the estimated mean; C. Number of alleles; D. The maximum value

Answer and explanation

B. The standard error quantifies sampling uncertainty of the mean. Standard deviation measures individual dispersion.

31. Part B: Which two terms describe distinct sources of variance in a mixture of genotypes?

A. Within-group variance and variance of group means; B. Mean and median only; C. Allele and locus count; D. Standard error and sample size only

Answer and explanation

A. The total variance comprises weighted within-group variance and between-group mean variation.

32. Part B: When a symmetric continuous liability distribution has its threshold exactly at its mean, what fraction exceeds the threshold?

A. Zero; B. One quarter; C. One half; D. It necessarily equals the variance

Answer and explanation

C. Symmetry places equal probability on either side of the mean when the distribution has no point mass at the threshold.

33. Part B: Which phenotype is a ratio rather than an absolute amount?

A. Oil mass per kernel; B. Kernel mass; C. Oil concentration as a percentage of kernel mass; D. Total oil produced per plant

Answer and explanation

C. A percentage is oil mass divided by total mass, and can change due to the numerator or denominator.

34. Part B: What does randomizing positions in a greenhouse principally accomplish?

A. Guarantees no variance; B. Reduces systematic confounding of groups with position; C. Increases the number of loci; D. Forces equal effects

Answer and explanation

B. Randomization helps distribute positional influences across groups. It does not remove all noise or alter the causal genes.

35. Part B: Which characteristic defines a factorial two-genotype, two-environment design?

A. Every genotype appears only in one environment; B. Both genotypes are represented in both environments; C. Only the largest genotype is sampled; D. Environmental treatments are unrecorded

Answer and explanation

B. All four combinations are necessary for a direct comparison of each genotype's environmental response.

36. Part B: What is the unit of variance when height is measured in centimetres?

A. cm; B. cm²; C. cm³; D. no units

Answer and explanation

B. Variance averages squared deviations, so its units are squared. The standard deviation uses centimetres.

37. Part B: Which feature of a phenotype histogram is sufficient by itself to count causal loci?

A. A bell shape; B. Number of visible peaks; C. Range; D. None of these

Answer and explanation

D. Genetic architecture, environmental effects, sampling and scoring can produce similar distributions.

38. Part C: Five heights are 16,18,20,22,24 cm. What is the usual sample variance?

A. 4 cm²; B. 8 cm²; C. 10 cm²; D. 40 cm²

Answer and explanation

C. Mean=20; sum of squared deviations=40; divide by N−1=4 to obtain 10 cm².

39. Part C: Sixteen independent plants have standard deviation 4 cm. What is the estimated standard error of their mean?

A. 0.25 cm; B. 1 cm; C. 4 cm; D. 16 cm

Answer and explanation

B. s/√N=4/4=1 cm. The individual standard deviation is still 4 cm.

40. Part C: Genotype means are 8,10,12 with probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4. Within-genotype variance is 3 for each. What is total variance?

A. 2; B. 3; C. 5; D. 10

Answer and explanation

C. The between-mean variance is 2 and the average within-group variance is 3, giving 5.

41. Part C: Two equally represented environments contain genetically identical organisms with means 10 and 20 and within-group variances 1 each. What is the pooled variance?

A. 1; B. 5; C. 25; D. 26

Answer and explanation

D. The pooled mean is 15; between-environment variance is 25, within variance is 1, total 26.

42. Part C: For X-low=10, X-high=18, Y-low=14 and Y-high=20, what is the X-minus-Y response contrast?

A. 0; B. 2; C. 4; D. 8

Answer and explanation

B. X responds by eight; Y by six. The contrast is two despite no rank reversal.

43. Part C: An ideal F2 has parental difference 12 and mean 26. Three equal-effect loci of 2 units each are proposed. If environmental variance is 1, what is predicted phenotypic variance?

A. 4; B. 6; C. 7; D. 13

Answer and explanation

C. Genetic variance is 2²×3/2=6; plus independent environmental variance one gives seven.

44. Part C: A second model for the same cross has per-copy effects 2 and 4. What F2 variance is predicted when environmental variance is 1?

A. 7; B. 9; C. 10; D. 11

Answer and explanation

D. The genetic component is (4+16)/2=10; add one to obtain eleven.

45. Part C: A fixed threshold lies above the mean of a normal liability distribution. The mean stays constant and the standard deviation increases. What is predicted?

A. The upper-tail fraction increases; B. It decreases; C. It stays zero; D. Locus number doubles

Answer and explanation

A. The standardized threshold distance (T−μ)/σ decreases, so a larger upper-tail fraction exceeds the fixed cutoff.

46. Part C: Line X has mean 10 in low nutrients and 18 in high nutrients; line Y has 14 and 22. Which model is compatible with the means?

A. Mean-scale additive genotype and nutrient effects; B. Rank-reversal interaction; C. No nutrient effect; D. Complete genetic identity

Answer and explanation

A. Both increase by eight and Y exceeds X by four in both conditions. The difference-in-differences is zero.

47. Part C: A two-locus score distribution has seven equally spaced means. Which additional result would support an unequal-effect two-locus model over a three-locus equal-effect model?

A. F2 class ratio 1:2:3:4:3:2:1; B. Seven classes alone; C. Both parents homozygous; D. F1 intermediate

Answer and explanation

A. The unequal effects 1 and 2 yield this ratio, whereas three equal loci predict 1:6:15:20:15:6:1.

48. Part C: A study measures 20 kernels from each of five independently segregating plants and treats all 100 kernels as independent plant genotypes. What is the problem?

A. Kernels cannot be weighed; B. Measurements are clustered within plants; C. The trait becomes qualitative; D. Alleles stop segregating

Answer and explanation

B. Kernels from a plant share its genetic background and plant environment; treating them as independent plant observations overstates replication.

49. Part C: A plant with the high parental score is observed in an F2, but genotyping shows it lacks one increasing copy. What explains this without invoking a mutation?

A. Environmental deviation can offset the genetic score difference; B. High phenotypes never have low alleles; C. Mendelian segregation is impossible; D. Chromosomes must have doubled

Answer and explanation

A. Phenotypes overlap because deviations within genotypes may offset their mean differences. Phenotypic resemblance does not guarantee identical genotype.

50. Part C: Two independent positive-effect loci have per-copy effects 1 and 5. What is the ideal F2 genetic variance?

A. 3; B. 6; C. 13; D. 26

Answer and explanation

C. The variance equals one-half of the sum of squared effects: (1+25)/2=13.

51. Part C: A plant is 6 cm taller than a mean of 30 cm in a population whose standard deviation is 3 cm. What is its standardized score?

A. −2; B. 0.2; C. 2; D. 12

Answer and explanation

C. z=(36−30)/3=2. A normal-tail interpretation additionally requires a normal model.

52. SET: Assertion: Independent assortment does not imply additive phenotypic effects. Reason: Assortment concerns gamete transmission, whereas additivity concerns genotype expression.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false

Answer and explanation

A. Segregation and expression are distinct. Independently transmitted loci can interact in a common biological pathway.

53. SET: Assertion: A polygenic phenotype must be continuous. Reason: A threshold can convert continuous liability into a discrete outcome.

A. Both true; B. Assertion true, reason false; C. Assertion false, reason true; D. Both false

Answer and explanation

C. A polygenic trait can be categorical. The threshold mechanism provides one explanation.

54. SET: Which paired observation is strongest evidence of a context-dependent genotype effect?

A. Two lines are grown in different fields; B. Matched replicate lines differ in their response to the same two temperature treatments; C. One line has a large standard deviation; D. Two families share a phenotype

Answer and explanation

B. A balanced comparison tests response differences while controlling genotype and treatment representation.

55. SET: A calculated effective number of factors is 1.6. Which interpretation is most defensible?

A. There are literally 1.6 genes; B. The estimate is an effective quantity under model assumptions; C. There must be two mutations in one cell; D. The data must always be discarded

Answer and explanation

B. Unequal effects and other departures can generate noninteger estimates. The value is not a direct molecular count.

Revision and mastery

Polygenic traits emerge from the combined effects of several loci. Under independent equal additive contributions in the F2, scores range from zero to 2n and follow a binomial distribution. Dominance, epistasis, linkage and environmental heterogeneity modify the simple prediction. A liability threshold can turn continuous susceptibility into a categorical observation.

  4. Derive 2n+1 and list its assumptions.
  5. Calculate both extreme frequencies for three independent loci.
  6. Distinguish F2 and low-parent backcross class ratios.
  7. Derive the one-locus dominance deviation.
  8. Compute an epistasis contrast from four homozygous means.
  9. Explain how linkage changes transmission without requiring epistasis.
