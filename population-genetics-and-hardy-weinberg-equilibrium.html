From Mendelian families to populations

A cross between two heterozygous individuals predicts the distribution of their offspring, but it cannot tell us how common those parental genotypes are among thousands of organisms. Population genetics supplies this missing scale of explanation. Its object is the distribution of inherited variants in a reproducing population and the changes in that distribution through time. Instead of beginning with the genotype of a known parent, the population geneticist begins with observations of many individuals and asks how often particular alleles are available for transmission. This distinction matters because a very uncommon allele may behave in an entirely ordinary Mendelian fashion when two carriers happen to mate.

A population must be defined with respect to the question being asked. All organisms assigned to a species need not constitute one freely interbreeding group. Individuals living in separate habitats, breeding at different times or separated by geographic barriers may exchange genes infrequently. A collection of samples from several such populations is therefore not automatically a single biological population. In a study of flowering plants, for example, the distribution of pollen among plants determines the appropriate scale of a breeding population more directly than an arbitrary political boundary. In an examination problem, the population is usually specified by the problem statement. In biological data, its boundaries are an inference that deserves scrutiny.

The gene pool is the collection of genetic variants carried by the members of the population. At an ordinary diploid autosomal locus each individual contributes two copies to the pool of sampled alleles. The two copies need not be different. A homozygote contributes two copies of the same allele; a heterozygote contributes one copy of each of two alleles. It is helpful to imagine counting copies on homologous chromosomes rather than counting organisms that possess a particular trait. This prevents the common error of treating a heterozygote as contributing two copies of a dominant allele because it displays the dominant phenotype.

An allele frequency is a proportion of copies of a specified locus, whereas a genotype frequency is a proportion of individuals with a particular pair of alleles. Both are dimensionless, and each complete set of categories sums to one. Neither frequency is identical to the rate at which a new mutation arises. A mutation may originate in one individual and subsequently change in frequency through inheritance and population processes. Similarly, an allele's frequency does not specify its effect on phenotype or reproductive fitness. The purpose of separating these quantities is to keep the evidence about inheritance distinct from the evidence about function.

Counting alleles and genotypes

Consider N diploid individuals with complete, reliable genotypes at a biallelic autosomal locus. Let the counts of AA, Aa and aa be x, y and z respectively. Then N = x + y + z. The genotype frequencies are x/N, y/N and z/N. Since there are 2N copies of the locus, the allele frequencies are p = (2x + y)/(2N) and q = (2z + y)/(2N). Adding the two numerators gives 2N, proving that p + q = 1. Equivalently, if genotype frequencies are denoted f(AA), f(Aa) and f(aa), p = f(AA) + f(Aa)/2. These are counting identities and remain valid even if mating is highly nonrandom or selection is occurring.

Suppose that a hypothetical sample contains 50 AA, 30 Aa and 20 aa individuals. The genotype proportions are 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20. There are 130 A copies and 70 a copies among 200 total copies, so p = 0.65 and q = 0.35. The heterozygote proportion is 0.30, not 0.35 or 0.65. No assumption of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was needed. A student who starts by taking the square root of the frequency of aa in this problem would obtain a different and unjustified result. When genotypes are directly observed, use the counts rather than an equilibrium assumption.

The distinction between allele frequency and genotype frequency is especially clear when two different samples have the same gene pool proportions. A population of only heterozygotes has p = q = 0.5. Another population with equal numbers of AA and aa homozygotes, but no heterozygotes, has exactly the same two allele frequencies. The populations differ maximally in their observed heterozygosity. The allele frequencies alone cannot reconstruct the current genotype distribution without an additional model. This is precisely the need answered by the Hardy–Weinberg relationship.

The denominator depends on the inheritance system and on the actual number of successful genotype observations. If ten out of one hundred individuals lack a genotype at a locus, dividing the counts from ninety complete diploids by two hundred would not correctly describe the observed samples. The appropriate denominator is one hundred and eighty called allele copies, assuming all ninety are diploid and the missing observations are excluded. Such an estimate may still be biased if missingness is related to genotype. Good counting is necessary but does not make an unrepresentative sample representative.

Why random union of gametes yields equilibrium proportions

Mendelian segregation predicts that an Aa parent transmits A and a with equal probability, provided there is no segregation distortion. If AA, Aa and aa adults contribute equally to a gamete pool on average, the frequency of A among their gametes equals p, and that of a equals q. Random union means that the identity of an allele supplied by one gamete does not alter the probability of the allele supplied by its partner. When both contributing gamete pools have the same allele frequencies, the probability of drawing A from both pools is p multiplied by p. The corresponding probability for aa is q multiplied by q. A heterozygote can be formed through two mutually exclusive routes: A from the first pool and a from the second, or a from the first pool and A from the second. Their probabilities add to pq + qp = 2pq.

These probabilities form the genotype distribution p², 2pq and q². Their sum is the expansion of (p + q)², which is one because the two alleles exhaust the alternatives at the locus. The mathematical expansion is therefore more than an exercise in algebra: each term corresponds to a biologically distinct outcome of gamete union. The factor two in the middle term does not imply that heterozygotes possess more chromosomes than homozygotes; it accounts for two possible parental origins of the alleles.

The new generation has allele frequency p' = p² + (2pq)/2 = p² + pq = p(p + q) = p. The same reasoning gives q' = q. Thus if the same ideal conditions continue, the genotype distribution is reproduced in subsequent generations. Alleles have not blended or disappeared simply because one phenotype masks another. In 1908 Godfrey Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg independently established the mathematical principle now named after them. Its significance was that particulate inheritance could coexist with stable proportions of genetic variants.

For a concrete example, take the two contrasting populations described previously, both with p = q = 0.5. If random union of equally frequent A and a gametes occurs, both generate offspring with expected proportions 0.25 AA, 0.50 Aa and 0.25 aa. It is not necessary for the parental adults to have begun with those proportions. One round of ideal random union is enough for an autosomal locus when the gamete pools have equal allele frequencies. This statement concerns expected zygote proportions; viability selection between fertilization and adult sampling could alter the observed adults.

The Hardy–Weinberg model should be understood as a null model rather than as a description of every natural population. In a finite population, the number of gametes that actually contribute is limited, and realized allele frequencies fluctuate. An expectation of 25 homozygotes among 100 offspring does not require every sample to contain exactly 25. Probability predicts the distribution of possible samples, not the exact result of each one.

Assumptions and the distinction between genotype and allele stability

Random mating at the focal locus and equal allele frequencies in the contributing gamete pools establish the simple autosomal genotype expectation. Additional idealizations establish stable allele frequencies through successive generations: there is no selection favoring a genotype, no mutation converting copies between allelic classes, no migration introducing or removing a disproportionate share of alleles, and a population sufficiently large that sampling fluctuations are negligible for the model. These assumptions do not all have identical logical roles. Random mating creates a particular arrangement of alleles into individuals; other processes can change the pool from which the alleles are drawn.

For instance, preferential mating among genetically similar individuals can increase homozygosity while initially leaving allele frequencies unchanged. Conversely, migration may change allele frequencies between generations, but random mating within the newly mixed breeding population can produce equilibrium proportions at its new allele frequencies. Therefore an observed fit to p², 2pq and q² is not proof that every evolutionary force is absent. It is evidence that the observed genotype proportions are compatible with the equilibrium model at that locus, stage and sampling scale.

Dominance is a relationship between alleles expressed in a heterozygote. It is not a force that automatically increases the frequency of one of them. A fully dominant allele may be rare, and a recessive allele may be common. A dominant phenotype can mask a recessive copy without changing the segregation of that copy during meiosis. If dominance is associated with a difference in fitness, selection may change the allele frequencies, but the effect then depends on differential reproductive contribution rather than dominance by itself.

Expected heterozygosity at a biallelic locus is 2pq. Replacing q with 1 − p gives 2p(1 − p). This expression has its maximum of one half at p = q = 0.5, as seen by completing the square: 2p(1 − p) = 0.5 − 2(p − 0.5)². When one allele is fixed, the expected heterozygosity is zero. The maximum applies only to a two-allele locus under the equilibrium model, not to arbitrary observed genotype distributions or loci with many alleles.

Recessive phenotypes and carrier estimation

A classic application occurs when a fully penetrant autosomal recessive phenotype is associated with genotype aa and the population can reasonably be modeled by Hardy–Weinberg proportions. If the prevalence of that phenotype is I, the model gives q² = I. The nonnegative solution q = √I is the estimated recessive allele frequency, and p = 1 − q. The heterozygote frequency is 2q(1 − q). When the recessive allele is rare, p is close to one and the carrier frequency is approximately 2q. The approximation neglects the term 2q², so it always overestimates the exact equilibrium heterozygote proportion for positive q.

For an illustrative prevalence of one affected individual per ten thousand, I = 0.0001 and q = 0.01. The exact heterozygote estimate is 2 × 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.0198, or 1.98 percent. The approximate answer is two percent. In a population of ten thousand, the corresponding expected counts would be 9801 AA, 198 Aa and 1 aa. The recessive allele is much more often found in heterozygotes than in affected homozygotes in this example. Consequently a rare recessive phenotype may coexist with a substantially larger number of phenotypically unaffected carriers.

Conditional probabilities require particular care. The proportion of all individuals who are carriers is 2pq. Among unaffected individuals, the proportion who are carriers is 2pq/(1 − q²). For q = 0.1, the unconditional carrier frequency is 0.18, but the conditional carrier frequency among unaffected people is 0.18/0.99, approximately 0.1818. In a family of two known carrier parents, the probability that an unaffected child is a carrier is instead two thirds under complete penetrance and Mendelian segregation. These different answers are not contradictory: they describe different conditioning events and sources of evidence.

The prevalence method has biological limits. A phenotype may result from several different genes, incomplete penetrance, environmental influences or ascertainment differences. Survival to the sampled age may differ between genotypes. A local population may have a different recessive allele frequency from a nationwide aggregate. Under such circumstances, applying a square root to a reported phenotype prevalence may not estimate the relevant allele frequency. An examination question normally specifies the model assumptions, but a real population estimate should establish them or qualify its inference. The calculation is an educational population model, not individual diagnostic advice.

More than two alleles

An autosomal locus can possess several alleles in a population even though a diploid individual has at most two copies at that locus. Let three alleles have frequencies p, q and r, where p + q + r = 1. The random union expansion of (p + q + r)² produces three homozygote probabilities p², q² and r² and three heterozygote probabilities 2pq, 2pr and 2qr. Each distinct heterozygote has two possible parental arrangements. For k alleles there are k homozygote classes and k(k − 1)/2 unordered heterozygote classes, giving k(k + 1)/2 diploid genotype categories.

The classical ABO blood group system provides a useful teaching example. The common allelic classes Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant with each other, while both are conventionally treated as dominant over i in the simplified model. Let their respective frequencies be p, q and r. Phenotype A combines IᴬIᴬ and Iᴬi, with total expected frequency p² + 2pr. Phenotype B combines IᴮIᴮ and Iᴮi, with expected frequency q² + 2qr. Phenotype AB corresponds to IᴬIᴮ, with frequency 2pq, and phenotype O corresponds to ii, with frequency r². This illustrates why phenotype counts and genotype counts are not interchangeable.

If p = 0.3, q = 0.2 and r = 0.5, the expected phenotype proportions are 0.39 A, 0.24 B, 0.12 AB and 0.25 O. Their sum is one. Genotype proportions can be recovered from the assumed allele frequencies, but a phenotype A individual may carry either two Iᴬ alleles or one Iᴬ and one i allele. The probability that an individual with phenotype A has genotype Iᴬi is 0.30/0.39, approximately 0.769. This is another example of conditioning on evidence. The elementary ABO model excludes rarer molecular and epistatic exceptions.

For a locus with k alleles of frequencies pᵢ, expected homozygosity is the sum of their squared frequencies. Expected heterozygosity is one minus this sum, since any diploid genotype is either homozygous or heterozygous at the locus. With four equally frequent alleles the expected heterozygosity is 1 − 4(0.25²) = 0.75. Thus the biallelic maximum of 0.5 is not a universal bound.

X-linked allele frequencies and generational dynamics

For a typical X-linked locus outside the pseudoautosomal regions of an XX/XY species, XX individuals possess two copies of the locus and XY individuals possess one. The latter are hemizygous rather than homozygous. If there are Nf completely genotyped XX individuals and Nm completely genotyped XY individuals, the total count of X-linked allele copies is 2Nf + Nm. The numerator is the number of copies of the focal allele across the two classes. Applying a diploid autosomal denominator to both groups would underestimate the frequency.

When the allele frequency q is the same in both sex-specific gamete pools, the expected XX genotype proportions are (1 − q)², 2q(1 − q) and q², while the fraction of XY individuals carrying the allele on their sole X is q. For an X-linked recessive trait with the usual simplified expression assumptions, the affected proportion among XY individuals is therefore q, not q². If q = 0.04, the model predicts four percent affected among XY individuals and 0.16 percent affected among XX individuals. It predicts 7.68 percent heterozygotes among XX individuals. These percentages have different denominators and should not be added to obtain a total population prevalence without weighting by the proportions of the two groups.

If the allele frequencies differ between the sexes, the ordinary one-generation autosomal conclusion cannot be applied unchanged. Let qf(t) denote the frequency in XX individuals and qm(t) that in XY individuals in generation t. Under random mating, Mendelian transmission and equal reproductive contribution within each group, the next generation has qm(t+1) = qf(t), since sons receive their X from their mother. Daughters receive one maternal and one paternal X, giving qf(t+1) = [qf(t) + qm(t)]/2. Subtracting these equations shows that the difference qf − qm reverses sign and is halved each generation. With equal numbers of the two sexes, the weighted total frequency [2qf + qm]/3 stays constant in this ideal model.

For example, with qf = 0.3 and qm = 0.6, the next values are 0.45 and 0.30. The following values are 0.375 and 0.45. The differences decrease in magnitude around a weighted total of 0.4. The expected daughter genotype distribution during this transient is obtained by multiplying the different maternal and paternal probabilities, not by squaring their average. This illustrates how inheritance architecture changes the route to equilibrium even though alleles remain particulate.

Testing expected genotype counts against observations

A genotype count seldom matches its theoretical expectation exactly. An equilibrium test asks whether the discrepancy is unusually large under an appropriate null model. For a biallelic diploid autosomal sample, estimate p and q by gene counting, then calculate expected counts Np², 2Npq and Nq². These are expected values, so they need not be integers. Rounding before the test introduces unnecessary error. Pearson's chi-square statistic is the sum, across genotype classes, of (observed minus expected)² divided by expected. A large discrepancy contributes more when the expected count is small.

Return to the example with 50 AA, 30 Aa and 20 aa. With p = 0.65 and q = 0.35, equilibrium predicts 42.25 AA, 45.5 Aa and 12.25 aa. The three contributions are approximately 1.42, 5.28 and 4.90, giving chi-square approximately 11.60. Under the usual large-sample approximation with one degree of freedom, this exceeds the five percent critical value of approximately 3.84. The data provide evidence against the equilibrium null. The excess of both homozygotes and deficit of heterozygotes invite investigation but do not uniquely establish a biological mechanism.

The degrees of freedom are not simply the number of genotype categories minus one. Their total is constrained by the sample size, and one independent allele frequency was estimated from the same sample. Three categories minus one total constraint minus one fitted allele parameter leaves one degree of freedom. If frequencies were specified independently rather than estimated from the counts, the test design and degrees of freedom would differ. For k alleles, with all categories included and regular large-sample conditions, there are k(k + 1)/2 genotype categories and k − 1 independently estimated allele frequencies, leaving k(k − 1)/2 degrees of freedom.

Sparse expected counts undermine the asymptotic chi-square approximation. A rare allele can yield very few expected homozygotes even in a large sample. Wigginton, Cutler and Abecasis (2005) demonstrated that conventional chi-square tests may have poorly calibrated false-positive rates at low minor-allele counts. Exact tests condition on the observed allele count and evaluate possible genotype configurations under random pairing. The resulting probability distribution is discrete. This is a more appropriate approach for many sparse samples, rather than interpreting a tiny expected homozygote count using the usual chi-square threshold without qualification.

A nonsignificant result means that the evidence is insufficient to reject the specified null at the chosen significance level. It does not prove random mating or biological stasis. Low sample size may reduce power, while very large samples may detect small departures. A significant departure also requires interpretation: related samples, population mixture, genotyping errors, selection and nonrandom mating are potential explanations. The next chapter distinguishes several of these biological mechanisms. Here the essential result is the separation of a calculable statistical discrepancy from its causal interpretation.
