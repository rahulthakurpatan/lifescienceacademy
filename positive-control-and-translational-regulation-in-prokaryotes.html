1. The problem of choosing a nutrient

A bacterial cell does not obtain an advantage merely by possessing a gene that encodes a useful enzyme. The enzyme has to be made at a time when its activity will support growth or survival. Consider an Escherichia coli cell that encounters two carbohydrates, glucose and lactose. Lactose can supply carbon and energy after it has been transported into the cell and hydrolysed. Glucose can enter familiar central metabolic reactions without first requiring the complete lactose-utilisation system. Producing large amounts of lactose permease and beta-galactosidase while an easily utilisable carbon source is already providing sufficient metabolic flux may consume resources without an equivalent immediate benefit. Conversely, suppressing those enzymes permanently would deprive the organism of a useful alternative carbon source. The regulatory challenge is therefore not simply to identify a sugar but to coordinate the use of different sugars with the existing metabolic state.

The distinction between a substrate and a signal is important. Lactose is a substrate for beta-galactosidase. The related disaccharide allolactose is the physiological inducer that changes the DNA-binding behaviour of LacI. Glucose availability, meanwhile, influences an intracellular signalling system that includes cyclic AMP and the transcriptional regulator CRP. These signals do not carry identical information. The lactose-dependent branch of regulation tells the cell whether there is a reason to express the lactose-utilisation genes, whereas the carbon-status branch helps determine whether their promoter receives additional transcriptional stimulation. A single nutrient can influence more than one molecular event, so the two branches are a useful starting model rather than two perfectly independent switches in an intact cell.

A useful way to recognise the physiological importance of the response is to imagine the experiment at two scales. In a culture flask, the investigator may observe an increase in turbidity, a change in extracellular sugar concentrations and a change in enzyme activity. In each cell, those observations depend on events such as transport across the membrane, phosphorylation of signalling proteins, binding of a regulatory protein to DNA, initiation of RNA synthesis and recruitment of ribosomes to the resulting RNA. The culture curve does not reveal which one of those events limits growth. A convincing mechanistic explanation must connect population measurements to molecular evidence and must allow for delays between a change in the medium and the appearance of new enzyme.

In the early investigation of bacterial enzyme adaptation, Jacques Monod and other researchers observed that growth with mixtures of carbon sources could occur in distinct phases. In the classic diauxic pattern, growth on one substrate is followed by a transition interval and renewed growth supported by the alternative substrate. The pause is not a universal requirement of mixed-substrate metabolism: organisms, strains, substrates and experimental conditions differ. Its significance is that an organism may possess the genes needed to consume both substrates but nevertheless change its metabolic programme over time. The genetic framework developed by Jacob and Monod explained regulated enzyme synthesis through regulatory products acting on DNA. Recognition of the importance of cAMP and CAP added a further layer: release of repression alone need not produce the maximal rate of transcription.

Consider why a simple explanation based only on LacI is insufficient. If lactose were the sole relevant input, the presence of lactose should produce the same lac promoter output whether glucose were present or absent, provided that inducer concentrations and all other cellular conditions were matched. Yet carbon status can influence lac expression even when the repressor has been relieved. The missing regulatory component is a positive contribution to transcription initiation. Removing a molecular obstacle and helping the transcription machinery proceed are different operations. A gene may have neither an active obstacle nor an effective activator; its output can then be intermediate rather than maximal. This distinction underlies much of the reasoning used to interpret carbon-control mutants.

The term catabolite repression describes the decreased expression of certain pathways for alternative nutrient utilisation in the presence of preferred carbon sources. It is named after the observed phenotype, not after a requirement that a repressor protein directly bind each target gene. At the lac promoter in the conventional E. coli model, an important component of this reduction is the loss of CRP-dependent activation. It is therefore possible to describe the physiological effect as repression while describing the local molecular mechanism as positive control. There is no contradiction as long as one specifies which process is being discussed. Other bacteria can use different regulatory systems to achieve a comparable preference, so the terms catabolite repression and cAMP–CRP must not be treated as synonyms across all organisms.

2. How a positive transcriptional regulator works

A promoter is a DNA region at which RNA polymerase assembles in a configuration that permits transcription initiation. The fact that a DNA sequence is recognised by RNA polymerase does not mean that every binding event gives rise to a full transcript. The enzyme may bind reversibly, undergo structural transitions associated with DNA opening, initiate short RNA products or escape the promoter to become an elongating complex. For a regulated promoter, one or more of these steps can be sufficiently unfavourable that the basal rate of productive transcription is low. An activator changes the probability or rate of productive events. The activator is not the enzyme that joins nucleotides into RNA; it alters the behaviour of the transcription machinery.

The catabolite activator protein, CAP, is also called the cyclic AMP receptor protein, CRP. The two names refer to the same regulatory protein in this E. coli example. CRP is a homodimer, meaning that two copies of the same protein subunit form the functional complex. Its molecular organisation permits interaction with a small signalling molecule and recognition of particular DNA sequences. Binding of cAMP changes the conformational distribution of the protein, favouring a form capable of effective sequence-specific DNA recognition under physiological conditions. The DNA-binding portions of the two subunits interact with related features of the recognition site. This is an example of allosteric regulation: binding at one region of a molecule modifies properties of a different region rather than simply occupying the site at which the final action takes place.

The chemical identity of cyclic AMP helps prevent several errors. ATP is the precursor from which adenylyl cyclase forms 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate. The product contains a cyclic phosphate linkage and is not equivalent to ordinary AMP. The reaction also releases pyrophosphate. Although the carbon atom numbering and chemistry matter to the identity of the molecule, its regulatory role here is not to transfer a phosphate to CRP. The protein binds the cyclic nucleotide noncovalently. cAMP is a signalling ligand, not a protein kinase, and CRP does not need to phosphorylate RNA polymerase to activate the lac promoter. Intracellular cAMP concentration reflects synthesis, degradation and export as well as the physiological condition of the cell.

At the wild-type lac promoter, the classic CRP site is centred approximately 61.5 base pairs upstream of the transcription start site. The negative coordinate means upstream relative to the first transcribed nucleotide; it does not mean a position inside the RNA at a negative distance from its own beginning. A cAMP-bound CRP dimer can bind this upstream site and induce bending of the DNA. Its appropriately positioned activating surface contacts the C-terminal domain of an alpha subunit of RNA polymerase. This contact increases favourable interactions involved in promoter recruitment and productive initiation. In this standard class I activation example, the activator is not required to occupy the operator at which LacI acts. The separate spatial arrangement makes it possible for the promoter to receive positive and negative regulatory influences at the same time.

Sequence-specific DNA recognition and transcriptional activation are experimentally separable functions. Imagine a CRP variant that binds its usual DNA site when cAMP is present but has a damaged surface needed to contact the alpha subunit of RNA polymerase. A DNA-binding assay could still show a protein–DNA complex. Yet transcription from the lac promoter might remain near the unactivated level because binding has not been converted into a productive interaction with polymerase. In a second variant, the activating surface may be functional but the DNA-binding portion may fail to recognise the site. In that case the activator is not correctly positioned and transcription again fails to receive normal stimulation. Similar phenotypes in enzyme-output measurements can therefore have distinct structural explanations.

The position of the DNA site is also informative. DNA is helical, so a protein bound to one side of the helix may be placed unfavourably to interact with a second protein bound elsewhere. In a conceptual experiment, moving the CRP site by roughly half a helical turn while preserving its sequence could change the relative orientation of the activator and RNA polymerase. A full-turn displacement could restore a favourable orientation, although distance and local DNA structure also matter. This prediction illustrates why the presence of a binding-site sequence alone is not enough to guarantee function. Correct sequence recognition, local geometry and the chemical properties of the interacting surfaces all contribute to activation.

Not all activators use the same promoter architecture. Depending on the site and regulatory context, an activator can assist recruitment, facilitate a transition after binding or counteract another inhibitory influence. Even a single regulatory protein can have different effects at different promoters. For example, a DNA-bound factor positioned where polymerase needs to bind can interfere with transcription instead of helping it. Calling CRP an activator in the lac system does not establish that every CRP–DNA interaction throughout the genome has an activating consequence. An examination question that changes the position of a recognition site must therefore be answered from the geometry and mechanism given, rather than from the historical name of the protein.

The local regulatory effect is also distinct from positive feedback. A CRP complex promoting transcription of lacZYA does not, merely by doing so, establish a positive feedback loop in CRP production. Feedback requires a downstream consequence to influence an upstream part of the pathway. Lactose transport and the production of inducer can participate in a reinforcing circuit in the native lac system, but this is a separate relationship from the direct activating contact between CRP and polymerase. In a well-drawn regulatory diagram each arrow should connect named molecular variables, rather than attaching the word positive to an entire pathway without specifying what increases.

3. From glucose uptake to a carbon-status signal

To understand why CRP changes activity in different media, it is necessary to connect the extracellular nutrient to an intracellular biochemical state. In E. coli, the phosphoenolpyruvate-dependent phosphotransferase system, abbreviated PTS, couples transport of many sugars to their phosphorylation. Phosphoenolpyruvate donates phosphoryl groups through a series of protein intermediates. In the glucose-specific route, the phosphorylation state of EIIA Glc is particularly relevant for gene regulation. It is not simply the presence of extracellular glucose that produces the response; phosphate transfer during sugar transport changes the distribution of phosphorylated and unphosphorylated forms of PTS components.

When glucose transport through this pathway is substantial, the phosphoryl group is passed onwards through the glucose transport machinery and ultimately to the incoming sugar. Under the usual conditions used to explain this system, EIIA Glc tends towards its unphosphorylated form. When glucose transport falls, transfer of phosphoryl groups to glucose falls and the phosphorylated form can accumulate. This statement describes a balance of reactions, not a permanent assignment of one chemical state to each nutrient. The relative pools depend on phosphate supply, metabolic flux, transport substrates and cellular conditions. Such qualification is useful because the PTS is not an isolated measuring device: its components participate in metabolism and regulation simultaneously.

Phosphorylated EIIA Glc promotes the activity of adenylyl cyclase in the conventional E. coli scheme. Consequently, when glucose uptake is low, cAMP can increase, and greater availability of cAMP favours formation of active CRP complexes. The causal sequence therefore runs from a change in the usage of the transport system to a change in protein phosphorylation, then to activity of a signalling enzyme, concentration of a diffusible nucleotide and finally the behaviour of a DNA-binding regulator. None of these intermediate steps requires glucose to attach directly to the lac promoter. The direction of the response is easily remembered once the intermediates are understood: active glucose uptake tends to reduce the pool of phosphorylated EIIA Glc, so the positive contribution to cAMP production is diminished.

A second effect of the PTS is important because it prevents a mistaken interpretation of the two sugar inputs. The unphosphorylated form of EIIA Glc can interact with lactose permease, LacY, and inhibit lactose transport. This is called inducer exclusion. Less lactose entry may mean less allolactose formation, leaving LacI more active at its operator. The PTS can thus influence both the positive regulatory branch through cAMP and the negative regulatory branch through substrate entry. The two effects are not identical. Inducer exclusion can alter the activity of LacY already present in the membrane, whereas loss of CRP stimulation changes the rate at which new transcripts are produced. Their time courses and experimental signatures can differ.

Imagine two cultures that are identical except that in one an experimental inducer can reach the intracellular repressor without relying substantially on native lactose transport. If glucose is then supplied, the direct activation effect mediated by CRP can be investigated while reducing the complication caused by inducer exclusion. IPTG, isopropyl beta-D-thiogalactoside, is commonly used as a relatively nonmetabolised lac inducer in experimental systems, but its uptake may still depend on permease under some conditions. A careful experiment should choose an appropriate concentration, transport background and measurement to justify its assumption of equivalent induction. Stating that IPTG always enters every bacterial cell independently of transport is not a valid simplification.

One can distinguish the two glucose effects using separate readouts. A transcriptional reporter whose promoter requires CRP but whose expression does not depend on LacY-mediated inducer uptake can report a carbon-status change at transcription. A transport measurement on cells containing comparable amounts of LacY can reveal a rapid change in uptake. Measurements of cAMP help connect the first reporter response to the signalling intermediate, but cAMP concentration alone cannot establish that transcription changed through CRP. An additional strain lacking functional CRP or carrying a mutated recognition site tests that dependence. If both responses change in a native lac assay, the final enzyme activity cannot be assigned entirely to either route without these additional controls.

This network also explains a common rescue experiment. A strain with an inactive adenylyl cyclase, encoded by cyaA, cannot produce its normal cAMP signal by that route. Supplying cAMP from outside the cell may restore a CRP-dependent response if enough nucleotide becomes available intracellularly, CRP and its recognition site are functional and other regulatory conditions permit expression. In contrast, providing cAMP does not replace a missing CRP protein. Even in the first strain, rescue of the promoter does not establish rescue of every glucose-dependent process, because cAMP need not correct inducer exclusion or other metabolic consequences. The ability to distinguish biochemical bypass from general physiological restoration is central to interpreting genetic evidence.

The term low glucose is therefore shorthand for a defined carbon-status context in which the canonical response is observed. A cell growing on a different carbon source may display regulatory conditions that are not predictable from a binary statement about glucose alone. Likewise, intracellular cAMP does not necessarily reach zero in the presence of glucose. The textbook model is useful because it describes a causal direction and predicts robust changes in carefully controlled comparisons. Its boundaries become important whenever an examination problem provides measured cAMP concentrations, transport rates or altered PTS components instead of merely listing the sugars in the medium.

4. Integrating lactose and glucose at one promoter

A model of the lac promoter can now combine two molecular conditions. First, effective LacI repression must be sufficiently relieved for transcription to proceed. Second, CRP-dependent activation determines whether an available promoter operates at a stimulated rate. It is convenient to denote relief of repression by I and activation by A, assigning each a value of zero or one in an idealised binary model. The largest output occurs when I and A are both one. This resembles a logical AND requirement for high expression. It does not mean that transcription is exactly zero whenever one condition fails; a weak promoter may retain basal activity, and repression itself may be incomplete.

With glucose available but lactose absent, the usual model predicts substantial LacI repression together with weak CRP activation. The cell has neither the inducer signal nor the favourable carbon-status signal for strong expression. With glucose absent and lactose absent, CRP may become active under appropriate carbon conditions, but the operator remains occupied frequently by LacI. The activator does not substitute for the inducer, because it does not remove the repressor from the operator. There can be rare productive events, but the usual expectation is low output. This condition is especially revealing when students assume that high cAMP is sufficient to turn on the operon regardless of lactose.

With both glucose and lactose available, the textbook two-input table predicts that CRP activation remains weak and lactose may relieve LacI repression, permitting limited transcription. In the native cell, however, inducer exclusion can reduce lactose entry and prevent full relief of repression. Consequently the actual output can be lower than the simplified table suggests. The table must state that effective induction is assumed when lactose is marked present. With lactose available and glucose unavailable, lactose entry and conversion to allolactose can relieve the operator block, while the carbon-status pathway favours activation. The two permissive influences coincide and lac transcription is high relative to the other three conditions under the conventional assumptions.

Consider a constitutive operator mutation that prevents normal LacI binding but leaves the promoter and CRP site intact. In such a mutant, lactose no longer controls the operator through LacI. Nevertheless, glucose can still influence transcription through CRP. The word constitutive therefore applies to the loss of dependence on a particular negative regulatory input, not automatically to a constant maximal expression rate across all environments. A related result is expected for a simple loss of functional LacI, although a diffusible wild-type LacI protein supplied by a second genetic copy could complement that mutation. The operator mutation affects its own linked promoter and cannot be corrected by supplying more wild-type repressor if the operator is unable to recognise it.

A noninducible LacI superrepressor provides the opposite comparison. Assuming its operator recognition is retained, it fails to respond normally to inducer. Raising cAMP does not ordinarily remove this inhibitory constraint. A mutation that weakens the CRP recognition sequence without affecting the operator has a different effect: lactose-dependent relief remains possible, but the promoter cannot receive its usual stimulation from CRP. Supplying additional cAMP or wild-type CRP may fail to restore the particular linked promoter if the recognition sequence is destroyed. This comparison is a practical method for distinguishing cis-acting DNA alterations from trans-acting regulator deficiencies while retaining the carbon input as part of the reasoning.

A genetically engineered CRP that functions independently of cAMP can be considered in an idealised problem if the mutation is explicitly described. Such a variant might maintain activation at a compatible promoter even when the usual carbon-status signal is low. It would not automatically restore lactose transport during glucose uptake, nor would it necessarily overcome LacI repression. It is important to specify whether a proposed mutation affects ligand binding, the allosteric transition, DNA recognition, interaction with polymerase or the expression of the regulator itself. A name such as crp mutant is insufficient to predict a unique phenotype without information about the type of molecular change.

The combined model makes it possible to explain diauxic growth without reducing the phenomenon to a single gene switch. During the first growth phase, high glucose flux can support rapid proliferation and reduce the expression or activity of alternative utilisation systems. As glucose declines, the signalling and transport constraints can relax. If lactose remains, induction and activation allow accumulation of the required proteins. The cell then uses the alternative substrate more effectively and growth can resume at a different rate. The transition duration also depends on the pre-existing protein pool, the ability to synthesise new molecules, population heterogeneity and other physiological adjustments. A lag is consistent with induction of metabolic capacity but does not alone prove that lac regulation is the only cause.

5. Quantitative interpretation of activation and growth

Binary tables describe the direction of regulation, but biochemical molecules interact at finite concentrations. Suppose an active regulator A binds one DNA site D reversibly to produce a complex AD. Define the dissociation constant Kd as the product of the free concentrations of A and D divided by the concentration of AD at equilibrium. If the concentration of DNA sites is small compared with the free regulator pool, binding to this one site does not substantially change that pool. Under the additional assumption that binding equilibrates much faster than the process being measured, the fraction of sites occupied is theta = [A]/(Kd + [A]). This relationship follows by expressing total sites as [D] + [AD] and using the definition of Kd to replace [AD] with [A][D]/Kd.

The quantity [A] in this expression represents free, DNA-binding competent regulator, not the total number of all CRP monomers and not the cAMP concentration itself. The actual relation between cAMP concentration, CRP conformation and DNA-site occupancy can be more complicated than a single binding equilibrium. Using this model for examination purposes requires that the active dimer be treated as one ligand for one DNA site. The equation then predicts that at [A] = Kd half the DNA sites are occupied. It also predicts saturation: doubling free activator when the site is already occupied most of the time produces much less additional occupancy than doubling it near the low-concentration part of the curve.

To connect occupancy with an observable, suppose an unrepressed promoter initiates at rate r0 while unoccupied and at rate r1 while occupied. The weighted average initiation rate is r = r0(1 − theta) + r1 theta. This model treats each occupancy state as having a constant characteristic rate. It neglects intermediate complexes, interactions with other regulators and changes in RNA stability. If r1 exceeds r0, increasing the active regulator increases the predicted average rate. The maximal rate under this model is r1, not an arbitrarily large number. If the DNA-binding complex is present but cannot contact polymerase, r1 might instead approach r0, causing the activation curve to become almost flat even though the occupancy curve remains normal.

As a hypothetical numerical example, let Kd be 20 nM, r0 be 2 transcripts per minute per promoter and r1 be 22 transcripts per minute per promoter. With 5 nM free active regulator, theta is 5/25 or 0.20 and the predicted output is 2 + 20 × 0.20 = 6 transcripts per minute. With 20 nM active regulator, theta is 0.50 and r is 12 transcripts per minute. With 80 nM activator, theta is 0.80 and r is 18 transcripts per minute. These values are constructed for teaching and are not measured lac promoter kinetics. They illustrate how a graded molecular response can underlie a qualitative classification into weak and strong activation.

If a second mutation increases Kd from 20 to 80 nM while all other quantities remain equal, an active regulator concentration of 20 nM yields occupancy 20/100 = 0.20 rather than 0.50. The corresponding rate falls from 12 to 6 transcripts per minute. This reduction is consistent with weakened affinity. However, observing the same fall in RNA production would not by itself establish an affinity change, because a reduction in r1 can also lower the output. Independent binding data are needed to discriminate these models. An experiment that observes DNA binding and transcription simultaneously can reveal whether the defect precedes or follows formation of the DNA-bound complex.

Population growth measurements introduce different mathematical variables. In an exponential phase, the increase in cell number N can be approximated by dN/dt = mu N, where mu is a specific growth rate with dimensions of inverse time. Separating the variables and integrating gives ln(N2/N1) = mu(t2 − t1). The doubling time is ln 2/mu. For a constructed culture that increases from one million to four million cells per millilitre in two hours during an exponential phase, mu = ln 4/2 = 0.693 per hour, and the doubling time is one hour. During a diauxic transition the assumption of constant exponential growth is not valid over the whole interval. A single fitted doubling time spanning both growth phases and the transition may obscure the underlying biology.

A hypothetical culture may have a rapid first exponential phase and a slower second phase because its rates of nutrient uptake and metabolic conversion differ. The observation that a promoter reporter is strongly expressed during the slower phase is not a contradiction. Expression of an alternative pathway is beneficial relative to being unable to use the remaining nutrient, not necessarily relative to unrestricted growth on the preferred nutrient. In an exam graph, enzyme expression, substrate depletion and optical density can change at different times. A mechanistic inference should identify which variable is directly measured, whether it represents a concentration or a rate, and whether the observed delay is consistent with synthesis and turnover of the relevant molecules.

6. Sigma-factor switching: changing a cell-wide transcription programme

The regulation of a single promoter illustrates how a particular gene can respond to a metabolite. A change in temperature, nutrient status or envelope condition, however, may require coordinated expression of many genes whose DNA sequences are scattered across a chromosome. These genes need not belong to one operon. A regulon is a collection of genes or operons sharing control by a particular regulator. A change in the availability or activity of a sigma factor is one mechanism that can redirect bacterial transcription across many such targets. The molecular issue is not how the cell can create a new genetic code but how existing RNA polymerase molecules can select different sets of initiation sites.

The bacterial RNA polymerase core is competent to catalyse RNA synthesis but does not, on its own, recognise the usual promoter classes with the specificity supplied by a sigma factor. Association of a sigma factor with the core forms a holoenzyme with characteristic promoter-recognition properties. In E. coli, the major sigma factor RpoD, also called sigma 70, is responsible for transcription of many genes needed during ordinary growth. Alternative sigma factors can confer recognition of other promoter groups. The same central catalytic machinery is thus used with different recognition partners. The sigma factor does not become a separate RNA polymerase and it does not translate the transcripts whose synthesis it directs.

A comparison of two promoter sequences can clarify the specificity problem. Suppose promoter X contains recognition elements appropriate for a holoenzyme carrying the primary sigma factor and promoter Y contains elements that favour a holoenzyme carrying a stress-associated sigma factor. If core polymerase is limiting and the two sigma factors compete for it, changes in the relative amounts of holoenzymes can influence the balance of transcription from the two promoters. This is not a simple exchange in which all primary-sigma-dependent genes stop the moment the alternative factor appears. Some primary-factor promoters may retain substantial transcription, and some alternative-factor promoters may require additional conditions for efficient initiation. The overall output reflects the combination of recognition, availability and promoter-specific regulation.

A useful terminology distinction is between a promoter and a regulon. Promoter recognition concerns the properties of a particular site at which RNA synthesis begins. Membership in a regulon concerns a collection of genes whose expression responds to a common regulatory influence. One promoter can respond to more than one influence, and a gene may receive transcripts from alternative promoters recognised by different holoenzymes. The protein encoded by the gene does not necessarily change when transcription begins at an alternative upstream site, but the resulting RNA leader can differ. Consequently the transcriptional decision can also alter the subsequent translational behaviour or stability of that RNA. An experimental measurement of total coding-region RNA may conceal differences in the proportions of promoter-specific transcripts.

During heat shock in E. coli, RpoH, or sigma 32, helps direct transcription of genes involved in protection from elevated temperature, including genes encoding protein-folding chaperones and proteolytic functions. Heat can disturb protein conformation and increase the need for quality control. The adaptive response requires both recognition of altered cellular conditions and production of proteins capable of limiting or repairing the resulting disruption. RpoH provides a regulatory link because its cellular abundance and activity respond to temperature and protein-folding status, and its association with RNA polymerase promotes transcription from appropriate heat-shock promoters.

The level of RpoH is influenced by more than one kind of regulation. The efficiency of translation of its own mRNA can change with temperature because RNA structural features alter accessibility near the initiation region. The stability and activity of the protein are also controlled by protein quality-control systems. At higher temperature, unfolded proteins can change the availability of chaperone machinery that normally helps restrain sigma 32 activity and turnover. As heat-shock proteins accumulate and the folding burden is reduced, feedback can contribute to attenuation of the response. A rise in the amount of a stress-associated sigma factor is therefore neither equivalent to a permanent rise in transcription of every target nor necessarily caused by increased transcription of the sigma-factor gene itself.

This distinction becomes especially valuable when a problem presents time courses. Suppose a culture is shifted to a higher temperature and RpoH protein rises rapidly while the measured abundance of rpoH RNA changes little. Translation or protein stability may have changed. If the protein subsequently falls despite continued elevation of temperature, negative feedback or renewed protein turnover may account for the transient response. The result does not establish exactly which mechanism operated. To support a translation-based explanation one could compare rates of synthesis from an appropriate RNA-leader reporter. To support a stability-based explanation one could measure turnover of the protein. An experiment observing only the final amount of the protein cannot separate its production from its removal.

RpoS, or sigma 38, provides a different example of an alternative sigma factor. It contributes to the general stress response of E. coli, especially in nutrient limitation and stationary-phase conditions. The term general stress response refers to a collection of adaptive outputs, not one single chemical pathway. A cell experiencing an unfavourable environment may reduce allocation towards rapid reproduction while improving protection against other challenges. RpoS-dependent regulation can contribute to that shift. Its synthesis, degradation and ability to compete for core polymerase are all subject to regulation. Several small RNAs also influence translation of its mRNA, establishing a useful bridge between RNA-level control and large-scale transcriptional reorganisation.

A sigma factor can be regulated even when its total abundance stays constant. In representative systems, an anti-sigma factor binds a sigma factor and prevents it from effectively participating in transcription initiation. A signal that changes the interaction can release the sigma factor without first requiring production of an additional protein molecule. The resulting change in available sigma factor can therefore occur on a different timescale from a response that relies mainly on transcription and translation of the sigma gene. The essential inference is that the total concentration of a regulatory molecule is not always the same as its active or available concentration.

One should not extrapolate the architecture of a specific anti-sigma system to every alternative sigma factor. Many sigma-family proteins recognise promoter elements through related structural principles, but their modes of regulation differ. Some promoter-recognition systems have specialised requirements. For instance, sigma 54-dependent transcription generally requires a suitable ATP-hydrolysing activator to help convert a promoter-bound complex into an open complex, rather than following exactly the standard sigma 70 pathway. This exception is useful at examination level because it shows that a promoter-bound holoenzyme is not always sufficient for productive initiation. Detailed regulatory subfamilies are not necessary to understand that general point.

The competition idea can be explored using a deliberately simplified model. Let C be core polymerase and S1 and S2 be free sigma factors. If the binding equilibria have dissociation constants K1 and K2, respectively, the ratios of holoenzyme concentrations to free core are [S1]/K1 and [S2]/K2. Under the assumptions of rapid equilibration, free sigma concentrations that can be treated as fixed and no other core-bound states, the fraction of core associated with S2 is ([S2]/K2)/(1 + [S1]/K1 + [S2]/K2). The extra term 1 in the denominator accounts for free core. The fraction is dimensionless because concentrations are divided by concentrations.

For a hypothetical example, suppose [S1]/K1 equals 8 and [S2]/K2 equals 1 before stress. The predicted fraction of core associated with S2 is 1/10 or 0.10. After a stress signal, suppose the second ratio becomes 9 while the first remains 8. The new fraction is 9/18 or 0.50. The first holoenzyme's fraction has decreased from 8/10 to 8/18, even though its free sigma concentration has not changed. This calculation captures the possible redistributive effect of a shared resource. The model is not a quantitative description of all transcription in living bacteria: core molecules can be engaged in elongation, sigma factors and polymerase can be dynamically exchanged, and the abundance of different promoters and accessory factors matters.

A consequence of this model is that increasing an alternative sigma factor can have indirect effects on genes that are not direct targets of that factor. Their RNA production may change because the distribution of core polymerase changes, because stress alters growth, or because an induced gene encodes another regulator. Distinguishing direct from indirect effects requires an appropriate experiment. Purified core and the selected sigma factor can be used to ask whether a proposed promoter supports specific initiation under defined conditions. Mutating relevant promoter elements tests whether the response requires those elements. In a whole-cell experiment, a deletion and a controlled complementation can establish that the factor is necessary for a response, but an indirect pathway may still explain part of the phenotype.

The distinction from CRP is now clear. At the classic lac promoter, CRP bound to DNA assists a particular transcription complex. An alternative sigma factor modifies the promoter-recognition properties of the core polymerase itself, potentially affecting many promoters. Both can increase transcription of defined genes, but they are not structurally interchangeable. A sigma factor does not normally substitute for the contact made by CRP at the lac activation site, and high cAMP cannot replace the promoter specificity supplied by a missing alternative sigma factor. In principle, the two kinds of regulation can operate together at promoters where compatible factors and conditions are present. The student's task is to identify the molecular role of each rather than assigning every positive transcriptional effect to the same generic activator.

7. From an RNA transcript to a translated message

Transcriptional activation increases the supply of an RNA, but an RNA that exists inside a cell is not necessarily translated efficiently. The bacterial ribosome has to locate the appropriate initiation region, associate with the message in a suitable configuration and position the initiator tRNA at the start codon. Control at this stage can alter the rate of protein synthesis from molecules already present in the cell. In a changing environment, this provides a way to modify the relationship between RNA abundance and protein production without waiting for an entirely new transcription programme.

A conventional bacterial messenger RNA contains a region upstream of the coding sequence called the 5′ untranslated region, or 5′ UTR. In many bacterial messages, a purine-rich Shine–Dalgarno sequence within or near the translation initiation region base-pairs with a complementary sequence near the 3′ end of 16S ribosomal RNA in the small ribosomal subunit. This interaction helps position the start codon at the ribosomal initiation site. The spacing between the recognition sequence and the start codon matters because the two must be geometrically compatible with the ribosome. The RNA sequence is therefore not simply a label identifying a gene; it contributes to assembly of the molecular complex that determines how frequently translation begins.

Not every bacterial mRNA has a canonical Shine–Dalgarno sequence. Some messages have a weak or absent conventional recognition motif, and some are leaderless, beginning at or very near the start codon. The relative importance of different initiation strategies varies among bacterial lineages. In this chapter the Shine–Dalgarno-dependent model is a representative and useful system for exploring regulated accessibility. A problem involving a leaderless message must not be solved by assuming that the message has an upstream Shine–Dalgarno sequence that can be hidden in the usual way.

Even in a message containing a suitable sequence, sequence identity and physical accessibility are separate variables. RNA is capable of forming intramolecular base pairs. If a purine-rich recognition sequence pairs with a complementary segment elsewhere in the same RNA, the resulting helix can make the sequence less available to the small subunit. The ribosome may need to compete with the paired state or rely on fluctuations in RNA conformation before it can associate productively. A sequence read from a gene or transcript therefore cannot, on its own, establish how efficiently that site is used inside a living cell.

To understand this effect, consider two otherwise similar mRNAs. Both have the same Shine–Dalgarno motif and the same coding sequence. In RNA A, the leader contains no strongly competing complementary segment. In RNA B, a complementary upstream segment can form a stable stem with the motif. The encoded protein is identical if initiation succeeds at the same start codon in both RNAs. Nevertheless, RNA B can yield fewer protein molecules per unit time because the initiation region is less accessible. This kind of mutation can occur entirely in a noncoding region. It changes expression without altering the sequence of amino acids in the protein product.

RNA folding reflects several physical influences. Complementary bases can form hydrogen bonds, and stacking interactions between adjacent bases contribute substantially to the stability of helices. The arrangement of loops, bulges and noncanonical pairs can also affect stability. Folding changes the number of available conformations, so entropic effects accompany favourable local interactions. Temperature, ion concentrations and molecular interactions can shift the distribution of structures. A diagram of one predicted stem–loop is therefore a proposed or representative structural state, not a photograph of an RNA permanently locked in a single shape.

The relevant energetic comparison is between states in the specified environment. If formation of an inhibitory helix has a favourable Gibbs free-energy change, the RNA may spend a relatively large fraction of its time in a folded state that occludes the initiation region. Raising temperature can make some structures less stable, but the response depends on the balance of enthalpic and entropic contributions and on the precise sequence. Ribosomes and regulatory molecules can also stabilise alternative states. An RNA helix that appears favourable in an isolated prediction may not have the same occupancy in a translating cell because protein binding, RNA–RNA interactions and ongoing transcription change the available pathways.

The folding pathway matters as well as the final equilibrium. Bacterial RNA emerges from RNA polymerase progressively in the 5′ to 3′ direction. An upstream segment can form a structure before the downstream segment required for an alternative structure has even been synthesised. Some structures can subsequently rearrange, whereas others persist long enough to influence a decision such as recruitment of the first ribosome. In bacterial cells, translation can begin before transcription of the entire message has finished. This coupling makes the timing of folding, translation initiation and RNA processing biologically relevant. It is not always appropriate to imagine that a full-length purified RNA equilibrates first and only then encounters a ribosome.

A simple two-state model helps organise these ideas. Let O denote an accessible initiation region and H denote a hidden one. If their free-energy difference is defined as delta G = G(H) − G(O), and if the two conformations equilibrate with comparable definitions of their states, the ratio of hidden to open populations is exp(−delta G/RT). Here R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. The open fraction is 1/(1 + exp(−delta G/RT)). A negative delta G favours the hidden conformation and reduces the predicted accessible fraction. The sign is easily checked by considering a very stable hidden state: a large negative free-energy difference produces a large hidden-to-open ratio.

For a constructed example at 310 K, RT is approximately 2.58 kJ mol−1 when R is 0.008314 kJ mol−1 K−1. If delta G is −5.16 kJ mol−1, the hidden-to-open ratio is exp(2), approximately 7.39. The open fraction is 1/8.39, about 0.119. If a mutation changes delta G to zero, the predicted open fraction becomes 0.50. If all other factors remain unchanged and initiation rate is proportional to the open fraction, the mutation raises initiation approximately 0.50/0.119, or 4.2-fold. The numerical example is an equilibrium illustration, not a claim that a particular native RNA has either of these free energies.

The assumptions matter when interpreting such a calculation. A ribosome can bind to an accessible state and thereby change its effective population. The RNA may have more than two relevant states, and association of initiation factors and initiator tRNA can involve additional rate-limiting steps. A rapidly fluctuating open state may also be less useful than a longer-lived state with the same average occupancy if productive initiation takes time. A model equating accessibility with protein output is therefore most appropriate as a first approximation in a controlled problem. It can be improved by using separate binding and initiation rates when those rates are supplied.

Several kinds of mutation can be used to test an RNA-structure hypothesis. Disrupting one side of a stem can release the masked sequence and raise translation. A compensatory mutation on the opposite side may restore base pairing and reduce translation again, provided that the critical ribosome-recognition sequence remains functional. This experimental logic tests a structural relationship rather than treating the effect of one nucleotide substitution as sufficient proof. The design becomes difficult if the intended compensatory change also damages the Shine–Dalgarno motif. In that case, loss of translation could result from weaker ribosome recognition rather than restoration of the inhibitory structure. A better design preserves the key recognition bases or uses additional controls to distinguish the alternatives.

The interpretation is strengthened when independent RNA measurements accompany protein measurements. A mutation that changes secondary structure can also alter susceptibility to ribonucleases, so it may change the number of RNA molecules in addition to initiation efficiency. A reporter whose upstream leader is changed but whose promoter and protein-coding sequence are held constant is useful, but equal promoter sequence does not guarantee equal steady-state RNA. Measuring RNA abundance and reporter output under matched conditions helps distinguish these effects. If both quantities change, the ratio of newly synthesised protein to available RNA is more directly related to translation than total protein abundance alone.

The biological reason for investing in such regulation is not simply to switch genes off. A masked initiation region can maintain an RNA pool with low output under one condition and permit protein production when the RNA adopts an accessible configuration. This can provide a different time course from complete repression of transcription. Alternatively, translation inhibition combined with accelerated RNA degradation can terminate expression efficiently. Which strategy is advantageous depends on the need for reversibility, the expected duration of the signal, RNA stability and the energetic cost of maintaining transcripts that are not currently translated.

8. Translational riboswitches and RNA thermometers

A riboswitch is a regulatory RNA element that responds to a signal through its own molecular recognition and structural properties. In a typical metabolite-sensing riboswitch, one region of the RNA, often called the aptamer domain, recognises a particular ligand. A connected expression platform determines how the binding event affects gene expression. These are functional descriptions: the aptamer and expression platform are linked parts of the same RNA rather than necessarily independent molecules. Binding stabilises some structural arrangements relative to others, and the resulting change can affect transcription, translation or RNA turnover depending on the regulatory architecture.

For a translational OFF riboswitch, consider an RNA leader with two competing structures. In the absence of ligand, the nucleotides needed to form a helix with the ribosome-binding region may instead pair with a different segment. The recognition motif can then remain accessible and initiation can occur. In the presence of ligand, the aptamer adopts a favoured conformation that redirects the alternative pairing pattern so that the ribosome-binding region becomes less accessible. The ligand does not have to occupy the start codon directly; a binding event at one part of the RNA can change the behaviour of a different part. This is a form of allosteric coupling in RNA.

The same design principle can produce the opposite response. In a translational ON riboswitch, ligand binding favours a structure in which the initiation region becomes available. The sign of the effect depends on how the aptamer is connected to the expression platform. A diagram or an examination stem that identifies the ligand-bound structure is therefore more informative than the generic term riboswitch. If the question gives only that a metabolite binds to a regulatory RNA, one cannot infer whether translation increases or decreases without knowing how binding changes the initiation region or another relevant step.

Thiamine pyrophosphate, a derivative of vitamin B1 that acts as a cofactor in metabolism, provides an example of a metabolite recognised by bacterial riboswitches. In appropriate bacterial genes, the RNA regulatory element links availability of the cofactor to expression of proteins involved in its synthesis or acquisition. A feedback principle is apparent: when the cell has sufficient cofactor, continued production of the corresponding biosynthetic or uptake machinery may be less necessary. In many systems ligand binding reduces expression, but the downstream mechanism depends on the specific organism and RNA. Some riboswitches influence transcription termination and others alter translation initiation. The existence of a metabolite-binding aptamer does not identify the expression mechanism without further structural or functional information.

A translational riboswitch differs from the cAMP–CRP system in the identity of the molecule sensing the ligand. In the latter, the signalling nucleotide binds a protein that then interacts with DNA and the transcription machinery. In the former, the ligand binds RNA whose conformational change directly influences its expression platform. Both systems couple chemical recognition to altered gene output, but the sensor and the primary regulatory step differ. A mutation in the CRP ligand-binding domain may destroy a transcriptional response, whereas a mutation in the riboswitch aptamer may destroy a translational response even though no transcription-factor protein is missing.

The structural logic also helps distinguish an RNA thermometer from a metabolite-binding riboswitch. In a simple RNA thermometer, temperature alters the conformational balance or dynamics of the RNA itself. At lower temperature an inhibitory structure may conceal part of the translation initiation region; as temperature rises, the structure can become less stable and allow more initiation. An external ligand need not bind the aptamer because an aptamer with a metabolite ligand is not an essential component of this thermosensing mechanism. RNA thermometers can thus couple environmental temperature directly to the accessibility of an RNA message, whereas sigma-factor switching changes transcription of sets of genes after the regulatory factor has responded to the condition.

The response of an RNA thermometer is not evidence that every RNA secondary structure is a biologically designed thermometer. Many RNAs change structure with temperature, but a physiological thermoregulatory element must connect that change to a meaningful alteration in expression over the relevant temperature range. A reporter containing the candidate RNA leader can be compared at different temperatures, along with a reporter lacking the structural element. Mutations predicted to stabilise or destabilise the proposed stem can be tested for shifts in the temperature dependence of output. Growth rate, general translation efficiency, transcript abundance and reporter-protein stability should be considered because a temperature shift can affect all of them.

A particularly informative experiment separates ligand recognition from the expression platform of a riboswitch. Suppose a mutation destroys a ligand-binding pocket while leaving the alternative pairing segments largely intact. The RNA may lose responsiveness to ligand but retain either high or low basal translation, depending on which structure is favoured. Conversely, an expression-platform mutation may retain ligand binding while disconnecting it from the initiation site. A binding assay can therefore remain positive even though a reporter no longer responds. This mirrors the earlier CRP example: recognition of a signal and transmission of its consequence are separable molecular functions.

Ligand specificity can be tested by comparing chemically related compounds. If the regulatory RNA responds to its natural ligand but not to related molecules at comparable free concentrations, the result supports specific recognition. A negative response to an alternative compound inside cells is not sufficient to demonstrate weak binding, because the compound may not enter the cells or may be metabolised differently. In vitro binding observations help separate those issues. Conversely, a ligand that binds an RNA in vitro does not necessarily regulate a target gene inside cells if the appropriate structural transitions or cellular concentrations are absent.

For quantitative reasoning, a riboswitch can be treated as a ligand-binding sensor in a simplified equilibrium model. If the ligand-bound fraction is L/(Kd + L) and the unbound and bound states have characteristic translation rates kU and kB, the average rate is kU(1 − f) + kB f. When kB is lower than kU, the curve decreases with ligand concentration; when kB is higher, the curve increases. At ligand concentration equal to Kd the model gives the arithmetic mean of the two state-specific rates. This is not a general law equating a regulatory half-maximal response with an isolated aptamer's binding constant. RNA folding kinetics, competing structures, timing during transcription and ribosome binding can alter the observed dose-response. The simplified calculation is useful only when its assumptions are explicit.

Riboswitches provide a connection to biotechnology because a regulatory RNA can be used in principle to couple detection of a molecule to a measurable reporter output. The engineering problem is not merely to find an RNA that binds a ligand. The binding event must reliably alter an expression step within the intended concentration range. An RNA device with excellent binding but no appropriate structural communication may be a poor sensor. Similarly, a very stable OFF state can yield excellent repression yet fail to activate adequately when desired. Sensitivity, dynamic range, basal output and response time are distinct properties. These concepts can be examined with standard biochemical and genetic reasoning without entering specialised design protocols.

9. Small regulatory RNAs: information carried by base pairing

The preceding sections considered regulatory instructions written into the leader of a messenger RNA. Bacteria can also produce separate RNA molecules whose expression changes in response to environmental signals and whose activity subsequently modifies the translation or stability of other messages. A small regulatory RNA, or sRNA, is a short RNA that contributes to regulation rather than necessarily serving as the template for a conventional protein. In the present context, the most important examples are sRNAs that recognise another RNA through complementary base pairing. The name small describes a broad class, not a fixed length or a single mechanism. Some regulatory RNAs interact chiefly with proteins, whereas others act on target messages. Our focus is the latter group.

One can distinguish the location from which a regulatory RNA is transcribed and the RNA molecule on which it acts. A cis-encoded antisense RNA is generally transcribed from the strand opposite its target region and may share extensive complementarity with the target RNA. A trans-encoded sRNA is transcribed from a distinct locus and can often recognise more than one target using limited complementary regions. The word cis in cis-encoded describes the genomic origin of the RNA in this usage; the RNA molecules can still interact with one another after they are produced. This terminology should not be confused with the effect of a DNA recognition-site mutation that acts only on its linked promoter. For a gene-regulation problem, determine whether the question concerns genomic linkage, molecular diffusion or sequence specificity.

Base pairing between an sRNA and its target depends on sequence complementarity, but the existence of a potentially complementary sequence does not guarantee an effective interaction. If the target sequence is buried in a stable intramolecular structure, the regulatory RNA may find it difficult to initiate pairing. The same constraint applies to the regulatory RNA itself. Short stretches of accessible complementary sequence can help nucleate an interaction, which can then extend or rearrange neighbouring base pairs. A protein that favours appropriate RNA conformations can assist this process. This is one reason many bacterial trans-encoded sRNAs act with the help of RNA-binding proteins, even though their target specificity is partly encoded by RNA sequence.

Hfq is a well-known RNA chaperone in E. coli and other bacteria. It belongs to a family of ring-shaped RNA-binding proteins and commonly functions as a hexamer. By interacting with regulatory RNAs and target messages, it can promote productive encounters and help overcome structural or kinetic barriers to RNA–RNA pairing. Hfq can also affect the stability of certain sRNAs and their associated targets. Calling it a chaperone does not imply that its sole function is to stabilise every RNA molecule in the cell. The consequences of Hfq loss depend on which regulatory RNAs and target messages are involved. Some bacterial sRNA systems function without Hfq, and alternative RNA-binding proteins can support regulation in other organisms. A negative result in an hfq mutant therefore has to be interpreted in the context of the particular pathway.

An inhibitory sRNA can pair with an mRNA near its translation initiation region so that productive ribosome recruitment becomes less likely. If the recognition region overlaps the Shine–Dalgarno sequence, the two possible interactions compete directly for access to a set of nucleotides. The regulatory RNA need not destroy the mRNA before inhibition begins. It can first make that RNA a poorer template for translation. In some cases the reduction in ribosome protection and interactions with the RNA-degradation machinery subsequently accelerate the loss of target RNA. The inhibition of translation and the change in stability are conceptually distinct even when they occur close together in time.

This distinction suggests a way to interpret an induction experiment. Suppose that after an inhibitory sRNA is produced, the synthesis rate of the target protein drops before a large change in the measured quantity of its mRNA. Such a time course is consistent with a direct translational effect followed by altered stability. If the mRNA disappears rapidly and protein production declines in parallel, either RNA loss or translation inhibition coupled to loss may be involved. A protein concentration measured much later is an even less direct indicator because pre-existing protein can remain functional. The student's mechanistic interpretation should use measurements of RNA, newly synthesised protein and existing protein pools separately whenever the data allow it.

The iron-responsive sRNA RyhB provides a clear example of how selective downregulation can serve a physiological purpose. Iron is an important component of numerous bacterial proteins, yet usable iron can be limited in the environment. In E. coli, the Fur regulatory system couples iron availability to the expression of genes involved in iron homeostasis. Under iron-replete conditions, iron-associated Fur represses transcription of ryhB among other targets. When iron becomes scarce, relief of this repression can increase RyhB. The sRNA then reduces expression of several iron-using and iron-storage proteins, helping reorganise the allocation of a limited resource. The regulatory response is therefore not merely a mechanism for eliminating one protein; it coordinates a change in metabolic priorities.

One well-known RyhB target is sodB, which encodes an iron-containing superoxide dismutase. When RyhB increases, sodB expression can decline through a combination of base-pairing-mediated translation inhibition and altered RNA stability. This example must be interpreted with care: reduction in one iron-containing defence protein does not imply that every antioxidant response is switched off during iron scarcity. Bacterial stress responses involve multiple enzymes and regulatory inputs. The specific conclusion supported by the regulatory relationship is that this target becomes less expressed under conditions that promote RyhB activity. The physiological advantage is understood by considering resource allocation across the relevant network rather than assuming that the effect on one protein describes every aspect of cellular adaptation.

Massé and Gottesman's 2002 investigation of RyhB helped establish the connection between a small RNA and multiple targets involved in iron metabolism. Changes in RNA levels for selected genes after perturbation of the iron-responsive system supported the idea that an sRNA can regulate a coordinated set of metabolic outputs. A central scientific lesson from the work is that an apparent transcriptional regulator can exert indirect effects through a second regulatory molecule. If loss of the DNA-binding protein Fur changes the abundance of sodB RNA, it is tempting to conclude that Fur acts at the sodB promoter. However, the regulatory chain through RyhB offers another explanation. Exam questions frequently ask the learner to distinguish a direct DNA-binding interaction from an indirect consequence of a regulator controlling an RNA that acts on the target message.

The same reasoning can be used to predict the effect of deleting ryhB in a strain lacking active Fur. In the simplified regulatory chain, loss of Fur increases RyhB, which decreases the expression of a susceptible target. Deleting RyhB removes that inhibitory connection. The target may therefore be expressed more strongly in the double mutant than in the Fur mutant alone, assuming no other regulatory effect changes the target. Such a double-mutant analysis is informative because it tests whether the downstream component is required to transmit the upstream perturbation. The result does not require Fur and the target mRNA to contact each other. It also does not prove that all effects of Fur are mediated by RyhB; many Fur-regulated genes respond through other routes.

An sRNA can also increase translation. DsrA provides a representative example in E. coli. The rpoS transcript contains a leader that can adopt a structure inhibitory to translation initiation. DsrA can pair with a region involved in that structure and favour rearrangement of the leader so that the initiation site becomes more available. In this case the target RNA possesses an inhibitory antisense segment within its own structure; an external regulatory RNA interacts with that segment and helps release the masked site. This mechanism is sometimes described as an anti-antisense interaction. The crucial causal difference from inhibitory pairing is the location of the sRNA interaction relative to the structure responsible for masking the initiation region.

At relatively low temperature, DsrA-dependent regulation can contribute to increased translation of rpoS. Other sRNAs, including RprA and ArcZ, can also promote rpoS translation in relevant physiological contexts. They provide a means by which different inputs can converge on synthesis of an alternative sigma factor. Once RpoS protein becomes available, it can alter transcription of compatible stress-associated genes. The full causal chain contains both translation regulation and transcription regulation. A question asking for the immediate effect of DsrA on rpoS requires an RNA-level explanation, whereas a question asking why expression of a distant RpoS-dependent gene changes requires the downstream transcriptional explanation as well.

The contrast between RyhB and DsrA shows why it is incorrect to assign a universal negative sign to antisense RNA regulation. A new RNA–RNA interaction can prevent a ribosome-binding event, but it can also disrupt an interaction that was already inhibitory. In a simplified circuit, if an intramolecular stem inhibits translation and an sRNA inhibits formation of that stem, the two inhibitory relationships together yield a positive effect on translation. The observed sign is an outcome of structural competition. Neither the size of the RNA nor the fact that it base-pairs with a message determines the sign in isolation.

A second form of positive regulation occurs when an sRNA increases stability of a target RNA, for instance by reducing accessibility of a cleavage-sensitive region in a suitable system. The target protein output may rise because more template persists rather than because initiation on each template becomes faster. This distinction is important in interpreting data. If RNA abundance and protein synthesis rate both double but their ratio remains constant, the result is consistent with a rise in template abundance. If RNA abundance stays constant while the synthesis rate doubles, altered translation efficiency is more directly suggested. Actual regulatory effects can include both mechanisms, so quantitative data should be analysed without assuming that all RNA-dependent activation works by exposing a Shine–Dalgarno sequence.

An sRNA can have more than one target because a limited recognition segment may pair with appropriate sequences on multiple RNAs. Conversely, one target message can receive inputs from more than one sRNA. This provides a molecular framework for integrating environmental signals without requiring each target to carry a distinct DNA-binding protein for every condition. It also creates potential competition: if two targets bind a regulatory RNA and the amount of that RNA is limiting, increasing the amount of one target may influence the regulatory effect on another. At NET/SET level the important principle is finite regulator availability and competing interactions. Detailed network kinetics are not necessary unless a problem supplies a suitable simple model.

RNA-based control also differs from a riboswitch in the origin of the regulatory molecule. A conventional riboswitch uses an element within the target RNA itself to recognise a ligand and influence expression. A trans-encoded sRNA is produced from another locus and recognises the target through intermolecular pairing. A mutation in the riboswitch aptamer can affect the response of its own linked transcript; loss of a diffusible sRNA can potentially affect multiple distinct messages. Both rely on RNA structure and noncovalent interactions, but their genetic organisation and modes of signal recognition are different. Combining these mechanisms into one vague class of RNA regulation obscures experimentally useful predictions.

It is also useful to compare bacterial base-pairing sRNAs with eukaryotic microRNAs without assuming that they share identical machinery. Eukaryotic microRNAs typically function with Argonaute-containing complexes and have their own biogenesis and targeting principles. Bacterial sRNAs in the examples above act through bacterial RNA chaperones, direct pairing and effects on translation or bacterial RNA turnover. The common conceptual theme is that an RNA sequence can contribute to target recognition through complementary interactions. The detailed protein partners and regulatory consequences should be treated separately. Eukaryotic RNA-mediated silencing belongs to a subsequent chapter and is not needed to explain the bacterial examples.

10. Experimental evidence: deciding between regulatory mechanisms

A scientifically convincing model must predict observations beyond the one from which it was originally proposed. The historical development of the cAMP–CRP model illustrates this point. In 1970, Geoffrey Zubay, Daniele Schwartz and Jon Beckwith reported evidence from cell-free gene-expression experiments supporting the participation of cyclic AMP and a protein activator in expression of catabolite-sensitive genes. Such a system made it possible to examine regulatory requirements outside the full complexity of bacterial growth. The significance of the experiments was that cAMP did not have to be interpreted merely as a nutrient-correlated molecule: its role could be related to a protein-dependent expression mechanism. Subsequent work on transcription, DNA binding and protein interactions refined the mechanistic explanation. The simplified teaching experiments that follow are constructed examples, not numerical reproductions of those historical experiments.

Consider a cell-free transcription experiment containing a defined DNA fragment with the lac promoter and its CRP site, bacterial RNA polymerase and the nucleoside triphosphates needed to synthesise RNA. Assume that there is no active LacI repression in the preparation. Four otherwise matched reactions receive neither CRP nor cAMP, CRP alone, cAMP alone, or both. Under conditions where CRP requires cAMP for effective activation, one predicts substantially more transcript in the reaction with both components than in reactions lacking either component. A control promoter that does not require CRP helps establish that the basic transcription machinery is functional in all preparations. The result supports a positive role for the combined regulatory components under the chosen conditions.

However, increased transcription in the combined reaction does not alone establish that CRP recognises a particular sequence upstream of the promoter. To test that possibility, one can compare the wild-type promoter fragment with a version carrying a selective mutation in the CRP site. The basal promoter must remain functional, otherwise loss of stimulated expression would be ambiguous. If the mutant retains basal transcription but loses the additional response to CRP and cAMP, a site-dependent regulatory effect is supported. An independent DNA-binding assay can test whether the mutation also reduces association of CRP with the fragment. A difference in activation accompanied by a difference in DNA binding is consistent with impaired recognition. It is not sufficient by itself to establish every structural detail of the interaction.

An electrophoretic mobility shift assay is one general method for detecting DNA–protein association. A DNA fragment in a native gel can migrate differently when bound by a protein. If CRP and cAMP promote formation of a complex with a fragment carrying the recognition site, a slower-migrating band may appear relative to free DNA under appropriate assay conditions. A competing unlabelled fragment containing a compatible sequence can reduce binding to the labelled fragment. A matched competitor lacking that recognition sequence may be less effective. Controls for equal DNA input, protein integrity and nonspecific interactions are essential. A band shift demonstrates a complex stable enough under assay conditions to affect mobility; it does not show directly that RNA polymerase was recruited or that transcription initiation increased.

DNA footprinting provides a complementary type of information. In a classical protection experiment, a DNA-binding protein can alter the accessibility of positions within a DNA fragment to a cleavage reagent. Comparing cleavage patterns with and without the protein can reveal a protected region or altered hypersensitive positions. For CRP, a protected region consistent with the expected upstream binding site supports localisation of the interaction. The protected region is not automatically identical to every nucleotide making direct contact with the protein, since protection can reflect steric accessibility or DNA shape. Mutational and structural evidence help refine which bases are functionally important.

The use of multiple assays is particularly valuable when a mutant displays normal DNA binding but weak transcriptional activation. If a binding assay shows comparable occupancy of wild-type and mutant CRP at the recognition sequence, yet the mutant fails to increase RNA synthesis in a defined transcription system, the evidence points towards a defect after or alongside binding. A defective contact with the transcription machinery is a plausible hypothesis. One might compare the response to RNA polymerase variants that retain basal initiation but alter the proposed interaction surface. A combination of selective mutations on the activator and polymerase can support a functional contact, especially if the changes affect activation more than basal transcription. Interpretation still requires care because altered protein conformation can influence more than one property.

The history of translation initiation provides a parallel example of evidence refining a molecular model. In 1974, John Shine and Lynn Dalgarno described the sequence at the 3′ end of E. coli 16S rRNA and proposed functional pairing between part of this region and complementary messenger RNA sequences near ribosome-binding sites. The important insight for this chapter is that the specificity of initiation involves an RNA–RNA interaction as well as protein-mediated events. Complementarity suggested a mechanistic hypothesis that could be tested by manipulating sequences and measuring initiation. The original work also discussed other proposed roles of the terminal rRNA sequence; its historical proposals must not all be treated as established modern mechanisms. In particular, standard bacterial termination is explained by release factors recognising stop codons, not by an obligatory pairing of the 16S rRNA tail with stop codons.

Imagine an experiment with two translational reporters driven by the same promoter. The coding region of each reporter is identical, but the leaders differ. One leader exposes the ribosome-binding sequence, and the second forms a predicted inhibitory hairpin. If the first reporter has higher protein output, the result is consistent with a difference in initiation. To make the comparison more informative, measure the amount of reporter RNA, use comparable growth conditions and ensure that the readout measures production of functional reporter rather than a difference in its protein maturation. If the RNA amounts are similar but the rates of reporter synthesis differ, an initiation-region effect is more strongly supported.

One can further use a compensatory mutation strategy to distinguish sequence-specific pairing from nonspecific effects. Suppose an sRNA contains an accessible recognition segment complementary to part of a target leader. A mutation in the regulatory RNA disrupts that pairing and eliminates the regulatory response. A complementary change in the target leader could restore pairing with the mutant sRNA. If the response is restored specifically in the double-mutant combination, the result supports a direct pairing relationship. The target change must not independently remove the structure responsible for regulation, and the sRNA mutation must not simply eliminate accumulation of the sRNA. Measurements of basal reporter output, sRNA abundance and pairing-dependent rescue make the inference substantially stronger.

A hypothetical four-combination experiment makes this logic concrete. Under matched conditions, wild-type target and wild-type sRNA yield 20 units of reporter synthesis from a basal value near 100. Mutating the sRNA recognition region yields 95 units with the wild-type target. Mutating the target recognition region yields 98 units with the wild-type sRNA. Combining the two complementary mutations restores repression to 24 units. The pattern is allele-specific: either single mismatch disrupts regulation while restored compatibility recovers much of the effect. These figures are hypothetical. The interpretation assumes comparable amounts of target and regulatory RNA, intact reporter function and no major indirect effect on other regulatory components. Recovery need not be exactly to 20 units to support the model, because the altered duplex can differ in stability or kinetics from the wild-type one.

An assay of ribosome positioning can provide additional evidence for an initiation defect. In a typical toeprinting concept, reverse transcriptase extends a primer along a message until a bound initiation complex obstructs progression at a characteristic position. Comparing extension products in the presence and absence of an inhibitory sRNA can indicate whether the initiation complex is less efficiently formed. The readout concerns positioning or occupancy of a complex rather than the entire lifetime production of protein. Structural probing of RNA can, independently, test whether a proposed initiation region is accessible or paired. A combination of such observations is more informative than relying on predicted secondary structure alone.

To distinguish altered RNA production from altered RNA loss, one can examine RNA decay after stopping or strongly reducing new transcription using an appropriate experimental design. If the amount of a target RNA declines more rapidly in the presence of an inhibitory sRNA, a stability effect is suggested. One must account for the possibility that the intervention used to stop transcription also changes cellular physiology and RNA turnover. The simplified interpretation is valid when the initial RNA abundance is known, the rate of disappearance is measured over a suitable interval, and the comparison strains differ in the intended regulatory variable. A lower steady-state amount before the intervention does not, by itself, imply a shorter RNA half-life.

The same logic applies to alternative sigma factors. A reporter driven by a heat-shock-associated promoter may increase after a temperature shift. Testing the same promoter in a background defective in the relevant sigma-factor activity can establish dependence on that factor, but the result may still reflect indirect effects if the mutant has impaired growth or protein folding. A control reporter using a promoter not specifically dependent on that factor and a measurement of physiological viability help assess whether general changes in transcription or translation could explain the result. A defined transcription system with the appropriate core polymerase and sigma factor can provide stronger evidence for direct promoter recognition, while the intact-cell experiment explains its significance in the stress response.

A useful general principle is that genetic necessity, biochemical association and physiological sufficiency are different kinds of evidence. If loss of a molecule abolishes an effect, the molecule may be necessary in that system. If a purified molecule binds a target, it has a direct biochemical interaction under the assay conditions. If adding the molecule restores a response in a defined system, the experiment can support sufficiency in the context of the other components present. No one experiment automatically establishes all three conclusions. For example, Hfq dependence of sRNA-mediated repression may show a requirement for the chaperone, but it does not by itself identify the target sequence. A binding result may show interaction with RNA without establishing a functional change in translation.

In examination problems, the correct interpretation often has to be narrower than a tempting causal narrative. A promoter reporter changing in a regulator mutant does not prove direct DNA binding. A protein level changing after a temperature shift does not prove a translational effect. A new band in a binding assay does not prove the complex activates expression. The student's task is to ask which molecular variable the experiment measures, which alternatives the controls eliminate, and which competing explanations remain. A strong answer proposes the simplest model consistent with the observations while distinguishing it from conclusions that require additional evidence.

11. Connecting transcription, translation and RNA turnover quantitatively

A cell may alter transcription initiation, RNA stability, translation initiation and protein stability in response to a single environmental change. Because each step contributes to the amount of final product, an observed protein concentration cannot usually identify which step changed. A simple production–loss model helps disentangle these influences. Let M be the average number of a particular messenger RNA per cell and let s be its production rate in molecules per cell per minute. Let gamma be a first-order effective loss constant in reciprocal minutes, which may include degradation and dilution if the quantity is defined per cell. The model is dM/dt = s − gamma M. At a steady state with constant parameters, production equals loss, so Mss = s/gamma.

Now define P as the average number of molecules of the encoded protein per cell. Let k be the average rate of completed protein synthesis per RNA per minute and delta the first-order protein loss constant. The equation dP/dt = kM − delta P implies Pss = kMss/delta = ks/(delta gamma). This model assumes that the encoded protein is produced in proportion to the amount of usable RNA and that synthesis and loss parameters can be approximated as constant. It ignores many processes, including changes in the fraction of RNA that is translationally competent, maturation delays and nonlinear enzyme activity. Nevertheless, it is a useful baseline because a change in any of four parameters can alter the final protein abundance.

Consider a hypothetical control cell with s = 10 RNA molecules per cell per minute and gamma = 0.20 min−1. The predicted steady-state abundance is 50 RNA molecules per cell. If k = 4 protein molecules per RNA per minute, the total synthesis rate is 200 protein molecules per cell per minute. If delta = 0.10 min−1, the model predicts a steady-state protein abundance of 2,000 molecules per cell. An activator that doubles s without altering the other parameters doubles the predicted RNA pool and protein pool. A translational regulator that doubles k while leaving s and gamma unchanged doubles protein synthesis and the final protein pool but leaves the RNA pool unchanged.

An inhibitory sRNA can produce a larger reduction if it affects both k and gamma. Suppose it reduces k from 4 to 1 and raises gamma from 0.20 to 0.40 min−1, with s and delta unchanged. The new RNA abundance is 10/0.40 = 25 molecules per cell. Protein synthesis becomes 25 × 1 = 25 molecules per cell per minute, and steady-state protein abundance is 250 molecules per cell. The RNA pool is reduced twofold, while protein abundance is reduced eightfold. The difference arises because the loss of template and the reduced synthesis rate per template combine multiplicatively in this simple model. These values describe an invented teaching example; they should not be cited as RyhB kinetics.

The ratio of protein synthesis rate to RNA abundance gives k in this model. If an assay provides 150 units of newly produced reporter per minute and a measured RNA abundance of 50 compatible units, the ratio is 3 reporter units per RNA unit per minute. In a second condition, 90 units per minute with 30 RNA units gives the same ratio. The decrease in total synthesis can be explained by the smaller template pool without requiring a decrease in synthesis per template. If a third condition yields 90 reporter units per minute but RNA abundance stays at 50, the ratio falls to 1.8, indicating that the output per RNA unit has declined. The inference depends on comparable units, appropriate normalisation and a measurement of synthesis rather than accumulated protein.

RNA half-life provides another useful calculation. If new production stops and first-order loss remains constant, the differential equation becomes dM/dt = −gamma M. Integrating gives M(t) = M(0)e^(−gamma t). At the half-life, the ratio M(t)/M(0) is one-half, so gamma t1/2 = ln 2. A hypothetical RNA with gamma = 0.20 min−1 has a half-life of approximately 3.47 minutes. Increasing gamma to 0.40 min−1 reduces the half-life to about 1.73 minutes. This prediction assumes that the same effective loss processes operate throughout the measurement interval and that the experimental manipulation has not itself introduced a change in those processes.

The time course after a new transcriptional signal differs from the decay curve following cessation of transcription. If transcription changes suddenly from s0 to s1 while gamma remains fixed, the RNA pool approaches its new steady state according to M(t) = s1/gamma + [M(0) − s1/gamma]e^(−gamma t). At the moment of the change, the RNA pool is continuous even though the production rate changes. In contrast, changing the translation parameter k can change the rate of protein synthesis at once in the simplified model if the accessible RNA pool is already present. Protein abundance itself is still continuous because existing molecules do not disappear or appear instantaneously. This separation of rates and quantities helps explain why a transcriptional response, an RNA signal and a protein-activity measurement can have different time profiles.

The distinction has practical consequences for interpreting growth experiments. An inhibitory signal might stop new enzyme synthesis promptly but have little immediate effect on the activity of enzymes already present. Conversely, a direct effect on transport proteins can reduce substrate uptake without first altering the amount of those proteins. In the glucose–lactose system, inducer exclusion can change lactose transport rapidly, while altered CRP-dependent transcription affects the future supply of LacY and other proteins. The molecular mechanism determines which variable can change immediately and which must wait for production or removal of biological material.

Measurements made in different growth conditions may also reflect dilution. A protein that is chemically stable can become less abundant per cell as cells divide if synthesis is not sufficient to replace the molecules distributed among daughter cells. In a balanced exponential culture, a useful approximate dilution rate is the specific growth rate when concentrations or average per-cell abundance are represented consistently. The effective loss constant can therefore depend on both molecular degradation and proliferation. A stress-associated decrease in growth rate can change measured protein abundance without a proportional change in synthesis. This is why matching physiological conditions or explicitly accounting for growth is important in comparisons of transcriptional and translational regulation.

The simplest useful exam approach is to specify the measured quantity and define the model assumptions before calculating. If the problem supplies RNA abundance and protein synthesis rate, their ratio can estimate a relative translational output per RNA. If the problem supplies accumulated protein, RNA abundance and protein half-life, a steady-state model may relate those quantities only if steady state is justified. If the experiment follows a transient response, a steady-state formula may be inappropriate. The mathematical model is not a substitute for the mechanism; it clarifies which measurements could support the mechanism and where conclusions depend on assumptions.

12. Biological integration, comparative limits and applications

The mechanisms in this chapter can be placed on a common causal framework without treating them as identical. A regulatory input changes the state or abundance of a sensor, the sensor influences a molecular step in gene expression, and the resulting RNA or protein output changes cellular behaviour. For cAMP–CRP, carbon status influences a protein regulator that acts on DNA and RNA polymerase. For sigma switching, environmental changes alter the availability or activity of promoter-recognition partners of core polymerase. For riboswitches and thermometers, the state of a target RNA changes its own suitability for initiation or other expression steps. For base-pairing sRNAs, a separately produced regulatory molecule changes the properties of a target transcript. The shared theme is selective control of gene output, not identical molecular machinery.

A comparison of regulation in E. coli with regulation in other bacteria illustrates the importance of evolutionary context. In many Gram-positive bacteria, carbon catabolite regulation uses mechanisms involving proteins such as CcpA and metabolic signals linked to the PTS. In a representative Bacillus system, a phosphorylated form of HPr can participate in regulation through CcpA. The regulatory consequences depend on the organism, target promoter and conditions. The major lesson for this chapter is that preferential carbon utilisation does not require an evolutionarily universal cAMP–CRP mechanism. Similar physiological demands can be met through different molecular architectures. A question referring specifically to E. coli lac regulation invites the standard CRP model; a question referring to another bacterial species requires attention to the system described.

The same caution applies to the word prokaryotes in the chapter title. Bacteria and archaea both lack a membrane-bound nucleus, but their transcription machinery and regulatory architectures differ in important respects. The bacterial sigma-factor paradigm cannot simply be imposed on archaeal transcription, which uses different basal initiation factors. Ribosome-binding mechanisms also vary across organisms. The examples in this chapter are predominantly bacterial because the approved syllabus scope emphasises representative bacterial control systems. It is more scientifically accurate to learn a robust model with explicit limits than to memorise an overly broad rule that fails when applied to another domain of life.

The regulatory mechanisms can interact across expression levels. Consider the low-temperature response involving DsrA and RpoS. An environmental change influences regulatory RNA abundance or function; the sRNA affects the efficiency with which an existing message is translated; the sigma-factor product changes the promoter-recognition behaviour of the transcription machinery; and expression of multiple downstream genes changes. In the same bacterium, carbon availability may simultaneously alter transcription of metabolic genes through CRP. The physiological response is not one linear pathway controlling everything, but a combination of partially connected regulatory processes. An exam question can simplify this network by fixing some variables and asking how a specified perturbation propagates through the remaining connections.

One should distinguish a beneficial response from a universally optimal response. Producing alternative nutrient-utilisation enzymes before depletion of a preferred substrate can sometimes reduce the time required for switching, but it also consumes biosynthetic resources. Maintaining high levels of protective proteins before stress can improve survival if conditions worsen, but it may divert resources from growth in favourable environments. Regulatory systems balance these demands in particular ecological contexts. The observation that a response is advantageous in one condition does not imply that it is beneficial in all conditions or that every molecular detail evolved for one simple purpose. At examination level, a useful explanation connects selective gene expression to resource allocation while acknowledging the conditions on which the benefit depends.

In biotechnology, bacterial promoters and translational leaders provide distinct ways of controlling recombinant protein production. An inducible promoter can allow expression to be increased after sufficient biomass has accumulated. However, a strong transcriptional signal does not guarantee high protein yield if the mRNA initiation region is poorly accessible, the protein is unstable or folding capacity is limiting. A construct producing abundant transcript but little protein invites investigation of translational efficiency and protein stability rather than automatic replacement of the promoter. The concepts in this chapter provide a framework for analysing such outcomes at the level of standard molecular biology.

For an engineered reporter controlled by a regulatory RNA, a small background signal and a large induced signal are often desirable but not sufficient to define a useful device. The response must occur under the intended physiological conditions and within the relevant range of signal. An engineered structure that masks the initiation region too effectively might give a small background yet remain difficult to activate. Conversely, a highly accessible initiation site can create a large constitutive output that obscures the regulated change. This tension can be understood from the folding and occupancy models already introduced. A comparison of multiple constructs should consider expression rates, RNA abundance, binding or folding evidence and the effect of regulatory inputs, rather than interpreting the largest fluorescence value as evidence of the best regulatory design.

The regulatory principles also guide interpretation of mutant phenotypes in basic research. A strain that grows normally with glucose but poorly with lactose may have a defect in lactose transport, hydrolysis, induction, transcriptional activation or another pathway necessary for growth. The growth phenotype alone does not locate the defect. A reporter under a lac promoter, a measurement of lactose uptake, a test of beta-galactosidase activity and a controlled complementation provide different information. If the reporter RNA is produced normally but enzyme abundance is low, a defect after transcription becomes more plausible. If enzyme abundance is normal but catalytic activity is low, a defect in the protein's function is possible. The distinction connects gene regulation with the structure–function relationships studied in earlier units.

The same approach applies when evaluating a stress-sensitive mutant. A loss of an alternative sigma factor can reduce expression of protective genes and make cells less able to tolerate particular conditions. But a mutant lacking a chaperone, a transcriptional regulator or a specific protective enzyme could also display stress sensitivity. The investigator needs measurements that separate upstream regulation from downstream function. Expression of a downstream protective gene from an independent promoter might partly rescue the phenotype if that gene contributes to the defect, yet incomplete rescue would be expected if the missing regulator normally controls many targets. The response of a regulon cannot usually be reduced to one gene without evidence.

These examples prepare the learner for the next curriculum topic, the lambda genetic switch. A phage can also use regulators whose effects depend on DNA site position, alternative expression programmes and feedback. The immediate molecular examples will differ from bacterial carbon control, but the reasoning framework transfers: identify the regulator, locate its target, determine the sign of its action, distinguish direct interaction from downstream consequence, and predict how a mutation changes the resulting expression pattern. A student who understands positive control, RNA-mediated regulation and resource redistribution is better equipped to interpret a new regulatory circuit without memorising its entire behaviour as an isolated diagram.
