1. The life of a messenger RNA after transcription

A newly transcribed RNA is not yet a guarantee of protein production. Consider two differentiated cells that contain the same gene for a metabolic enzyme and have the same number of mature messenger RNA molecules for that enzyme. One cell may continually recruit ribosomes to these messages, while the other keeps a large fraction of the messages in a poorly translated state. The first can therefore produce much more enzyme per minute. Even if both cells initially contain the same number of enzyme molecules, their protein pools may gradually diverge. The distinction is between the availability of information and the frequency with which that information is used. Post-transcriptional regulation concerns changes in the fate of RNA after its synthesis, whereas translational regulation concerns changes in the production of polypeptides from the available messages. The two categories overlap, because the rate of translation can influence how long a message survives.

In a typical eukaryotic nuclear gene, RNA polymerase II produces a precursor transcript in the nucleus. Capping, processing of the 3′ end and, where required, splicing help generate the mature message. Export connects the nuclear phase of the RNA life cycle to cytoplasmic protein synthesis. The message may encounter ribosomes immediately or may spend some time associated with proteins that inhibit initiation. It may move to a particular cytoplasmic region, enter a reversible storage state, undergo regulated degradation or be removed by a surveillance pathway. None of these events requires the gene itself to be mutated. They represent different ways of using an existing sequence of information.

This arrangement is particularly valuable in cells that need to alter protein production on short timescales. Transcribing a new RNA, processing it and transporting it to its destination can impose a delay. A cell that has already accumulated a population of suitable messages can respond by altering their recruitment to ribosomes. The response still depends on energy, amino acids, functioning ribosomes and other cellular resources, but it does not necessarily require a new wave of transcription. An early increase in protein synthesis from pre-existing messages may subsequently be reinforced by transcriptional changes. In development, maternal messages provide an especially clear example of temporal separation between transcription and protein production, because RNA accumulated during oogenesis can be translated later.

A mature mRNA is better regarded as an RNA–protein assembly than as an isolated nucleotide chain. The messenger ribonucleoprotein particle, or mRNP, contains proteins interacting with the cap, untranslated regions, coding region or tail. These associations can change during the lifetime of the message. A protein that accompanied the message during export need not remain associated with every later round of translation. A cytoplasmic RNA-binding protein can compete with another protein for a sequence, or recognize a structure that was not accessible earlier. The resulting dynamic composition is important: an identical RNA sequence can have different regulatory properties in two cells because the cellular supply and activity of its binding partners differ.

The distinction between transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation must be defined carefully in experiments. A decrease in mature RNA does not prove that RNA polymerase is less active. If processing becomes inefficient or the message is removed more rapidly, mature RNA can fall even while transcription continues. Conversely, a large increase in protein with little change in RNA abundance is consistent with increased translation but could also arise from reduced protein degradation. We will repeatedly distinguish production rates, removal rates and measured amounts. That separation turns gene expression from a list of named factors into a testable mechanistic model.

Regulatory specificity can occur at several scales. One mechanism may alter many thousands of messages by changing a shared initiation factor. Another may alter only a set of messages containing a particular nucleotide motif. The former permits coordination of cellular resource allocation; the latter makes selective responses possible. The two need not be mutually exclusive. During stress a cell can reduce initiation on much of its transcriptome while allowing selected stress-response messages to remain active or even to be translated more effectively. The particular sequence architecture of each message helps explain such exceptions.

2. The mRNA as a regulatory architecture

A conventional mature nuclear-encoded eukaryotic mRNA has a chemically modified 5′ end, a 5′ untranslated region, a coding sequence, a 3′ untranslated region and, for most messages, a polyadenylated tail. The 5′ cap contains a methylated guanosine joined to the first transcribed nucleotide through an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate bridge. This structure is both a recognition site and a barrier to the action of many nucleases that act from exposed 5′ ends. It is not simply another nucleotide in the protein-coding sequence. The poly(A) tail, added after cleavage of the precursor, consists of adenosine residues and can bind poly(A)-binding proteins. The tail has functions in translation, RNA survival and the assembly of the mRNP. Neither terminal structure is permanent: regulated enzymes can shorten the tail or remove the cap.

The 5′ untranslated region, often called the 5′ leader, lies between the cap and the initiation codon of the principal protein-coding sequence. It is read by the initiation machinery in the sense that scanning complexes traverse it and respond to its structure and nucleotide context. However, it is not usually translated into the main protein. A leader may contain an upstream start codon and a short upstream open reading frame, so untranslated does not mean incapable of ever directing peptide synthesis. A stem-loop close to the cap can impede recruitment or scanning. A weaker structure may be tolerated, depending on the available RNA-remodelling factors. The position of a regulatory element is therefore important as well as its sequence.

The coding region extends from the principal start codon through the stop codon. A change in codon identity may alter the amino acid sequence, but even a synonymous change can affect RNA structure or the speed of decoding under some circumstances. Coding sequences are not universally passive with respect to gene regulation. Nevertheless, experiments that aim to test the effect of an untranslated region are easiest to interpret when both constructs encode the same protein. Keeping the coding sequence identical avoids confusion between altered regulation and altered intrinsic stability or activity of the protein product.

The 3′ untranslated region begins after the main stop codon and continues to the processed 3′ end before the tail. It can carry multiple binding sites for RNA-binding proteins, determinants of RNA stability and signals influencing transcript localization. Its activity can depend on the spacing and number of motifs, the accessibility of those motifs in the folded RNA, and the presence of competing or cooperating cellular proteins. Two messages encoding the same amino acid sequence can have different 3′ regions and therefore different half-lives, localization or translational activity. A 3′ UTR can thus determine the amount and distribution of a protein without contributing amino acids to that protein.

The cap-associated initiation factor eIF4E recognizes the cap, while the scaffold eIF4G can connect cap-associated factors to the small-subunit recruitment machinery and to cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein. These interactions support efficient initiation and can bring the ends of an mRNA into functional communication. The familiar closed-loop illustration captures the possibility of cooperation between the two termini, but it should not be interpreted as proof that every translating message is permanently circular. The functional result is more important than a rigid geometric model: interference with a terminal recognition event can change how effectively a ribosome is recruited.

An RNA sequence is a cis-regulatory element when its regulatory action is linked to the molecule in which it occurs. An RNA-binding protein is a trans-acting product because it can encounter many separate RNA molecules containing compatible recognition features. Mutating a 3′ UTR recognition site on one reporter should primarily alter regulation of that reporter, while removing the RNA-binding protein may affect many endogenous targets. The distinction can be tested in a cell containing two distinguishable reporter messages. A regulator supplied from a separate gene can complement a loss of the regulator but cannot generally restore a missing recognition sequence to an RNA. In that sense, the conceptual tools developed for transcriptional cis and trans regulation remain useful after transcription has occurred.

Protein–RNA recognition depends on reversible noncovalent interactions. Exposed bases can provide hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, aromatic protein side chains can interact with bases, and positively charged regions can interact with the negatively charged backbone. A recognition motif may be a linear sequence, a structural element or a combination of both. Mutating a base can remove a direct protein contact or change the folding of a larger region. These alternatives matter in interpreting a mutation: reduced binding does not automatically establish that the mutated nucleotide directly contacted the protein. A compensatory mutation that restores a disrupted stem without restoring the original sequence can help distinguish a requirement for structure from a requirement for specific base identities.

3. Why messages are removed and how the major decay routes work

The cellular stock of a messenger RNA reflects both its entry into and its removal from the functional pool. A long-lived transcript can support repeated translation and persist through temporary reductions in transcription. A short-lived transcript allows protein production to be adjusted rapidly when the signal that induced it disappears. Cells therefore benefit not from making every message maximally stable but from assigning suitable lifetimes to different classes of message. Inflammatory signaling provides a useful example: persistent production of an inducible mediator after a stimulus ends could be harmful, so rapid RNA turnover may be as important as rapid induction. A message encoding a protein used continuously may require a different regulatory design.

For many conventional polyadenylated cytoplasmic messages, removal begins with shortening of the poly(A) tail, a process called deadenylation. Poly(A)-binding proteins associated with the tail participate in productive interactions with other mRNP components. Tail shortening alters these interactions and can make a message more susceptible to subsequent decay. In widely studied eukaryotes, the PAN2–PAN3 and CCR4–NOT complexes contribute to deadenylation. Their relative roles vary with organism, transcript and condition; the simplest textbook picture of successive shortening is a useful framework rather than a universal timetable. Deadenylation is not the same as removal of nucleotides from the coding region. A shortened transcript can still contain its entire coding sequence and can in some contexts still be translated.

Once a message becomes susceptible to decapping, the cap can be hydrolysed by the DCP2 catalytic protein, assisted by DCP1 and associated factors. Removal of the cap exposes a 5′ monophosphate RNA end that can be used by the cytoplasmic 5′ to 3′ exonuclease XRN1. The nuclease degrades the RNA body in the direction from its exposed 5′ terminus towards its 3′ end. The chemical order explains why cap removal matters. A nuclease that recognizes a 5′ monophosphate cannot use an intact capped terminus in the same manner. Protecting or exposing an end is therefore a direct mechanism for regulating access to degradation machinery rather than merely a symbolic indication that a message is old.

An alternative major pathway removes the RNA body from the 3′ end. The cytoplasmic RNA exosome and associated factors can carry out 3′ to 5′ degradation after the tail and terminal architecture become permissive. Residual capped products can then be processed by scavenger decapping activity. This is distinct from the 5′ to 3′ XRN1 route. Both routes can eliminate the information contained in the message. A block in one route need not eliminate all degradation because the other may provide a route of removal. The precise contribution of each pathway must be measured in the system studied rather than inferred solely from a canonical arrow diagram.

Endonucleolytic cleavage provides another route into RNA decay. An enzyme can cut within the RNA chain, generating two RNA fragments whose newly exposed ends may become substrates for exonucleolytic removal. The 5′ fragment and 3′ fragment do not necessarily have identical terminal chemistry or protein associations. Consequently, a mutation or experimental inhibition of one exonuclease may lead to accumulation of a particular intermediate rather than preservation of intact, translatable RNA. A northern blot probed at two positions on the same transcript can help distinguish an intact full-length message from partial RNA fragments.

Tail shortening, cap removal and body degradation need not occur with identical kinetics. Consider a population of messages with long tails at the beginning of an experiment. At a later time, the distribution of tail lengths may shift towards shorter tails while the count of intact coding regions changes little. At still later times, the full-length messages may disappear. This temporal ordering supports an interpretation in which tail shortening precedes body loss for that population. It does not establish that every individual molecule follows the same route. A small fraction might instead undergo cleavage or another decay mechanism. The analysis is strengthened when a perturbation of a deadenylating enzyme alters the tail-length distribution and the eventual disappearance of the message.

Inhibiting decay can produce indirect effects. For instance, a cell lacking an important decay component can accumulate transcripts that encode other regulators. Those regulators can alter transcription or translation, complicating interpretation of a later steady-state measurement. A short time course and a direct measure of RNA disappearance can help distinguish a primary effect on turnover from a downstream response. Rescue with a catalytically competent decay factor, compared with a catalytically inactive version, is a useful way to test whether the enzymatic activity is required for the observed phenotype. The distinction between substrate accumulation and a direct molecular interaction remains essential.

The dominant pathway also depends on message class. Replication-dependent histone messages in metazoans provide an important exception to the common polyadenylated architecture. Their processed 3′ ends contain a conserved stem-loop associated with a specialized binding protein rather than a conventional long poly(A) tail. Their expression and degradation are closely linked to DNA replication. Applying the standard deadenylation-first model without modification to such messages would overlook their characteristic regulation. The presence of an alternative terminal architecture illustrates why students should describe the major cytoplasmic decay route as common, not obligatory for all eukaryotic RNA.

4. Quantitative reasoning about RNA lifetime

Consider a population of intact cytoplasmic messages with number M per cell. Let s denote their rate of entry into the measured pool, in molecules per cell per minute, and let k denote a first-order removal constant, in inverse minutes. This model treats each available message as having a constant instantaneous probability of removal per unit time. During a short time interval dt, the expected gain is s dt and the expected loss is kM dt. Dividing the difference by dt gives dM/dt = s − kM. The first-order assumption is a simplification: it is appropriate when the measured population behaves approximately as a homogeneous pool and decay is not strongly saturated. If age-dependent decay or multiple distinct populations are important, a more elaborate model may be necessary.

At steady state the expected gain and loss balance. Setting dM/dt equal to zero yields Mss = s/k. This equation explains why two cells with equal message abundance need not have equal transcriptional activity. If one has twice the entry rate and twice the removal constant, both may maintain the same number of cytoplasmic messages despite different turnover fluxes. Strictly, s is the rate at which messages enter the measured mature pool, not automatically the RNA polymerase initiation rate. A processing defect can lower s without necessarily changing transcription. To use the equation as a direct model of transcription, processing and export must be sufficiently efficient and stable between the conditions being compared.

When entry of new messages is negligible, the differential equation reduces to dM/dt = −kM. Separating variables gives dM/M = −k dt. Integration from the initial count M0 to the count M at time t yields ln(M/M0) = −kt, or M(t) = M0 exp(−kt). The ratio M/M0 is dimensionless and the product kt is also dimensionless. The half-life is the elapsed time at which the expected amount becomes half the initial amount. Substitution of M/M0 = 1/2 gives k t½ = ln 2. Therefore t½ = ln 2/k. A larger removal constant means a shorter half-life. A tenfold increase in half-life is equivalent to a tenfold reduction in k under this model, not a tenfold increase in the transcription rate.

A hypothetical comparison illustrates the distinction. A reporter message enters the cytoplasm at 12 molecules per cell per minute and has a removal constant of 0.2 min⁻¹. Its steady-state count is 12/0.2 = 60 molecules per cell. If a mutation removes a destabilizing RNA element and reduces k to 0.05 min⁻¹ while s remains unchanged, the new steady-state count is 240. The original half-life was about 3.47 minutes and the new half-life is about 13.86 minutes. The fourfold RNA increase is fully explained by altered removal in this model. If the protein synthesis rate per RNA and the protein lifetime are unchanged, the steady-state protein count will also increase fourfold. That last prediction is conditional and must not be treated as a direct measurement.

A change in decay rate does not instantaneously change the count of RNA. Suppose the cell initially has 60 molecules and the removal constant suddenly changes to 0.05 min⁻¹. The new steady-state level is 240 but the initial count is still 60. Solving the production–removal equation with constant new parameters gives M(t) = 240 + (60 − 240) exp(−0.05t). After one new half-life the exponential factor is one half, so the count is 150 molecules. After two new half-lives the count is 195. The approach to the new state is controlled by the new removal constant. The initial count does not double at each interval. Rather, the remaining distance from the new steady state is halved. This is a frequent distinction in examination questions involving a switch in RNA stabilization.

A decay experiment can be analysed with a semilogarithmic plot. If the natural logarithm of the fraction of message remaining is plotted against time, a simple first-order loss process gives a straight line with slope −k. A plot of raw RNA abundance instead produces a curved exponential decline. Curvature in the logarithmic representation may indicate heterogeneous populations, time-varying decay, incomplete cessation of entry, or technical measurement problems. For example, if half the messages are protected by a binding protein and half are unprotected, the summed signal can reflect two different removal constants. A single half-life may then describe an empirical midpoint but should not be mistaken for a unique microscopic rate.

When measuring concentrations rather than molecule counts per cell, changes in cell volume and cell division can complicate the equations. A growing population dilutes labelled material among descendants even if molecular degradation is unchanged. If cells divide exponentially and an appropriate per-cell amount is followed, dilution can contribute an effective first-order loss term. A measured decline under such conditions should not be assigned entirely to nuclease activity without checking the growth rate and normalization method. In non-dividing cells or short experiments where growth is negligible, the simple decay model may be an adequate approximation.

5. RNA sequence elements and selective message turnover

The existence of general degradation enzymes raises a question: why are some intact mRNAs removed much faster than others in the same cytoplasm? One answer is that the degradation machinery is recruited selectively to particular mRNPs. The RNA contains recognition elements, and proteins that bind these elements can promote or inhibit the relevant decay steps. A destabilizing RNA element is not itself necessarily an enzyme or a cleavage site. It can function as an address for a protein that connects the RNA to a deadenylase, a decapping complex or another regulatory partner. Conversely, a stabilizing factor may reduce the access of a destabilizing factor or protect an RNA region from cleavage.

A historically important example concerns AU-rich elements in the 3′ untranslated regions of certain short-lived mammalian messages. The expression of growth factors and inflammatory mediators often needs to be transient. In a 1986 study, Gary Shaw and Robert Kamen examined a conserved AU-rich region associated with the message encoding granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor. By placing a sequence from the 3′ UTR into a reporter context, they demonstrated that an RNA region could confer selective instability on another message. The essential experimental insight is the transfer of a property: a reporter whose coding region is not changed can acquire a different lifetime when it carries a regulatory RNA segment. The finding helped establish that mRNA degradation is not simply a uniform consequence of age or random nuclease encounters.

Many AU-rich elements contain repeated AUUUA-related sequences in a U-rich context. However, the presence of one AUUUA pentamer does not prove that a message is short-lived. Neighbouring bases, the number and arrangement of motifs, the conformation of the RNA, and the proteins available in a particular cell all affect the outcome. Some AU-rich regions can be associated with different proteins in different physiological states. A useful examination question therefore asks what a defined mutation does in a particular reporter experiment rather than requiring the student to assign a universal half-life to every message containing the pentamer.

Tristetraprolin, frequently abbreviated TTP or ZFP36, is an example of an RNA-binding protein that can promote degradation of certain AU-rich messages. Its tandem CCCH zinc-finger region recognizes suitable AU-rich sequences, and its other regions interact with regulatory partners that contribute to decay. In a simplified mechanism, TTP binding increases recruitment of deadenylation machinery to the message, increasing the chance that the RNA moves into downstream degradation pathways. The RNA-binding domain establishes specificity, while interactions outside that domain connect recognition to the effector step. A protein that retains the RNA-binding domain but cannot recruit the relevant machinery may occupy a target without producing the usual destabilizing effect.

HuR, also called ELAVL1, illustrates that AU-rich binding proteins do not all have the same regulatory sign. HuR can stabilize or otherwise influence selected target messages, depending on context, and its cellular distribution and interactions are regulated. When a destabilizing protein and a stabilizing protein recognize overlapping or functionally interacting RNA elements, their relative activities can determine the net fate of a message. It would be incorrect to conclude that all proteins binding a 3′ UTR activate decay. The binding event is the recognition step; the downstream effect depends on the associated proteins, their state and the architecture of the target mRNP.

Consider a hypothetical reporter bearing an AU-rich element. Under basal conditions, a destabilizing factor binds and the reporter has a half-life of ten minutes. Following a signal, phosphorylation of the factor or an associated regulatory protein reduces productive recruitment of a deadenylase. The reporter may now survive longer even if the RNA-binding factor is still detectable on the RNA. This distinction matters because RNA association measured by immunoprecipitation is not identical to functional effector activity. If the binding assay shows no difference but the decay rate changes, a model involving changed activity downstream of binding remains plausible. The signal need not change the nucleotide sequence or the number of available binding sites.

A reporter experiment can test whether an AU-rich sequence is necessary or sufficient for a particular regulatory effect. To examine necessity, compare the original regulatory region with a mutant in which key bases are changed while the rest of the message is preserved. If the mutation abolishes the stimulus-dependent difference in stability, the sequence is necessary under the conditions tested. To examine sufficiency, transfer the element into a reporter that previously lacked the response. Acquisition of a response supports sufficiency in the new context. Neither result establishes that the isolated element functions identically in every natural RNA. A failed transfer may reflect incorrect placement, loss of structural context or absence of the appropriate binding factor.

A complementary experiment can test the trans-acting protein. Compare the reporters in cells with and without the candidate factor. If the wild-type reporter becomes more stable after factor loss while the motif mutant is little affected, this pattern supports the hypothesis that the protein acts through the motif. Restoring the factor at a near-physiological level should recover the response if the deletion was responsible. A second rescue using an RNA-binding mutant distinguishes the requirement for target recognition from other functions of the protein. Such a combined cis mutation and trans perturbation is more informative than either intervention alone.

The exact lifetime of a regulated RNA is a population property shaped by competing pathways. A single binding factor may affect several distinct steps, and the same factor may behave differently with another target. The task is not to memorize an exhaustive list of AU-rich element-binding proteins. It is to understand how a cis recognition element, a trans recognition protein and an effector process can be separated experimentally. That conceptual pattern applies to other mRNA regulatory elements, including the iron-responsive elements studied next.

6. Iron homeostasis: one sensor, opposite responses at different RNA positions

Iron is required for many cellular processes, including the activity of iron-containing enzymes and electron-transfer proteins. At the same time, an excess of chemically available iron can promote damaging reactions. Cells must therefore coordinate iron uptake and storage rather than simply maximize both. Ferritin is a protein complex involved in iron storage, whereas transferrin receptor 1 participates in uptake of transferrin-bound iron in mammalian cells. When available iron is scarce, it is useful to favour uptake and limit the investment of newly acquired iron in storage. When iron is abundant, an increase in storage capacity and a reduction in further uptake help restore balance. A single type of RNA recognition system contributes to this coordinated response.

Iron regulatory proteins IRP1 and IRP2 recognize iron-responsive elements, abbreviated IREs, in selected messages. An IRE is a structured RNA element, typically represented as a stem-loop with features that permit specific protein recognition. IRP activity changes with cellular iron status, but the two proteins do not use identical sensing mechanisms. IRP1 can function as a cytosolic aconitase when it carries a suitable iron–sulfur cluster. Under conditions favouring the RNA-binding form, loss of the functional cluster is associated with increased ability to recognize IREs. IRP2 is regulated importantly through changes in its stability, including iron-dependent degradation. The examination-relevant shared result is that active IRE-binding capacity generally increases when available iron is low.

Ferritin mRNA contains a regulatory IRE in its 5′ untranslated region near the cap. When IRP binds this element under low-iron conditions, the assembly or productive action of the cap-dependent initiation machinery is hindered. Because the regulatory obstacle lies near the 5′ end, it affects the ability to initiate protein synthesis rather than changing the amino acid sequence of ferritin. An intact message can remain in the cytoplasm in a poorly translated state. The cell can later increase its use when IRP dissociates. This is an example of regulation of translational efficiency that need not be accompanied by an immediate change in the abundance of the target mRNA.

Transferrin receptor 1 mRNA contains several IREs in its 3′ untranslated region. IRP binding to these sites protects the message from regulated destabilization under low-iron conditions. Consequently, the lifetime and abundance of the receptor message can increase, making more templates available for receptor production. The protective mechanism concerns the RNA's susceptibility to degradation, not an alteration of the promoter. When iron availability rises, IRP binding decreases, the protection is reduced and receptor mRNA becomes less stable. The two target classes therefore respond in opposite directions even though the trans-acting regulator recognizes related structural RNA motifs.

The positional distinction makes biological sense. A protein bound close to the cap can obstruct the early recruitment events needed for initiation. A protein bound within a 3′ regulatory region can instead influence the recruitment or action of decay factors. Location alone is not sufficient to predict every effect, because the relevant mRNP interactions also matter. Moving an IRE to an arbitrary position in an artificial RNA does not guarantee that it acquires every property of a natural transferrin receptor regulatory region. Nevertheless, the natural ferritin and receptor examples establish the principle that a shared recognition system can regulate different molecular steps depending on the context of its target.

Under high-iron conditions, the standard mammalian model predicts reduced binding of IRPs to these targets. The ferritin leader becomes more permissive for translation, allowing increased production of an iron-storage protein. The transferrin receptor transcript loses protection and its abundance tends to decline. This response is negative feedback in a physiological sense: the increased iron availability promotes processes that reduce additional iron accumulation in the freely available pool. The regulator need not bind either promoter for these changes to occur. This example connects molecular recognition, RNA stability and protein synthesis directly to cellular homeostasis.

A hypothetical quantitative example makes the dual mechanism clear. Suppose ferritin mRNA abundance remains at 100 messages per cell in both conditions. When iron is low, productive translation generates 0.5 completed ferritin chains per message per minute; when iron is high, the rate rises to 2.0. The production flux changes from 50 to 200 chains per cell per minute, a fourfold increase without any change in the measured RNA abundance. Now consider transferrin receptor mRNA entering its cytoplasmic pool at 8 molecules per cell per minute. Let its removal constant rise from 0.04 to 0.16 min⁻¹ when iron rises. Its predicted steady-state abundance falls from 200 to 50 messages. If the receptor's translation rate per message remains constant, its production flux falls fourfold. The values are invented for calculation, not measured concentrations or rates from an actual iron experiment.

A mutation in the ferritin IRE that prevents IRP recognition would be predicted to reduce the low-iron-dependent translational repression mediated by that element. The mutant might therefore produce ferritin more readily during iron shortage. This does not necessarily mean that ferritin protein becomes completely insensitive to all physiological influences, because transcription, other regulatory pathways and protein turnover can still operate. A mutation that removes protective IRE function from the receptor's 3′ region would be expected to reduce the stabilization normally associated with low iron, provided the mutation does not simultaneously remove a separate degradation determinant. In an actual experiment the mutation must be designed so that the loss of IRP recognition can be separated from other structural changes.

One can compare a ferritin-leader reporter with a transferrin-receptor-3′-region reporter in matched cells. Measuring the amount of each reporter RNA, its half-life and its rate of new protein production is more informative than measuring fluorescence alone. The ferritin construct is expected to show a strong difference in protein synthesis per RNA under conditions changing IRP binding. The receptor construct is expected to show a difference in RNA lifetime. An RNA-binding assay can confirm that the regulator recognizes the tested elements. A control reporter lacking a functional IRE is important because iron manipulation can affect many aspects of cellular physiology independently of the particular regulatory system.

The iron system also teaches a broader lesson about interpreting the term activation. IRP activity rises under iron scarcity, but it does not activate expression of every target. Binding reduces production of one class of protein while increasing the supply of message for another. The direction of physiological change can therefore be coordinated even when the molecular signs of regulation differ. Students should reason from the affected molecular step and its contribution to iron balance rather than from a label such as activator or repressor applied to the regulator in isolation.

7. Surveillance, alternative processing and the functional message pool

Not every RNA molecule containing part of a coding sequence is equally capable of producing a normal protein. Errors during RNA production, mutations in DNA and alternative processing can generate messages in which translation terminates earlier than expected. Some such messages would produce shortened polypeptides that lack important regions. Eukaryotic surveillance systems can reduce the accumulation of certain defective messages. Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, or NMD, is particularly useful for understanding how translation itself supplies information about the quality and regulatory fate of an RNA.

During splicing in many metazoan messages, an exon junction complex is deposited upstream of an exon–exon junction, commonly about twenty to twenty-four nucleotides upstream. A translating ribosome moving through a normal coding sequence can displace complexes encountered on its path. For many normal messages the principal stop codon lies in the final exon, downstream of the exon junctions traversed by translation. If a mutation introduces a premature termination codon far enough upstream of a remaining exon junction, the ribosome may terminate while an exon junction complex persists downstream. The relative positions of termination and the remaining complex provide a signal that termination occurred in an unusual context.

In the familiar mammalian model, a premature stop more than about fifty to fifty-five nucleotides upstream of a downstream exon–exon junction is likely to promote NMD. This approximate rule reflects the positioning of the complex and the ability of a terminating ribosome to remove or interfere with nearby complexes. It is not an absolute rule for all organisms or all transcripts. A stop codon in the final exon often escapes this exon-junction-dependent mechanism, but some messages can be targeted through other features of termination context. The accurate inference is probabilistic and mechanistic: the presence of a suitably positioned downstream junction strengthens the case for surveillance-dependent decay.

UPF proteins participate in recognition and execution of NMD. UPF1, an ATP-dependent RNA helicase and central factor in the pathway, interacts with other surveillance components during an abnormal termination event. Regulatory interactions involving UPF2, UPF3 and phosphorylation of UPF1 can help connect the termination context to recruitment of RNA decay machinery. Downstream routes may involve endonucleolytic cleavage or exonucleolytic pathways following changes in the terminal architecture. It is unnecessary to memorize every accessory complex at the introductory examination level. The causal chain is that translation detects an abnormal context, surveillance factors respond, and degradation reduces the abundance of the affected message.

The dependence on translation distinguishes this pathway from an RNA polymerase response to a nonsense mutation. A premature stop codon is recognized by a translating ribosome and its associated termination machinery, not by a ribosome reading genomic DNA in the nucleus. A mutation changing a sense codon into a stop codon can therefore alter both the length of the possible protein and the lifetime of its RNA. If a nonsense-mutant allele produces little detectable protein, the absence of a full-length product is not by itself proof that the ribosome never initiated. The ribosome may have initiated, encountered the early stop and contributed to subsequent RNA elimination.

Alternative splicing can regulate output through similar logic. Consider a gene with two splicing outcomes, one yielding a productive coding sequence and another introducing a premature stop in a suitable NMD-triggering context. Changing the relative frequency of these outcomes changes the supply of stable productive mRNA even if the rate of primary transcription remains fixed. A splicing regulator can therefore influence protein production by directing a fraction of transcripts into a nonproductive, rapidly removed isoform. This is a regulatory use of surveillance rather than simply a response to accidental errors. Some genes encoding RNA-binding or splicing regulators use such coupling as a form of feedback.

Not all alternative splicing changes protein output in this manner. Inclusion or exclusion of an exon may instead change a protein domain, alter an untranslated region, change localization or have little effect under the conditions examined. To distinguish these possibilities, the experiment must measure individual isoforms. A primer pair located in constitutive exons can report the combined abundance of multiple messages and may conceal a large change in the productive isoform. Junction-specific measurements can identify the relative abundance of the splice variants, while protein analysis can distinguish full-length products from alternative products if suitable detection reagents are available.

Consider a hypothetical gene whose mature transcript pool contains 100 molecules in both cell types. In the first cell 80 messages contain a productive coding region and 20 are poorly translated into the principal protein. In the second cell 30 are productive and 70 are poorly translated. If each productive RNA generates two completed proteins per minute and the other messages contribute negligibly, the fluxes are 160 and 60 proteins per minute. The ratio of the second flux to the first is 0.375, despite the same total RNA count. An assay that treats all isoforms as equivalent would miss the change in the pool actually supporting production of the principal protein.

NMD also affects some natural transcripts without a classical disease-associated nonsense mutation. Upstream reading frames, particular long 3′ regions and alternative processing can create termination contexts that favour surveillance. Organism-specific differences are important; the exon junction model is particularly informative in mammals but should not be imposed unchanged on every eukaryote. Other surveillance pathways respond to transcripts lacking a usable stop codon or to ribosomes encountering severe elongation problems. Those pathways illustrate the general coupling between translation and RNA quality control, but a detailed catalogue of ribosome rescue factors is beyond the present regulatory focus.

In an experimental test, a researcher could compare otherwise matched reporters carrying an early stop in a penultimate exon, an early stop in a final exon and an intact coding region. A targeted reduction in a core NMD factor could selectively increase the abundance of the first reporter if it is a substrate of the pathway. Nascent RNA measurements and suitable normalization are needed to exclude major transcriptional differences. The recovery of RNA does not guarantee recovery of normal protein; the premature stop remains in the sequence. A short polypeptide may become more detectable after the surveillance block, or it may still be unstable. Separating RNA rescue from restoration of normal protein function is crucial when predicting the consequences of a surveillance mutation.

8. Alternative untranslated regions and regulatory choice

The same gene can produce mRNAs with different untranslated regions even when the main coding sequence is unchanged. Alternative promoter use can change the 5′ leader; alternative splicing can include or exclude segments of that leader; and alternative selection of a 3′ cleavage and polyadenylation site can change the length and content of the 3′ untranslated region. These events create different regulatory architectures. A cell may switch from one message variant to another, altering the effective binding sites or structural obstacles encountered by regulatory proteins and the translation machinery. The resulting differences need not be visible in the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein.

Alternative polyadenylation offers a clear conceptual example. Suppose a gene contains two alternative 3′ end processing sites downstream of the same stop codon. Use of the proximal site generates a shorter 3′ UTR, whereas use of the distal site includes additional downstream RNA sequence. If the additional sequence contains a destabilizing element, the longer transcript may decay more rapidly in a cell expressing the relevant factor. If the additional region contains a localization signal, the longer transcript may be transported to a particular cellular domain while the shorter one remains more broadly distributed. The same protein can thus be produced in different amounts or locations from different versions of the message.

It is tempting to conclude that shorter 3′ UTRs always increase gene expression because regulatory sites are lost. This is an overgeneralization. A long region may contain stabilizing sites, sequences supporting efficient translation, or localization signals needed for productive synthesis in a particular cellular region. Removing such a region could decrease the functional output of the gene even if total cytoplasmic RNA is unchanged. The net result depends on which elements are removed and which regulatory proteins are active. Length is a readily measurable property but is not by itself a molecular mechanism.

Alternative 5′ leaders likewise influence access to the principal start codon. One variant may contain a stable stem-loop, an upstream initiation codon or an inhibitory RNA-binding site that is absent from another variant. Their coding regions may be identical, yet they recruit productive initiation complexes at different rates. A change in promoter usage could therefore have a downstream translational consequence in addition to any change in transcriptional output. To classify the mechanism properly, it is necessary to distinguish the upstream event that generates the RNA variant from the downstream event that determines its use.

A controlled experiment can compare RNA variants by cloning their untranslated regions around the same reporter coding sequence. The reporter constructs should ideally use a common promoter and comparable end-processing signals, so that the effect of the tested sequence is isolated. A reporter whose RNA abundance doubles while its protein synthesis per RNA remains constant demonstrates an effect on RNA supply under the tested conditions. Another reporter whose RNA remains unchanged but whose newly synthesized protein increases threefold demonstrates a change in productive use. If both variables change, the total difference in protein output may reflect the product of both effects.

Some regulatory events occur before export. A spliced message that fails to acquire appropriate export competence may accumulate in the nucleus but contribute little to cytoplasmic protein production. A measurement of total cellular RNA can therefore be misleading. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation, with controls for the purity of each fraction, can reveal a redistribution of messages between compartments. An RNA that is retained in the nucleus is not necessarily degraded; depending on its fate, it may be removed later or become available for export. This consideration connects the RNA processing and export chapters with the regulatory mechanisms studied here.

The distinction between nuclear processing and cytoplasmic expression becomes particularly important in a time-course experiment. Shortly after a signal, the relative abundance of a newly produced isoform may change first in the nucleus and later in the cytoplasm. A protein response may occur still later, after the new messages have been translated and the pre-existing protein pool has turned over. Observing a lag is consistent with a sequence of events, but the lag alone cannot identify the specific responsible step. Direct measurements at several stages provide a more reliable causal test.

A useful general principle is to identify the molecular population that the measurement actually reports. An RNA assay targeting a common coding exon may combine nuclear precursors, cytoplasmic mature RNAs and several isoforms, depending on its design. A poly(A)-selected RNA assay may omit certain non-polyadenylated species and may represent very short-tailed messages differently. A protein assay may combine products from multiple isoforms if its antibody recognizes a shared region. Careful matching of the measurement to the hypothesis is more valuable than simply collecting a large number of observations without considering what they represent.
