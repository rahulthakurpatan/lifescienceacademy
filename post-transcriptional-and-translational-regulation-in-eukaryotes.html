1. The life of a messenger RNA after transcription

A newly transcribed RNA is not yet a guarantee of protein production. Consider two differentiated cells that contain the same gene for a metabolic enzyme and have the same number of mature messenger RNA molecules for that enzyme. One cell may continually recruit ribosomes to these messages, while the other keeps a large fraction of the messages in a poorly translated state. The first can therefore produce much more enzyme per minute. Even if both cells initially contain the same number of enzyme molecules, their protein pools may gradually diverge. The distinction is between the availability of information and the frequency with which that information is used. Post-transcriptional regulation concerns changes in the fate of RNA after its synthesis, whereas translational regulation concerns changes in the production of polypeptides from the available messages. The two categories overlap, because the rate of translation can influence how long a message survives.

In a typical eukaryotic nuclear gene, RNA polymerase II produces a precursor transcript in the nucleus. Capping, processing of the 3′ end and, where required, splicing help generate the mature message. Export connects the nuclear phase of the RNA life cycle to cytoplasmic protein synthesis. The message may encounter ribosomes immediately or may spend some time associated with proteins that inhibit initiation. It may move to a particular cytoplasmic region, enter a reversible storage state, undergo regulated degradation or be removed by a surveillance pathway. None of these events requires the gene itself to be mutated. They represent different ways of using an existing sequence of information.

This arrangement is particularly valuable in cells that need to alter protein production on short timescales. Transcribing a new RNA, processing it and transporting it to its destination can impose a delay. A cell that has already accumulated a population of suitable messages can respond by altering their recruitment to ribosomes. The response still depends on energy, amino acids, functioning ribosomes and other cellular resources, but it does not necessarily require a new wave of transcription. An early increase in protein synthesis from pre-existing messages may subsequently be reinforced by transcriptional changes. In development, maternal messages provide an especially clear example of temporal separation between transcription and protein production, because RNA accumulated during oogenesis can be translated later.

A mature mRNA is better regarded as an RNA–protein assembly than as an isolated nucleotide chain. The messenger ribonucleoprotein particle, or mRNP, contains proteins interacting with the cap, untranslated regions, coding region or tail. These associations can change during the lifetime of the message. A protein that accompanied the message during export need not remain associated with every later round of translation. A cytoplasmic RNA-binding protein can compete with another protein for a sequence, or recognize a structure that was not accessible earlier. The resulting dynamic composition is important: an identical RNA sequence can have different regulatory properties in two cells because the cellular supply and activity of its binding partners differ.

The distinction between transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation must be defined carefully in experiments. A decrease in mature RNA does not prove that RNA polymerase is less active. If processing becomes inefficient or the message is removed more rapidly, mature RNA can fall even while transcription continues. Conversely, a large increase in protein with little change in RNA abundance is consistent with increased translation but could also arise from reduced protein degradation. We will repeatedly distinguish production rates, removal rates and measured amounts. That separation turns gene expression from a list of named factors into a testable mechanistic model.

Regulatory specificity can occur at several scales. One mechanism may alter many thousands of messages by changing a shared initiation factor. Another may alter only a set of messages containing a particular nucleotide motif. The former permits coordination of cellular resource allocation; the latter makes selective responses possible. The two need not be mutually exclusive. During stress a cell can reduce initiation on much of its transcriptome while allowing selected stress-response messages to remain active or even to be translated more effectively. The particular sequence architecture of each message helps explain such exceptions.

2. The mRNA as a regulatory architecture

A conventional mature nuclear-encoded eukaryotic mRNA has a chemically modified 5′ end, a 5′ untranslated region, a coding sequence, a 3′ untranslated region and, for most messages, a polyadenylated tail. The 5′ cap contains a methylated guanosine joined to the first transcribed nucleotide through an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate bridge. This structure is both a recognition site and a barrier to the action of many nucleases that act from exposed 5′ ends. It is not simply another nucleotide in the protein-coding sequence. The poly(A) tail, added after cleavage of the precursor, consists of adenosine residues and can bind poly(A)-binding proteins. The tail has functions in translation, RNA survival and the assembly of the mRNP. Neither terminal structure is permanent: regulated enzymes can shorten the tail or remove the cap.

The 5′ untranslated region, often called the 5′ leader, lies between the cap and the initiation codon of the principal protein-coding sequence. It is read by the initiation machinery in the sense that scanning complexes traverse it and respond to its structure and nucleotide context. However, it is not usually translated into the main protein. A leader may contain an upstream start codon and a short upstream open reading frame, so untranslated does not mean incapable of ever directing peptide synthesis. A stem-loop close to the cap can impede recruitment or scanning. A weaker structure may be tolerated, depending on the available RNA-remodelling factors. The position of a regulatory element is therefore important as well as its sequence.

The coding region extends from the principal start codon through the stop codon. A change in codon identity may alter the amino acid sequence, but even a synonymous change can affect RNA structure or the speed of decoding under some circumstances. Coding sequences are not universally passive with respect to gene regulation. Nevertheless, experiments that aim to test the effect of an untranslated region are easiest to interpret when both constructs encode the same protein. Keeping the coding sequence identical avoids confusion between altered regulation and altered intrinsic stability or activity of the protein product.

The 3′ untranslated region begins after the main stop codon and continues to the processed 3′ end before the tail. It can carry multiple binding sites for RNA-binding proteins, determinants of RNA stability and signals influencing transcript localization. Its activity can depend on the spacing and number of motifs, the accessibility of those motifs in the folded RNA, and the presence of competing or cooperating cellular proteins. Two messages encoding the same amino acid sequence can have different 3′ regions and therefore different half-lives, localization or translational activity. A 3′ UTR can thus determine the amount and distribution of a protein without contributing amino acids to that protein.

The cap-associated initiation factor eIF4E recognizes the cap, while the scaffold eIF4G can connect cap-associated factors to the small-subunit recruitment machinery and to cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein. These interactions support efficient initiation and can bring the ends of an mRNA into functional communication. The familiar closed-loop illustration captures the possibility of cooperation between the two termini, but it should not be interpreted as proof that every translating message is permanently circular. The functional result is more important than a rigid geometric model: interference with a terminal recognition event can change how effectively a ribosome is recruited.

An RNA sequence is a cis-regulatory element when its regulatory action is linked to the molecule in which it occurs. An RNA-binding protein is a trans-acting product because it can encounter many separate RNA molecules containing compatible recognition features. Mutating a 3′ UTR recognition site on one reporter should primarily alter regulation of that reporter, while removing the RNA-binding protein may affect many endogenous targets. The distinction can be tested in a cell containing two distinguishable reporter messages. A regulator supplied from a separate gene can complement a loss of the regulator but cannot generally restore a missing recognition sequence to an RNA. In that sense, the conceptual tools developed for transcriptional cis and trans regulation remain useful after transcription has occurred.

Protein–RNA recognition depends on reversible noncovalent interactions. Exposed bases can provide hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, aromatic protein side chains can interact with bases, and positively charged regions can interact with the negatively charged backbone. A recognition motif may be a linear sequence, a structural element or a combination of both. Mutating a base can remove a direct protein contact or change the folding of a larger region. These alternatives matter in interpreting a mutation: reduced binding does not automatically establish that the mutated nucleotide directly contacted the protein. A compensatory mutation that restores a disrupted stem without restoring the original sequence can help distinguish a requirement for structure from a requirement for specific base identities.

3. Why messages are removed and how the major decay routes work

The cellular stock of a messenger RNA reflects both its entry into and its removal from the functional pool. A long-lived transcript can support repeated translation and persist through temporary reductions in transcription. A short-lived transcript allows protein production to be adjusted rapidly when the signal that induced it disappears. Cells therefore benefit not from making every message maximally stable but from assigning suitable lifetimes to different classes of message. Inflammatory signaling provides a useful example: persistent production of an inducible mediator after a stimulus ends could be harmful, so rapid RNA turnover may be as important as rapid induction. A message encoding a protein used continuously may require a different regulatory design.

For many conventional polyadenylated cytoplasmic messages, removal begins with shortening of the poly(A) tail, a process called deadenylation. Poly(A)-binding proteins associated with the tail participate in productive interactions with other mRNP components. Tail shortening alters these interactions and can make a message more susceptible to subsequent decay. In widely studied eukaryotes, the PAN2–PAN3 and CCR4–NOT complexes contribute to deadenylation. Their relative roles vary with organism, transcript and condition; the simplest textbook picture of successive shortening is a useful framework rather than a universal timetable. Deadenylation is not the same as removal of nucleotides from the coding region. A shortened transcript can still contain its entire coding sequence and can in some contexts still be translated.

Once a message becomes susceptible to decapping, the cap can be hydrolysed by the DCP2 catalytic protein, assisted by DCP1 and associated factors. Removal of the cap exposes a 5′ monophosphate RNA end that can be used by the cytoplasmic 5′ to 3′ exonuclease XRN1. The nuclease degrades the RNA body in the direction from its exposed 5′ terminus towards its 3′ end. The chemical order explains why cap removal matters. A nuclease that recognizes a 5′ monophosphate cannot use an intact capped terminus in the same manner. Protecting or exposing an end is therefore a direct mechanism for regulating access to degradation machinery rather than merely a symbolic indication that a message is old.

An alternative major pathway removes the RNA body from the 3′ end. The cytoplasmic RNA exosome and associated factors can carry out 3′ to 5′ degradation after the tail and terminal architecture become permissive. Residual capped products can then be processed by scavenger decapping activity. This is distinct from the 5′ to 3′ XRN1 route. Both routes can eliminate the information contained in the message. A block in one route need not eliminate all degradation because the other may provide a route of removal. The precise contribution of each pathway must be measured in the system studied rather than inferred solely from a canonical arrow diagram.

Endonucleolytic cleavage provides another route into RNA decay. An enzyme can cut within the RNA chain, generating two RNA fragments whose newly exposed ends may become substrates for exonucleolytic removal. The 5′ fragment and 3′ fragment do not necessarily have identical terminal chemistry or protein associations. Consequently, a mutation or experimental inhibition of one exonuclease may lead to accumulation of a particular intermediate rather than preservation of intact, translatable RNA. A northern blot probed at two positions on the same transcript can help distinguish an intact full-length message from partial RNA fragments.

Tail shortening, cap removal and body degradation need not occur with identical kinetics. Consider a population of messages with long tails at the beginning of an experiment. At a later time, the distribution of tail lengths may shift towards shorter tails while the count of intact coding regions changes little. At still later times, the full-length messages may disappear. This temporal ordering supports an interpretation in which tail shortening precedes body loss for that population. It does not establish that every individual molecule follows the same route. A small fraction might instead undergo cleavage or another decay mechanism. The analysis is strengthened when a perturbation of a deadenylating enzyme alters the tail-length distribution and the eventual disappearance of the message.

Inhibiting decay can produce indirect effects. For instance, a cell lacking an important decay component can accumulate transcripts that encode other regulators. Those regulators can alter transcription or translation, complicating interpretation of a later steady-state measurement. A short time course and a direct measure of RNA disappearance can help distinguish a primary effect on turnover from a downstream response. Rescue with a catalytically competent decay factor, compared with a catalytically inactive version, is a useful way to test whether the enzymatic activity is required for the observed phenotype. The distinction between substrate accumulation and a direct molecular interaction remains essential.

The dominant pathway also depends on message class. Replication-dependent histone messages in metazoans provide an important exception to the common polyadenylated architecture. Their processed 3′ ends contain a conserved stem-loop associated with a specialized binding protein rather than a conventional long poly(A) tail. Their expression and degradation are closely linked to DNA replication. Applying the standard deadenylation-first model without modification to such messages would overlook their characteristic regulation. The presence of an alternative terminal architecture illustrates why students should describe the major cytoplasmic decay route as common, not obligatory for all eukaryotic RNA.

4. Quantitative reasoning about RNA lifetime

Consider a population of intact cytoplasmic messages with number M per cell. Let s denote their rate of entry into the measured pool, in molecules per cell per minute, and let k denote a first-order removal constant, in inverse minutes. This model treats each available message as having a constant instantaneous probability of removal per unit time. During a short time interval dt, the expected gain is s dt and the expected loss is kM dt. Dividing the difference by dt gives dM/dt = s − kM. The first-order assumption is a simplification: it is appropriate when the measured population behaves approximately as a homogeneous pool and decay is not strongly saturated. If age-dependent decay or multiple distinct populations are important, a more elaborate model may be necessary.

At steady state the expected gain and loss balance. Setting dM/dt equal to zero yields Mss = s/k. This equation explains why two cells with equal message abundance need not have equal transcriptional activity. If one has twice the entry rate and twice the removal constant, both may maintain the same number of cytoplasmic messages despite different turnover fluxes. Strictly, s is the rate at which messages enter the measured mature pool, not automatically the RNA polymerase initiation rate. A processing defect can lower s without necessarily changing transcription. To use the equation as a direct model of transcription, processing and export must be sufficiently efficient and stable between the conditions being compared.

When entry of new messages is negligible, the differential equation reduces to dM/dt = −kM. Separating variables gives dM/M = −k dt. Integration from the initial count M0 to the count M at time t yields ln(M/M0) = −kt, or M(t) = M0 exp(−kt). The ratio M/M0 is dimensionless and the product kt is also dimensionless. The half-life is the elapsed time at which the expected amount becomes half the initial amount. Substitution of M/M0 = 1/2 gives k t½ = ln 2. Therefore t½ = ln 2/k. A larger removal constant means a shorter half-life. A tenfold increase in half-life is equivalent to a tenfold reduction in k under this model, not a tenfold increase in the transcription rate.

A hypothetical comparison illustrates the distinction. A reporter message enters the cytoplasm at 12 molecules per cell per minute and has a removal constant of 0.2 min⁻¹. Its steady-state count is 12/0.2 = 60 molecules per cell. If a mutation removes a destabilizing RNA element and reduces k to 0.05 min⁻¹ while s remains unchanged, the new steady-state count is 240. The original half-life was about 3.47 minutes and the new half-life is about 13.86 minutes. The fourfold RNA increase is fully explained by altered removal in this model. If the protein synthesis rate per RNA and the protein lifetime are unchanged, the steady-state protein count will also increase fourfold. That last prediction is conditional and must not be treated as a direct measurement.

A change in decay rate does not instantaneously change the count of RNA. Suppose the cell initially has 60 molecules and the removal constant suddenly changes to 0.05 min⁻¹. The new steady-state level is 240 but the initial count is still 60. Solving the production–removal equation with constant new parameters gives M(t) = 240 + (60 − 240) exp(−0.05t). After one new half-life the exponential factor is one half, so the count is 150 molecules. After two new half-lives the count is 195. The approach to the new state is controlled by the new removal constant. The initial count does not double at each interval. Rather, the remaining distance from the new steady state is halved. This is a frequent distinction in examination questions involving a switch in RNA stabilization.

A decay experiment can be analysed with a semilogarithmic plot. If the natural logarithm of the fraction of message remaining is plotted against time, a simple first-order loss process gives a straight line with slope −k. A plot of raw RNA abundance instead produces a curved exponential decline. Curvature in the logarithmic representation may indicate heterogeneous populations, time-varying decay, incomplete cessation of entry, or technical measurement problems. For example, if half the messages are protected by a binding protein and half are unprotected, the summed signal can reflect two different removal constants. A single half-life may then describe an empirical midpoint but should not be mistaken for a unique microscopic rate.

When measuring concentrations rather than molecule counts per cell, changes in cell volume and cell division can complicate the equations. A growing population dilutes labelled material among descendants even if molecular degradation is unchanged. If cells divide exponentially and an appropriate per-cell amount is followed, dilution can contribute an effective first-order loss term. A measured decline under such conditions should not be assigned entirely to nuclease activity without checking the growth rate and normalization method. In non-dividing cells or short experiments where growth is negligible, the simple decay model may be an adequate approximation.

5. RNA sequence elements and selective message turnover

The existence of general degradation enzymes raises a question: why are some intact mRNAs removed much faster than others in the same cytoplasm? One answer is that the degradation machinery is recruited selectively to particular mRNPs. The RNA contains recognition elements, and proteins that bind these elements can promote or inhibit the relevant decay steps. A destabilizing RNA element is not itself necessarily an enzyme or a cleavage site. It can function as an address for a protein that connects the RNA to a deadenylase, a decapping complex or another regulatory partner. Conversely, a stabilizing factor may reduce the access of a destabilizing factor or protect an RNA region from cleavage.

A historically important example concerns AU-rich elements in the 3′ untranslated regions of certain short-lived mammalian messages. The expression of growth factors and inflammatory mediators often needs to be transient. In a 1986 study, Gary Shaw and Robert Kamen examined a conserved AU-rich region associated with the message encoding granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor. By placing a sequence from the 3′ UTR into a reporter context, they demonstrated that an RNA region could confer selective instability on another message. The essential experimental insight is the transfer of a property: a reporter whose coding region is not changed can acquire a different lifetime when it carries a regulatory RNA segment. The finding helped establish that mRNA degradation is not simply a uniform consequence of age or random nuclease encounters.

Many AU-rich elements contain repeated AUUUA-related sequences in a U-rich context. However, the presence of one AUUUA pentamer does not prove that a message is short-lived. Neighbouring bases, the number and arrangement of motifs, the conformation of the RNA, and the proteins available in a particular cell all affect the outcome. Some AU-rich regions can be associated with different proteins in different physiological states. A useful examination question therefore asks what a defined mutation does in a particular reporter experiment rather than requiring the student to assign a universal half-life to every message containing the pentamer.

Tristetraprolin, frequently abbreviated TTP or ZFP36, is an example of an RNA-binding protein that can promote degradation of certain AU-rich messages. Its tandem CCCH zinc-finger region recognizes suitable AU-rich sequences, and its other regions interact with regulatory partners that contribute to decay. In a simplified mechanism, TTP binding increases recruitment of deadenylation machinery to the message, increasing the chance that the RNA moves into downstream degradation pathways. The RNA-binding domain establishes specificity, while interactions outside that domain connect recognition to the effector step. A protein that retains the RNA-binding domain but cannot recruit the relevant machinery may occupy a target without producing the usual destabilizing effect.

HuR, also called ELAVL1, illustrates that AU-rich binding proteins do not all have the same regulatory sign. HuR can stabilize or otherwise influence selected target messages, depending on context, and its cellular distribution and interactions are regulated. When a destabilizing protein and a stabilizing protein recognize overlapping or functionally interacting RNA elements, their relative activities can determine the net fate of a message. It would be incorrect to conclude that all proteins binding a 3′ UTR activate decay. The binding event is the recognition step; the downstream effect depends on the associated proteins, their state and the architecture of the target mRNP.

Consider a hypothetical reporter bearing an AU-rich element. Under basal conditions, a destabilizing factor binds and the reporter has a half-life of ten minutes. Following a signal, phosphorylation of the factor or an associated regulatory protein reduces productive recruitment of a deadenylase. The reporter may now survive longer even if the RNA-binding factor is still detectable on the RNA. This distinction matters because RNA association measured by immunoprecipitation is not identical to functional effector activity. If the binding assay shows no difference but the decay rate changes, a model involving changed activity downstream of binding remains plausible. The signal need not change the nucleotide sequence or the number of available binding sites.

A reporter experiment can test whether an AU-rich sequence is necessary or sufficient for a particular regulatory effect. To examine necessity, compare the original regulatory region with a mutant in which key bases are changed while the rest of the message is preserved. If the mutation abolishes the stimulus-dependent difference in stability, the sequence is necessary under the conditions tested. To examine sufficiency, transfer the element into a reporter that previously lacked the response. Acquisition of a response supports sufficiency in the new context. Neither result establishes that the isolated element functions identically in every natural RNA. A failed transfer may reflect incorrect placement, loss of structural context or absence of the appropriate binding factor.

A complementary experiment can test the trans-acting protein. Compare the reporters in cells with and without the candidate factor. If the wild-type reporter becomes more stable after factor loss while the motif mutant is little affected, this pattern supports the hypothesis that the protein acts through the motif. Restoring the factor at a near-physiological level should recover the response if the deletion was responsible. A second rescue using an RNA-binding mutant distinguishes the requirement for target recognition from other functions of the protein. Such a combined cis mutation and trans perturbation is more informative than either intervention alone.

The exact lifetime of a regulated RNA is a population property shaped by competing pathways. A single binding factor may affect several distinct steps, and the same factor may behave differently with another target. The task is not to memorize an exhaustive list of AU-rich element-binding proteins. It is to understand how a cis recognition element, a trans recognition protein and an effector process can be separated experimentally. That conceptual pattern applies to other mRNA regulatory elements, including the iron-responsive elements studied next.

6. Iron homeostasis: one sensor, opposite responses at different RNA positions

Iron is required for many cellular processes, including the activity of iron-containing enzymes and electron-transfer proteins. At the same time, an excess of chemically available iron can promote damaging reactions. Cells must therefore coordinate iron uptake and storage rather than simply maximize both. Ferritin is a protein complex involved in iron storage, whereas transferrin receptor 1 participates in uptake of transferrin-bound iron in mammalian cells. When available iron is scarce, it is useful to favour uptake and limit the investment of newly acquired iron in storage. When iron is abundant, an increase in storage capacity and a reduction in further uptake help restore balance. A single type of RNA recognition system contributes to this coordinated response.

Iron regulatory proteins IRP1 and IRP2 recognize iron-responsive elements, abbreviated IREs, in selected messages. An IRE is a structured RNA element, typically represented as a stem-loop with features that permit specific protein recognition. IRP activity changes with cellular iron status, but the two proteins do not use identical sensing mechanisms. IRP1 can function as a cytosolic aconitase when it carries a suitable iron–sulfur cluster. Under conditions favouring the RNA-binding form, loss of the functional cluster is associated with increased ability to recognize IREs. IRP2 is regulated importantly through changes in its stability, including iron-dependent degradation. The examination-relevant shared result is that active IRE-binding capacity generally increases when available iron is low.

Ferritin mRNA contains a regulatory IRE in its 5′ untranslated region near the cap. When IRP binds this element under low-iron conditions, the assembly or productive action of the cap-dependent initiation machinery is hindered. Because the regulatory obstacle lies near the 5′ end, it affects the ability to initiate protein synthesis rather than changing the amino acid sequence of ferritin. An intact message can remain in the cytoplasm in a poorly translated state. The cell can later increase its use when IRP dissociates. This is an example of regulation of translational efficiency that need not be accompanied by an immediate change in the abundance of the target mRNA.

Transferrin receptor 1 mRNA contains several IREs in its 3′ untranslated region. IRP binding to these sites protects the message from regulated destabilization under low-iron conditions. Consequently, the lifetime and abundance of the receptor message can increase, making more templates available for receptor production. The protective mechanism concerns the RNA's susceptibility to degradation, not an alteration of the promoter. When iron availability rises, IRP binding decreases, the protection is reduced and receptor mRNA becomes less stable. The two target classes therefore respond in opposite directions even though the trans-acting regulator recognizes related structural RNA motifs.

The positional distinction makes biological sense. A protein bound close to the cap can obstruct the early recruitment events needed for initiation. A protein bound within a 3′ regulatory region can instead influence the recruitment or action of decay factors. Location alone is not sufficient to predict every effect, because the relevant mRNP interactions also matter. Moving an IRE to an arbitrary position in an artificial RNA does not guarantee that it acquires every property of a natural transferrin receptor regulatory region. Nevertheless, the natural ferritin and receptor examples establish the principle that a shared recognition system can regulate different molecular steps depending on the context of its target.

Under high-iron conditions, the standard mammalian model predicts reduced binding of IRPs to these targets. The ferritin leader becomes more permissive for translation, allowing increased production of an iron-storage protein. The transferrin receptor transcript loses protection and its abundance tends to decline. This response is negative feedback in a physiological sense: the increased iron availability promotes processes that reduce additional iron accumulation in the freely available pool. The regulator need not bind either promoter for these changes to occur. This example connects molecular recognition, RNA stability and protein synthesis directly to cellular homeostasis.

A hypothetical quantitative example makes the dual mechanism clear. Suppose ferritin mRNA abundance remains at 100 messages per cell in both conditions. When iron is low, productive translation generates 0.5 completed ferritin chains per message per minute; when iron is high, the rate rises to 2.0. The production flux changes from 50 to 200 chains per cell per minute, a fourfold increase without any change in the measured RNA abundance. Now consider transferrin receptor mRNA entering its cytoplasmic pool at 8 molecules per cell per minute. Let its removal constant rise from 0.04 to 0.16 min⁻¹ when iron rises. Its predicted steady-state abundance falls from 200 to 50 messages. If the receptor's translation rate per message remains constant, its production flux falls fourfold. The values are invented for calculation, not measured concentrations or rates from an actual iron experiment.

A mutation in the ferritin IRE that prevents IRP recognition would be predicted to reduce the low-iron-dependent translational repression mediated by that element. The mutant might therefore produce ferritin more readily during iron shortage. This does not necessarily mean that ferritin protein becomes completely insensitive to all physiological influences, because transcription, other regulatory pathways and protein turnover can still operate. A mutation that removes protective IRE function from the receptor's 3′ region would be expected to reduce the stabilization normally associated with low iron, provided the mutation does not simultaneously remove a separate degradation determinant. In an actual experiment the mutation must be designed so that the loss of IRP recognition can be separated from other structural changes.

One can compare a ferritin-leader reporter with a transferrin-receptor-3′-region reporter in matched cells. Measuring the amount of each reporter RNA, its half-life and its rate of new protein production is more informative than measuring fluorescence alone. The ferritin construct is expected to show a strong difference in protein synthesis per RNA under conditions changing IRP binding. The receptor construct is expected to show a difference in RNA lifetime. An RNA-binding assay can confirm that the regulator recognizes the tested elements. A control reporter lacking a functional IRE is important because iron manipulation can affect many aspects of cellular physiology independently of the particular regulatory system.

The iron system also teaches a broader lesson about interpreting the term activation. IRP activity rises under iron scarcity, but it does not activate expression of every target. Binding reduces production of one class of protein while increasing the supply of message for another. The direction of physiological change can therefore be coordinated even when the molecular signs of regulation differ. Students should reason from the affected molecular step and its contribution to iron balance rather than from a label such as activator or repressor applied to the regulator in isolation.

7. Surveillance, alternative processing and the functional message pool

Not every RNA molecule containing part of a coding sequence is equally capable of producing a normal protein. Errors during RNA production, mutations in DNA and alternative processing can generate messages in which translation terminates earlier than expected. Some such messages would produce shortened polypeptides that lack important regions. Eukaryotic surveillance systems can reduce the accumulation of certain defective messages. Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, or NMD, is particularly useful for understanding how translation itself supplies information about the quality and regulatory fate of an RNA.

During splicing in many metazoan messages, an exon junction complex is deposited upstream of an exon–exon junction, commonly about twenty to twenty-four nucleotides upstream. A translating ribosome moving through a normal coding sequence can displace complexes encountered on its path. For many normal messages the principal stop codon lies in the final exon, downstream of the exon junctions traversed by translation. If a mutation introduces a premature termination codon far enough upstream of a remaining exon junction, the ribosome may terminate while an exon junction complex persists downstream. The relative positions of termination and the remaining complex provide a signal that termination occurred in an unusual context.

In the familiar mammalian model, a premature stop more than about fifty to fifty-five nucleotides upstream of a downstream exon–exon junction is likely to promote NMD. This approximate rule reflects the positioning of the complex and the ability of a terminating ribosome to remove or interfere with nearby complexes. It is not an absolute rule for all organisms or all transcripts. A stop codon in the final exon often escapes this exon-junction-dependent mechanism, but some messages can be targeted through other features of termination context. The accurate inference is probabilistic and mechanistic: the presence of a suitably positioned downstream junction strengthens the case for surveillance-dependent decay.

UPF proteins participate in recognition and execution of NMD. UPF1, an ATP-dependent RNA helicase and central factor in the pathway, interacts with other surveillance components during an abnormal termination event. Regulatory interactions involving UPF2, UPF3 and phosphorylation of UPF1 can help connect the termination context to recruitment of RNA decay machinery. Downstream routes may involve endonucleolytic cleavage or exonucleolytic pathways following changes in the terminal architecture. It is unnecessary to memorize every accessory complex at the introductory examination level. The causal chain is that translation detects an abnormal context, surveillance factors respond, and degradation reduces the abundance of the affected message.

The dependence on translation distinguishes this pathway from an RNA polymerase response to a nonsense mutation. A premature stop codon is recognized by a translating ribosome and its associated termination machinery, not by a ribosome reading genomic DNA in the nucleus. A mutation changing a sense codon into a stop codon can therefore alter both the length of the possible protein and the lifetime of its RNA. If a nonsense-mutant allele produces little detectable protein, the absence of a full-length product is not by itself proof that the ribosome never initiated. The ribosome may have initiated, encountered the early stop and contributed to subsequent RNA elimination.

Alternative splicing can regulate output through similar logic. Consider a gene with two splicing outcomes, one yielding a productive coding sequence and another introducing a premature stop in a suitable NMD-triggering context. Changing the relative frequency of these outcomes changes the supply of stable productive mRNA even if the rate of primary transcription remains fixed. A splicing regulator can therefore influence protein production by directing a fraction of transcripts into a nonproductive, rapidly removed isoform. This is a regulatory use of surveillance rather than simply a response to accidental errors. Some genes encoding RNA-binding or splicing regulators use such coupling as a form of feedback.

Not all alternative splicing changes protein output in this manner. Inclusion or exclusion of an exon may instead change a protein domain, alter an untranslated region, change localization or have little effect under the conditions examined. To distinguish these possibilities, the experiment must measure individual isoforms. A primer pair located in constitutive exons can report the combined abundance of multiple messages and may conceal a large change in the productive isoform. Junction-specific measurements can identify the relative abundance of the splice variants, while protein analysis can distinguish full-length products from alternative products if suitable detection reagents are available.

Consider a hypothetical gene whose mature transcript pool contains 100 molecules in both cell types. In the first cell 80 messages contain a productive coding region and 20 are poorly translated into the principal protein. In the second cell 30 are productive and 70 are poorly translated. If each productive RNA generates two completed proteins per minute and the other messages contribute negligibly, the fluxes are 160 and 60 proteins per minute. The ratio of the second flux to the first is 0.375, despite the same total RNA count. An assay that treats all isoforms as equivalent would miss the change in the pool actually supporting production of the principal protein.

NMD also affects some natural transcripts without a classical disease-associated nonsense mutation. Upstream reading frames, particular long 3′ regions and alternative processing can create termination contexts that favour surveillance. Organism-specific differences are important; the exon junction model is particularly informative in mammals but should not be imposed unchanged on every eukaryote. Other surveillance pathways respond to transcripts lacking a usable stop codon or to ribosomes encountering severe elongation problems. Those pathways illustrate the general coupling between translation and RNA quality control, but a detailed catalogue of ribosome rescue factors is beyond the present regulatory focus.

In an experimental test, a researcher could compare otherwise matched reporters carrying an early stop in a penultimate exon, an early stop in a final exon and an intact coding region. A targeted reduction in a core NMD factor could selectively increase the abundance of the first reporter if it is a substrate of the pathway. Nascent RNA measurements and suitable normalization are needed to exclude major transcriptional differences. The recovery of RNA does not guarantee recovery of normal protein; the premature stop remains in the sequence. A short polypeptide may become more detectable after the surveillance block, or it may still be unstable. Separating RNA rescue from restoration of normal protein function is crucial when predicting the consequences of a surveillance mutation.

8. Alternative untranslated regions and regulatory choice

The same gene can produce mRNAs with different untranslated regions even when the main coding sequence is unchanged. Alternative promoter use can change the 5′ leader; alternative splicing can include or exclude segments of that leader; and alternative selection of a 3′ cleavage and polyadenylation site can change the length and content of the 3′ untranslated region. These events create different regulatory architectures. A cell may switch from one message variant to another, altering the effective binding sites or structural obstacles encountered by regulatory proteins and the translation machinery. The resulting differences need not be visible in the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein.

Alternative polyadenylation offers a clear conceptual example. Suppose a gene contains two alternative 3′ end processing sites downstream of the same stop codon. Use of the proximal site generates a shorter 3′ UTR, whereas use of the distal site includes additional downstream RNA sequence. If the additional sequence contains a destabilizing element, the longer transcript may decay more rapidly in a cell expressing the relevant factor. If the additional region contains a localization signal, the longer transcript may be transported to a particular cellular domain while the shorter one remains more broadly distributed. The same protein can thus be produced in different amounts or locations from different versions of the message.

It is tempting to conclude that shorter 3′ UTRs always increase gene expression because regulatory sites are lost. This is an overgeneralization. A long region may contain stabilizing sites, sequences supporting efficient translation, or localization signals needed for productive synthesis in a particular cellular region. Removing such a region could decrease the functional output of the gene even if total cytoplasmic RNA is unchanged. The net result depends on which elements are removed and which regulatory proteins are active. Length is a readily measurable property but is not by itself a molecular mechanism.

Alternative 5′ leaders likewise influence access to the principal start codon. One variant may contain a stable stem-loop, an upstream initiation codon or an inhibitory RNA-binding site that is absent from another variant. Their coding regions may be identical, yet they recruit productive initiation complexes at different rates. A change in promoter usage could therefore have a downstream translational consequence in addition to any change in transcriptional output. To classify the mechanism properly, it is necessary to distinguish the upstream event that generates the RNA variant from the downstream event that determines its use.

A controlled experiment can compare RNA variants by cloning their untranslated regions around the same reporter coding sequence. The reporter constructs should ideally use a common promoter and comparable end-processing signals, so that the effect of the tested sequence is isolated. A reporter whose RNA abundance doubles while its protein synthesis per RNA remains constant demonstrates an effect on RNA supply under the tested conditions. Another reporter whose RNA remains unchanged but whose newly synthesized protein increases threefold demonstrates a change in productive use. If both variables change, the total difference in protein output may reflect the product of both effects.

Some regulatory events occur before export. A spliced message that fails to acquire appropriate export competence may accumulate in the nucleus but contribute little to cytoplasmic protein production. A measurement of total cellular RNA can therefore be misleading. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation, with controls for the purity of each fraction, can reveal a redistribution of messages between compartments. An RNA that is retained in the nucleus is not necessarily degraded; depending on its fate, it may be removed later or become available for export. This consideration connects the RNA processing and export chapters with the regulatory mechanisms studied here.

The distinction between nuclear processing and cytoplasmic expression becomes particularly important in a time-course experiment. Shortly after a signal, the relative abundance of a newly produced isoform may change first in the nucleus and later in the cytoplasm. A protein response may occur still later, after the new messages have been translated and the pre-existing protein pool has turned over. Observing a lag is consistent with a sequence of events, but the lag alone cannot identify the specific responsible step. Direct measurements at several stages provide a more reliable causal test.

A useful general principle is to identify the molecular population that the measurement actually reports. An RNA assay targeting a common coding exon may combine nuclear precursors, cytoplasmic mature RNAs and several isoforms, depending on its design. A poly(A)-selected RNA assay may omit certain non-polyadenylated species and may represent very short-tailed messages differently. A protein assay may combine products from multiple isoforms if its antibody recognizes a shared region. Careful matching of the measurement to the hypothesis is more valuable than simply collecting a large number of observations without considering what they represent.

Maternal RNA regulation demonstrates why a short poly(A) tail does not invariably indicate imminent degradation. In many vertebrate oocytes, selected messages are deposited and stored in a translationally repressed state with relatively short tails. During maturation, signals activate the use of particular stored messages. A cytoplasmic polyadenylation element in the 3′ UTR can recruit the RNA-binding protein CPEB. Changes in CPEB-associated regulatory interactions, including phosphorylation-dependent changes in well studied Xenopus systems, allow selected messages to acquire longer tails through cytoplasmic polyadenylation. Association with poly(A)-binding proteins and productive initiation interactions can then increase. The principal change occurs on RNA already present in the cytoplasm, not necessarily through a new transcription event.

The timing of the response is biologically useful. Proteins needed for transitions during oocyte maturation need not be synthesized at a high rate throughout the earlier storage period. Changing the functional state of a maternal message can permit a rapid increase in production at a later developmental stage. Not every maternal message is activated by CPEB or by tail lengthening, and a short tail may have different consequences in an oocyte than in a typical proliferating somatic cell. A tail-length assay combined with an RNA abundance measurement and a new-protein synthesis measurement can distinguish translational activation of a stored pool from increased accumulation of new RNA. A mutation in the cytoplasmic polyadenylation element that disrupts the response, while preserving the coding region, would support the importance of that element in the particular regulatory program.

9. Spatial regulation: transporting the message rather than only the protein

In a cell with a large or strongly polarized cytoplasm, the location of protein synthesis can matter as much as its total rate. A neuron has long processes that may be far from the nucleus, an epithelial cell has different apical and basal domains, and an oocyte can contain molecular asymmetries that will influence the developing embryo. If every protein were synthesized near the nucleus and then distributed uniformly, many local responses would require transport of newly made protein from a distant site. Localized messages provide an alternative. An RNA can be transported or selectively retained near the region where its protein is needed, allowing local control over the time and place of synthesis.

An RNA localization signal is sometimes called a zipcode. The analogy refers to a feature that helps specify a destination, not necessarily a simple universal sequence code. A zipcode may consist of a particular sequence, a secondary structure or several cooperating elements, often in the 3′ untranslated region. RNA-binding proteins recognize the signal and can associate with additional components involved in transport or anchoring. Molecular motors moving along cytoskeletal filaments provide one important mechanism of directed RNA movement. Other messages can reach their destinations by diffusion followed by trapping or by selective stabilization at a particular location. A concentrated RNA signal in a microscopic image does not by itself establish which of these mechanisms produced it.

The beta-actin message is a classic example in mammalian cell biology. A regulatory sequence within its 3′ untranslated region is recognized by zipcode-binding protein 1, also known as ZBP1 or IGF2BP1 in relevant vertebrate systems. This association contributes to localization of the message towards regions involved in cell movement, such as the leading edge of certain motile cells. Beta-actin is a component of the cytoskeleton, and local synthesis can contribute to spatially organized actin-dependent processes. The example does not imply that all actin at the leading edge must be synthesized locally. Transport and redistribution of existing actin proteins also contribute to the cellular supply.

Transport and translation can be coordinated. A newly formed transport particle may contain a message whose translation is repressed while it travels. Repression avoids producing the protein prematurely at an intermediate location and can prevent the message from being occupied by multiple translating ribosomes during transport. Upon reaching the destination, a local signal may change the regulatory protein's state or interactions and allow translation. In the ZBP1 system, phosphorylation by Src has been associated with relief of translational repression in appropriate cellular contexts. The important concept is that the same transport-associated RNA-binding protein can help connect a positional cue with a change in productive translation.

The Drosophila oocyte provides another informative model. Maternal bicoid mRNA is localized towards the anterior region, where subsequent translation contributes to a spatial distribution of Bicoid protein important for embryonic patterning. The early embryo need not establish this entire asymmetry by differential transcription from newly active embryonic nuclei. Instead, localization established during oogenesis and subsequent protein production contribute to the positional signal. Other maternal messages use different combinations of directed transport, anchoring, selective stability and translational repression. The developmental consequences of these gradients belong to the developmental biology unit; here the focus is the regulatory principle linking message location and protein production.

The localization of an mRNA and the distribution of its protein should not be treated as interchangeable. A protein may diffuse away from the site of synthesis, become anchored to a particular structure or be transported by its own targeting machinery. Conversely, a localized message may be translationally silent until a signal arrives. An RNA localization image reveals where the transcript is found, while a protein localization image reveals where the protein is found. Demonstrating local synthesis requires evidence of new protein production at or near the RNA's location, ideally with controls for protein transport and for the distribution of the message itself.

Consider a hypothetical polarized cell with two equal-volume regions, A and B. It contains 200 copies of a particular message. In the wild type, 160 copies occur in region A and 40 in region B; after deletion of the localization element, 100 occur in each region. If each message produces one completed protein per minute and proteins do not move during the short observation period, the regional production fluxes change from 160 and 40 to 100 and 100. The total flux remains 200 proteins per minute in both conditions. A whole-cell assay can therefore miss a substantial regulatory phenotype. A spatial measurement is essential when the biological question concerns where production occurs.

Suppose instead that localization is coupled to translational repression during transport. The same deletion might release a transported message from repression and raise its whole-cell synthesis rate even while impairing production at the correct location. In that situation, an increase in total protein does not necessarily represent improved biological function. The protein may be produced in an inappropriate compartment or at an inappropriate time. This type of reasoning is valuable in questions about mutations that affect regulatory RNA elements but leave the protein-coding sequence unchanged.

To test a localization signal experimentally, a researcher can attach a candidate 3′ UTR to a fluorescently detectable reporter RNA and compare its intracellular distribution with that of a matched reporter lacking the candidate element. RNA imaging methods, such as fluorescence in situ hybridization, can identify the location of the message. Measurements should account for cell geometry, total RNA abundance and the distribution of an appropriate control transcript. If the mutant is less abundant overall, an apparent loss of signal in a particular region may reflect destabilization rather than failure of transport. Quantifying the fraction of the RNA pool at the destination helps separate these possibilities.

Perturbation of cytoskeletal transport can provide supporting evidence, but broad disruption of microtubules or actin may alter cell shape and many other cellular processes. A localization change following such a perturbation is therefore consistent with a role for the cytoskeleton but is not conclusive proof of a particular motor–cargo interaction. A more specific perturbation of the RNA-binding or transport factor, combined with rescue and direct observation of the RNA distribution, can narrow the mechanism. As in stability experiments, a candidate cis signal and its trans-acting recognition machinery should be tested separately.

The spatial organization of translation illustrates how regulation can operate even when the amount of RNA and the intrinsic protein sequence are preserved. The cell has not changed the information carried by the main coding region; it has changed the conditions under which the information is used. This principle connects post-transcriptional control to cell polarity, intracellular transport and developmental asymmetry without requiring separate regulation at every protein destination.

10. Translational efficiency and cap-dependent initiation as a control point

A message can be structurally intact, correctly exported and relatively stable yet make little protein. The missing variable is the efficiency with which it is used by the translation machinery. For quantitative purposes, let a represent the mean rate of completed production of a particular polypeptide per available message, in proteins per RNA per minute. If M is the number of available messages, the total rate of new protein production is aM. This definition refers to completed productive synthesis, not merely binding of the small ribosomal subunit. A message may attract initiation complexes that fail to select the principal start codon, or ribosomes may initiate but fail to complete a normal product.

Initiation is a major point of control because productive loading is required before elongation can occur. In the usual cap-dependent model of cytoplasmic eukaryotic initiation, cap recognition and assembly of a recruitment-competent mRNP must be coordinated with a small ribosomal subunit carrying the initiator methionyl-tRNA. eIF4E recognizes the cap, eIF4G acts as an interaction scaffold and eIF4A contributes RNA-unwinding activity. The small-subunit preinitiation complex includes the initiator tRNA associated with eIF2 and other factors. These two sets of requirements can be regulated independently: a message may be poorly recruited because cap-associated interactions are impaired, or a recruited message may lack a supply of competent preinitiation complexes.

When the complex scans the 5′ leader, it examines potential start sites in the context of the surrounding nucleotides and the configuration of the RNA. In vertebrates, a purine at position −3 and a G at position +4 relative to the A of an AUG codon are particularly influential features of a favourable start context. These positions are not an absolute instruction to initiate. The rest of the context and the initiation state also contribute. If an upstream AUG occurs in a weak context, a proportion of scanning complexes may bypass it and initiate farther downstream. This process is called leaky scanning. If the upstream AUG is placed in a stronger context, more complexes may initiate there and fewer may reach a downstream start.

Secondary structure in the leader changes the physical difficulty of scanning. An RNA hairpin is stabilized by base pairing and stacking interactions, and its stability depends on nucleotide composition, sequence and environmental conditions. Initiation factors that rearrange RNA can use energy to help overcome structures. A strongly stabilized cap-proximal hairpin can inhibit productive recruitment or scanning even if the coding sequence and message abundance are unchanged. The effect of a stem-loop inserted far downstream may differ from the effect of one placed immediately adjacent to the cap. Experiments must therefore control both the sequence and its position.

It is useful to distinguish a mutation that changes initiation from one that changes the elongation rate. If the time required for an individual ribosome to traverse a coding region is unchanged, increasing initiation frequency can increase the number of ribosomes simultaneously associated with the message and increase protein output. If elongation slows, however, ribosomes may remain on the message for longer. The average number of bound ribosomes can rise despite unchanged or decreased production of completed proteins. The intensity of a polysome-associated signal is thus a proxy for translation state, not an unqualified measurement of protein synthesis.

Under a simple no-queue steady-state model, mean ribosome occupancy L is approximately equal to the successful initiation rate i multiplied by mean transit time T. If a message admits three ribosomes per minute and each requires two minutes to complete translation, the expected occupancy is six ribosomes. If initiation remains at three per minute but transit time doubles, occupancy becomes twelve while the eventual completion rate remains three per minute once the new steady state is established, assuming no loss or interference. With strong crowding, ribosome collisions or premature termination, that simple relationship no longer predicts the rate of completed product accurately. Occupancy and flux must be interpreted with their assumptions.

Several regulatory events can be understood as changes in access to a limiting factor. A particular leader containing substantial RNA structure may be more sensitive to a reduction in effective RNA-remodelling capacity than a leader that is easy to scan. Likewise, messages whose initiation is particularly dependent on efficient cap-associated recruitment can respond more strongly to a reduction in available eIF4E. The difference in sensitivity does not require the global regulator to recognize the sequence of each affected mRNA directly. Instead, a general change in initiation capacity interacts with intrinsic differences among messages.

This logic explains why a global translation response should not be described as an identical proportional change in every gene. A reduction in a shared resource shifts the operating conditions for all messages, but their relative dependence on that resource differs. Additional message-specific RNA-binding proteins may amplify or counteract the effect. In a stressed cell, one message may lose most of its productive initiation, another may decline modestly and a third may benefit from a particular upstream reading-frame arrangement. The next two sections examine two major pathways in which global initiation capacity is adjusted to cellular needs.

11. Stress control of the eIF2 nucleotide cycle

The initiator methionyl-tRNA must be delivered to the small ribosomal subunit in an appropriate initiation complex. In the conventional pathway, eIF2 bound to GTP associates with the initiator tRNA to form the ternary complex. Following start-codon recognition and the associated initiation events, eIF2 is released in a GDP-bound form. Repeated initiation therefore depends on a way of converting eIF2–GDP back into the GTP-bound state. The guanine nucleotide exchange factor eIF2B catalyses the exchange process, allowing the pool of eIF2 to be reused for successive initiation events.

A critical regulatory event is phosphorylation of the alpha subunit of eIF2, commonly described in mammals as phosphorylation at serine 51. This modification changes the interaction between eIF2 and eIF2B so that phosphorylated eIF2 inhibits the exchange factor rather than serving simply as an ordinary substrate. Because eIF2B is required to regenerate active eIF2, a reduction in its effective exchange activity can decrease the supply of ternary complex. The result is typically a decline in the rate of initiation on many cytoplasmic messages. The modification is not a phosphorylation of the mRNA, the initiator tRNA or the ribosome. It changes the activity of a shared initiation-factor recycling system.

The inhibitory effect is biologically important because eIF2B can be limiting relative to its substrate. A modification affecting a fraction of the eIF2 pool can therefore strongly influence the rate at which nonphosphorylated eIF2 is reactivated. Students sometimes imagine that phosphorylating half the eIF2 molecules simply removes exactly half of the active initiation capacity. That inference assumes the phosphorylated molecules are merely inactive and do not affect the recycling of other molecules. The inhibitory interaction with eIF2B makes the relationship potentially more nonlinear. A precise quantitative prediction would require information about factor concentrations, exchange activity and the relevant binding interactions.

In mammals, several stress-sensitive kinases converge on this regulatory site. GCN2 is associated particularly with amino acid limitation and signals involving uncharged tRNAs. PERK responds to stress affecting protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum. PKR is activated by appropriate double-stranded RNA signals, and HRI was classically identified in the context of heme availability in erythroid cells, with additional stress-responsive roles. These kinases detect distinct cellular problems but act through a shared initiation-factor control mechanism. Their inputs and regulation are not identical, and the presence of one stress signal does not establish that all four kinases are active.

When amino acids are limiting, continuing maximal protein synthesis can consume scarce substrates and increase the risk of translation problems. Decreasing initiation reduces the arrival of new ribosomes on messages, thereby lowering the demand placed on aminoacyl-tRNA supplies and the associated energy-consuming processes. When unfolded proteins accumulate in the endoplasmic reticulum, reducing the arrival of newly synthesized proteins can help lower the folding burden. The response can provide time for recovery, although prolonged or severe stress may have additional outcomes. The same molecular event can thus contribute to adaptation to different types of cellular stress.

The stress response is reversible. Phosphatase-associated regulatory proteins can promote removal of the inhibitory phosphate from eIF2α. In mammalian cells, GADD34 provides a well-known inducible feedback component in association with protein phosphatase 1, while related regulatory machinery also contributes to basal control. Dephosphorylation can restore exchange competence and increase ternary-complex availability. A cell needs such a recovery mechanism because permanent suppression of protein synthesis would eventually interfere with growth, repair and routine maintenance. The balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation helps determine the intensity and duration of the response.

An experiment examining the pathway can compare wild-type cells with cells carrying a nonphosphorylatable substitution at the regulated eIF2α site. A stress that normally increases phosphorylation and reduces global initiation might have a smaller effect through this route in the mutant. However, the mutant does not eliminate every possible response to stress. Energy limitation, changes in mTOR signaling, altered elongation or other pathways may still affect protein synthesis. To attribute a phenotype specifically to the eIF2 pathway, measure the modification, a direct readout of new protein synthesis and suitable pathway-sensitive reporters, rather than relying exclusively on one bulk protein measurement.

A kinase inhibitor or genetic deletion can be informative only when its specificity and the relevant stress context are considered. Deleting GCN2 is more directly relevant to a response driven by amino acid limitation than to a response known to depend on another kinase. Even then, compensatory mechanisms may operate. A nonphosphorylatable eIF2α mutant tests a convergence point, whereas individual kinase mutations test the importance of particular upstream branches. Comparing their phenotypes can help determine whether different stresses produce similar translational effects through shared or independent signaling components.

Bulk inhibition of initiation is not the whole story of the integrated stress response. Some transcripts show enhanced productive translation under conditions of reduced ternary-complex availability. At first this seems paradoxical: how can a reduction in an initiation resource promote production of a specific protein? The answer lies in how the scanning machinery encounters upstream open reading frames, and in the timing of its reacquisition of a competent initiation state. A shared reduction in initiation capacity can change which start sites are selected on a particular message, thereby changing the allocation of translation events between regulatory upstream regions and the principal coding sequence.

12. Upstream reading frames and the selective translation of ATF4

An upstream open reading frame, or uORF, is a region beginning at an initiation codon upstream of the principal coding sequence and ending at an appropriate stop codon. Its translation can interfere with initiation at the principal start site because some ribosomes dissociate after translating the upstream region. In other cases a small ribosomal subunit remains associated with the RNA and can resume scanning. Productive reinitiation then depends on reacquiring the factors and initiator tRNA needed to begin another round of translation. The distance between the upstream termination site and the next possible start codon becomes important, because it provides a time window for this reacquisition.

The ATF4 message in mammals is a classic example of regulation through the arrangement of upstream reading frames. ATF4 encodes a transcription factor involved in responses to stress, including changes in the expression of genes important for amino acid metabolism and cellular adaptation. Its leader contains a short upstream reading frame that can permit subsequent reinitiation and another upstream reading frame whose translation interferes with production of the main ATF4 protein. In the canonical model, the second upstream reading frame overlaps the main coding region in a different reading frame. A ribosome translating this inhibitory region does not simultaneously translate the principal ATF4 coding sequence.

Under conditions in which ternary complex is readily available, a small subunit that resumes scanning after the first upstream reading frame can reacquire initiation competence relatively rapidly. It therefore has a substantial probability of initiating at the second, inhibitory upstream start. As a consequence, fewer ribosomes reach and initiate the principal ATF4 coding sequence in a productive state. The abundant supply of initiation resources, which favours translation of many ordinary messages, can thus favour use of the inhibitory region on this particular RNA.

During the canonical integrated stress response, eIF2α phosphorylation lowers effective ternary-complex availability. A scanning subunit that has resumed movement after translation of the first upstream reading frame may pass the second upstream start before reacquiring initiation competence. If it acquires the needed components before encountering the principal ATF4 start, it can initiate the main coding sequence. Under appropriate stress conditions, this shift in the probability of start-site choice increases productive ATF4 translation even though global protein synthesis generally declines. The effect is a consequence of timing and arrangement, not a rule that phosphorylation directly activates translation of every message containing an upstream reading frame.

At extreme levels of initiation inhibition, the available supply of competent complexes may become too low even for efficient production of stress-induced proteins. The textbook comparison refers to a range of conditions under which selective reinitiation supports the stress response. It does not imply that ATF4 production increases indefinitely as ternary-complex availability approaches zero. Similarly, mutating the inhibitory uORF can raise basal ATF4 reporter expression but may reduce the contrast between unstressed and stressed conditions. A response measured as fold induction can therefore shrink even when the absolute output in one or both conditions increases.

A related but distinct model occurs in budding yeast with the GCN4 message. Multiple upstream reading frames contribute to the regulated use of the main coding sequence during amino acid limitation. In the classic model, translation of an upstream permissive reading frame and the timing of reinitiation influence whether downstream inhibitory reading frames are selected. When ternary-complex supply is reduced, a fraction of scanning subunits bypasses inhibitory sites and can initiate GCN4. The number and arrangement of upstream reading frames differ from mammalian ATF4, but both illustrate how changes in initiation-factor availability can be decoded into a selective protein response.

A reporter test of this principle can compare a wild-type ATF4 leader with a mutant lacking the inhibitory upstream start codon and a mutant disrupting the upstream permissive region. The main reporter coding sequence, promoter and RNA measurement should be matched. A hypothetical pattern is low basal reporter synthesis and higher synthesis under stress for the wild-type leader, high basal output with reduced induction for the inhibitory-start mutant, and impaired stress-dependent output for a mutation that disrupts the normal permissive reinitiation arrangement. The precise phenotype depends on the substitutions and the resulting RNA structure, so the pattern is a mechanistic prediction rather than a universal experimental result.

The central distinction between leaky scanning and reinitiation must remain clear. During leaky scanning, a scanning complex passes an upstream start without beginning translation there. During reinitiation, a ribosome has already translated an upstream coding region and a retained small subunit resumes scanning after termination. The availability of initiation factors following termination becomes particularly significant for reinitiation. Both mechanisms can influence access to a main start codon, and in a natural leader more than one mechanism may contribute. A diagram showing ribosomes at different positions should therefore specify whether the upstream region was actually translated.

These examples are important because they challenge the assumption that every inhibitory uORF is a fixed obstacle. The effect of a uORF can change with the surrounding initiation environment. Students should identify the order of possible start and stop sites, whether the scanning complex remains competent, and which downstream start is accessible under the specified conditions. Reasoning through these events provides a stronger explanation of the stress response than memorizing that ATF4 is an exception to global translational repression.

13. Nutrient-responsive cap recruitment and eIF4E availability

Growth demands the synthesis of many proteins, including enzymes, structural components and the machinery needed to make additional proteins. Such investment is worthwhile when energy and nutrients are sufficiently available, but it can be wasteful during deprivation. A major regulatory connection between nutrient availability and cytoplasmic protein synthesis in mammalian cells involves the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1, abbreviated mTORC1. This protein kinase complex integrates information about cellular resources and growth-related signals. Its upstream control belongs in the cell-signaling unit. Here the central question is how its activity can alter the productive use of an mRNA that already exists.

The cap-binding protein eIF4E must associate with suitable partners to help recruit initiation machinery. Recognition of the cap alone is not the entire function. The interaction between eIF4E and eIF4G helps connect the capped mRNA with other components required for initiation. A family of eIF4E-binding proteins, called 4E-BPs, can bind to eIF4E and compete with eIF4G for association. When a 4E-BP occupies the relevant interaction surface, eIF4E is less available to form a productive eIF4F complex. This represents regulation of a protein–protein interaction rather than destruction of the cap itself.

When mTORC1 activity is high under appropriate nutrient and growth conditions, phosphorylation of 4E-BPs generally reduces their affinity for eIF4E through a sequence of phosphorylation events. More eIF4E can then participate in cap-associated initiation. When mTORC1 activity falls, the more weakly phosphorylated forms of 4E-BPs can associate strongly with eIF4E and compete with eIF4G. Consequently, recruitment through this route becomes less effective for sensitive messages. The direction of this regulatory relationship is sometimes confusing: phosphorylation of eIF2α typically inhibits its recycling pathway, whereas phosphorylation of a 4E-BP by mTORC1 can relieve repression of cap-dependent initiation. The identity and function of the phosphorylated protein determine the outcome.

The mTORC1 response should not be reduced to a single linear connection between nutrients and a cap-binding factor. Other substrates, including S6 kinases, contribute to control of cellular growth and the protein synthesis apparatus. Some classes of transcripts have particularly recognizable nutrient-dependent responses. Messages with a terminal oligopyrimidine tract at the extreme 5′ end, including many messages encoding ribosomal proteins and translation-related components, often show strong sensitivity to nutrient-responsive translational regulation. The tract is distinct from an arbitrary pyrimidine-rich region in the middle of a leader. Additional RNA-binding machinery, including LARP1 in mammalian models, contributes to regulation of such messages. A student need not infer that every mTOR-sensitive message contains a terminal oligopyrimidine sequence.

A comparison with the eIF2 pathway clarifies what the cell is controlling. Reduced ternary-complex availability primarily affects the supply of initiation complexes capable of selecting a start codon. Reduced availability of eIF4E for productive eIF4G association primarily affects cap-associated recruitment. Both can lower translation, and both can be influenced by nutrient or stress conditions. However, the molecular lesions are different. A mutation that prevents 4E-BP binding to eIF4E may weaken one form of cap-dependent repression but does not automatically restore ternary-complex supply in a cell with phosphorylated eIF2α. Restoring one initiation resource will be insufficient if the other has become limiting.

Imagine a hypothetical system in which a certain message can complete four polypeptides per minute under resource-rich conditions. A nutrient intervention reduces its effective cap-recruitment capacity by half while ternary-complex availability remains adequate. In a simple proportional model its output falls to two polypeptides per minute. If ternary-complex availability subsequently falls, the output might decline further. The two effects cannot generally be multiplied or added without specifying the kinetic model. If one step already severely limits productive initiation, altering a different step can have little additional effect. A serial pathway does not necessarily respond independently to every change in component abundance.

A suitable experiment can compare cells under nutrient-replete and nutrient-limited conditions while measuring 4E-BP phosphorylation and the association of eIF4E with eIF4G. An interaction assay based on cap-affinity capture or protein immunoprecipitation can indicate whether the associated protein composition changes. A decrease in eIF4E–eIF4G association together with increased eIF4E–4E-BP association is consistent with competition for eIF4E. Parallel measurements of nascent protein production and reporter RNA abundance are needed to connect this biochemical change to actual translational output. Association alone does not establish that the measured reporter's synthesis has changed.

A genetic test can compare wild-type 4E-BP with a version that cannot associate with eIF4E or a version that retains inhibitory binding despite conditions normally favouring phosphorylation. The first version is expected to have a reduced capacity to compete with eIF4G. The second can make cap recruitment less responsive to growth-promoting signals if it remains present and functional. Such predictions depend on the actual properties of the engineered protein and its expression level. Excessive expression of a binding protein can create artificial competition that is not representative of the physiological state. Matching expression and testing the interaction directly strengthen the causal interpretation.

Cap-dependent initiation is a predominant mode of translation for nuclear-encoded cytoplasmic eukaryotic messages, but it is not the only possible way to recruit ribosomes. Some RNAs use alternative initiation mechanisms under suitable conditions. The existence of such mechanisms does not mean that every RNA remaining translated during mTOR inhibition is using an internal ribosome entry site. Differences in residual cap-associated capacity, leader architecture and other regulators can also explain selective translation. Establishing a cap-independent mechanism requires a specifically designed experiment, with controls excluding alternative transcription, processing and other artifacts. The correct examination inference is to distinguish the prevailing mechanism from possible exceptions, without using those exceptions as a blanket explanation.

14. Integrating RNA supply, productive synthesis and protein loss

The previous sections describe molecular mechanisms that change different variables of the same expression system. A useful quantitative representation connects these variables rather than treating them as independent lists of proteins. Suppose that M is the number of functional messages per cell and P is the number of molecules of their protein product. If a is the rate of completed polypeptide production per message and g is the effective first-order loss constant of the protein, the simplest model is dP/dt = aM − gP. The term aM has units of protein molecules per cell per minute, and gP has the same units. Protein degradation, and where appropriate dilution, contribute to the loss term. This model concerns abundance of the product, not necessarily its biochemical activity.

If the mature message pool has a constant entry rate s and first-order removal constant k, its steady abundance is s/k. Provided translation per message and protein removal are constant, protein abundance at steady state is Pss = aMss/g = as/(kg). The expression separates four regulatory levers. Increasing mature RNA entry, increasing translation per message, decreasing RNA loss and decreasing protein loss can all increase the final stock of protein. A steady-state protein measurement by itself cannot determine which of the four variables changed. Even if one has measured RNA abundance, the variables a and g remain confounded unless further evidence is collected.

Suppose two hypothetical cell types contain 120 functional messages per cell. In cell A, each message produces 1.5 completed proteins per minute and the protein loss constant is 0.03 min⁻¹. Its production flux is 180 proteins per minute and its steady protein stock is 6000. Cell B has the same RNA abundance but a translation rate of 0.75 proteins per message per minute and a loss constant of 0.015 min⁻¹. Its flux is 90 proteins per minute but its steady protein stock is also 6000. Thus both the RNA amount and protein amount are identical, even though the second cell makes protein at half the rate. Only a measurement of synthesis or turnover would distinguish the two states.

The converse also matters. If the protein concentration falls while RNA abundance remains unchanged, reduced translation is a plausible explanation, but a rise in degradation can produce an identical steady-state phenotype. To distinguish these hypotheses, measure incorporation of a suitable label into newly produced protein during a short interval, and separately measure the disappearance of labelled protein or an appropriately tracked protein population. The synthesis measurement should be normalized to the available messages and the relevant cell number or mass. If new synthesis per message is unchanged but the protein disappears more rapidly, altered turnover becomes the stronger explanation.

Time-dependent responses provide additional information. Consider a protein pool initially at 1000 molecules per cell, maintained by production of 100 molecules per minute and loss constant 0.1 min⁻¹. A translation-specific switch halves synthesis immediately to 50 molecules per minute while the loss constant is unchanged. The new steady state is 500, but the old protein does not vanish instantaneously. With constant parameters, P(t) = 500 + 500 exp(−0.1t). After approximately 6.93 minutes, one protein half-life, the expected stock is 750. A short experiment measuring only the existing protein can therefore underestimate a rapid decrease in new synthesis. The earliest detectable event depends on which molecular quantity is measured.

If RNA stability changes instead, the protein response may exhibit two successive timescales: the message approaches its new steady state, and protein then responds to the changing synthesis flux. It is generally incorrect to use a single exponential for the protein simply because the RNA follows one. The protein equation contains a time-varying source term aM(t). When RNA turnover is much faster than protein turnover, a step approximation for the RNA can sometimes simplify the prediction. When protein turnover is fast relative to RNA turnover, protein abundance may follow the RNA more closely. These limiting cases are useful for graph interpretation, but should not be assumed without considering the two timescales.

Combined changes can oppose one another. Suppose a stimulus doubles the abundance of a particular message by increasing its stability, while reducing productive synthesis per message to one quarter of its original value. With unchanged protein turnover, the predicted steady protein abundance is one half of the original: the relative production flux is 2 × 0.25 = 0.5. If protein loss also falls to half its former rate, the protein stock returns to the original value despite the decreased synthesis flux. Such examples demonstrate why the common idea that more RNA means more protein is not a reliable universal inference.

Real cells can contain multiple functional isoforms, varying translation rates and protein pools in distinct locations. The expression model is still useful if the variables are defined for a particular compartment or message class. For a mixture, the total production flux is the sum of the rates from each productive message class, rather than the total RNA number multiplied by an arbitrary rate assigned to one class. Similarly, a protein that is modified after synthesis may enter a functional state only after additional processes. Changes in protein activity are not necessarily changes in protein synthesis. Examination questions often specify constant degradation, identical protein coding sequences or equal compartment sizes precisely to make one variable identifiable. Those assumptions should be used explicitly, not silently extended to real biological systems.

15. Experimental separation of transcription, stability and translation

A convincing explanation of post-transcriptional regulation needs more than a correlation between RNA and protein abundance. An experimental strategy begins with several competing hypotheses. If a stimulus increases a reporter protein, the cell could have increased transcription, processed more primary RNA into a mature form, stabilized the mature message, translated it more efficiently or slowed protein removal. A mechanism can also combine several of these effects. Instead of choosing an explanation based on which pathway is most familiar, identify measurements that produce different predictions under the alternatives.

A measurement of newly synthesized RNA can provide information about transcription or entry into the measured RNA pool. Pulse labeling of RNA with a suitable nucleotide analogue, followed by an appropriate separation or detection strategy, can distinguish recently produced molecules from older ones. A subsequent chase can follow the labelled population as it disappears. The experimental design must account for residual label, processing delays and the possibility that the label affects cell physiology. The advantage over measuring total RNA alone is that production and disappearance can be studied separately. A transcriptional inhibitor can also be used to estimate decay after stopping new RNA synthesis, but the inhibitor may cause stress or alter the very decay machinery being studied. Independent approaches are therefore desirable.

Northern blotting and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR measure RNA abundance through different principles. A northern blot separates RNA by size before detection and can help identify different transcript lengths or degradation intermediates. Quantitative PCR can sensitively measure a chosen sequence after reverse transcription, but it may not distinguish full-length messages from fragments containing that sequence. An amplicon located in one exon can also combine different isoforms. When RNA integrity or processing is central to the hypothesis, primer placement, sample quality and complementary measurements matter. A reference gene must be validated under the experimental condition; a transcript that itself changes during stress can produce misleading normalization.

For protein abundance, immunoblotting can provide information about the amount and apparent size of an immunoreactive product, while an enzymatic or fluorescent reporter measures a property of its product that depends on reporter maturation and activity as well as abundance. A short pulse of labelled amino acids, or another suitable new-protein labeling approach, is more directly connected to synthesis during a defined interval. The total labeling signal can be affected by amino acid uptake and pool size, so comparison requires appropriate controls. A decline in label incorporation during nutrient limitation may indicate lower synthesis but should not automatically be attributed to a particular initiation factor without biochemical or genetic evidence.

Polysome profiling provides a different perspective. Cytoplasmic extracts can be separated on a density gradient so that free subunits, monosomes and complexes containing multiple ribosomes are distributed among fractions. A transcript associated with heavy polysomes generally has multiple ribosomes bound at the time of collection. A shift towards lighter fractions can support reduced initiation when elongation remains comparable. However, slower ribosome movement or stalling can increase occupancy without increasing completed synthesis. An 80S-associated message can also be translated, especially if its coding region is short. A polysome profile is most informative when it is interpreted together with RNA abundance, coding-region length and an independent measure of synthesis.

Ribosome profiling extends this principle by examining ribosome-protected RNA fragments. Mapping the fragments to transcripts can reveal where ribosomes are associated with coding regions or upstream open reading frames. It is useful for studying relative ribosome occupancy and changes in start-site use. Normalizing footprint counts to the abundance of the corresponding RNA can give an operational estimate of translational efficiency. However, footprints measure occupancy rather than directly counting completed proteins. A pause at a specific codon may increase local footprint density; loss of downstream ribosomes may reflect reduced continuation rather than a decrease in initial recruitment. The basic limitation is shared with polysome profiling.

Reporter experiments are especially powerful for testing untranslated regions. Suppose a control reporter and a candidate 3′ UTR reporter share a promoter and coding sequence. A stimulus causes the candidate RNA to rise fourfold, its measured half-life to increase fourfold and its new protein production to increase fourfold. The production rate per RNA has not changed, and a matched nascent RNA measurement shows no increase. These hypothetical observations support stabilization as the principal mechanism. A second stimulus increases new protein production fourfold without changing the candidate RNA or its lifetime. That pattern supports translational activation, assuming that measurement of newly produced protein is reliable. Mutating the suspected recognition site provides a direct test of the sequence dependence.

When interpreting rescue experiments, the distinction between necessary and sufficient evidence is important. If removing an RNA-binding protein eliminates a reporter response, that protein is necessary for the response in the tested system, subject to possible indirect consequences. Restoring the protein and recovering the response strengthens the causal argument. However, expression of the factor alone in another cell may not produce the same response if an essential partner is absent. A binding assay using the RNA and the purified protein can establish direct association under the tested conditions, but association is not by itself proof of a regulatory outcome. Functional measurements must connect binding with altered lifetime, localization or protein synthesis.

A particularly informative examination problem combines a cis mutation and a trans mutation. Suppose a destabilizing element is proposed to act through a particular binding protein. Compare four conditions: an intact reporter in wild-type cells, a motif-mutant reporter in wild-type cells, an intact reporter in cells lacking the protein, and the motif-mutant reporter in cells lacking the protein. If both individual perturbations stabilize the RNA to a similar degree and the double perturbation has little additional effect, the results are consistent with a shared pathway. This pattern does not establish direct binding, since two changes could converge indirectly on the same limiting process. Direct binding and rescue measurements remain valuable.

A robust experimental conclusion thus combines several kinds of evidence: association between regulator and target, dependence on a specific RNA element, a measurable change in the predicted molecular step, and a perturbation or rescue supporting causality. No single assay is appropriate for every post-transcriptional problem. The central skill is to choose the observation that distinguishes competing explanations rather than to memorize an instrument as a universal test of gene expression.

The broad principles of RNA protection, regulated recruitment and differential use of messages are shared across eukaryotic lineages, but particular mechanisms should not be imposed indiscriminately on every species. Budding yeast provides a model of the GCN2–eIF2–GCN4 response. Mammals possess multiple eIF2α kinases and use ATF4 as an important downstream transcriptional regulator. Plants also possess GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation in response to several conditions, including some forms of nutrient limitation, but the extent to which the downstream responses reproduce the mammalian exchange-factor and selective uORF models is context dependent. Arabidopsis findings cannot be predicted simply by assuming that every mammalian eIF2α kinase has an identical plant counterpart. In an examination problem, the organism and experimentally defined pathway matter.

The nuclear–cytoplasmic distinction is especially important when comparing the usual expression of eukaryotic nuclear genes with bacterial expression. Bacterial ribosomes can often engage a message while it is still being transcribed, whereas eukaryotic nuclear transcripts usually undergo processing and export before cytoplasmic translation. Classical bacterial initiation can use a Shine–Dalgarno interaction rather than the cap-associated scanning system examined here, though bacterial initiation mechanisms also vary. Bacterial polyadenylation can promote RNA decay in some systems rather than conferring the conventional stabilizing role often associated with eukaryotic mRNA tails. These differences reflect different terminal architectures, recognition machinery and cellular organization. Both systems nevertheless exploit the accessibility of regulatory RNA sequences and the lifetime of messages to control protein output.

Biotechnology uses these principles when comparing expression constructs. A researcher who wants a reporter protein to be produced at a suitable level may vary the 5′ leader, the 3′ UTR or the terminal architecture while keeping the principal coding sequence constant. This permits evaluation of regulatory properties independently of protein sequence. The aim is not automatically to maximize translation at any cost: an RNA intended to support a brief pulse of expression may require a different lifetime from one intended to support a longer response. A candidate sequence that performs well in one cell type may not perform identically in another because their RNA-binding proteins, initiation capacity and degradation machinery differ. The same principle is relevant to the conceptual design of mRNA-based therapeutics, in which stability and productive protein synthesis must be considered together. Functional evaluation requires measured RNA and protein output, rather than an assumption that one optimized sequence will be universal.

An integrated perspective also clarifies the division of labour among the surrounding chapters. Transcriptional regulatory elements determine how much primary RNA is produced; processing and export determine which molecular forms enter the cytoplasmic pool; the post-transcriptional mechanisms considered here regulate survival, localization and productive use. Protein maturation and degradation then influence how much functional product persists. RNA interference supplies a further route by which small RNAs direct selective silencing and is examined separately in the later node. These controls can act on a single gene at the same time, so an observed expression phenotype may involve changes at more than one level. Mechanistic explanations should therefore identify both the immediate regulated step and its downstream consequences.

Examination traps

  1. More RNA always means more protein. Measure translation and protein loss too; RNA is a supply term, not the complete output. Exam cue: equal transcript counts with unequal synthesis or protein half-lives. The mistake comes from treating a template count as a product count.
  2. An unchanged RNA stock proves unchanged transcription. Production and loss may change together. Measure newly made RNA. Exam cue: a steady-state stock after compensating changes in two rates. The error confuses a constant amount with a constant flux.
  3. The 3′ UTR is an intron. A 3′ UTR remains in mature RNA; an excised intron normally does not. Exam cue: compare a mature UTR mutation and a splice-junction mutation. The mistake comes from equating noncoding sequence with removed sequence.
  4. A UTR cannot be translated. Upstream ORFs can encode short peptides; the term describes the region relative to the main coding sequence. Exam cue: ask whether a leader contains a translated uORF. The mistake arises from interpreting untranslated as an absolute property.
  5. All AU-rich motifs destabilize RNA. Context and trans factors determine the outcome; assess a matched reporter. Exam cue: motif transfer succeeds in one cell but fails in another. The mistake assumes the RNA motif acts alone.
  6. Every RNA-binding protein promotes decay. Some stabilize, localize or inhibit translation; identify the effector process. Exam cue: compare two proteins recognizing related RNA sequences with opposite effects. The mistake confuses recognition with effector function.
  7. Every mRNA decays through XRN1. Exosome-mediated and cleavage-dependent routes also exist. Exam cue: inhibit XRN1 and observe partial rather than complete stabilization. The error assumes a dominant pathway is the only pathway.
  8. Every mRNA is polyadenylated. Replication-dependent metazoan histone mRNAs provide a classic exception. Exam cue: ask which RNA uses a specialized 3′ stem-loop. The error treats the usual textbook mRNA diagram as universal.
  9. IRP has the same sign on every target. Ferritin initiation is restricted while TfR1 RNA is stabilized under low iron. Exam cue: move or delete IRE recognition sites and measure translation versus lifetime. The mistake assigns a single regulatory sign to IRP.
  10. All premature stops trigger NMD. Termination context and organism matter; final-exon stops often escape the EJC-dependent route. Exam cue: compare early stops in penultimate and final exons. The mistake ignores the context in which termination occurs.
  11. NMD rescue repairs a mutant protein. The premature stop can remain even when the RNA survives. Exam cue: a surveillance knockout restores RNA but not full-length protein. The error confuses RNA rescue with repair of the sequence.
  12. eIF2α phosphorylation simply removes a proportional fraction of eIF2. It can inhibit eIF2B and thereby alter recycling of other eIF2 molecules. Exam cue: a relatively small phosphorylated pool strongly limits initiation. The mistake ignores inhibition of the shared exchange factor.
  13. 4E-BP phosphorylation has the same inhibitory sign as eIF2α phosphorylation. mTORC1-dependent 4E-BP phosphorylation can relieve repression; eIF2α phosphorylation generally restricts ternary-complex supply. Exam cue: compare the two phosphorylation events in a pathway diagram. The mistake assumes phosphorylation always has the same sign.
  14. A stress-induced rise in ATF4 means bulk translation rose. uORF arrangement permits selective translation despite broad inhibition. Exam cue: ATF4 reporter increases during lower total amino acid incorporation. The mistake assumes all mRNAs respond identically.
  15. Heavy polysomes always prove faster synthesis. Slow elongation can raise occupancy; measure new protein production. Exam cue: more ribosome footprints but unchanged new protein pulse. The mistake equates ribosome stock with completed flux.
  16. Shorter UTRs always raise expression. A deleted segment may be stabilizing, inhibitory or needed for localization. Exam cue: delete a distal stabilizing site. The error assumes all additional UTR sequence is repressive.

Revision and mastery

Protein output reflects the availability, location, productive use and survival of RNA and protein. The cap and tail communicate with the initiation and decay machinery; regulatory UTR elements connect individual messages to trans-acting factors. IRP illustrates position-dependent control, NMD links termination to message survival, and stress and nutrient signaling modulate distinct initiation resources. Interpret experimental data by separating synthesis flux from abundance.

Equations and assumptions

  • dM/dt = s − kM; Mss = s/k for constant entry and first-order removal.
  • M(t) = M0 exp(−kt) when entry ceases; t½ = ln 2/k.
  • After a step change, M(t) = Mss,new + (M0 − Mss,new) exp(−kt).
  • Production flux = aM; dP/dt = aM − gP; Pss = as/(kg) for constant parameters.
  • Mean ribosome occupancy ≈ initiation frequency × mean transit time under a steady, no-queue model.
