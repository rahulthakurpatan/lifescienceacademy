1. From a translated chain to a functional protein

When a ribosome releases a newly synthesized polypeptide, it has completed a remarkable chemical task: it has joined amino acids in the order specified by a messenger RNA. Yet the ability of that chain to carry out a biological task is not determined by sequence alone. A receptor may need to receive a chemical group before it can recruit a partner. A digestive enzyme may need to lose a short inhibitory segment before its active site becomes useful. A secreted protein may need a carbohydrate chain to survive passage through the endoplasmic reticulum. In each case the genetic message specifies the original amino acid sequence, while an additional cellular process establishes the chemical form that performs the task.

Consider two cells carrying identical copies of a gene for a metabolic enzyme. Both cells can synthesize the same amino acid sequence at similar rates. If one cell experiences a nutritional signal that activates a protein kinase, a fraction of its enzyme molecules may acquire covalently attached phosphate groups. The other cell may contain mostly unmodified molecules. The two populations can now differ in catalytic activity even though neither transcription nor translation has changed. This distinction gives cells a way to respond over seconds or minutes without waiting for new RNA and protein synthesis.

A post-translational modification, commonly abbreviated PTM, is an alteration of the covalent chemical structure of a protein after a residue has been incorporated into a polypeptide. Many modifications happen after the chain has been released, but some begin while the chain is still attached to the ribosome. The word post-translational describes modification relative to formation of the encoded polypeptide rather than imposing an absolute chronological rule for termination. N-terminal acetylation and N-linked glycosylation provide important examples of processes that may be cotranslational. It would therefore be incorrect to draw a universal boundary at the moment a release factor hydrolyses peptidyl-tRNA.

Three phenomena must be distinguished. Folding rearranges the spatial positions of atoms in the polypeptide, largely through noncovalent interactions. Binding a regulatory ligand may change protein conformation without adding a covalent bond. Modification changes chemical connectivity, for example by linking a phosphoryl group to a hydroxyl oxygen. These events can influence one another. A folded domain may expose a modification site; attachment of a phosphate may change the favored conformation; binding of a regulatory partner may prevent a modifying enzyme from reaching a residue. Nevertheless, a change in conformation alone does not demonstrate a PTM. Formation of a disulfide bond is a covalent oxidative event, although its significance for folding was addressed in the preceding node.

Some modifications add groups from cellular donors. Some alter groups already present, and some hydrolyse peptide bonds to release segments. A mature insulin molecule, for example, is not simply a folded precursor with a different shape: specific peptide bonds have been broken. The excised sequence cannot be replaced by a simple reverse reaction, because synthesizing a peptide bond within the appropriate chain would require a specialized biosynthetic process. In comparison, phosphatases can remove a phosphate from a modified protein and allow the same molecule to enter another cycle of phosphorylation. The biological meaning of a modification therefore depends on its chemistry and the lifetime of its product.

The idea that one gene produces one chemically uniform protein is a useful initial simplification but inadequate for cellular biology. A population of polypeptides having identical sequence may contain an unmodified form, a phosphorylated form, an acetylated form or a molecule carrying several groups simultaneously. Each chemically distinct molecular form is a proteoform. Alternative splicing or sequence variants may also contribute to proteoform diversity, but those are separate from changes introduced chemically into an existing chain. A protein's abundance measurement can conceal major differences in the proportions of its proteoforms.

Imagine a particular enzyme is present at a total concentration of ten micromolar. If phosphorylation completely inhibits its activity and eighty percent of the molecules are phosphorylated, only two micromolar is available in its active unmodified state. In another condition, twenty percent phosphorylation leaves eight micromolar active. Provided that substrate availability and the catalytic properties of the active molecules are identical, the maximal catalytic rate would differ fourfold even though the total amount of protein was unchanged. This illustrates why a western blot against the total enzyme and a direct activity measurement answer different biological questions. The former measures abundance, the latter measures function under the assay conditions.

The chemical identity of the modifying group cannot by itself predict whether the modification activates or inhibits a protein. Phosphorylation activates glycogen phosphorylase in a classic example, but phosphorylation can inhibit glycogen synthase. Attachment of carbohydrate may assist folding, protect a surface from degradation or provide a recognition element. It may also be unimportant for the catalytic activity measured with a purified protein in vitro while being essential for correct delivery inside the cell. To determine a modification's role, one must identify its site, examine its structural environment and connect the chemistry with the cellular outcome.

A useful way to organize every example in this chapter is to ask what substrate is modified, what atom participates in the linkage, what donor supplies the chemical group, which enzyme catalyses the reaction, where that enzyme can encounter the protein, and what consequence follows. A seventh question asks whether a reverse enzyme or another removal pathway exists. This sequence gives a common intellectual framework for studying diverse modification types without memorizing them as disconnected names.

2. Chemical foundations and site selection

The amino acids of a protein present different reactive chemical groups. Serine and threonine contain alcoholic hydroxyl groups, while tyrosine has a phenolic hydroxyl group on an aromatic ring. All three can participate in oxygen-linked phosphorylation. Asparagine contains a carboxamide side chain, whose nitrogen forms the attachment point of a typical N-linked glycan. Lysine carries a terminal amino group on its side chain. At physiological pH this group is usually positively charged when free, although its precise protonation depends on its local environment. It can receive an acetyl group, a methyl group or a small protein such as ubiquitin. Arginine has a guanidinium group and can be methylated. Cysteine bears a thiol group, capable of participating in thioester formation with fatty acids or thioether formation with isoprenoid groups.

In addition to side chains, the termini of a polypeptide are chemically distinct. The alpha amino group at the amino terminus can be acetylated; a terminal glycine can accept a myristoyl group through its amino nitrogen if the proper enzyme and sequence are present. This amino group is not the epsilon amino group of a lysine side chain. Both may participate in acetylation, but the enzymes recognizing the two sites and the biological consequences can differ profoundly. The carboxyl terminus likewise becomes important in certain maturation processes, including recognition of a terminal motif for prenylation. Whenever an examination problem specifies the identity of the modified residue, determine whether the functional group belongs to the side chain or to a terminus.

Covalent bonds are not made efficiently merely because two molecules collide. A reaction can require activation of a donor, correct alignment of reacting groups and stabilization of a transition state. Cellular modifying enzymes supply specificity and lower activation barriers. A kinase typically uses ATP as a phosphoryl donor; an acetyltransferase commonly transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA; a methyltransferase usually uses S-adenosylmethionine. Glycosyltransferases use activated sugars, and many lipid-transfer enzymes use activated lipid donors. The cell invests metabolic resources in producing these donors. A modification is consequently connected to metabolism even when its biological consequence appears to involve signaling rather than energy production.

For a transfer reaction, it is helpful to distinguish the chemical group that becomes attached from the remainder of its donor. ATP does not generally remain attached to a substrate after a conventional protein kinase reaction. The terminal phosphoryl group is transferred and ADP is released. When an acetyl group is transferred from acetyl-CoA to a lysine amino group, CoA is released. When methyl transfer occurs from S-adenosylmethionine, the product S-adenosylhomocysteine is formed. Correct donor accounting avoids a frequent examination error in which an entire donor molecule is incorrectly treated as the modification.

Specificity involves more than chemical compatibility. A protein containing fifty serines does not necessarily acquire fifty phosphates. An enzyme may recognize a local sequence motif near one serine. Other interactions can recruit that enzyme through a remote docking region, raising the local concentration of substrate near the catalytic site. In a folded protein, a suitable residue may be buried and inaccessible even when its sequence resembles a preferred motif. A region may become exposed after ligand binding or another modification. Protein association with a scaffold may bring an enzyme and substrate into proximity, whereas separation by an organelle membrane can prevent them from meeting.

The recognition sequence for typical eukaryotic N-glycosylation offers a particularly clear illustration. An asparagine followed by a non-proline residue and then serine or threonine is a candidate for glycan attachment. This motif, frequently written N-X-S/T with X not proline, provides a useful predictor. It is not sufficient to guarantee occupancy. The segment must reach the ER lumen and be accessible to the luminally oriented glycosylation machinery. A cytosolic enzyme may carry several sequences matching the motif without acquiring conventional secretory-pathway N-glycans. A sequence near a transmembrane segment or a protein terminus may also be less efficiently modified depending on context. A mutation that changes N-X-T to N-P-T therefore eliminates the standard motif, while the reverse change creates a candidate rather than proof of attachment.

Localization is itself a determinant of chemical specificity. A plasma membrane protein has extracellular portions and cytosol-facing portions. During synthesis along the secretory pathway, the extracellular portions face the lumen of the ER and later the Golgi. Conventional N-linked glycans can therefore occur on these portions. The cytosolic tail of the very same protein may be phosphorylated by a cytosolic kinase or ubiquitinated by cytosolic machinery. The protein crosses a membrane but its enzyme accessibility is not uniform. A problem that states only that a protein is membrane-associated gives insufficient information to decide the glycosylation potential of every region.

A modification may be stoichiometric, affecting nearly every molecule of a certain protein, or substoichiometric, affecting only a fraction. Occupancy is the fraction of the molecules bearing the particular mark at a defined site. For a population containing P unmodified molecules and M modified molecules, site occupancy is M divided by the sum P plus M. Occupancy is distinct from the concentration of M, which changes with the total abundance of the substrate. If a treatment halves the total protein but leaves half of its molecules modified in both conditions, occupancy remains constant while the amount of modified protein decreases by half.

Consider a cell containing two micromolar of a target protein. An assay calibrated to distinguish the forms detects 0.6 micromolar carrying a particular phosphate. Occupancy is 0.6 divided by 2, or thirty percent. After stimulation, the total protein increases to four micromolar and the amount of phosphorylated protein increases to 1.2 micromolar. A phospho-specific signal may have doubled, yet occupancy has not changed. This example explains why normalizing a site-specific measurement to a measurement of total protein is often necessary before concluding that the balance of modification and removal has changed.

A modifier may alter charge, hydrogen bonding, steric shape, hydrophobicity or affinity for recognition proteins. Acetylation of lysine eliminates its ordinary protonatable side-chain amino group by forming an amide. Methylation of lysine, unlike acetylation, does not ordinarily neutralize the positive charge of its side chain at physiological pH. Phosphorylation introduces a bulky polar group that is substantially negatively charged under many cellular conditions. Lipid addition may strengthen association with membranes. Carbohydrate addition creates a hydrated surface with possibilities for lectin recognition. The student should relate each effect to the actual group rather than treating all PTMs as equivalent switches.

The term writer is sometimes used for an enzyme installing a mark, eraser for an enzyme removing it and reader for a protein recognizing it. This vocabulary is useful as long as it is not mistaken for a universal mechanism. Some modifications do not possess a direct eraser, and some act by changing the substrate's intrinsic catalytic properties rather than through a dedicated reader. A phosphatase removes a phosphate, whereas an enzyme that recognizes a phosphotyrosine docking site does not necessarily modify it. An enzyme could possess more than one activity, but substrate recognition and covalent catalysis must still be distinguished experimentally.

A chemical change is not necessarily an intentional regulatory event. Oxidation or nonenzymatic glycation may occur as a consequence of cellular conditions. Some such changes are repaired or have regulatory roles, whereas others represent damage. Glycation refers to nonenzymatic reaction with reducing sugars and must not be confused with enzyme-directed glycosylation. In the remainder of this chapter the primary emphasis is on controlled, biologically purposeful modifications, while chemically related nonenzymatic reactions provide useful contrasts.

3. Discovery of reversible phosphorylation

The biochemical discovery of reversible phosphorylation transformed understanding of enzyme regulation. Glycogen phosphorylase was known to participate in mobilization of stored glycogen. Its reaction uses inorganic phosphate to cleave certain glycosidic bonds and produce glucose-1-phosphate. A key question was how a cell could alter the activity of this enzyme rapidly in response to a physiological demand. Allosteric effects of metabolites provided one possible explanation, but they did not explain every difference observed between preparations of the enzyme.

Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs investigated conversion between forms of muscle glycogen phosphorylase in the 1950s. Their work established that the conversion involved an enzyme-mediated phosphorylation reaction and that the phosphate could subsequently be removed. A second enzyme, phosphorylase kinase, catalysed addition of phosphate using ATP. The modified state and its catalytic behavior could be distinguished from the unmodified state. This was a direct demonstration that changing covalent protein chemistry could regulate enzyme function reversibly. The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1992 recognized Fischer and Krebs for these discoveries.

The experimental logic is more important than memorizing the prize date. An increase in enzyme activity upon adding ATP could initially be interpreted in several ways. ATP might bind noncovalently to the enzyme and change its behavior. It might serve as a substrate for a separate reaction producing an activator. Alternatively, part of ATP might become covalently incorporated into the target. Testing activity after removal of free ATP helps distinguish persistent modification from an effect requiring continuously bound soluble nucleotide, although retention of a tightly bound ligand remains another possibility. Detecting phosphate associated with a purified target under conditions disrupting noncovalent binding provides stronger evidence of covalent modification. Enzyme-mediated removal of that phosphate accompanied by reversal of the functional change supports the regulatory cycle.

An educational reconstruction of a suitable experiment would compare purified phosphorylase incubated with ATP and active phosphorylase kinase against an otherwise identical mixture without kinase. A further control would contain a kinase lacking catalytic activity. Measuring phosphorylase activity under matched substrate conditions tests function, and measuring phosphate incorporation into the isolated substrate tests chemical change. If ATP is labeled at its terminal phosphate, radioactivity associated with purified phosphorylase after separation from free ATP would support transfer of that group. A phosphatase treatment followed by a second measurement could test whether the attached mark and functional effect are lost together. This is a hypothetical teaching design, not a reproduction of any one historical dataset.

Even such a result must be interpreted carefully. A phosphatase preparation might contain additional enzymes or cause an unrelated alteration if it is impure. A target preparation containing another phosphorylated protein could show radioactivity in the same fraction. Appropriate purification, controls and independent detection of the modified site strengthen the conclusion. An increase in activity and an increase in phosphorylation do not on their own establish causation if both can be caused by a third factor. Reconstituting the reaction with defined components helps narrow the possible explanations.

The muscle glycogen example also illustrates the distinction between allosteric and covalent regulation. Adenosine monophosphate can influence muscle glycogen phosphorylase through reversible noncovalent binding. It need not become attached to the enzyme's amino acids. Phosphorylation instead changes the covalent chemical state. A phosphorylase molecule can be subject to both kinds of influence, so observing the existence of one does not exclude the other. Describing the phosphorylated a form as generally more active and the b form as responsive to allosteric regulation is more accurate than assuming that the b form is entirely inactive under every condition.

The same physiological situation can require different proteins to respond in opposite directions. During mobilization of glycogen, stimulation of phosphorylase contributes to breakdown, while inhibitory phosphorylation of glycogen synthase reduces synthesis. If phosphorylation always activated enzymes, this coordinated response would be impossible. The phosphoryl group instead changes a particular structural and regulatory environment in each enzyme. The biological outcome depends on that environment. This principle extends far beyond carbohydrate metabolism, including regulation of translation initiation, membrane receptors and cell cycle proteins.

4. Phosphorylation: molecular mechanism

A protein kinase catalyses transfer of a phosphoryl group, usually from ATP, to a target protein. In the most familiar eukaryotic examples the acceptor is the oxygen of a hydroxyl group on serine, threonine or tyrosine. A phosphomonoester linkage results. Magnesium ions commonly participate in positioning ATP and stabilizing its negatively charged groups. The kinase binds both nucleotide and substrate, creates an environment conducive to transfer, and releases ADP and the phosphorylated protein. The enzyme itself is not consumed by a single catalytic cycle. It can act on many molecules as long as suitable substrates and donors remain available.

The net reaction can be represented as protein-OH plus ATP yielding protein-O-phosphate plus ADP, with protonation states omitted. This is a simplified chemical equation rather than a full description of the ionic species in solution. The terminal phosphate of ATP is the usual source of the transferred group. The phosphate closest to the ribose is called alpha; the middle one is beta and the terminal one is gamma. If a student wants to follow incorporation using labeled ATP, gamma-labeled ATP is therefore an appropriate conceptual choice for conventional kinase activity. An alpha label ordinarily remains on the ADP product instead of appearing on the target hydroxyl.

A protein phosphatase catalyses hydrolytic removal of an attached phosphate group from a substrate. The net reaction is phosphorylated protein plus water yielding unmodified protein and inorganic phosphate. ATP is not regenerated by this hydrolysis. Combining one kinase cycle with one phosphatase cycle gives net ATP hydrolysis to ADP and inorganic phosphate. This accounting explains why maintaining a dynamic population of reversibly modified proteins can consume energy even when average phosphorylation occupancy remains constant. The same individual molecules are not necessarily permanently modified or unmodified at steady state.

Kinases are often classified by the residues they preferentially phosphorylate. Protein kinase A is a standard serine/threonine kinase example; receptor tyrosine kinases phosphorylate tyrosine residues. Some enzymes can act on more than one residue class. The labels describe catalytic specificity rather than asserting that every available residue of that class will be modified. Recognition frequently requires a compatible local sequence together with appropriate spatial accessibility and interaction with the kinase. Phosphatases also exhibit substrate and residue preferences. Consequently the extent of phosphorylation does not generally depend on a single universal kinase or phosphatase.

A phosphoserine side chain is chemically different from the original hydroxyl group in several ways. It is bulkier, adds strong negative charge and provides additional oxygen atoms for interactions. A nearby positively charged arginine may be stabilized in a new orientation. Repulsion between negative groups can disrupt a previous interaction. A conformational transition can change the geometry of a catalytic site or the accessibility of a binding surface. The extent and direction of these changes are protein specific. A kinase may phosphorylate an enzyme at a distant regulatory region rather than modifying its catalytic residues directly.

In some proteins the phosphorylated residue supplies a recognition element for a second protein. A well-known example is a phosphotyrosine-containing sequence recognized by an SH2 domain. The domain does not bind every phosphotyrosine indiscriminately; nearby amino acids contribute to specificity. Such recruitment converts a chemical change into a change in physical association between proteins. Removal of the phosphate can weaken the interaction and reverse the recruitment. A receptor that becomes phosphorylated on cytosolic tyrosines can thereby recruit signaling proteins without increasing its own abundance or crossing an additional membrane. Detailed receptor signaling pathways belong to the cell communication unit; the present point is that a phosphate can become a docking signal.

Another kind of response occurs when phosphorylation changes localization by exposing or obscuring a transport signal or altering binding to a protein that retains the substrate in a compartment. A phosphorylated protein might enter the nucleus less efficiently because it interacts with a cytoplasmic anchor. In a different system phosphorylation might promote nuclear accumulation. These are alternative mechanisms, not contradictory rules. Measuring localization alone does not identify which interaction has changed. The result needs to be linked to the particular modified site and binding partner.

The phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha, introduced in translation regulation, supplies a useful cross-node connection. Modification of this initiation factor under certain stress conditions inhibits efficient nucleotide exchange by eIF2B and can reduce general translation initiation. The same chemical class that activates one metabolic enzyme can suppress the activity of a translation-related system. The regulatory effect in this case reflects altered interactions between factors rather than disappearance of the entire translation apparatus. A mutation in the acceptor serine can alter the stress response even when total eIF2 alpha abundance remains unchanged.

Although serine, threonine and tyrosine dominate common eukaryotic kinase examples, phosphorylation is not exclusive to their hydroxyl groups. Many bacterial two-component systems involve transfer through a phosphohistidine intermediate to an aspartate in a response regulator. These bonds differ chemically from conventional phosphoserine and phosphotyrosine linkages. A sensor kinase may first autophosphorylate on histidine and then transfer the phosphoryl group to the receiver protein. This illustrates conservation of the broader principle of reversible chemical control despite differences in substrate chemistry and pathway organization. It does not mean that every bacterium employs only histidine and aspartate, nor that eukaryotic cells never contain other phosphorylated residues.

Some kinase reactions can phosphorylate another molecule of the same type of kinase, an event termed trans-autophosphorylation when molecules of that enzyme phosphorylate one another. In cis-autophosphorylation a kinase modifies a site in its own chain. Both are distinct from transfer by an unrelated upstream kinase. An observation that purified kinase becomes phosphorylated does not establish whether the process is cis or trans. Changing enzyme concentration, mixing catalytic and acceptor-site mutants, and excluding contaminating enzymes can help distinguish these alternatives. This illustrates a broader lesson: the existence of a modification and the identity of the enzyme placing it are separate claims.

The fate of a phosphorylated protein is determined by competing processes. A kinase must encounter an accessible substrate, and adequate ATP must be available. A phosphatase must encounter the modified form and hydrolyse the linkage. Substrate turnover, transport, binding interactions and new protein synthesis can also influence the population. A stimulated kinase may raise occupancy but might fail to do so if an opposing phosphatase becomes equally activated. Conversely inhibiting a phosphatase can raise occupancy without any increase in kinase activity. A single phospho-specific measurement cannot uniquely identify the altered enzyme.

5. Quantitative reasoning: reversible modification cycles

A simple mathematical model captures the competition between addition and removal of a chemical mark. Assume a protein exists in exactly two forms, unmodified U and modified M, with total concentration T equal to U plus M. Assume no protein synthesis or degradation during the observation interval and a sufficiently low substrate concentration that effective first-order rate constants describe both reactions. Let k_a be the first-order modification rate constant and k_r the removal rate constant, both in inverse minutes. These constants summarize the effects of available enzyme and donor under the stated conditions; they are not catalytic turnover numbers of purified enzymes.

The rate of formation of M is k_a times U, and its rate of removal is k_r times M. Substituting U equal to T minus M gives dM/dt = k_a(T − M) − k_r M. At steady state, the left-hand side vanishes. Rearranging gives k_a T = (k_a + k_r)M, and therefore the modified fraction f = M/T equals k_a/(k_a + k_r). The expression contains no unit because both rate constants have the same dimension. The unmodified fraction is k_r/(k_a + k_r). These expressions add to one, providing a useful check on the derivation.

If k_a is 0.12 per minute and k_r is 0.08 per minute, the steady-state modified fraction is 0.12/0.20 = 0.60. Suppose a stimulus doubles the modification rate constant without altering removal. The new value is 0.24/(0.24 + 0.08) = 0.75. Doubling kinase effectiveness does not double occupancy, which is bounded by one. If instead removal is halved from the original 0.08 to 0.04 per minute while addition stays 0.12, the fraction is also 0.75. Identical occupancies can arise from distinct changes to the kinetic system. This mathematical ambiguity mirrors the experimental limitation of measuring one endpoint.

The time required to approach a new steady state depends on the sum of the rate constants. Dividing the differential equation by the constant T gives df/dt = k_a − (k_a + k_r)f. Denote the steady fraction by f_ss. Since k_a = (k_a + k_r)f_ss, the equation becomes df/dt = −(k_a + k_r)(f − f_ss). Integration yields f(t) = f_ss + [f(0) − f_ss] exp[−(k_a + k_r)t]. The exponent is dimensionless because an inverse-time quantity is multiplied by time. The sign is negative, ensuring that the difference from steady state decays rather than grows.

The characteristic time is one divided by the sum of the constants. The time to remove half of the initial difference from steady state is ln(2)/(k_a + k_r). In the first example the sum is 0.20 per minute, giving a half-time of about 3.47 minutes. If both constants double, the steady fraction remains 0.60 but the half-time becomes 1.73 minutes. The population responds faster even though its final composition has not changed. This distinction is important when interpreting time-course graphs. Two treatments with identical final modified fractions can produce different biological responses if a downstream process operates within a brief time window.

At steady state the forward and reverse fluxes are equal but need not be zero. In a ten micromolar population with f = 0.60, there are six micromolar modified and four micromolar unmodified. The addition flux is 0.12 per minute times four micromolar, giving 0.48 micromolar per minute. The removal flux is 0.08 times six, also 0.48 micromolar per minute. If one ATP is consumed for each phosphorylation event in this model, the cycle uses ATP at 0.48 micromolar per minute. Multiplying both constants by two leaves the composition unchanged but doubles ATP consumption by the cycle. An unchanged phosphoprotein measurement is therefore not evidence that the cellular process is inactive.

If the modifying enzyme is suddenly and completely inhibited, k_a becomes zero. Under the same assumptions f(t) = f(0) exp(−k_r t). This case represents decay of the attached mark through the opposing enzyme, rather than decay of the protein itself. When k_r is 0.08 per minute the mark's apparent half-time is ln(2)/0.08 = 8.66 minutes. Compare this with the 3.47-minute relaxation time of the original active cycle. A student's calculation must distinguish the rate of recovery toward steady state with both enzymes functioning from the rate of disappearance when the modifying reaction is disabled.

Modification can also affect catalytic output. Let the unmodified form have turnover number c_U and the modified form have turnover number c_M, expressed in inverse seconds. At saturating substrate, assuming independent enzyme molecules and that the modification does not affect the total amount of enzyme, the maximal reaction velocity is T[(1 − f)c_U + f c_M]. This expression is derived by adding the velocities of the two populations. If c_U = 2 per second, c_M = 10 per second, and T = 1 micromolar, at occupancy 0.25 the velocity is 1[0.75 × 2 + 0.25 × 10] = 4 micromolar per second. At occupancy 0.75 it becomes 8 micromolar per second. The result is a twofold activity increase, not a threefold increase proportional simply to the change in occupancy.

Away from saturating substrate, modified and unmodified molecules can have different apparent Michaelis constants as well as different turnover numbers. In a simple independent-subpopulation model one can use the sum of two Michaelis–Menten rate expressions, each weighted by the concentration of its corresponding form. An observed change in activity at only one substrate concentration does not distinguish a change in catalytic turnover from a change in substrate affinity. Measuring the full substrate-dependence curve for isolated modified and unmodified forms provides more insight. The additive model can fail if the two forms assemble into mixed oligomers whose catalytic properties are not the weighted sum of individual subunits.

The first-order modification model has boundaries. A kinase or phosphatase can approach saturation at high substrate concentration, in which case the rate no longer scales linearly with the amount of available substrate. Multiple sites can have different addition and removal rates, and a first modification can increase or decrease the probability of a second. Protein synthesis or degradation may make T change with time. ATP availability may vary under cellular stress. The model is not intended to describe every regulatory circuit; it identifies the expected behavior of one explicitly defined minimal system. If observed data depart from its predictions, those departures can become clues to additional biology rather than reasons to silently change the equation.

For example, consider a hypothetical time-course experiment following a kinase inhibitor. The modified fraction initially equals 0.80, falls to 0.40 after five minutes, and falls to 0.20 after ten minutes. With constant total protein and complete inhibition of addition, this is consistent with a first-order removal process having a five-minute half-time and k_r approximately 0.139 per minute. If the fraction instead falls to 0.40 after five minutes and remains near 0.40 for the next hour, several explanations become possible. The inhibitor may incompletely block kinase activity, a protected substrate pool may be inaccessible to the phosphatase, another kinase may compensate, or an assay background may impose a detection floor. The plateau alone cannot discriminate among them. Testing inhibitor action independently and comparing different time points and total protein signals would improve interpretation.

A two-site protein introduces combinatorial reasoning. If each of two distinct sites is either modified or unmodified, four occupancy patterns are possible: neither, first only, second only and both. For n distinguishable binary sites the maximum number of patterns is two raised to n, under the assumption that every combination is physically possible. If five sites are binary and independent in principle, there are thirty-two patterns. In a real substrate some combinations may never arise because one modification blocks another or a site is exposed only following a previous change. The mathematical maximum is not evidence that all forms exist at appreciable concentration. Also, if a site accepts mutually exclusive chemical alternatives such as acetylation or ubiquitination at a single lysine, the binary model no longer describes that site correctly.

If the probabilities of two site modifications are 0.6 and 0.4, and the events are statistically independent, the fraction carrying both is 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24. The fraction carrying neither is 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.24; first only is 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36; second only is 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.16. The four fractions sum to one. Without independence the marginal occupancies alone cannot determine the joint distribution. For example, the fraction carrying both could be zero if the two marks are mutually exclusive, or could reach 0.4 if every molecule with the second mark also carries the first. Detecting separate phosphopeptides in a sample may establish site-specific occupancies without establishing whether the two phosphates are present on the same protein molecule.

6. Testing phosphorylation and causal function

A phospho-specific antibody can distinguish a modified epitope from an unmodified one when appropriately validated. In a western blot, proteins are separated and transferred to a membrane, and a primary antibody binds its target. A secondary detection system can then reveal the bound antibody. For a phosphorylation-specific antibody the relevant chemical mark must contribute to recognition. A parallel antibody recognizing total protein supplies an abundance control. The intensity of each band is meaningful only within the calibrated response range; saturation, background and differences in antibody affinity can otherwise distort interpretation.

A phosphatase-treated aliquot can help validate the modification dependence of the phospho-specific signal. If the signal disappears while the total-protein signal remains unchanged, it is consistent with an antibody recognizing the phosphorylated epitope. If both signals disappear, protein loss or degradation during treatment is a possible explanation. Site mutation can provide another control. Substitution of an acceptor serine with alanine removes the hydroxyl group, preventing conventional phosphorylation at that position. Loss of antibody binding in this mutant supports site specificity but can also reflect changes to the epitope sequence independently of phosphorylation. Combining several controls makes the interpretation stronger.

Aspartate or glutamate substitutions are sometimes used as approximate phosphomimetic mutations because their carboxylate groups supply negative charge. These substitutions are not chemically equivalent to a phosphate. They differ in geometry, charge distribution, hydrogen bonding and recognition by phosphate-binding domains. They cannot undergo normal phosphorylation and dephosphorylation at the replaced residue. A phosphomimetic mutant that fails to recruit an SH2 domain is therefore not proof that the natural phosphorylated protein cannot recruit a binding partner. The appropriate mutation depends on the question being asked, and a chemically faithful modified protein is preferable when the specific phosphate structure matters.

Suppose a receptor protein has a proposed cytosolic tyrosine phosphorylation site required for recruitment of a partner. A wild-type receptor and a receptor with tyrosine changed to phenylalanine can be compared. Phenylalanine retains the aromatic ring but lacks the hydroxyl group. Following stimulation, the wild type recruits the partner while the mutant does not. This supports the importance of the tyrosine site, provided that the mutant reaches the membrane and retains comparable abundance. Yet the mutation could also alter binding in a phosphorylation-independent way. Demonstrating that a corresponding phosphorylated peptide binds the partner while an otherwise identical unphosphorylated peptide does not provides orthogonal evidence for direct recognition.

A mobility shift in a gel can suggest a change in covalent modification, but its direction and magnitude should not be inferred solely from the added mass of a phosphate. A single phosphate adds approximately eighty daltons, which is small compared with a protein weighing tens of thousands of daltons. Changes in charge or detergent interaction can contribute to altered migration, and specialized phosphate-binding gels can retard phosphorylated species. Absence of an obvious mobility shift on an ordinary gel does not exclude phosphorylation. Conversely a shift can arise from other covalent changes or proteolytic processing. Site-directed tests, phosphatase sensitivity or mass spectrometry supply complementary information.

A simplified mass spectrometric experiment begins by digesting a protein to smaller peptides, separating those peptides and measuring mass-to-charge ratios. A phosphorylated peptide is heavier by about 79.966 daltons relative to the same peptide without the modification, when comparing the appropriate neutral molecular masses. Fragmentation can help locate the modified residue within the peptide. If a peptide contains two adjacent serines, a precursor mass increase alone cannot establish which one is modified. Fragment ions distinguishing the positions are required for confident localization. Relative ion intensities may also be influenced by differing ionization behavior, so raw intensities should not automatically be treated as absolute occupancies.

A direct biochemical test of functional consequences compares enzyme activity before and after controlled modification. The strongest simple design uses equivalent purified protein populations, an appropriate modifying enzyme and donor, matched incubation conditions and removal or control of those added reagents before measuring target activity. A reversal treatment can test whether removal of the mark restores the original function. Controls ensure the modifying enzyme itself does not contribute to the measured target reaction. Using equivalent quantities of target prevents a change in abundance from masquerading as a change in activity. A time course relating chemical occupancy to functional output can reveal whether the modification acts directly or whether a delay suggests intermediate events.

A whole-cell experiment supplies physiological context that an isolated system cannot. A kinase may modify a substrate efficiently in vitro but rarely encounter it in an intact cell. Conversely a cellular effect could depend on a scaffold absent in purified mixtures. Comparison of cellular perturbation, in vitro reconstitution and site-directed changes is especially powerful because each approach addresses a different weakness of the others. In a Part C question, the most informative answer often identifies which conclusion follows from the given experiment and which additional control would distinguish remaining explanations, rather than simply selecting the name of a kinase.

Consider hypothetical results in which a kinase inhibitor reduces phosphorylation of a metabolic enzyme by seventy percent, reduces activity by forty percent, and leaves protein abundance unchanged. The measurements support a connection between phosphorylation and activity, but do not establish that the inhibitor acts exclusively through the proposed kinase. A direct test of the kinase's capacity to modify the purified target, verification of inhibitor specificity and assessment of an appropriate acceptor-site mutant would help. If removal of the phosphate increases activity instead, the cellular activity decline after inhibitor treatment must reflect another effect, possibly phosphorylation of a second protein. Such contradictions are useful: they require students to distinguish correlations within networks from the direct effect of a single covalent bond.

7. Glycosylation and the biological meaning of a carbohydrate coat

A protein carrying one or more covalently attached carbohydrate chains is called a glycoprotein. The carbohydrate portion is not encoded as a string of sugars by a nucleic acid template. Instead, specific enzymes build and modify glycans using activated precursors. The amino acid sequence constrains possible attachment sites and protein structure, while the machinery of the cell determines which glycans are actually assembled at those sites. A gene introduced into two different expression systems can therefore yield proteins with the same amino acid sequence but distinct carbohydrate structures.

This difference is biologically consequential. A carbohydrate chain contains multiple hydroxyl groups capable of interacting with water. Attachment of such a chain can influence the solubility and surface properties of the protein. A glycan may occupy space that would otherwise permit access by a protease or another protein. Some carbohydrates are recognized by lectins, proteins that discriminate particular sugar structures. In the secretory pathway, lectin-mediated interactions can assist protein folding and quality control. On the cell surface, carbohydrates may participate in cell recognition. The effects arise from the size, composition, branching, linkage and position of the glycans, not simply from the number of sugar units.

Glycosylation is distinguished from glycation. Glycation occurs when a reducing sugar reacts nonenzymatically with a nucleophilic group on a protein. The formation of glycated haemoglobin is a clinically familiar example of a product influenced by glucose exposure. The sugar-derived group in that case does not demonstrate a requirement for passage through the ER or activity of an oligosaccharyltransferase. Enzyme-directed glycosylation generally uses an activated donor and follows the substrate and compartment specificity of the responsible enzyme. Both phenomena can alter protein mass, but the chemistry, regulation and interpretation of the product differ.

The terms N-linked and O-linked specify the atom through which the carbohydrate is connected to the protein. They do not refer to the amino-terminal and carboxyl-terminal ends of a protein. Typical N-linked glycosylation attaches a glycan to the side-chain amide nitrogen of asparagine. Typical mucin-type O-linked glycosylation attaches a sugar to the hydroxyl oxygen of serine or threonine. Both modifications can occur on a single glycoprotein, at distinct residues, and both can contribute substantially to its final properties. Other glycan linkages exist, but the central comparison for this curriculum is between conventional N-glycans, representative secretory O-glycans and the dynamic intracellular O-GlcNAc modification.

The location of the reaction follows the topology of the protein. A soluble secreted protein passes from the cytosolic ribosome into the ER lumen, traverses secretory compartments and eventually reaches the outside of the cell. The lumen-facing portion of a membrane protein remains on the noncytosolic side throughout this process, even as vesicles bud and fuse. When the vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane, the former luminal side becomes the extracellular side. This conservation of topology explains why N-glycans found on extracellular domains can have been installed long before the protein reached the cell surface. The carbohydrate does not need to be attached extracellularly.

In a membrane protein with an extracellular glycosylated domain and a phosphorylated cytosolic tail, both modifications can report distinct aspects of its history. A change in the glycan may reflect altered processing within the ER or Golgi. A change in phosphorylation can reflect signal-dependent kinase activity at the cell membrane or another cytosol-facing compartment. The fact that the two marks are on the same protein does not mean the enzymes that install them share a compartment. A membrane topology diagram is therefore often a better starting point for an exam problem than a list of modification names.

8. N-linked glycosylation: donor assembly and transfer

Conventional N-glycosylation in the eukaryotic secretory pathway starts with assembly of a carbohydrate precursor on a long hydrophobic lipid, dolichol. Dolichol phosphate provides a membrane-associated carrier that can support construction of a defined oligosaccharide. Its hydrophobic chain associates with the ER membrane while its phosphate-containing head participates in the chemical linkage to the growing carbohydrate. The lipid carrier solves a spatial problem: activated carbohydrate donors originate from cellular metabolism, but the final transfer to many secretory proteins must occur on the luminal side of the ER membrane.

Assembly begins on the cytosolic face of the ER, where sugar-transfer reactions produce an intermediate containing two N-acetylglucosamine residues and five mannose residues. The intermediate is moved to the luminal leaflet, and further sugar additions generate the canonical precursor containing three glucose, nine mannose and two N-acetylglucosamine residues. Its composition is written Glc3Man9GlcNAc2. Counting these residues gives fourteen monosaccharides. The lipid-linked precursor is a single connected, branched oligosaccharide; it is not a collection of fourteen separate free sugars that must diffuse independently toward the substrate.

Additional mannose and glucose units on the luminal side are supplied by appropriate lipid-linked sugar donors. The distinction between lipid carrier and glycan product is essential. The final glycoprotein does not ordinarily retain the entire dolichol chain used to assemble its N-glycan. Oligosaccharyltransferase transfers the preassembled carbohydrate from the lipid-linked precursor to an appropriate asparagine residue on a protein. Dolichol can be recycled through additional reactions. A student should distinguish this temporary membrane-associated assembly platform from a GPI anchor, in which the lipid-containing structure remains attached to the mature protein.

The catalytic machinery has access to the ER lumen. A newly synthesized protein segment entering that lumen can receive a glycan when an appropriate site is exposed. In the usual pathway a large preassembled group is transferred in one event, rather than by adding each of fourteen sugars separately to the asparagine. The typical recognition motif contains asparagine, any amino acid other than proline, and serine or threonine. The hydroxyl on the third residue is not the atom receiving the glycan in this reaction. It contributes to the recognition context; the glycan forms a linkage to the nitrogen on the asparagine side chain.

This provides a useful reasoning exercise. Suppose a luminal domain contains the sequence Asn-Ala-Ser. Replacing the alanine with proline disrupts the canonical recognition motif, while replacing serine with threonine retains it. Replacing the asparagine with glutamine preserves a chemically related amide side chain but removes the ordinary N-glycosylation acceptor. Replacing serine with alanine likewise destroys the usual sequence context. All four substitutions may influence protein folding to differing extents, so a mutant phenotype must be compared with the chemical and structural properties of the altered site rather than attributed automatically to loss of a glycan.

The canonical motif does not guarantee that every candidate is occupied. The motif may lie in a segment that is inaccessible to the transferase or inadequately positioned relative to the membrane. A protein can contain several sites with different occupancy probabilities. It is also possible for different glycan structures to be attached at the same occupied site in different molecules. These two sources of heterogeneity should be distinguished. Site occupancy asks whether a particular acceptor is modified at all; glycan microheterogeneity asks which structure is attached when it is occupied. A molecular analysis that reports a mixture of carbohydrate masses may reflect differences in either property or both.

Consider an experimentally expressed protein having three candidate N-glycosylation sites. Its total population contains eighty percent occupancy at the first site, fifty percent at the second and ten percent at the third. The average number of occupied sites per protein is the sum of marginal occupancy fractions, or 1.4. This expectation does not require independence. However, the fraction of proteins with all three sites occupied is not determined by the three marginal measurements. Under independence it would be 0.8 × 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.04, but coordinated accessibility or folding could alter the joint probability. A common examination error is to interpret an average of 1.4 glycans as meaning that a single protein carries a fractional sugar chain.

The complete fourteen-sugar precursor contains glucose, mannose and N-acetylglucosamine, but this does not mean that all mature N-glycans have exactly fourteen sugars. Its transfer initiates a series of processing steps. The earliest steps can remove sugar residues in the ER, while the Golgi contains enzymes that trim or add sugars according to their compartment, substrate specificity and donor availability. A mature protein may therefore carry glycans differing considerably from the original precursor in size and composition. The genetic template specifies a candidate acceptor site; it does not specify the final arrangement of every sugar in the mature glycan.

Interfering with precursor assembly or transfer can alter many proteins simultaneously, because numerous substrates share the same glycosylation machinery. Tunicamycin, a standard experimental inhibitor, interferes with an early step in formation of the dolichol-linked N-glycan precursor. A decrease in N-glycosylated proteins can therefore follow treatment. However, a resulting reduction in secretion may reflect impaired folding, ER stress, changes in protein synthesis or degradation, not solely a direct inability of a single cargo to be exported. A targeted mutation of one acceptor site is more selective but can itself change local protein structure. Using both strategies, together with controls for total protein and cellular viability, helps separate direct and indirect effects.

9. ER quality control and Golgi maturation

The presence of a carbohydrate on a nascent secretory protein provides a means by which the ER can recognize aspects of its maturation state. Following initial N-glycan transfer, glucose residues are removed by glucosidases. Calnexin and calreticulin are lectin chaperones capable of recognizing a monoglucosylated glycan state. Calnexin is membrane associated, while calreticulin is a soluble ER luminal protein. Their interaction with glycoproteins can support folding and retention of substrates that are not ready to proceed. The glycan is functioning as a recognition element, not as an amino acid template.

Subsequent removal of the remaining glucose can permit release from these chaperones. A folding-sensitive glucosyltransferase, commonly abbreviated UGGT, can reglucosylate certain incompletely folded glycoproteins, returning them to a state that can interact with the lectin chaperones again. This cycle creates repeated opportunities for appropriate folding. It also links a sugar-processing reaction to a decision about protein maturation. The cycle is not a perfect guarantee that every damaged molecule is repaired, nor does every secretory protein use it identically. Persistently misfolded molecules may be retained and ultimately directed toward degradation through quality-control systems.

A student can interpret this cycle without learning every specialized glycan-processing enzyme. The logic is that covalent sugar states influence recognition, while recognition can help determine the time available for folding and subsequent transport. If an acceptor site is lost, one possible consequence is altered lectin interaction. If a trimming enzyme is inhibited, a glycoprotein may remain in a carbohydrate state that is not recognized normally. The final phenotype depends on which step is interrupted and whether alternative folding pathways are available. Increased abundance of an immature glycoprotein does not necessarily indicate improved folding; it may represent accumulation upstream of a block.

In the Golgi, further processing yields diverse mature N-glycan structures. High-mannose glycans retain multiple mannose residues; complex glycans have undergone trimming and further addition of other sugars; hybrid structures have features of both categories. These are useful classes rather than three universally consecutive obligatory states. Access to processing enzymes, the structure surrounding an attachment site and the cell's glycosylation machinery all influence which products are produced. A mature glycoprotein may retain a high-mannose glycan at a protected site even after passing through the Golgi, so glycan structure supplies evidence about transport history but does not always uniquely identify the protein's compartment.

One important example connects glycan processing with protein targeting. Many soluble lysosomal hydrolases acquire a mannose-6-phosphate recognition mark on N-linked glycans. A specialized transferase initiates the process by transferring a phospho-containing group to appropriate mannose residues; subsequent removal of a covering sugar exposes the phosphate mark. Receptors recognizing mannose-6-phosphate can then help select these enzymes for delivery toward lysosomes. The phosphate in this example belongs to a sugar residue of an attached glycan rather than to a serine, threonine or tyrosine in the polypeptide. The term phosphorylation alone is therefore insufficient to identify the acceptor atom or mechanism.

A deficiency in this tagging pathway can cause lysosomal enzymes to be incorrectly secreted rather than efficiently delivered to the lysosome. The enzymes may be translated and may retain catalytic potential but be absent from the compartment in which their substrates accumulate. This illustrates an especially important form of PTM failure: a modification can be required for correct localization rather than for catalytic chemistry itself. In the classic mucolipidosis II example, impaired formation of the mannose-6-phosphate targeting marker contributes to defective lysosomal enzyme delivery. Not all lysosomal proteins depend identically on this pathway, so the phenotype should not be interpreted as proof that every lysosomal resident uses the same tag.

Carbohydrate chains also provide structural surfaces recognized beyond the secretory system. Terminal sugars on cell-surface glycoproteins can alter interactions with lectins. Changes in glycan branching and composition may modify the lifetime of a circulating protein by influencing receptor-mediated recognition or susceptibility to clearance. These effects are particularly relevant to therapeutic glycoproteins, whose properties can depend on the cell type chosen to express them. A manufacturer may produce the same amino acid sequence in two expression systems yet obtain different distributions of glycans and different functional properties. Demonstrating equivalence of sequence is therefore not always sufficient to establish equivalence of a glycoprotein product.

Comparative biology adds a further qualification. Protein glycosylation is not exclusive to eukaryotic organisms, and some bacteria and archaea possess their own glycosylation machinery. Nevertheless, the standard eukaryotic N-glycosylation model uses the ER and Golgi, compartments absent from prokaryotes. It is erroneous to describe bacterial glycosylation as occurring in a bacterial Golgi apparatus. A bacterial periplasm or a membrane-associated enzyme can provide a distinct spatial context for related chemistry. On examinations, the organism and the relevant compartment should be identified before transferring mechanistic statements from one system to another.

10. O-linked glycosylation and dynamic O-GlcNAc

In a typical mucin-type O-glycosylation reaction, a glycosyltransferase transfers N-acetylgalactosamine from an activated nucleotide-sugar donor to the oxygen of a serine or threonine side chain. This reaction often takes place in the Golgi. Subsequent transferases may extend the carbohydrate by adding further residues one at a time. In contrast with the transfer of a large preassembled precursor in conventional N-glycosylation, the initial attachment in this representative O-linked pathway is a single sugar transfer. This comparison is a strong organizing principle, provided it is not turned into a rule covering every rare O-linked glycan system.

Mucins are useful examples because their protein regions can contain many serine and threonine residues that support extensive O-glycosylation. Dense carbohydrate chains can create an extended, hydrated structure contributing to a protective surface. A single short O-glycan on another protein may have a quite different function. The biological effect depends on the distribution and properties of attached groups. O-linked glycosylation does not require the canonical N-X-S/T motif because its acceptor chemistry differs. A serine or threonine residue is a candidate but its presence alone does not demonstrate a glycosylated site.

One of the most important exceptions to the general association between glycosylation and the secretory pathway is intracellular O-GlcNAcylation. Here N-acetylglucosamine, rather than the GalNAc used in typical mucin-type initiation, is attached to a serine or threonine hydroxyl. Many cytosolic and nuclear proteins can carry this modification. The enzyme O-GlcNAc transferase, or OGT, uses UDP-GlcNAc as the sugar donor, and O-GlcNAcase, or OGA, can remove the attached group. In the common nucleocytoplasmic pathway the modification generally remains a single sugar rather than forming a long secretory glycan chain.

The availability of UDP-GlcNAc connects the reaction with cellular metabolism. The presence of the donor is not by itself sufficient to establish occupancy of a site, because OGT activity, substrate accessibility and opposing removal also matter. A change in nutrient supply may change the cellular level of O-GlcNAc, yet interpretation of that effect must take into account these additional variables. It is not valid to infer directly from a higher glucose concentration that every modified protein gains a sugar. The rate of formation of a donor and the modification of an individual target are distinct steps.

A serine or threonine may be a potential substrate for either phosphorylation or O-GlcNAcylation. If both modifications require the same hydroxyl on the same individual residue, the two groups cannot simultaneously occupy that ordinary acceptor. However, a single protein can carry a phosphate at one site and O-GlcNAc at another. Changes in one mark can influence the other through altered enzyme recognition or conformation, but their relationship is not universally antagonistic. A pair of whole-cell measurements showing increased O-GlcNAc and decreased phosphorylation does not prove direct competition on a particular residue. Establishing such competition requires site-resolved evidence and tests of causation.

A comparison of the three major glycosylation examples clarifies the exam logic. A luminal protein carrying an asparagine-linked glycan synthesized on dolichol represents conventional N-glycosylation. A secreted mucin bearing extended O-linked sugars on many serines and threonines represents an important Golgi pathway. A nuclear regulatory protein carrying reversible single O-GlcNAc residues represents a distinct intracellular enzyme system. All are genuine protein glycosylation events, and only the first requires the usual asparagine acceptor sequence. The word glycosylation cannot alone identify a compartment or a donor, and the word O-linked cannot alone distinguish the two O-linked examples.

11. Acetylation: changing the chemistry of amino groups

Acetylation attaches an acetyl group, containing a methyl group adjacent to a carbonyl group, to an acceptor in a protein. The usual donor for enzymatic protein acetylation is acetyl-coenzyme A. The thioester bond in acetyl-CoA provides an activated acetyl group that can be transferred to an amino nitrogen. In the familiar lysine reaction, the side-chain amino group becomes an amide. Coenzyme A is released, and the modified protein contains the acetyl group rather than the whole coenzyme A molecule.

At physiological pH a free lysine side chain is commonly protonated and contributes positive charge. Acetylation changes the nitrogen's chemical environment and removes that ordinary positive charge, producing a relatively neutral amide. A nearby negatively charged residue or nucleic acid may then interact less strongly with the modified lysine. This principle helps explain why acetylation can alter protein interactions, but charge is only part of the mechanism. An acetyl group also changes shape and hydrogen-bonding properties, and can form part of a recognition surface. The net effect may be to weaken an existing interaction or to create a different interaction.

Histones offer an accessible example of the importance of this change. Their lysine-rich regions can interact with negatively charged DNA. Acetylating some lysine residues can reduce the electrostatic contribution of those particular groups and alter the properties of chromatin. In addition, acetylated lysines can be recognized by proteins containing bromodomains. Thus, the consequence of acetylation is not exhausted by a simple calculation of charge. The location and combination of the marks and the recruitment of regulatory proteins can matter. Detailed chromatin states and their gene-expression consequences belong to Subunit 3.D; the chemical distinction here is that acetylation neutralizes a lysine side-chain amino group and can create a recognition element.

Histone acetyltransferase was historically a convenient name for enzymes studied using histones as substrates. Many such enzymes can also acetylate nonhistone proteins, and the wider term lysine acetyltransferase is useful. Conversely the term histone deacetylase does not mean that every enzyme in this family acts exclusively on histones. Enzymes may remove acetyl groups from metabolic enzymes, cytoskeletal proteins and other substrates. The names of enzymes should not substitute for evidence of their actual substrate specificity in a particular setting.

One standard family of lysine deacetylases catalyses hydrolytic removal of the acetyl group with the aid of a metal ion, often zinc. Sirtuins constitute a chemically distinct class that requires NAD+ in the deacetylation reaction. Their mechanism produces nicotinamide and an acetylated ADP-ribose derivative as well as the deacetylated protein. NAD+ acts as a reaction substrate, not merely as an inert structural cofactor retained unchanged. This distinction matters for metabolic regulation because availability of acetyl-CoA and NAD+ can influence the opposing enzymatic processes. It is not valid to assume that every deacetylase uses NAD+ or that adding NAD+ by itself guarantees deacetylation of any acetylated protein.

Reversible side-chain acetylation must also be distinguished from N-terminal acetylation. A polypeptide can possess one alpha amino group at its amino terminus in addition to multiple epsilon amino groups on its lysines. N-terminal acetyltransferases recognize terminal sequence contexts and can modify many proteins during or after their synthesis. For some substrates the initiator methionine is removed first, creating a new amino-terminal residue. The resulting terminal acetyl group can influence stability, assembly or recognition. Unlike many lysine acetylation events, ordinary N-terminal acetylation is generally stable and is not routinely reversed by a dedicated deacetylase cycle. An assay detecting total protein acetylation therefore needs to distinguish the types of acetylated site.

The biological consequences of terminal acetylation depend on the protein. Blocking a free amino group can alter the recognition of the terminus by other proteins. In some systems an acetylated terminus contributes to a degradation signal, while in others terminal acetylation can stabilize a protein or promote appropriate complex assembly. The presence of the same chemical group in different locations does not guarantee the same function. A change that removes the first residue of a protein may affect acetylation specificity, but it can also alter the intrinsic structure of the terminus or the recognition of another modification enzyme.

A metabolic enzyme can provide a relatively clean way to study side-chain acetylation. Suppose a lysine helps stabilize a negatively charged substrate through a favorable electrostatic interaction. Acetylation of that lysine could diminish the interaction and reduce catalytic efficiency. This is a mechanistic prediction, not a rule for all metabolic enzymes. In another enzyme the unmodified lysine could help stabilize an inactive conformation, and acetylation might increase activity. To assess the prediction, compare purified protein with and without the mark, measure binding or catalytic behavior under matched conditions, and test whether deacetylation restores the initial properties.

A lysine-to-arginine substitution is often used to retain a positive side-chain charge while preventing lysine acetylation at the replaced residue. A lysine-to-glutamine substitution supplies a neutral amide-containing side chain and is sometimes used to approximate acetylated lysine. Neither is chemically identical to the natural target form. Arginine contains a guanidinium group with different geometry, and glutamine has a shorter side chain than acetyllysine. These mutants can help test a mechanistic model, but they cannot on their own prove that observed effects are caused by acetylation. If a lysine is also a site for ubiquitination or methylation, substitution prevents those lysine-specific events too. Interpretation requires distinguishing loss of one mark from loss of an entire chemical attachment site.

Nonenzymatic lysine acetylation also occurs in biologically relevant settings, particularly where reactive acetyl donors are available. This means an increase in an acetylated protein after a change in metabolism does not necessarily demonstrate direct activation of an acetyltransferase. Inhibition of removal, chemical acetylation and changes in substrate accessibility are alternative explanations. Evidence that a particular enzyme installs the group should ideally include a test in which defined components are sufficient for catalysis. Evidence that the mark matters physiologically should include the target's functional response in the appropriate cellular context.

Consider a hypothetical experiment on a metabolic enzyme. Following exposure to a deacetylase inhibitor, a lysine-acetylation signal rises while the target's total protein concentration is unchanged. The enzyme's catalytic rate falls when measured at saturating substrate. A direct inhibitory effect of acetylation is plausible, but the inhibitor may also change acetylation of other proteins. If purified target acetylated in vitro shows a similar reduction in catalytic rate, and removal of the mark restores activity, the direct model gains support. A site mutant that cannot be acetylated could strengthen the conclusion if its untreated activity and folding are demonstrably comparable to wild type. If the mutant is already inactive before treatment, failure to observe additional inhibition becomes difficult to interpret.

12. Methylation: modifying recognition without necessarily neutralizing charge

Protein methylation transfers one or more methyl groups to suitable acceptor atoms, most commonly discussed on lysine and arginine side chains. The usual methyl donor is S-adenosylmethionine, abbreviated SAM or AdoMet. Transfer converts the donor into S-adenosylhomocysteine. This chemistry connects protein modification to methionine metabolism and other methyl-transfer reactions. It is important to specify that protein methylation modifies an amino acid residue in a protein; DNA methylation modifies a nucleotide base in DNA. Both can use SAM, but they involve different substrates and different enzymes.

The lysine side-chain nitrogen can exist in monomethylated, dimethylated and trimethylated states. Each successive transfer adds one methyl group relative to the preceding state, replacing a hydrogen in the overall comparison. A trimethyllysine side chain has a permanently charged quaternary ammonium group. The free lysine side chain and its common methylated forms are generally positively charged under physiological conditions. This makes methylation fundamentally different from acetylation in a simple charge model. A student should not infer that methylating a lysine necessarily weakens a protein's electrostatic interaction with a negatively charged partner in the same manner as acetylation.

Methylation changes steric bulk and the way a side chain presents its chemical groups. Particular recognition domains can bind methylated lysines and can distinguish different methylation states or neighboring sequences. For example, some chromatin-associated proteins use aromatic side chains in binding pockets to accommodate methylated lysine. The result can be recruitment of a protein that would bind the unmodified sequence poorly. The structural specificity of recognition explains why the same methylated residue class can occur in proteins associated with different biological outcomes.

Arginine can also receive methyl groups on nitrogens in its guanidinium group. Monomethylarginine, asymmetric dimethylarginine and symmetric dimethylarginine are common conceptual categories. The latter two differ in the distribution of methyl groups between guanidino nitrogens rather than the total number of added groups. Consequently they have the same nominal methyl-group count but distinct molecular arrangements. This example demonstrates why mass measurements alone may not resolve all chemically distinct modifications. For CSIR-NET preparation the central points are the acceptor type, SAM-dependent transfer, and the existence of structurally different methylated forms.

Several lysine methyl marks can be removed enzymatically by demethylases. Some employ flavin-dependent oxidative chemistry; others use iron and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent reactions. The enzyme's mechanism determines which methylation states it can process. An enzyme acting on mono- and dimethyllysine need not remove trimethyllysine. One must avoid extending the reversibility of some lysine methyl marks to every methylated protein site. In particular, the simple assertion that all arginine methylation events have a well-established direct demethylase counterpart would be too strong. Reversal of a population-level methylation signal may also occur through protein degradation and replacement rather than removal from an existing molecule.

Histone lysine methylation is a familiar application, but it is not accurate to say that methylation always represses transcription. Different methylated residues and different methylation states can be associated with active or repressed chromatin, because they recruit distinct recognition systems. In this node it is sufficient to understand how a modification creates a chemical signal that a reader can recognize. The detailed regulation of chromatin by specific marks is assigned to a later part of the curriculum. Nonhistone proteins can likewise undergo methylation, which may alter interactions, assembly or function without directly involving DNA.

The methyl donor itself is not attached in its entirety. In a simple accounting exercise, conversion of ten picomoles of unmodified lysine sites into trimethylated sites through successive methyl transfer requires thirty picomoles of methyl-transfer events, and hence thirty picomoles of SAM consumed, assuming no reverse reactions and no other donor-consuming events. If the starting sites are already monomethylated, only twenty picomoles of additional transfer are necessary. This is donor stoichiometry, not a statement that the cellular SAM pool decreases by the same amount, because metabolism can replenish the donor.

A single methyl transfer produces a net monoisotopic mass increment of approximately 14.016 daltons. Three such increments give approximately 42.047 daltons. Lysine acetylation produces a mass increment near 42.011 daltons. Both modifications therefore produce a nominal mass shift of about forty-two daltons, but their exact masses and structures differ. A low-resolution mass measurement reporting only the rounded mass cannot necessarily distinguish trimethylation from acetylation. Higher-resolution measurements, informative fragmentation and independent chemical evidence can be necessary. Their biological effects on lysine charge are also different, providing an important reason not to identify them solely by nominal mass.

Suppose a protein binds a particular regulatory partner only when a lysine is dimethylated. Inhibiting a methyltransferase reduces the dimethylated population and lowers binding, while total amounts of both proteins remain constant. This observation is consistent with mark-dependent recognition. If a purified dimethylated peptide binds the partner and an otherwise identical unmodified peptide does not, the case for direct recognition becomes stronger. It would still be necessary to assess whether the peptide models the accessibility of that sequence in the intact protein. A binding assay with the full-length protein can address that limitation.

Acetylation and methylation can affect the same lysine in mutually exclusive ways. Under ordinary chemistry the same side-chain nitrogen cannot simultaneously bear a conventional acetyl group and the usual trimethyllysine modification. At the level of a protein population, however, some molecules may be acetylated while others are methylated at that position. Different residues on the same molecule may carry the two marks simultaneously. If increased acetylation is accompanied by decreased methylation in a bulk measurement, direct competition at a shared site is one possible explanation, but it is not the only one. The treatment could affect enzyme expression, donor metabolism or accessibility independently. Site-resolved evidence is needed before assigning a mechanism.

13. Lipidation: attaching proteins to membrane surfaces

A hydrophobic lipid can increase a protein's association with a lipid bilayer even when the polypeptide lacks a conventional membrane-spanning helix. The attached group can insert into the hydrophobic part of a membrane while the rest of the protein remains on one side of the bilayer. Different types of lipidation use different donors, acceptor atoms, attachment sites and membrane topologies. The common feature is an altered relationship between a protein and its lipid environment.

N-myristoylation commonly involves attachment of a fourteen-carbon saturated fatty acyl group to the alpha amino group of an amino-terminal glycine. The donor is myristoyl-CoA, and N-myristoyltransferase catalyses formation of an amide linkage. A protein synthesized initially with methionine followed by glycine may expose the required glycine through removal of the initiator methionine. The lipid modification can then occur during synthesis. Some proteolytic events can expose an internal glycine as a new amino-terminal residue later in a protein's life, providing another route to this modification. A glycine located in the middle of an intact polypeptide does not have a free alpha amino group available for this ordinary N-terminal reaction.

The lipid attachment can promote membrane binding, but one myristoyl group does not necessarily force a protein to remain permanently associated with every membrane it encounters. Interactions between positively charged protein regions and negatively charged membrane lipids can cooperate with the acyl group. In some proteins conformational changes alter exposure of the attached lipid, thereby regulating association. The usual N-terminal myristoyl amide is relatively stable; regulated membrane binding can occur without cycles of attachment and cleavage of that bond. This differs from the common reversible thioester linkage discussed next.

S-palmitoylation is a familiar form of protein S-acylation in which a fatty acyl group, often the sixteen-carbon saturated palmitoyl group, is attached to the sulfur of a cysteine side chain. Enzymatic S-acylation often uses an activated fatty acyl-CoA donor and membrane-associated acyltransferases. The linkage is a thioester, which can be cleaved by enzymes with appropriate thioesterase activity. Reversible addition and removal can influence the protein's partition between membranes and other cellular locations. The general term S-acylation is more inclusive because fatty acyl groups other than palmitate can be involved.

A cysteine-to-serine mutant removes the thiol group and thereby prevents ordinary S-palmitoylation at that cysteine. If a normally membrane-associated protein becomes more soluble after the mutation, lipid attachment could be important for membrane binding. However, the mutation might also change a local protein-protein interaction or remove a different cysteine modification. Direct measurement of the acylated form and a check that protein abundance and folding remain comparable strengthen the inference. If the protein has another lipid anchor, mutation of one cysteine may reduce but not abolish its membrane association.

Prenylation supplies a chemically different hydrophobic anchor. Protein farnesylation and geranylgeranylation transfer isoprenoid groups containing fifteen and twenty carbon atoms respectively. Typical substrates contain appropriate cysteine-containing sequences near the carboxyl terminus, such as the CAAX motif recognized by certain prenyltransferases. In this notation C represents cysteine, the two A positions usually represent aliphatic residues and X contributes to enzyme specificity. The group attaches to the sulfur through a thioether bond rather than the thioester characteristic of ordinary S-palmitoylation. Farnesyl pyrophosphate or geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate serves as the activated donor in the relevant reaction.

For CAAX substrates, further processing may remove the terminal AAX segment and methylate the newly exposed carboxyl group of the prenylated cysteine. This sequence combines multiple modifications to produce the mature terminus. Not every prenylated protein uses precisely the same motif or subsequent reactions; some proteins have other terminal cysteine arrangements and different recognition requirements. The Ras family provides common exam-relevant examples of prenylated signaling proteins. A disrupted prenylation site can change membrane localization and hence impair access to a signaling partner without necessarily destroying the protein's intrinsic capacity to bind a guanine nucleotide. The broader Ras signaling pathway belongs to Unit 4.

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol attachment is another way to anchor proteins, with an important topological distinction. A protein destined for GPI anchoring enters the secretory pathway and has a carboxyl-terminal signal region that is recognized in the ER. During attachment, the signal segment is removed and a preassembled GPI structure becomes covalently linked to the newly generated carboxyl terminus. The anchor contains a carbohydrate-containing bridge connected to a lipid moiety. As the protein travels through the Golgi and toward the plasma membrane, it retains the noncytosolic orientation of its protein domain. At the cell surface the mature protein lies extracellularly, attached to the outer leaflet through the lipid.

Unlike a conventional transmembrane receptor, a mature GPI-anchored protein has no peptide segment traversing the membrane and no intrinsic cytosolic tail. It may still influence intracellular processes indirectly through associations with other membrane components. Its lipid-dependent attachment also differs from that of a myristoylated cytosolic protein associated with the inner leaflet. A diagram showing the two proteins on opposite sides of the membrane is essential for reasoning about the accessibility of their domains. An extracellular enzyme cannot ordinarily reach the cytosolic protein's attached group across an intact bilayer.

Certain phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipases can release susceptible GPI-anchored proteins from membranes by acting on the anchor structure. A protein released from intact cells by an externally applied suitable phospholipase may therefore be a candidate for GPI-dependent attachment. Failure of release is not absolute proof against GPI anchoring, since anchor composition, accessibility and enzyme specificity matter. A transmembrane protein with an ordinary membrane-spanning helix would not be expected to lose its primary anchorage simply because such an enzyme acts on GPI. An experiment comparing a GPI-anchored protein with an integral membrane protein and a soluble extracellular protein provides useful controls for interpreting release.

The distinction among lipid modifications can now be made on three axes. First, the acceptor may be a terminal amino nitrogen, a cysteine sulfur or the carboxyl terminus linked through a GPI bridge. Second, the bond may be a stable amide, reversible thioester, stable thioether or an anchor linkage with distinct cleavage possibilities. Third, the protein may face the cytosol or the extracellular side of a membrane. A student who uses these axes can predict the consequence of a mutation or chemical treatment without memorizing the answer for every known lipidated protein.

14. Proteolytic maturation and the importance of irreversible processing

A protease catalyses hydrolysis of a peptide bond. When a protein is processed at a specific peptide bond to generate a functional product, the reaction represents an irreversible change in the ordinary cellular context. In contrast to removal of a small chemical group from a side chain, cleavage separates parts of the original amino acid sequence and creates new termini. The new protein may have a different domain organization, an exposed binding site or a catalytic configuration that was not accessible in the precursor.

The importance of limited proteolysis becomes clear when an active protein would be harmful in the place where it is synthesized. Proteolytic enzymes can potentially digest proteins in a cell or tissue. Producing certain enzymes as inactive or weakly active precursors, called zymogens, permits transport or storage before activation. A specific cleavage event can then produce the mature enzyme in the appropriate environment. This strategy differs fundamentally from a phosphorylation cycle because the activated molecule cannot simply be returned to its original zymogen state by removal of a reversible group. Inactivation requires another mechanism, such as an inhibitor or degradation, while replenishment of the precursor requires new synthesis.

Trypsinogen provides an instructive example. The digestive protease precursor is synthesized in pancreatic cells and is normally activated in the intestinal environment by cleavage of its activation segment, initiated by enteropeptidase. Activated trypsin can participate in activation of additional digestive precursors. The biological advantage of the arrangement is that potent proteolytic activity is spatially controlled. The cleavage is not merely a loss of molecular mass; it reorganizes the enzyme so that its catalytic function becomes effective. Premature activation in the wrong place is dangerous because the same catalytic chemistry that contributes to digestion can damage cellular proteins.

The zymogen example makes it possible to distinguish synthesis, activation and inhibition in an experiment. Imagine cells expressing a normal precursor and a mutant whose activation cleavage site is altered. Both proteins are synthesized in comparable quantities and reach the same destination. When exposed to the activating protease, the normal precursor produces a smaller polypeptide with high activity, while the mutant remains largely uncleaved and has low activity. Under the stated controls, the data support a requirement for cleavage in activation. However, if the mutation also disturbs the folded catalytic domain, the same low activity could result even after cleavage. Testing an engineered mature form or independently assessing folding would help separate these possibilities.

A different example is insulin maturation. Human preproinsulin contains an amino-terminal signal sequence directing its entry into the ER. Removal of that signal sequence produces proinsulin, which folds and forms the disulfide connections required for the mature hormone. Its sequence contains future B and A chains separated by the connecting peptide, or C-peptide. In the regulated secretory pathway, prohormone convertases cleave at appropriate junctions and carboxypeptidase activity removes residual basic residues. The mature hormone consists of an A chain and a B chain linked by disulfide bonds, with an additional intrachain disulfide within the A chain.

For standard human insulin, the mature A chain contains twenty-one amino acids and the B chain thirty. The mature protein therefore contains fifty-one amino acid residues in two linked chains, whereas the original translation product is a single longer polypeptide. This does not violate the genetic code or require independent translation of the two mature chains. Both originate from one precursor encoded in the correct order. Cleavage removes intervening sequence and changes covalent connectivity, while disulfide bonds hold the mature chains together. A reducing agent that breaks the disulfide bonds can separate the chains, whereas a treatment that preserves disulfides may maintain the two-chain structure even after peptide cleavage.

An experiment using a pulse of labeled amino acid followed by an excess of unlabeled amino acid can reveal a precursor-product relationship. Immediately after the pulse, newly synthesized labeled protein is concentrated in precursor forms. During the chase, previously labeled molecules can be processed, creating labeled mature products. A decline in precursor label accompanied by appearance of label in a mature form supports conversion. The inference is strongest when the identity of both molecular species is established and the total label is accounted for. The disappearance of a labeled precursor alone could reflect degradation rather than maturation. Pulse-chase evidence was important in establishing the relationship between proinsulin and insulin.

C-peptide is produced along with mature insulin through processing of endogenous proinsulin. The release of one molecule of C-peptide accompanies generation of one molecule of insulin from one proinsulin in the ideal complete processing reaction. Measurements of their circulating concentrations need not be equal because the molecules have different clearance and other physiological properties. This is an example in which reaction stoichiometry must be distinguished from steady-state concentration. It also illustrates how a cleavage product can provide useful biological information even when it is not the principal mature hormone. The medical context is relevant here as an application of precursor processing rather than an invitation to infer clinical diagnoses from a single measurement.

Not all proteolytic maturation activates an enzyme. Removal of a targeting sequence may be necessary because the sequence served its purpose during transport and is not part of the mature protein. Removal of an initiator methionine changes the terminus and may permit subsequent modification such as N-myristoylation. Cleavage of a proprotein may expose a new interaction surface or separate two biologically useful peptides. In other cases, a specific cleavage inactivates a protein. The common feature is limited change to covalent backbone structure. Complete proteolytic degradation, in contrast, ultimately destroys the functional protein and releases peptides or amino acids. The boundary between these processes is determined by the product and its biological fate rather than by whether a protease participates.

One can model a simplified maturation process as first-order conversion of precursor P to mature product M with no new synthesis, degradation or reverse reaction. If P initially has concentration P0 and the cleavage rate constant is k, then P(t) = P0 exp(−kt), obtained by integrating dP/dt = −kP. The concentration of mature product is M(t) = M0 + P0[1 − exp(−kt)] for a one-to-one conversion. The sum of precursor and mature molecule counts remains constant in this ideal case, but their masses differ because a peptide segment is released. At a half-time of four minutes, twelve minutes corresponds to three half-times, so one eighth of the initial precursor remains and seven eighths has been converted. If P0 was eighty nanomolar and M0 zero, the predicted concentrations are ten nanomolar precursor and seventy nanomolar mature protein. A loss of both precursor and mature signals would violate the assumptions and suggest additional processes or measurement problems.

15. Ubiquitination: a covalent protein tag and a decision about fate

Ubiquitin is a small protein of seventy-six amino acids in its common eukaryotic form. Instead of transferring a single small group such as phosphate or methyl, ubiquitination covalently attaches an entire ubiquitin molecule to a substrate. The usual linkage joins the carboxyl group of ubiquitin's terminal glycine to an amino group on a substrate lysine, producing an isopeptide bond. The bond is called isopeptide because it involves a side-chain amino group rather than the usual alpha amino group participating in the main polypeptide backbone. Alternative ubiquitin linkages exist, but the lysine example is the appropriate starting point.

Ubiquitin is first activated in an ATP-dependent process by an E1 enzyme, passed through an E2 conjugating enzyme and transferred to selected substrates with the help of an E3 ligase. The ligase frequently contributes to target selection. The molecular details of this cascade, chain assembly, proteasomal recognition and alternative degradation routes belong to Node 3.C.14. The essential principle in this chapter is that the covalent tag can alter the fate of an already synthesized protein and can be installed without changing its encoded amino acid sequence. Deubiquitinating enzymes can cleave many ubiquitin linkages, permitting the tag to be removed and ubiquitin to be recycled.

A substrate can carry a single ubiquitin, several single ubiquitins at different sites, or a chain of ubiquitins linked to one another. The arrangement matters because different recognition proteins distinguish different ubiquitin configurations. Certain chain types, including the familiar Lys48-linked chains, commonly participate in proteasomal targeting. However, ubiquitin can also regulate trafficking and signaling without necessarily causing immediate proteasomal degradation. A single ubiquitin detected on a protein is therefore not sufficient to conclude that the protein must be destroyed. Conversely, a rapidly degraded substrate might be difficult to detect in its tagged state if the tagged form is short-lived.

Research by Aaron Ciechanover, Avram Hershko, Irwin Rose and colleagues established fundamental principles of ATP-dependent ubiquitin-mediated selective degradation, recognized by the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The work helped explain why cellular protein breakdown is not simply an indiscriminate consequence of protease activity. A tag and its recognition machinery allow a cell to distinguish substrates. This selective processing is particularly useful when a protein must be present transiently, or when a damaged molecule must be removed while related functional molecules remain available. The specificity of tagging can be as important as the activity of the degradation apparatus itself.

A ubiquitin mark can affect stability and localization in distinct ways. Suppose a cell-surface receptor receives a ubiquitin tag that promotes its internalization or subsequent delivery to an intracellular sorting pathway. A mutation preventing the tag might increase receptor residence at the surface without altering its intrinsic binding affinity for extracellular ligand. In another system ubiquitination could increase destruction by the proteasome and thereby lower total protein abundance. A single assay of receptor abundance in an entire cell would not necessarily distinguish those scenarios. Measurements of the surface pool, intracellular pool and degradation rate would be more informative.

A lysine that accepts ubiquitin may also be available for acetylation or methylation, depending on the protein. Acetylation of its ordinary epsilon amino group blocks that group's use for the standard lysine isopeptide linkage while the acetyl mark remains. A protein may therefore show interactions between different PTMs through competition at the same site, altered recognition elsewhere or changes in accessibility. Yet a lysine-to-arginine mutation does not selectively remove ubiquitination while leaving every other lysine modification unchanged. If the experiment changes several potential chemical processes at once, the observed phenotype must be attributed cautiously.

The distinction between ubiquitin attachment and proteolytic destruction can be tested conceptually by separately perturbing the tagging machinery and the machinery that recognizes or destroys tagged proteins. If tagging is reduced, one might observe accumulation of untagged substrate when ubiquitination normally promotes degradation. If proteasomal processing is impaired while tagging remains active, tagged substrates may accumulate instead. These are conditional predictions, not universal responses, because deubiquitination, alternate degradation routes and feedback can influence the measured distribution. One must measure the actual tagged and total populations rather than assume that every perturbation has a single direct consequence.

16. Integrated analytical reasoning: connecting a mark to its biological outcome

At this point several chemical strategies can be compared using the same experimental logic. A modification may directly alter catalytic activity, change physical binding, direct localization, influence the rate of degradation or enable maturation. These outcomes may coexist. A glycan may assist folding and also influence clearance of the mature protein; a phosphate may alter catalytic function and reveal a docking site; a lipid anchor may change localization and thereby alter signaling. A successful causal explanation specifies the primary effect being tested and acknowledges that secondary changes can follow.

Consider an educational dataset comparing a secretory enzyme with one mutant form. In wild-type cells the total amount of enzyme is one hundred arbitrary units and the extracellular amount is sixty units. In mutant cells total enzyme is ninety units and extracellular enzyme is nine units. The mutation removes an N-glycosylation acceptor. The fraction of the total enzyme pool detected extracellularly decreases from sixty percent to ten percent. The primary observation is redistribution accompanied by a modest abundance decline. The data are consistent with impaired folding or trafficking, but do not by themselves prove either. A direct activity measurement on equally purified molecules, localization by microscopy and analysis of glycan processing could distinguish additional possibilities.

Glycosidase treatment offers a way to investigate glycan processing. Endoglycosidase H cleaves susceptible oligomannose and certain hybrid N-glycans but does not normally release complex N-glycans. Peptide N-glycosidase F removes most common N-glycans from the protein, though particular core modifications in some organisms can prevent cleavage. If an early labeled glycoprotein shifts after treatment with both enzymes, while a later form shifts only after PNGase F, the observations are consistent with a conversion from susceptible to resistant N-glycans during processing. The data do not prove the exact location of every molecule, because different glycan sites and processing histories can produce exceptions. Untreated controls, comparable enzyme conditions and knowledge of the expression system are needed.

Protein fractionation can test a lipidation hypothesis. A cell extract can be separated into a soluble fraction and a membrane-associated fraction under defined conditions. Suppose a candidate membrane-binding protein is detected predominantly in the membrane fraction in wild type but primarily in the soluble fraction after alteration of an amino-terminal glycine. A lipid-dependent change in association is plausible. A membrane marker and soluble marker help assess fraction purity, while the total protein measurement addresses recovery. Direct evidence of myristoylation and tests of the protein's folded integrity would distinguish loss of lipid attachment from unrelated changes in binding. Centrifugation alone does not prove whether a protein is integral, peripheral or associated with another membrane protein.

The time scale of a phenotype supplies useful clues. Rapid loss of a phospho-specific signal with constant total protein after inhibiting a kinase may indicate a reversible mark being removed. Gradual replacement of a processed protein by a larger precursor after inhibiting a maturation protease may reflect new synthesis and failure of conversion. The modified molecules already present cannot be converted back to their original precursor by the inhibited enzyme. A decline in total abundance might instead reflect degradation or reduced synthesis. Experimental time courses thus separate several mechanisms that can look similar in a single endpoint.

The central reasoning strategy is to follow the actual molecular population. Identify the chemical group and the atom receiving it; locate the reaction and its enzymes; determine whether reversal can occur on the same molecule; identify what the modified form can do that the unmodified form cannot; and choose an experiment that independently measures both states. The strategy extends from phosphorylation of translation factors to glycosylation of membrane receptors, lipid anchoring of signaling proteins, histone acetylation and selective ubiquitin tagging. The next chapters will explain in greater detail how targeting and degradation systems act on these chemically distinct proteins, completing the connection between protein synthesis and its ultimate cellular fate.

1. Which event is a covalent modification rather than noncovalent regulation?

  1. ATP binding to a kinase
  2. Phosphate transfer to a serine
  3. Chaperone binding a nascent chain
  4. Assembly of a noncovalent dimer
Answer and explanation

B. Phosphate transfer to a serine

Phosphate transfer creates a new covalent linkage on the protein. ATP binding, chaperone association and the specified dimerization change interactions without necessarily changing chemical connectivity.

2. What part of ATP is normally transferred by a conventional protein kinase?

  1. Adenosine
  2. Alpha phosphate
  3. Beta phosphate
  4. Gamma phosphate
Answer and explanation

D. Gamma phosphate

The terminal gamma phosphoryl group is transferred, leaving ADP. The alpha and beta phosphates remain on ADP; adenosine is not covalently transferred by a conventional kinase.

3. Which is the usual acceptor atom of a conventional N-linked glycan?

  1. Asparagine side-chain nitrogen
  2. Serine side-chain oxygen
  3. N-terminal alpha nitrogen
  4. Lysine side-chain nitrogen
Answer and explanation

A. Asparagine side-chain nitrogen

The asparagine amide nitrogen receives the glycan. Ser/Thr hydroxyl oxygen is a common O-glycan acceptor. N-terminal nitrogen and lysine are acceptors for other modifications.

4. Which motif is a candidate for conventional eukaryotic N-glycosylation?

  1. Asn-Pro-Ser
  2. Gln-Ala-Thr
  3. Asn-Ala-Thr
  4. Asn-Ala-Gly
Answer and explanation

C. Asn-Ala-Thr

The usual sequon is Asn-X-Ser/Thr, with X not proline. The second option lacks Asn; the first contains Pro at X and the fourth lacks Ser/Thr at the third position.

5. What is the canonical composition of the precursor oligosaccharide transferred from dolichol to an N-glycosylation site?

  2. Glc9Man3GlcNAc2
  3. Glc3Man9GalNAc2
  4. Man14
Answer and explanation

A. Glc3Man9GlcNAc2

Three glucose, nine mannose and two N-acetylglucosamine residues make fourteen sugars. Replacing GlcNAc with GalNAc or changing the relative glucose and mannose counts produces the wrong composition.

6. Which enzyme transfers a preassembled oligosaccharide to an ER-luminal protein?

  1. Protein kinase
  2. O-GlcNAcase
  3. Oligosaccharyltransferase
  4. Lysine methyltransferase
Answer and explanation

C. Oligosaccharyltransferase

Oligosaccharyltransferase catalyses the N-glycan transfer. O-GlcNAcase removes intracellular O-GlcNAc; kinase and methyltransferase transfer different groups.

7. Which statement about O-GlcNAc is correct?

  1. It is always a complex extracellular glycan
  2. It is attached to asparagine by OST
  3. It requires a transmembrane helix in the target
  4. It can cycle on cytosolic Ser/Thr through OGT and OGA
Answer and explanation

D. It can cycle on cytosolic Ser/Thr through OGT and OGA

OGT adds a single GlcNAc to appropriate hydroxyl groups and OGA removes it. It does not require conventional ER N-glycosylation or a transmembrane helix.

8. Which common modification neutralizes the usual positive charge of lysine?

  1. Lysine acetylation
  2. Lysine trimethylation
  3. Tyrosine phosphorylation
  4. Cysteine prenylation
Answer and explanation

A. Lysine acetylation

Acetylation forms a neutral amide at the lysine side chain. Trimethyllysine remains positively charged, and the other modifications affect different residues.

9. Which donor is normally used for protein methylation?

  1. ATP
  2. UDP-glucose
  3. S-adenosylmethionine
  4. Myristoyl-CoA
Answer and explanation

C. S-adenosylmethionine

SAM transfers methyl groups and becomes S-adenosylhomocysteine. ATP, UDP-glucose and myristoyl-CoA are donors in different chemical reactions.

10. Which feature characterizes sirtuin-mediated deacetylation?

  1. Use of dolichol
  2. Requirement for NAD+
  3. Use of SAM as a methyl donor
  4. Transfer of ubiquitin

B. Requirement for NAD+

Sirtuins use NAD+ during deacetylation. Other deacetylase families may instead use metal-dependent hydrolysis. Dolichol, SAM and ubiquitin are unrelated donors in the listed reactions.

  1. Internal lysine
  2. C-terminal asparagine
  3. Internal serine
  4. N-terminal glycine
Answer and explanation

D. N-terminal glycine

The free alpha amino group of an N-terminal glycine forms an amide with myristate. An internal glycine is not an N-terminus unless cleavage exposes it.

  1. Thioester
  2. Phosphodiester
  3. Thioether
  4. Glycosidic bond
Answer and explanation

A. Thioester

Typical S-palmitoylation produces a thioester that may be hydrolysed by thioesterases. Prenylation creates a thioether; the other choices describe unrelated chemistry.

13. Which location is typical of the mature domain of a plasma-membrane GPI-anchored protein?

  1. Cytosol
  2. Nuclear interior
  3. Extracellular side
  4. Mitochondrial matrix
Answer and explanation

C. Extracellular side

GPI attachment occurs on the luminal side of the ER; the luminal orientation becomes extracellular at the plasma membrane. Cytosolic lipid anchors have different topology.

14. Which is an example of limited irreversible maturation?

  1. Phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha
  2. Proteolytic conversion of proinsulin to insulin
  3. Binding of AMP to phosphorylase
  4. Removal of O-GlcNAc

B. Proteolytic conversion of proinsulin to insulin

Specific peptide-bond cleavage creates new termini and cannot be simply reversed in the cell. The first and last involve reversible chemical marks, and AMP binding is noncovalent.

Glycation is a nonenzymatic reaction involving reducing sugars. Glycosylation is generally enzyme-directed; not all glycosylation occurs in the ER, and glycation can modify proteins.

Answer and explanation

C. Mannose-6-phosphate tagging—lysosomal hydrolase sorting

Mannose-6-phosphate is a targeting tag on many lysosomal enzymes' glycans. Other choices confuse chemically different processes.

Part C: Analytical, numerical and experimental practice

Original practice problems, not previous-year questions. All numerical and experimental datasets in these questions are hypothetical teaching examples.

31. A reversible modification has k_add = 0.18 min⁻¹ and k_remove = 0.06 min⁻¹. What steady fraction is modified?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.90
Answer and explanation

C. 0.75

Use f = k_add/(k_add+k_remove) = 0.18/0.24 = 0.75. The unmodified fraction is 0.25; the ratio 0.18/0.06 is not a fraction.

32. A system has k_add = k_remove = 0.10 min⁻¹. Both constants are doubled. What changes?

  1. Occupancy rises to 1
  2. Occupancy remains 0.5; relaxation half-time halves
  3. Occupancy halves; time unchanged
  4. Both occupancy and relaxation time double
Answer and explanation

B. Occupancy remains 0.5; relaxation half-time halves

The ratio remains 0.20/0.40 = 0.5, but ln2/(k_add+k_remove) falls from 3.47 to 1.73 minutes. Equal scaling changes flux and dynamics, not the steady fraction.

33. After complete inhibition of a kinase, a mark decays with a four-minute half-time. From 80% occupancy, what remains after twelve minutes?

  1. 10%
  2. 20%
  3. 40%
  4. 60%
Answer and explanation

A. 10%

Three half-times give 80% × (1/2)^3 = 10%. This assumes fixed total protein and first-order removal without new phosphorylation.

34. An enzyme has total concentration 2 µM, unmodified turnover 3 s⁻¹ and modified turnover 9 s⁻¹. At f = 0.5, its maximal rate is?

  1. 6 µM/s
  2. 9 µM/s
  3. 18 µM/s
  4. 12 µM/s
Answer and explanation

D. 12 µM/s

One micromolar of each form contributes 3 and 9 µM/s, respectively, for 12 µM/s. The total rate requires both concentration and turnover.

35. Three independent binary sites have occupancies 0.5, 0.6 and 0.2. What fraction carries all three?

  1. 0.12
  2. 0.06
  3. 0.30
  4. 0.80
Answer and explanation

B. 0.06

Under independence, multiply the site probabilities: 0.5 × 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.06. Without independence this joint occupancy cannot be determined from marginal measurements.

36. Four N-glycan sites have marginal occupancies 0.9, 0.8, 0.4 and 0.2. What is the average number of occupied sites per protein?

  1. 2.3
  2. 0.0576
  3. 4
  4. 1.0
Answer and explanation

A. 2.3

The expected number is the sum of occupancies, 2.3. Multiplication would yield the all-four probability only under independence; the mean does not require independence.

37. A steady-state phosphorylation system contains 5 µM protein, 40% modified, with k_add = 0.2 min⁻¹. What is its forward ATP consumption flux under one ATP per event?

  1. 0.4 µM/min
  2. 1.0 µM/min
  3. 0.6 µM/min
  4. 2.0 µM/min
Answer and explanation

C. 0.6 µM/min

Unmodified concentration is 5 × 0.6 = 3 µM; flux is 0.2 × 3 = 0.6 µM/min. The modified fraction is used for reverse flux, not forward flux.

38. A precursor converts irreversibly with a six-minute half-time. Starting with 64 nM precursor and no mature product, what mature concentration appears after 18 min if no degradation occurs?

  1. 8 nM
  2. 16 nM
  3. 32 nM
  4. 56 nM
Answer and explanation

D. 56 nM

Three half-times leave 64/8 = 8 nM precursor. The one-to-one mature product concentration is 64 − 8 = 56 nM.

39. Twenty pmol of sites are monomethylated. How much SAM is required to convert all to trimethylated sites with no reverse or competing reactions?

  1. 20 pmol
  2. 40 pmol
  3. 60 pmol
  4. 80 pmol
Answer and explanation

B. 40 pmol

Each site requires two additional methyl transfers; 20 × 2 = 40 pmol. The first methyl group has already been installed.

40. A total-protein signal decreases from 100 to 50 calibrated units and phosphosite signal from 40 to 20 units, with comparable proportionality. What does the ratio show?

  1. Occupancy unchanged
  2. Occupancy doubles
  3. Occupancy halves
  4. Kinase activity necessarily rises
Answer and explanation

A. Occupancy unchanged

The normalized signal is 40/100 = 20/50 = 0.4. Absolute modified abundance decreased but fractional occupancy appears unchanged; kinase activity is not directly inferred.

41. A phosphatase treatment eliminates a phospho-specific band while total target remains unchanged. What is most directly supported?

  1. The protein has been degraded
  2. The antibody detects a phosphatase-sensitive state
  3. The target must be N-glycosylated
  4. The upstream kinase is identified
Answer and explanation

B. The antibody detects a phosphatase-sensitive state

The result supports dependence of the detected epitope on phosphate. It neither identifies the kinase nor demonstrates degradation; other assay controls are still important.

42. Wild-type receptor recruits a partner after stimulation; Y→F mutant fails. Both reach the membrane. Which additional evidence best supports direct phosphotyrosine recognition?

  1. More receptor mRNA
  2. Equal cell sizes
  3. Binding to a phosphorylated peptide but not its unmodified counterpart
  4. Loss of an unrelated glycan
Answer and explanation

C. Binding to a phosphorylated peptide but not its unmodified counterpart

Matched peptides directly test dependence on the chemical mark. Equal localization already reduces one alternative explanation; mRNA and unrelated glycan changes do not establish direct recognition.

43. A cytosolic reporter with Asn-Ala-Ser is not glycosylated. Adding an ER signal peptide enables PNGase F-sensitive glycosylation. What is supported?

  1. Asn motif requires ATP binding
  2. The reporter changed its genetic code
  3. The original reporter was a GPI-anchored protein
  4. Access to the secretory compartment is important
Answer and explanation

D. Access to the secretory compartment is important

The motif existed in both forms, but signal-dependent entry provides access to luminal OST. The result is consistent with conventional N-glycosylation, not a genetic-code change.

44. An early pulse-labeled protein is Endo H-sensitive; a later form is resistant but PNGase F-sensitive. What is the strongest conclusion?

  1. The protein has acquired a different processed N-glycan state
  2. The protein has no carbohydrate
  3. Every molecule is definitively at the plasma membrane
  4. The polypeptide has been phosphorylated
Answer and explanation

A. The protein has acquired a different processed N-glycan state

The differential glycosidase susceptibility supports N-glycan maturation. It does not identify the exact location of every protein or prove phosphorylation.

45. Tunicamycin reduces secretion of a glycoprotein. Which follow-up best distinguishes loss of a particular glycan from global ER stress?

  1. Measure only drug concentration
  2. Compare a site-specific mutant and assess folding, abundance and secretion
  3. Assume all mRNA is destroyed
  4. Count total ribosomes only
Answer and explanation

B. Compare a site-specific mutant and assess folding, abundance and secretion

The site-specific mutation and multiple readouts address a direct glycan role versus widespread perturbation. The inhibitor alone affects numerous substrates and may induce stress.

46. A glycoprotein lacking its N-glycan is equally active in purified form but poorly secreted. Which interpretation is most plausible?

  1. The glycan is essential to peptide-bond formation
  2. The glycan must directly constitute the active site
  3. The glycan may influence folding/transport or stability in the cell
  4. The glycan must be a DNA modification
Answer and explanation

C. The glycan may influence folding/transport or stability in the cell

Intrinsic activity is preserved in the purified population, so a cellular role in processing, trafficking or stability is plausible. Additional localization and turnover data would distinguish those mechanisms.

47. After OGA inhibition an intracellular Ser/Thr-linked sugar increases, while total target is constant. What is most directly consistent?

  1. Reduced removal of O-GlcNAc
  2. Increased OST-mediated transfer of fourteen sugars
  3. Removal of every phosphate from the target
  4. Decreased protein synthesis necessarily
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced removal of O-GlcNAc

OGA removes O-GlcNAc, so inhibition can increase occupancy. Other marks and synthesis need independent measurement; the ER precursor transfer is a distinct mechanism.

48. A K→Q mutant behaves like an acetylated form in one binding assay. What conclusion is justified?

  1. K→Q is chemically identical to acetyllysine
  2. The mutant can be dynamically acetylated at Q
  3. The target has no other modification sites
  4. The neutral substitution supports a model but does not prove the native mark
Answer and explanation

D. The neutral substitution supports a model but does not prove the native mark

Glutamine approximates neutralization but differs in length and chemistry. Direct measurement using the genuinely acetylated substrate is stronger.

49. A 42 Da nominal increase is found on a lysine-containing peptide. What is the best next step?

  1. Call it phosphorylation
  2. Use accurate mass, fragmentation and independent evidence
  3. Call it glycosylation
  4. Assume it is acetylation with certainty
Answer and explanation

B. Use accurate mass, fragmentation and independent evidence

Acetylation and trimethylation have similar nominal increments but different exact masses and chemical structures. Precursor nominal mass alone is insufficient.

50. A cytosolic signaling protein becomes soluble after mutating its N-terminal Gly. Which control best supports a myristoylation-dependent mechanism?

  1. Measure unrelated histone acetylation
  2. Assume no folding defect
  3. Confirm loss of lipid label and compare total protein and fraction markers
  4. Remove all membrane proteins
Answer and explanation

C. Confirm loss of lipid label and compare total protein and fraction markers

Direct lipid evidence plus fraction purity and abundance controls addresses anchoring. Mutation may otherwise disrupt structure or a distinct interaction.

51. An extracellular phospholipase releases a cell-surface protein, but a transmembrane control is retained. Which modification is consistent with the observation?

  1. GPI attachment
  2. Cytosolic prenylation
  3. Nuclear methylation
  4. Ribosomal phosphorylation
Answer and explanation

A. GPI attachment

A susceptible GPI anchor can be cleaved from the extracellular side. Cytosolic lipid modifications are inaccessible to extracellular enzyme across an intact bilayer.

52. A precursor and its cleavage-resistant mutant accumulate equally. The mutant is inactive even with activating protease. What follow-up separates defective cleavage from catalytic misfolding?

  1. Measure unrelated kinase
  2. Add SAM to both
  3. Measure only mRNA
  4. Test a correctly engineered mature mutant form and folding
Answer and explanation

D. Test a correctly engineered mature mutant form and folding

If a mature version bypassing cleavage is active and correctly folded, the activation defect is better explained by failed processing. Equal precursor abundance alone does not establish correct folding.

53. A pulse-chase shows disappearance of precursor label but no mature label. Which conclusion is warranted?

  1. Complete maturation has been demonstrated
  2. Disappearance alone cannot distinguish processing from loss
  3. Every precursor has become phosphorylated
  4. All protein synthesis has stopped
Answer and explanation

B. Disappearance alone cannot distinguish processing from loss

The labeled precursor may be degraded, exported, or otherwise lost. Detection and accounting of the expected mature species are necessary to establish conversion.

54. A protein accumulates with reduced ubiquitin tag after deletion of its ligase. A proteasome inhibitor instead causes accumulation of tagged protein. What is consistent with both?

  1. The ligase removes every glycan
  2. Proteasomes synthesize the ubiquitin tag
  3. Tagging can precede degradation of this target
  4. All ubiquitination necessarily has the same outcome
Answer and explanation

C. Tagging can precede degradation of this target

The two perturbations affect distinct steps: tagging versus destruction. The result supports a degradative tag for this particular substrate, not all ubiquitin marks.

55. A membrane receptor has a lumen-facing Asn-X-Thr motif and a cytosolic Tyr. The Asn mutation changes secretion, the Tyr mutation changes stimulated binding without changing surface abundance. What model fits?

  1. Separate glycan-dependent maturation and phosphate-dependent recruitment mechanisms
  2. Both sites are necessarily modified by the same enzyme
  3. The cytosolic Tyr receives the canonical fourteen-sugar N-glycan
  4. The receptor is synthesized by separate ribosomes for each domain
Answer and explanation

A. Separate glycan-dependent maturation and phosphate-dependent recruitment mechanisms

Topology and phenotypes fit distinct modification systems. An Asn-linked luminal glycan may influence processing, while a cytosolic phosphotyrosine can alter recruitment. Direct mark measurements would strengthen the model.

Revision and active recall

Core mechanisms and equations

For a fixed two-state population with first-order addition and removal, f = kₐ/(kₐ + kᵣ), relaxation half-time = ln(2)/(kₐ + kᵣ), and steady forward flux = kₐT(1 − f). After complete addition blockade f(t) = f(0)e^(−kᵣt). Independent two-state sites allow a maximum of 2ⁿ patterns; joint occupancy requires independence or measured joint data. For irreversible first-order cleavage, P(t) = P₀e^(−kt), and M(t) = M₀ + P₀(1 − e^(−kt)) for one-to-one conversion without losses.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

PTMs do not always follow termination. Many N-glycans and terminal acetyl marks are introduced during translation.

Folding is not synonymous with covalent modification. Noncovalent rearrangement can occur without a change in chemical connectivity.

Phosphorylation can activate or inhibit. Its effect depends on the target and modified site.

A phosphatase does not make ATP. It hydrolyses the phosphate linkage and releases Pi.

A phospho-band is not an occupancy measurement by itself. Normalize to total target and verify assay behavior.

A sequon is not proof of N-glycosylation. Topology, accessibility and active OST also matter.

N-linked does not mean N-terminal. The acceptor is typically the Asn side-chain nitrogen.

O-linked does not mean exclusively Golgi. Nucleocytoplasmic O-GlcNAc is a distinct pathway.

Glycation is not enzyme-directed glycosylation. Nonenzymatic sugar reaction and glycan transfer have different chemistry.

Methylation is not acetylation. Lysine methylation generally retains positive charge, unlike acetylation.

Mutants are not identical to authentic modifications. K→Q and S→D/E can be useful approximations but have different structures.

Lipid anchoring is not necessarily membrane spanning. GPI anchors attach extracellular domains; other lipid anchors associate with cytosolic surfaces.

Irreversible cleavage is not necessarily destruction. Limited proteolysis can create a functional mature product.

Ubiquitin is not an automatic death sentence. Some tags regulate sorting and signaling.

A single endpoint rarely proves causation. Use time courses, direct chemical-state measurements and controlled functional assays.
