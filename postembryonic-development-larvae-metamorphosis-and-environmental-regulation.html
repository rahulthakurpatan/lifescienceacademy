Postembryonic Development: Larvae, Metamorphosis and Environmental Regulation

Understand larval development, insect and amphibian metamorphosis, hormone-dependent tissue remodeling and environmental regulation through experimental and quantitative reasoning.

1. Development continues beyond the embryo

A butterfly emerging from a chrysalis and a small frog leaving water illustrate a general developmental problem. The embryo has already constructed a functional body, yet that body is not necessarily the one that will reproduce. A newly hatched animal may possess a working digestive system, a nervous system and a set of locomotory structures suited to its immediate surroundings. It may later have to acquire a different way of obtaining food, exchanging gases or moving. The developmental program must therefore accomplish two tasks that can be separated in time: building a viable young organism and converting that organism into a form appropriate to subsequent stages of its life. A change of body plan at this later point is not a second fertilization, nor does it require production of a different genome. It is a coordinated change in the behavior and composition of tissues descended from the original embryo.

Postembryonic development is the collection of developmental events that occur after an animal has left the embryonic condition. Hatching or birth is a convenient observable boundary, although neither event guarantees that all organs have completed differentiation. A mammalian newborn, for example, continues to grow, modifies its skeleton and nervous connections, and later becomes capable of reproduction. These changes are development even if there is no distinct larval form. A juvenile animal is one that has not yet reached adulthood. A larva is more specifically a postembryonic stage with characteristics specialized for a particular part of the life history, often substantially different from those of the adult. A tadpole is not simply a frog with shorter legs, and a caterpillar is not a butterfly with small wings. Each has a distinctive architecture suited to its activities at that stage.

Direct development describes a life history in which the young animal has the basic organization of the adult without a separate, conspicuously distinct free-living larval stage. A chicken hatching from an egg has a beak, legs and feathers appropriate for a young bird, even though the proportions, physiology and behavior will change during maturation. Indirect development includes a distinct larval stage and a transition to the adult or juvenile adult-like organization. The term does not imply that larval development is less organized or that an adult form is absent from the genome. A larval body may be essential for survival and growth before adult structures become useful. In several direct-developing frogs, features of the transformation are reorganized into development inside the egg. Thus, the absence of a free-swimming tadpole is not proof that all underlying developmental processes have disappeared.

The distinction between growth and differentiation is crucial. Growth increases mass or size; differentiation changes cellular structure and function; morphogenesis changes shape and spatial organization. These events often accompany one another, but they need not do so. A caterpillar can increase in mass substantially while retaining the basic larval organization. A tadpole may lose mass when its tail disappears while simultaneously developing an adult digestive tract and improving the function of its limbs. An insect pupa may not feed, yet extensive differentiation occurs using material and energy previously acquired. An increase in development can therefore coexist with a decrease in mass. Developmental stage should be identified by an appropriate structural or molecular criterion rather than inferred solely from body weight.

Metamorphosis denotes a relatively conspicuous developmental transformation between life-history forms. It involves an organized change rather than an injury, a random loss of organs or generalized tissue degeneration. A functional adult requires old structures to disappear when they are no longer appropriate, retained tissues to adapt, and newly useful structures to differentiate. The transitions must be synchronized sufficiently that the organism remains viable. For instance, aquatic locomotion cannot cease long before an animal acquires a suitable alternative means of movement. In insects, the protective cuticle must be replaced while the developing structures remain covered. Developmental control over time is as biologically necessary as control over position during embryogenesis.

A useful experimental distinction is the difference between chronological age and developmental age. Two larvae hatched at the same time may be at different morphological stages if one receives less food or develops at a lower temperature. Conversely, animals at the same stage may differ substantially in size or time since hatching. Age can be measured with a clock, whereas stage is a classification of developmental organization. A study comparing hormone concentration across animals should therefore specify whether the sampling is matched by age, morphological stage, body size or some combination. Failing to make this distinction may make an indirect consequence of slower development appear to be a direct effect of an environmental factor.

Many larvae feed and accumulate resources independently; others rely substantially on reserves inherited from the egg. An echinoderm feeding larva and a nonfeeding larva are not interchangeable examples of energy acquisition, although both may have an organization different from the adult. A free-living larva may also disperse farther than an adult that is relatively immobile. Such differences help explain why indirect development is maintained in numerous animal lineages. They are not proof that larvae necessarily evolved to reduce competition with adults. Separating ecological consequences from evolutionary explanations prevents an observation about present-day function from becoming an unsupported claim about the historical origin of a life cycle.

The cellular problem is analogous to induction and competence in the embryo. A developing tissue must receive a signal and must be capable of responding to it. Competence can change over time as receptors, regulatory proteins and chromatin states change. A hormone circulating through the entire body can trigger different responses in different tissues because those tissues do not possess identical regulatory states. A limb rudiment may grow while a larval appendage regresses, even if both receive a systemic signal. This principle will link the endocrine mechanisms of insects with those of amphibians without implying that their hormones or anatomical structures are homologous.

2. Insect life cycles and the necessity of molting

An insect is enclosed by a cuticle secreted by its epidermis. The cuticle provides mechanical support, protection against physical damage and, in terrestrial insects, an important barrier to water loss. It is an extracellular structure with chitin and proteins as principal components in many regions; the relative amounts and properties vary between regions and stages. A hardened cuticle does not expand continuously in proportion to internal growth. A young insect consequently undergoes successive episodes in which the old external covering is replaced. These episodes create opportunities to alter external morphology and are essential for understanding the timing of insect development.

An instar is the developmental interval between successive molts, or the corresponding form of the insect during that interval. An instar can be identified by cuticular structures and other species-specific markers. A molt involves more than the conspicuous moment at which the old cuticle is shed. During apolysis, epidermal cells separate from the old cuticle. A new protective layer and a new cuticle are secreted beneath it. Components of the old inner cuticle may be digested and reclaimed while its protective outer parts remain until shedding. Ecdysis is the actual escape from the old cuticular covering. The new covering can expand and then mature through processes including hardening, depending on its region and type. Endocrine regulation must coordinate these events so that the newly secreted cuticle is not destroyed by the enzymes involved in recycling the old one.

Molting is not synonymous with metamorphosis. A larva that undergoes a molt and emerges as a larger larva has experienced cuticle replacement without acquiring a new life-history identity. In contrast, a molt that produces a pupa or an adult can be part of metamorphosis. Endocrine regulation of the physical molt must be distinguished from regulation of the identity of the stage produced by the molt. This distinction is particularly important in experiments where a treatment triggers molting but preserves larval features, or prevents a normal molt despite the presence of an adult developmental program in the tissues.

Ametabolous insects, such as silverfish, develop without a dramatic metamorphic change and may continue molting after sexual maturity. In hemimetabolous insects, eggs hatch into nymphs. A grasshopper nymph resembles the adult in many aspects of its organization, although its wings and reproductive apparatus are not yet mature. Wing pads become progressively conspicuous in suitable species. The final nymphal molt produces an adult without an intervening pupal stage. Aquatic nymphs of dragonflies demonstrate that gradual or incomplete metamorphosis need not mean the young and adult occupy the same habitat or use identical respiratory structures. A comparison must distinguish the number and identity of stages from their ecological similarity.

Holometabolous insects, such as flies, butterflies and beetles, pass through larval, pupal and adult forms. The larval and adult organizations may be markedly different. In a typical butterfly, the caterpillar has mouthparts and locomotory structures useful for its larval feeding activities, while the adult has wings and a different feeding apparatus. The pupal interval permits extensive reorganization between those body forms. A pupa is neither an embryo nor a physiologically inactive object: biochemical reactions, gene expression, tissue differentiation and selective destruction of larval structures continue. The external immobility of many pupae reflects their life-history role, not a cessation of cellular activity.

Drosophila melanogaster is a particularly useful holometabolous model because its stages can be distinguished with relatively clear developmental criteria. Under appropriate conditions, the embryo hatches into a first-instar larva and subsequently passes through second and third instars. At the end of the third instar, feeding ceases, the larva pupariates, and the pupal transformation proceeds within the protective puparium. The puparium is formed from the hardened last larval cuticle; it is not identical to the developing pupa inside it. Eclosion produces the adult fly. The exact durations depend on temperature, nutrition and genotype. It is more accurate to learn the ordered sequence and the criteria for its transitions than to memorize one set of hours as though it applied in every environment.

A practical biological consequence of this arrangement is that a defect may become apparent several stages after it originated. A mutation that disrupts proliferation of an imaginal disc in a feeding larva may permit much larval behavior to continue because the adult wing is not required at that stage. The adult, however, may emerge with defective wings or fail to emerge at all. By contrast, a mutation that prevents functional larval feeding may stop development before adult structures are tested. Stage-specific phenotypes therefore help distinguish immediate functions from future developmental requirements. Examination questions often exploit this distinction by presenting a normal-looking larva followed by a defective adult.

Hemimetabolous and holometabolous transitions have common endocrine features, but their stage-specific molecular programs are not identical. The presence of a pupal interval is not simply the consequence of an insect undergoing one extra ordinary molt. A pupa has a distinctive developmental identity and program of tissue reorganization. Therefore, comparing equivalent endocrine changes in two insects requires attention to the species, the relevant instar, the competence of the tissue and whether the normal life history includes a pupal form.

3. Classical evidence for circulating developmental signals

Before identifying individual hormones, investigators had to establish that metamorphosis was regulated by signals that could pass between tissues rather than only by the age of each cell. The blood-feeding hemimetabolous bug Rhodnius prolixus provided a useful experimental system because feeding and the subsequent molt could be related in time. Vincent Wigglesworth's experiments in the 1930s helped establish that signals originating in the head influence molting and that a separate endocrine activity maintains juvenile characteristics. The experimental logic, described in his 1934 report and reviewed by Lynn Riddiford, is more important for developmental reasoning than the historical priority of every individual observation.

Consider a nymph whose head is removed after an appropriate feeding stimulus but before the circulating signal needed for molting has appeared. If it fails to molt, the head may be necessary during that particular interval. Nevertheless, head removal alone cannot identify a hormone. Injury, interruption of neural signals, altered feeding and disruption of other physiological processes are alternative explanations. The causal interpretation becomes stronger if appropriate controls survive and a suitable donor can restore the missing developmental response. Parabiosis is a classical experimental arrangement that permits transfer of circulating substances between animals. It can test whether an activity produced in one animal influences developmental behavior in another without requiring identical nervous connections.

A historically important observation was that a younger Rhodnius nymph receiving endocrine influence from a final-instar donor could undergo an unusually early adult transformation under appropriate experimental conditions. Other combinations caused retention of juvenile features. These findings are not explained adequately by a single signal that merely increases the rate of growth. They support the distinction between the signal initiating a molt and an influence that specifies juvenile rather than adult character. Later work connected the latter activity with the corpora allata, paired endocrine organs in insects. The chemical identification of juvenile hormones and the characterization of their receptors subsequently made the developmental interpretation more precise.

The experimental design can be understood with a hypothetical set of results. A stage-matched untreated control proceeds through its usual juvenile molt. A second group deprived of the molting stimulus does not undergo ecdysis. A third group receiving a suitable circulating molting stimulus recovers the ability to molt. If the developmental identity of the new stage varies according to a separate juvenile-maintaining influence, molting and stage identity have been experimentally separated. A rescue of molting is not necessarily a rescue of normal adult or juvenile morphology. Each endpoint must be scored independently. This is a general rule in developmental biology: rescuing one outcome of a perturbation is not evidence that every downstream event has returned to its normal state.

Another classical logic involves tissue-specific hormone application. Suppose that a region of epidermis is exposed locally to a juvenile-maintaining signal during an interval in which the rest of the epidermis is competent to produce adult cuticle. If that small region forms juvenile cuticle surrounded by adult cuticle, the result supports a local effect on the developmental identity of epidermal cells. Wigglesworth used localized juvenile-hormone-containing preparations to demonstrate this kind of tissue response. It is necessary to control for abrasion or carrier solvent because these might alter secretion or tissue integrity independently. A mosaic adult with a patch of juvenile cuticle is strong evidence that developmental identity is not always dictated by a single indivisible state of the whole organism.

The terms necessary and sufficient must be used with care in interpreting these findings. If removing the corpora allata at a particular juvenile stage advances metamorphosis, their normal activity may be necessary to preserve juvenile identity during that interval. If adding juvenile hormone restores juvenile features when a molting signal is present, the hormone is sufficient for that particular rescue under the specified conditions. It is not sufficient to make every tissue juvenile at every developmental stage. Prior commitment, species differences, hormone dose and receptor expression can determine whether the same treatment is effective. The experiments establish conditional developmental relationships rather than a universal rule that any amount of a hormone produces the same response.

A later molecular experiment can strengthen this interpretation by perturbing the hormone receptor rather than its source. If a tissue cannot respond to juvenile hormone because its receptor is nonfunctional, adding the hormone may fail even though it circulates at a normal concentration. Conversely, if the receptor is intact but the source is absent, supplying the hormone at an appropriate stage might restore the response. These two experiments distinguish an endocrine deficiency from a target-tissue defect. The logic is identical in many developmental signaling systems, but the identities of the ligands and receptors are specific to each organism.

4. Ecdysteroids: linking endocrine signals to the physical molt

The endocrine system solves the problem of coordinating tissues separated by distance. In an insect, endocrine products are distributed through the hemolymph and reach cells with appropriate receptors. A representative larval endocrine pathway includes neurosecretory cells in the brain, the prothoracic glands and peripheral tissues. Prothoracicotropic hormone, commonly abbreviated PTTH, stimulates ecdysteroid production by the prothoracic glands in many insects. The pathway is not a universal single switch: additional endocrine, nutritional and local inputs control steroid production, and PTTH is not absolutely required for every developmental transition in every species. In the standard examination model, however, it is useful to distinguish the neuroendocrine input from the steroid-producing gland and the target tissues that respond to the steroid.

Ecdysone is an ecdysteroid synthesized from sterol precursors; insects generally depend on dietary sterols because they cannot synthesize sterols de novo in the same way as vertebrates. In many insect larvae, the prothoracic gland releases ecdysone, which can be hydroxylated in peripheral tissues to the highly active 20-hydroxyecdysone, abbreviated 20E. Ecdysone and 20E must not be treated as entirely unrelated hormones. They belong to the same endocrine system, with conversion contributing to the amount of active ligand available. The biologically relevant steroid concentration depends on production, enzymatic conversion, transport, metabolism and removal. A reduction in active 20E could therefore arise from a defect in synthesis of the precursor or from failure to convert it.

The ecdysone receptor EcR belongs to the nuclear receptor family. It acts with Ultraspiracle, a receptor related to vertebrate RXR. Hormone binding modifies the activity of this receptor complex at regulatory DNA sequences, allowing changes in transcription of responsive genes. An increase in active hormone can thus be converted into a change in cellular RNA and protein composition. Some induced regulatory proteins subsequently influence other genes, creating an ordered cascade rather than a single instantaneous change. Early-response factors and later effector genes must be distinguished: blocking translation of a newly synthesized transcriptional regulator can preserve some primary transcriptional responses while preventing secondary changes dependent on that regulator.

This organization explains why the concentration of hormone and the time of its measurement matter. A pulse of ecdysteroid can activate a transcriptional program, but the receptor-dependent products can persist beyond the initial rise of ligand. Some events are associated with increasing hormone, others with its decline. In many insects, subsequent ecdysis behavior involves a coordinated neuropeptide system rather than a simple direct contraction of muscles caused by high steroid concentration. Ecdysis-triggering hormone and other peptides participate in this coordination. The details vary by species, and the steroid pulse should not be identified with every later movement of the insect. A high concentration at one sampling time is not sufficient evidence that shedding will occur immediately.

At the epidermis, successful replacement of the cuticle requires coordinated secretion, degradation of recyclable layers, and changes in cellular attachment. At other tissues, steroid-responsive transcription can regulate differentiation, remodeling or survival. A uniform circulating hormone is therefore not a uniform instruction for all cells to make cuticle. The epidermis has the machinery to secrete cuticular components, whereas cells in an imaginal primordium possess a different transcriptional landscape. Their outputs depend on pre-existing identity as well as endocrine stimulation. This is the postembryonic version of a general developmental principle: the same signaling molecule can have different consequences in different competent tissues.

A loss of prothoracic gland function is expected to impair normal ecdysteroid-dependent transitions. If a suitable active steroid is supplied and target-cell receptors remain functional, a subset of those transitions may be restored. By contrast, target cells lacking a functional EcR cannot recover normal EcR-dependent transcription simply by increasing circulating hormone. A mutation in a late cuticular protein gene may allow upstream endocrine changes and primary transcriptional responses to proceed while producing an abnormal cuticle. These contrasting predictions are useful because the endpoint of failed ecdysis alone cannot identify the defective component. A molecular readout and a structural readout together locate the lesion more effectively.

5. Juvenile hormone and developmental identity

A larva can undergo several molts without entering metamorphosis. The mechanism that starts a molt cannot therefore explain what kind of body will emerge afterward. Juvenile hormones, abbreviated JH, provide a second endocrine input that helps retain juvenile identity until the relevant developmental transition is appropriate. They are sesquiterpenoid molecules produced by the corpora allata rather than steroid hormones secreted by the prothoracic gland. Different insects use different molecular forms of JH. Their effective concentrations depend on secretion, distribution and enzymatic inactivation, including the activities of juvenile hormone esterase and epoxide hydrolase. These processes allow juvenile signaling to change over a developmental interval.

In a representative late nymph of a hemimetabolous insect, an ecdysteroid pulse in the presence of a juvenile-maintaining signal tends to produce another nymphal form. Reduction of juvenile signaling during an appropriate competent interval permits the adult program to proceed in response to the molting signal. Holometabolous insects undergo an additional distinct pupal transition. The pupal identity is not merely an intermediate value of a continuous hormone concentration. It involves its own transcriptional program, and hormone requirements differ among species and tissues. The traditional high JH equals larva, low JH equals pupa, no JH equals adult shorthand should only be understood as a contextual simplification, not as a universal concentration equation.

The JH receptor Methoprene-tolerant, abbreviated Met, is a transcriptional regulator in the basic helix-loop-helix PAS family. Ligand binding enables appropriate interactions with a partner such as Taiman, promoting expression of downstream targets including Krüppel homolog 1, or Kr-h1. In many insects Kr-h1 helps maintain juvenile identity and restrains premature adult differentiation, partly through effects on the adult-associated transcription factor E93. When juvenile signaling falls during a competent stage, this restraint is relaxed, enabling an adult developmental program. This sequence illustrates how a hormonal change can become a change in gene expression rather than merely a change in the rate of metabolism.

In holometabolous models, transcription factors of the Broad-Complex contribute to pupal specification. Broad, Kr-h1 and E93 should not be classified as three hormones; they are components of the regulatory machinery that translates endocrine input into developmental identity. Broad is associated with a pupal program, while E93 is important in adult morphogenesis. The precise interactions differ among species and developmental intervals. Drosophila imaginal discs, for example, can undergo metamorphic development despite experimental JH exposure that affects other tissues. A student must therefore identify which organism and which target tissue an experiment describes before predicting an outcome.

A loss of JH production differs mechanistically from loss of the receptor. If the source is impaired but the target pathway is intact, supplying a biologically appropriate ligand before commitment may restore a juvenile response. If Met cannot transduce the signal, adding ligand is unlikely to restore Met-dependent transcription. In a simplified pathway, expression of a functional downstream factor might rescue one output in a receptor mutant. Such rescue does not establish that this factor is the receptor's only target. Likewise, loss of E93 can interfere with the adult program even if the normal fall in JH takes place. Premature differentiation and failure of adult differentiation represent different causal errors.

The time of hormone exposure is essential. A tissue can acquire commitment to a later identity while the animal still looks externally like its previous instar. JH applied before this point may change the outcome whereas a later application may have much less effect. A negative result after a late treatment cannot establish that JH was irrelevant earlier. To investigate the timing of commitment, compare treatments at several defined stages and examine both early gene expression and the final morphology. This design distinguishes an altered decision from an altered execution of a decision already made.

6. Imaginal primordia and selective tissue remodeling

The feeding Drosophila larva contains tissues that serve immediate larval functions alongside groups of cells that will contribute to adult structures. Imaginal discs are epithelial primordia originating in embryogenesis and growing during larval development. They form many adult structures, including the wings, legs and parts of the head and thorax. Their existence provides an explanation for an important feature of complete metamorphosis: much of the future adult can acquire spatial organization before the animal abandons its larval body plan. Adult differentiation does not require every larval cell to return to an undifferentiated state.

The wing imaginal disc is a folded epithelial structure with territories that contribute to particular adult regions. Growth and patterning during larval life establish an organized primordium. During metamorphosis, changes in cell shape, adhesion and tissue geometry help transform this folded epithelium into an extended adult appendage. Cells differentiate in accordance with their regional developmental information. The connection with vertebrate limb development is conceptual rather than anatomical: both demonstrate that a primordium must grow and establish positional information before it can produce an organized structure. An insect wing disc is not a vertebrate limb bud or an apical ectodermal ridge.

The adult body also receives contributions from cells that are not part of the familiar imaginal discs. In Drosophila, histoblasts contribute to much of the adult abdominal epidermis. Some larval neurons survive and remodel their connections. Other tissues undergo extensive removal. These different histories can be tested with lineage markers. If a marked wing-disc population produces cells in an adult wing, the result establishes its normal contribution under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the same population has the developmental potential to form every adult tissue. To test potency rather than fate, the cells must be challenged under a suitably altered developmental context.

A genetic mosaic is useful for testing whether an endocrine response requires receptor activity within a particular population. Consider a wing primordium containing both receptor-deficient cells and genetically normal neighbors. The two populations share the same circulating steroid environment. A defect restricted to receptor-deficient cells supports a local requirement. A defect extending into nearby normal cells suggests that local signaling or tissue mechanics may transmit some of the consequences. Neither result can be interpreted fully without knowing whether the mutant cells are alive, whether they proliferate normally and when the receptor function was lost.

Larval tissues do not all undergo the same destructive process. Programmed cell death and intracellular degradation can participate in removal of structures that are no longer needed. The larval salivary gland in Drosophila is a familiar example of regulated tissue elimination. Other cells persist while replacing proteins, altering shape or reconnecting with new partners. Thus, metamorphosis includes cell death, remodeling and new differentiation in the same animal. It is inaccurate to describe a pupa as a completely liquefied larva from which an adult forms without continuity of tissues. This misconception obscures the relationship between embryonic patterning, larval growth and adult organogenesis.

A pupa generally does not obtain new food through the normal larval feeding system, but metabolic activity continues. Protein synthesis, cell movement, membrane transport and the production of new extracellular structures require energy. Previously accumulated reserves and recycled cellular constituents support the transformation. The final adult size can consequently depend on both the quantity of resources accumulated during larval feeding and the endocrine timing of the end of that feeding period. A nutritional treatment that reduces adult size may act through limited materials, accelerated or delayed commitment, or a combination. Distinguishing these mechanisms requires measuring growth and developmental stage separately.

7. Amphibian larvae and metamorphic stages

A typical frog tadpole possesses a body adapted to aquatic life. It uses a muscular tail for locomotion and has respiratory, digestive and sensory structures appropriate to its larval condition. The frog-like juvenile produced after metamorphosis possesses a substantially different locomotory organization and must perform a different combination of functions. It is not necessarily reproductively mature when its tail has disappeared. Metamorphic completion and sexual maturity are distinct developmental endpoints. This distinction is particularly important in comparative studies of amphibian life histories.

Three broad phases are often used to describe anuran metamorphosis. During premetamorphosis, the larval body grows and tissues acquire the capacity to undergo subsequent transformation. During prometamorphosis, visible metamorphic changes increase, including growth and differentiation of limbs in typical frogs. At metamorphic climax, extensive changes occur in many tissues, including major tail regression. These phases overlap and must be interpreted in relation to the organism and staging scheme. Xenopus is commonly staged using Nieuwkoop and Faber criteria; Gosner stages are frequently used for other anurans. The numbers in these schemes are not interchangeable.

The hindlimbs become conspicuous earlier than the external forelimbs in a familiar anuran sequence. Tail reduction occurs later and must be coordinated with the ability of the limbs to support useful movement. The intestine may shorten and change its epithelial organization; the nature of this transition depends partly on the species-specific feeding habits of larva and juvenile. Many tadpoles consume plant material or detritus and many frogs consume animal prey, but the existence of carnivorous larvae and other dietary exceptions prevents treating this change as universal. In the respiratory system, the relative contribution of different surfaces changes as development proceeds. The organization of the skin, musculature and nervous system also changes.

A common endocrine signal coordinates these events, but the signal cannot specify every anatomical detail independently of each tissue's earlier development. A limb bud and a tail already possess different cellular compositions and regulatory states. A circulating signal that stimulates limb differentiation may simultaneously initiate regression of a larval structure. The direction of the response depends on tissue competence and downstream gene expression. This is analogous to the use of a morphogen by different embryonic fields, although the distribution and mechanisms of the relevant signals differ.

Axolotls illustrate an important departure from the usual biphasic amphibian life cycle. These salamanders can become sexually mature while retaining larval-like features such as external gills. This form of paedomorphosis demonstrates that maturation of reproductive functions and transformation of somatic tissues need not remain obligatorily coupled. Under suitable conditions exogenous thyroid hormone can induce substantial metamorphic changes in axolotls, showing that retained larval characteristics do not necessarily reflect a complete loss of the tissue's capacity to transform. The exact response depends on lineage and physiological context.

The study of different timing patterns introduces heterochrony, a change in the relative timing or rate of developmental events. Evolutionary modification of developmental timing can produce distinct adult forms without requiring a new collection of structural genes. In contrast, an environmentally caused shift in the rate of development within a genotype illustrates phenotypic plasticity. These concepts are related because the capacity for a plastic response can itself evolve, but the observed appearance of two animals at one age is not enough to decide whether their difference is environmental, genetic or both.

8. Classical evidence for thyroid hormone action

Friedrich Gudernatsch's experiments reported in 1912 showed that feeding thyroid gland material to tadpoles accelerated metamorphosis relative to relevant comparisons. The observation suggested that a chemical activity originating in a gland could alter the course of development. It was not initially proof of the exact molecular identity of the active substance. Thyroid tissue contains many constituents, and feeding experiments can be influenced by general nutritional effects. Subsequent studies using more defined interventions supported a role for thyroid hormone itself. The history illustrates why biological causation is strengthened by different types of experiments converging on the same explanation.

A complementary source-removal experiment asks whether normal development depends on thyroid activity. When thyroid hormone production is effectively eliminated in a typical anuran larva, the normal transformation is impaired or delayed even if some larval growth continues. A properly controlled experiment must distinguish loss of gland function from injury, illness or inadequate food. Sham-operated controls address effects of the procedure, and an appropriate replacement hormone can test whether lack of endocrine activity explains the phenotype. An incomplete rescue may indicate a mismatch in timing or local hormone availability rather than absence of a thyroid-dependent developmental program.

An isolated organ offers a different experimental opportunity. If a competent tadpole tail explant regresses after exposure to thyroid hormone compared with an appropriate vehicle-treated explant, the observation indicates that a response can occur within the tissue without an intact brain-pituitary-thyroid axis. It does not demonstrate that every cell in the tail is directly hormone responsive. An explant contains several cell types that can communicate with one another. A gene perturbation restricted to one population can be used to investigate whether its receptor activity is required within that population or whether signals from neighboring cells are sufficient.

Experiments involving receptors require special caution. An unliganded thyroid hormone receptor can actively regulate transcription. A receptor mutant unable to respond to hormone may remain bound to regulatory DNA and repress targets, whereas a receptor deletion can remove some of that repression. These two mutations are not necessarily equivalent. Students should distinguish a dominant-negative receptor, a complete receptor knockout and a reduction in hormone concentration. All can disrupt normal metamorphosis, but the direction of change in an early target gene and the developmental timing of the phenotype can differ.

Measurements of thyroid hormone concentration through development provide a valuable descriptive framework, but a rise in hormone accompanying a morphological change is not alone proof of necessity. Both could respond to a third signal. Source removal, hormone replacement, organ culture and receptor manipulation each address a different part of the causal chain. Together they link a systemic endocrine factor to local developmental mechanisms. An examination question may present only one of these forms of evidence; the conclusion should not exceed the inference supported by the actual design.

9. Thyroid hormone availability and target-cell responses

The thyroid hormones thyroxine, abbreviated T4, and triiodothyronine, abbreviated T3, are iodine-containing derivatives of tyrosine. T4 contains four iodine atoms; T3 contains three. T3 usually has greater activity at nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, so conversion of T4 to T3 can enhance local signaling. The two molecules should not be treated as unrelated messengers. Their concentrations and interconversion contribute to the total endocrine response. Iodine availability, thyroid synthetic capacity, transport and peripheral metabolism can all influence the active signal.

Hypothalamic factors stimulate pituitary release of thyroid-stimulating hormone, abbreviated TSH, which promotes thyroid hormone production by the thyroid gland. Corticotropin-releasing hormone, or CRH, has important functions in larval amphibian thyroid and stress-axis regulation. It acts at a different level from TSH. A mammal-centered description of hypothalamic thyroid control must not simply be copied into an amphibian developmental context. The importance of releasing hormones and feedback relationships depends on stage and species. The endocrine pathway links sensory or internal information to the production of a signal distributed to the body's tissues.

Feedback regulates hormone availability, and the sensitivity of this control system itself changes during larval development. A measured hormone concentration reflects the balance between entry into and removal from the circulation. The fact that a concentration has increased does not necessarily mean that the instantaneous rate of production is high at the time of sampling. For example, a concentration might remain high after a secretory pulse has ceased if clearance is slow. Measurements of the hormone, gland activity and regulatory signals provide different pieces of the endocrine explanation.

The deiodinase D2 can convert T4 into active T3. D3 can inactivate thyroid hormones by removing iodine at other positions, generating reverse T3 from T4 or less active T2 from T3. A tissue with abundant D2 may experience a larger active signal from the same circulating T4 supply than a tissue with little D2. Higher D3 activity may have the opposite effect. However, these relationships also depend on hormone transport, substrate availability and other routes of metabolism. An enzyme-expression measurement is not identical to a measurement of enzyme activity or intracellular free ligand.

Thyroid hormone receptors, TRs, are nuclear receptors. They can interact with retinoid X receptors, RXRs, and associate with regulatory DNA elements. In an important developmental model, unliganded TR recruits corepressors at certain responsive genes. Hormone binding alters receptor interactions, enabling activation of appropriate transcriptional programs through coactivators. Some genes respond directly to the receptor, whereas other changes depend on the products of earlier response genes. This explains why exposure to thyroid hormone may cause an immediate molecular change followed by a delayed morphological event.

TR alpha and TR beta differ in expression and developmental roles. TR beta is itself induced by thyroid hormone in many relevant tissues. The resulting increase in receptor abundance may help amplify or alter subsequent tissue responsiveness. Local positive regulation of the receptor can coexist with negative feedback on systemic hormone secretion because these mechanisms operate at different organizational levels. This distinction is useful when a dataset shows an increasing receptor concentration despite a decline in circulating TSH.

Two tissues exposed to the same amount of circulating T4 may transform on different schedules. One could express D2 and a suitable receptor early; another could initially inactivate hormone or lack the complete downstream response machinery. Later developmental changes might permit the second tissue to respond. In this way, a systemic endocrine signal can coordinate multiple organs without forcing identical responses at identical times. The biological result depends on local signal availability, receptor competence, prior cell identity and the specific genes that the tissue can regulate.
