1. Development continues beyond the embryo

A butterfly emerging from a chrysalis and a small frog leaving water illustrate a general developmental problem. The embryo has already constructed a functional body, yet that body is not necessarily the one that will reproduce. A newly hatched animal may possess a working digestive system, a nervous system and a set of locomotory structures suited to its immediate surroundings. It may later have to acquire a different way of obtaining food, exchanging gases or moving. The developmental program must therefore accomplish two tasks that can be separated in time: building a viable young organism and converting that organism into a form appropriate to subsequent stages of its life. A change of body plan at this later point is not a second fertilization, nor does it require production of a different genome. It is a coordinated change in the behavior and composition of tissues descended from the original embryo.

Postembryonic development is the collection of developmental events that occur after an animal has left the embryonic condition. Hatching or birth is a convenient observable boundary, although neither event guarantees that all organs have completed differentiation. A mammalian newborn, for example, continues to grow, modifies its skeleton and nervous connections, and later becomes capable of reproduction. These changes are development even if there is no distinct larval form. A juvenile animal is one that has not yet reached adulthood. A larva is more specifically a postembryonic stage with characteristics specialized for a particular part of the life history, often substantially different from those of the adult. A tadpole is not simply a frog with shorter legs, and a caterpillar is not a butterfly with small wings. Each has a distinctive architecture suited to its activities at that stage.

Direct development describes a life history in which the young animal has the basic organization of the adult without a separate, conspicuously distinct free-living larval stage. A chicken hatching from an egg has a beak, legs and feathers appropriate for a young bird, even though the proportions, physiology and behavior will change during maturation. Indirect development includes a distinct larval stage and a transition to the adult or juvenile adult-like organization. The term does not imply that larval development is less organized or that an adult form is absent from the genome. A larval body may be essential for survival and growth before adult structures become useful. In several direct-developing frogs, features of the transformation are reorganized into development inside the egg. Thus, the absence of a free-swimming tadpole is not proof that all underlying developmental processes have disappeared.

The distinction between growth and differentiation is crucial. Growth increases mass or size; differentiation changes cellular structure and function; morphogenesis changes shape and spatial organization. These events often accompany one another, but they need not do so. A caterpillar can increase in mass substantially while retaining the basic larval organization. A tadpole may lose mass when its tail disappears while simultaneously developing an adult digestive tract and improving the function of its limbs. An insect pupa may not feed, yet extensive differentiation occurs using material and energy previously acquired. An increase in development can therefore coexist with a decrease in mass. Developmental stage should be identified by an appropriate structural or molecular criterion rather than inferred solely from body weight.

Metamorphosis denotes a relatively conspicuous developmental transformation between life-history forms. It involves an organized change rather than an injury, a random loss of organs or generalized tissue degeneration. A functional adult requires old structures to disappear when they are no longer appropriate, retained tissues to adapt, and newly useful structures to differentiate. The transitions must be synchronized sufficiently that the organism remains viable. For instance, aquatic locomotion cannot cease long before an animal acquires a suitable alternative means of movement. In insects, the protective cuticle must be replaced while the developing structures remain covered. Developmental control over time is as biologically necessary as control over position during embryogenesis.

A useful experimental distinction is the difference between chronological age and developmental age. Two larvae hatched at the same time may be at different morphological stages if one receives less food or develops at a lower temperature. Conversely, animals at the same stage may differ substantially in size or time since hatching. Age can be measured with a clock, whereas stage is a classification of developmental organization. A study comparing hormone concentration across animals should therefore specify whether the sampling is matched by age, morphological stage, body size or some combination. Failing to make this distinction may make an indirect consequence of slower development appear to be a direct effect of an environmental factor.

Many larvae feed and accumulate resources independently; others rely substantially on reserves inherited from the egg. An echinoderm feeding larva and a nonfeeding larva are not interchangeable examples of energy acquisition, although both may have an organization different from the adult. A free-living larva may also disperse farther than an adult that is relatively immobile. Such differences help explain why indirect development is maintained in numerous animal lineages. They are not proof that larvae necessarily evolved to reduce competition with adults. Separating ecological consequences from evolutionary explanations prevents an observation about present-day function from becoming an unsupported claim about the historical origin of a life cycle.

The cellular problem is analogous to induction and competence in the embryo. A developing tissue must receive a signal and must be capable of responding to it. Competence can change over time as receptors, regulatory proteins and chromatin states change. A hormone circulating through the entire body can trigger different responses in different tissues because those tissues do not possess identical regulatory states. A limb rudiment may grow while a larval appendage regresses, even if both receive a systemic signal. This principle will link the endocrine mechanisms of insects with those of amphibians without implying that their hormones or anatomical structures are homologous.

2. Insect life cycles and the necessity of molting

An insect is enclosed by a cuticle secreted by its epidermis. The cuticle provides mechanical support, protection against physical damage and, in terrestrial insects, an important barrier to water loss. It is an extracellular structure with chitin and proteins as principal components in many regions; the relative amounts and properties vary between regions and stages. A hardened cuticle does not expand continuously in proportion to internal growth. A young insect consequently undergoes successive episodes in which the old external covering is replaced. These episodes create opportunities to alter external morphology and are essential for understanding the timing of insect development.

An instar is the developmental interval between successive molts, or the corresponding form of the insect during that interval. An instar can be identified by cuticular structures and other species-specific markers. A molt involves more than the conspicuous moment at which the old cuticle is shed. During apolysis, epidermal cells separate from the old cuticle. A new protective layer and a new cuticle are secreted beneath it. Components of the old inner cuticle may be digested and reclaimed while its protective outer parts remain until shedding. Ecdysis is the actual escape from the old cuticular covering. The new covering can expand and then mature through processes including hardening, depending on its region and type. Endocrine regulation must coordinate these events so that the newly secreted cuticle is not destroyed by the enzymes involved in recycling the old one.

Molting is not synonymous with metamorphosis. A larva that undergoes a molt and emerges as a larger larva has experienced cuticle replacement without acquiring a new life-history identity. In contrast, a molt that produces a pupa or an adult can be part of metamorphosis. Endocrine regulation of the physical molt must be distinguished from regulation of the identity of the stage produced by the molt. This distinction is particularly important in experiments where a treatment triggers molting but preserves larval features, or prevents a normal molt despite the presence of an adult developmental program in the tissues.

Ametabolous insects, such as silverfish, develop without a dramatic metamorphic change and may continue molting after sexual maturity. In hemimetabolous insects, eggs hatch into nymphs. A grasshopper nymph resembles the adult in many aspects of its organization, although its wings and reproductive apparatus are not yet mature. Wing pads become progressively conspicuous in suitable species. The final nymphal molt produces an adult without an intervening pupal stage. Aquatic nymphs of dragonflies demonstrate that gradual or incomplete metamorphosis need not mean the young and adult occupy the same habitat or use identical respiratory structures. A comparison must distinguish the number and identity of stages from their ecological similarity.

Holometabolous insects, such as flies, butterflies and beetles, pass through larval, pupal and adult forms. The larval and adult organizations may be markedly different. In a typical butterfly, the caterpillar has mouthparts and locomotory structures useful for its larval feeding activities, while the adult has wings and a different feeding apparatus. The pupal interval permits extensive reorganization between those body forms. A pupa is neither an embryo nor a physiologically inactive object: biochemical reactions, gene expression, tissue differentiation and selective destruction of larval structures continue. The external immobility of many pupae reflects their life-history role, not a cessation of cellular activity.

Drosophila melanogaster is a particularly useful holometabolous model because its stages can be distinguished with relatively clear developmental criteria. Under appropriate conditions, the embryo hatches into a first-instar larva and subsequently passes through second and third instars. At the end of the third instar, feeding ceases, the larva pupariates, and the pupal transformation proceeds within the protective puparium. The puparium is formed from the hardened last larval cuticle; it is not identical to the developing pupa inside it. Eclosion produces the adult fly. The exact durations depend on temperature, nutrition and genotype. It is more accurate to learn the ordered sequence and the criteria for its transitions than to memorize one set of hours as though it applied in every environment.

A practical biological consequence of this arrangement is that a defect may become apparent several stages after it originated. A mutation that disrupts proliferation of an imaginal disc in a feeding larva may permit much larval behavior to continue because the adult wing is not required at that stage. The adult, however, may emerge with defective wings or fail to emerge at all. By contrast, a mutation that prevents functional larval feeding may stop development before adult structures are tested. Stage-specific phenotypes therefore help distinguish immediate functions from future developmental requirements. Examination questions often exploit this distinction by presenting a normal-looking larva followed by a defective adult.

Hemimetabolous and holometabolous transitions have common endocrine features, but their stage-specific molecular programs are not identical. The presence of a pupal interval is not simply the consequence of an insect undergoing one extra ordinary molt. A pupa has a distinctive developmental identity and program of tissue reorganization. Therefore, comparing equivalent endocrine changes in two insects requires attention to the species, the relevant instar, the competence of the tissue and whether the normal life history includes a pupal form.

3. Classical evidence for circulating developmental signals

Before identifying individual hormones, investigators had to establish that metamorphosis was regulated by signals that could pass between tissues rather than only by the age of each cell. The blood-feeding hemimetabolous bug Rhodnius prolixus provided a useful experimental system because feeding and the subsequent molt could be related in time. Vincent Wigglesworth's experiments in the 1930s helped establish that signals originating in the head influence molting and that a separate endocrine activity maintains juvenile characteristics. The experimental logic, described in his 1934 report and reviewed by Lynn Riddiford, is more important for developmental reasoning than the historical priority of every individual observation.

Consider a nymph whose head is removed after an appropriate feeding stimulus but before the circulating signal needed for molting has appeared. If it fails to molt, the head may be necessary during that particular interval. Nevertheless, head removal alone cannot identify a hormone. Injury, interruption of neural signals, altered feeding and disruption of other physiological processes are alternative explanations. The causal interpretation becomes stronger if appropriate controls survive and a suitable donor can restore the missing developmental response. Parabiosis is a classical experimental arrangement that permits transfer of circulating substances between animals. It can test whether an activity produced in one animal influences developmental behavior in another without requiring identical nervous connections.

A historically important observation was that a younger Rhodnius nymph receiving endocrine influence from a final-instar donor could undergo an unusually early adult transformation under appropriate experimental conditions. Other combinations caused retention of juvenile features. These findings are not explained adequately by a single signal that merely increases the rate of growth. They support the distinction between the signal initiating a molt and an influence that specifies juvenile rather than adult character. Later work connected the latter activity with the corpora allata, paired endocrine organs in insects. The chemical identification of juvenile hormones and the characterization of their receptors subsequently made the developmental interpretation more precise.

The experimental design can be understood with a hypothetical set of results. A stage-matched untreated control proceeds through its usual juvenile molt. A second group deprived of the molting stimulus does not undergo ecdysis. A third group receiving a suitable circulating molting stimulus recovers the ability to molt. If the developmental identity of the new stage varies according to a separate juvenile-maintaining influence, molting and stage identity have been experimentally separated. A rescue of molting is not necessarily a rescue of normal adult or juvenile morphology. Each endpoint must be scored independently. This is a general rule in developmental biology: rescuing one outcome of a perturbation is not evidence that every downstream event has returned to its normal state.

Another classical logic involves tissue-specific hormone application. Suppose that a region of epidermis is exposed locally to a juvenile-maintaining signal during an interval in which the rest of the epidermis is competent to produce adult cuticle. If that small region forms juvenile cuticle surrounded by adult cuticle, the result supports a local effect on the developmental identity of epidermal cells. Wigglesworth used localized juvenile-hormone-containing preparations to demonstrate this kind of tissue response. It is necessary to control for abrasion or carrier solvent because these might alter secretion or tissue integrity independently. A mosaic adult with a patch of juvenile cuticle is strong evidence that developmental identity is not always dictated by a single indivisible state of the whole organism.

The terms necessary and sufficient must be used with care in interpreting these findings. If removing the corpora allata at a particular juvenile stage advances metamorphosis, their normal activity may be necessary to preserve juvenile identity during that interval. If adding juvenile hormone restores juvenile features when a molting signal is present, the hormone is sufficient for that particular rescue under the specified conditions. It is not sufficient to make every tissue juvenile at every developmental stage. Prior commitment, species differences, hormone dose and receptor expression can determine whether the same treatment is effective. The experiments establish conditional developmental relationships rather than a universal rule that any amount of a hormone produces the same response.

A later molecular experiment can strengthen this interpretation by perturbing the hormone receptor rather than its source. If a tissue cannot respond to juvenile hormone because its receptor is nonfunctional, adding the hormone may fail even though it circulates at a normal concentration. Conversely, if the receptor is intact but the source is absent, supplying the hormone at an appropriate stage might restore the response. These two experiments distinguish an endocrine deficiency from a target-tissue defect. The logic is identical in many developmental signaling systems, but the identities of the ligands and receptors are specific to each organism.

4. Ecdysteroids: linking endocrine signals to the physical molt

The endocrine system solves the problem of coordinating tissues separated by distance. In an insect, endocrine products are distributed through the hemolymph and reach cells with appropriate receptors. A representative larval endocrine pathway includes neurosecretory cells in the brain, the prothoracic glands and peripheral tissues. Prothoracicotropic hormone, commonly abbreviated PTTH, stimulates ecdysteroid production by the prothoracic glands in many insects. The pathway is not a universal single switch: additional endocrine, nutritional and local inputs control steroid production, and PTTH is not absolutely required for every developmental transition in every species. In the standard examination model, however, it is useful to distinguish the neuroendocrine input from the steroid-producing gland and the target tissues that respond to the steroid.

Ecdysone is an ecdysteroid synthesized from sterol precursors; insects generally depend on dietary sterols because they cannot synthesize sterols de novo in the same way as vertebrates. In many insect larvae, the prothoracic gland releases ecdysone, which can be hydroxylated in peripheral tissues to the highly active 20-hydroxyecdysone, abbreviated 20E. Ecdysone and 20E must not be treated as entirely unrelated hormones. They belong to the same endocrine system, with conversion contributing to the amount of active ligand available. The biologically relevant steroid concentration depends on production, enzymatic conversion, transport, metabolism and removal. A reduction in active 20E could therefore arise from a defect in synthesis of the precursor or from failure to convert it.

The ecdysone receptor EcR belongs to the nuclear receptor family. It acts with Ultraspiracle, a receptor related to vertebrate RXR. Hormone binding modifies the activity of this receptor complex at regulatory DNA sequences, allowing changes in transcription of responsive genes. An increase in active hormone can thus be converted into a change in cellular RNA and protein composition. Some induced regulatory proteins subsequently influence other genes, creating an ordered cascade rather than a single instantaneous change. Early-response factors and later effector genes must be distinguished: blocking translation of a newly synthesized transcriptional regulator can preserve some primary transcriptional responses while preventing secondary changes dependent on that regulator.

This organization explains why the concentration of hormone and the time of its measurement matter. A pulse of ecdysteroid can activate a transcriptional program, but the receptor-dependent products can persist beyond the initial rise of ligand. Some events are associated with increasing hormone, others with its decline. In many insects, subsequent ecdysis behavior involves a coordinated neuropeptide system rather than a simple direct contraction of muscles caused by high steroid concentration. Ecdysis-triggering hormone and other peptides participate in this coordination. The details vary by species, and the steroid pulse should not be identified with every later movement of the insect. A high concentration at one sampling time is not sufficient evidence that shedding will occur immediately.

At the epidermis, successful replacement of the cuticle requires coordinated secretion, degradation of recyclable layers, and changes in cellular attachment. At other tissues, steroid-responsive transcription can regulate differentiation, remodeling or survival. A uniform circulating hormone is therefore not a uniform instruction for all cells to make cuticle. The epidermis has the machinery to secrete cuticular components, whereas cells in an imaginal primordium possess a different transcriptional landscape. Their outputs depend on pre-existing identity as well as endocrine stimulation. This is the postembryonic version of a general developmental principle: the same signaling molecule can have different consequences in different competent tissues.

A loss of prothoracic gland function is expected to impair normal ecdysteroid-dependent transitions. If a suitable active steroid is supplied and target-cell receptors remain functional, a subset of those transitions may be restored. By contrast, target cells lacking a functional EcR cannot recover normal EcR-dependent transcription simply by increasing circulating hormone. A mutation in a late cuticular protein gene may allow upstream endocrine changes and primary transcriptional responses to proceed while producing an abnormal cuticle. These contrasting predictions are useful because the endpoint of failed ecdysis alone cannot identify the defective component. A molecular readout and a structural readout together locate the lesion more effectively.

5. Juvenile hormone and developmental identity

A larva can undergo several molts without entering metamorphosis. The mechanism that starts a molt cannot therefore explain what kind of body will emerge afterward. Juvenile hormones, abbreviated JH, provide a second endocrine input that helps retain juvenile identity until the relevant developmental transition is appropriate. They are sesquiterpenoid molecules produced by the corpora allata rather than steroid hormones secreted by the prothoracic gland. Different insects use different molecular forms of JH. Their effective concentrations depend on secretion, distribution and enzymatic inactivation, including the activities of juvenile hormone esterase and epoxide hydrolase. These processes allow juvenile signaling to change over a developmental interval.

In a representative late nymph of a hemimetabolous insect, an ecdysteroid pulse in the presence of a juvenile-maintaining signal tends to produce another nymphal form. Reduction of juvenile signaling during an appropriate competent interval permits the adult program to proceed in response to the molting signal. Holometabolous insects undergo an additional distinct pupal transition. The pupal identity is not merely an intermediate value of a continuous hormone concentration. It involves its own transcriptional program, and hormone requirements differ among species and tissues. The traditional high JH equals larva, low JH equals pupa, no JH equals adult shorthand should only be understood as a contextual simplification, not as a universal concentration equation.

The JH receptor Methoprene-tolerant, abbreviated Met, is a transcriptional regulator in the basic helix-loop-helix PAS family. Ligand binding enables appropriate interactions with a partner such as Taiman, promoting expression of downstream targets including Krüppel homolog 1, or Kr-h1. In many insects Kr-h1 helps maintain juvenile identity and restrains premature adult differentiation, partly through effects on the adult-associated transcription factor E93. When juvenile signaling falls during a competent stage, this restraint is relaxed, enabling an adult developmental program. This sequence illustrates how a hormonal change can become a change in gene expression rather than merely a change in the rate of metabolism.

In holometabolous models, transcription factors of the Broad-Complex contribute to pupal specification. Broad, Kr-h1 and E93 should not be classified as three hormones; they are components of the regulatory machinery that translates endocrine input into developmental identity. Broad is associated with a pupal program, while E93 is important in adult morphogenesis. The precise interactions differ among species and developmental intervals. Drosophila imaginal discs, for example, can undergo metamorphic development despite experimental JH exposure that affects other tissues. A student must therefore identify which organism and which target tissue an experiment describes before predicting an outcome.

A loss of JH production differs mechanistically from loss of the receptor. If the source is impaired but the target pathway is intact, supplying a biologically appropriate ligand before commitment may restore a juvenile response. If Met cannot transduce the signal, adding ligand is unlikely to restore Met-dependent transcription. In a simplified pathway, expression of a functional downstream factor might rescue one output in a receptor mutant. Such rescue does not establish that this factor is the receptor's only target. Likewise, loss of E93 can interfere with the adult program even if the normal fall in JH takes place. Premature differentiation and failure of adult differentiation represent different causal errors.

The time of hormone exposure is essential. A tissue can acquire commitment to a later identity while the animal still looks externally like its previous instar. JH applied before this point may change the outcome whereas a later application may have much less effect. A negative result after a late treatment cannot establish that JH was irrelevant earlier. To investigate the timing of commitment, compare treatments at several defined stages and examine both early gene expression and the final morphology. This design distinguishes an altered decision from an altered execution of a decision already made.

6. Imaginal primordia and selective tissue remodeling

The feeding Drosophila larva contains tissues that serve immediate larval functions alongside groups of cells that will contribute to adult structures. Imaginal discs are epithelial primordia originating in embryogenesis and growing during larval development. They form many adult structures, including the wings, legs and parts of the head and thorax. Their existence provides an explanation for an important feature of complete metamorphosis: much of the future adult can acquire spatial organization before the animal abandons its larval body plan. Adult differentiation does not require every larval cell to return to an undifferentiated state.

The wing imaginal disc is a folded epithelial structure with territories that contribute to particular adult regions. Growth and patterning during larval life establish an organized primordium. During metamorphosis, changes in cell shape, adhesion and tissue geometry help transform this folded epithelium into an extended adult appendage. Cells differentiate in accordance with their regional developmental information. The connection with vertebrate limb development is conceptual rather than anatomical: both demonstrate that a primordium must grow and establish positional information before it can produce an organized structure. An insect wing disc is not a vertebrate limb bud or an apical ectodermal ridge.

The adult body also receives contributions from cells that are not part of the familiar imaginal discs. In Drosophila, histoblasts contribute to much of the adult abdominal epidermis. Some larval neurons survive and remodel their connections. Other tissues undergo extensive removal. These different histories can be tested with lineage markers. If a marked wing-disc population produces cells in an adult wing, the result establishes its normal contribution under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the same population has the developmental potential to form every adult tissue. To test potency rather than fate, the cells must be challenged under a suitably altered developmental context.

A genetic mosaic is useful for testing whether an endocrine response requires receptor activity within a particular population. Consider a wing primordium containing both receptor-deficient cells and genetically normal neighbors. The two populations share the same circulating steroid environment. A defect restricted to receptor-deficient cells supports a local requirement. A defect extending into nearby normal cells suggests that local signaling or tissue mechanics may transmit some of the consequences. Neither result can be interpreted fully without knowing whether the mutant cells are alive, whether they proliferate normally and when the receptor function was lost.

Larval tissues do not all undergo the same destructive process. Programmed cell death and intracellular degradation can participate in removal of structures that are no longer needed. The larval salivary gland in Drosophila is a familiar example of regulated tissue elimination. Other cells persist while replacing proteins, altering shape or reconnecting with new partners. Thus, metamorphosis includes cell death, remodeling and new differentiation in the same animal. It is inaccurate to describe a pupa as a completely liquefied larva from which an adult forms without continuity of tissues. This misconception obscures the relationship between embryonic patterning, larval growth and adult organogenesis.

A pupa generally does not obtain new food through the normal larval feeding system, but metabolic activity continues. Protein synthesis, cell movement, membrane transport and the production of new extracellular structures require energy. Previously accumulated reserves and recycled cellular constituents support the transformation. The final adult size can consequently depend on both the quantity of resources accumulated during larval feeding and the endocrine timing of the end of that feeding period. A nutritional treatment that reduces adult size may act through limited materials, accelerated or delayed commitment, or a combination. Distinguishing these mechanisms requires measuring growth and developmental stage separately.

7. Amphibian larvae and metamorphic stages

A typical frog tadpole possesses a body adapted to aquatic life. It uses a muscular tail for locomotion and has respiratory, digestive and sensory structures appropriate to its larval condition. The frog-like juvenile produced after metamorphosis possesses a substantially different locomotory organization and must perform a different combination of functions. It is not necessarily reproductively mature when its tail has disappeared. Metamorphic completion and sexual maturity are distinct developmental endpoints. This distinction is particularly important in comparative studies of amphibian life histories.

Three broad phases are often used to describe anuran metamorphosis. During premetamorphosis, the larval body grows and tissues acquire the capacity to undergo subsequent transformation. During prometamorphosis, visible metamorphic changes increase, including growth and differentiation of limbs in typical frogs. At metamorphic climax, extensive changes occur in many tissues, including major tail regression. These phases overlap and must be interpreted in relation to the organism and staging scheme. Xenopus is commonly staged using Nieuwkoop and Faber criteria; Gosner stages are frequently used for other anurans. The numbers in these schemes are not interchangeable.

The hindlimbs become conspicuous earlier than the external forelimbs in a familiar anuran sequence. Tail reduction occurs later and must be coordinated with the ability of the limbs to support useful movement. The intestine may shorten and change its epithelial organization; the nature of this transition depends partly on the species-specific feeding habits of larva and juvenile. Many tadpoles consume plant material or detritus and many frogs consume animal prey, but the existence of carnivorous larvae and other dietary exceptions prevents treating this change as universal. In the respiratory system, the relative contribution of different surfaces changes as development proceeds. The organization of the skin, musculature and nervous system also changes.

A common endocrine signal coordinates these events, but the signal cannot specify every anatomical detail independently of each tissue's earlier development. A limb bud and a tail already possess different cellular compositions and regulatory states. A circulating signal that stimulates limb differentiation may simultaneously initiate regression of a larval structure. The direction of the response depends on tissue competence and downstream gene expression. This is analogous to the use of a morphogen by different embryonic fields, although the distribution and mechanisms of the relevant signals differ.

Axolotls illustrate an important departure from the usual biphasic amphibian life cycle. These salamanders can become sexually mature while retaining larval-like features such as external gills. This form of paedomorphosis demonstrates that maturation of reproductive functions and transformation of somatic tissues need not remain obligatorily coupled. Under suitable conditions exogenous thyroid hormone can induce substantial metamorphic changes in axolotls, showing that retained larval characteristics do not necessarily reflect a complete loss of the tissue's capacity to transform. The exact response depends on lineage and physiological context.

The study of different timing patterns introduces heterochrony, a change in the relative timing or rate of developmental events. Evolutionary modification of developmental timing can produce distinct adult forms without requiring a new collection of structural genes. In contrast, an environmentally caused shift in the rate of development within a genotype illustrates phenotypic plasticity. These concepts are related because the capacity for a plastic response can itself evolve, but the observed appearance of two animals at one age is not enough to decide whether their difference is environmental, genetic or both.

8. Classical evidence for thyroid hormone action

Friedrich Gudernatsch's experiments reported in 1912 showed that feeding thyroid gland material to tadpoles accelerated metamorphosis relative to relevant comparisons. The observation suggested that a chemical activity originating in a gland could alter the course of development. It was not initially proof of the exact molecular identity of the active substance. Thyroid tissue contains many constituents, and feeding experiments can be influenced by general nutritional effects. Subsequent studies using more defined interventions supported a role for thyroid hormone itself. The history illustrates why biological causation is strengthened by different types of experiments converging on the same explanation.

A complementary source-removal experiment asks whether normal development depends on thyroid activity. When thyroid hormone production is effectively eliminated in a typical anuran larva, the normal transformation is impaired or delayed even if some larval growth continues. A properly controlled experiment must distinguish loss of gland function from injury, illness or inadequate food. Sham-operated controls address effects of the procedure, and an appropriate replacement hormone can test whether lack of endocrine activity explains the phenotype. An incomplete rescue may indicate a mismatch in timing or local hormone availability rather than absence of a thyroid-dependent developmental program.

An isolated organ offers a different experimental opportunity. If a competent tadpole tail explant regresses after exposure to thyroid hormone compared with an appropriate vehicle-treated explant, the observation indicates that a response can occur within the tissue without an intact brain-pituitary-thyroid axis. It does not demonstrate that every cell in the tail is directly hormone responsive. An explant contains several cell types that can communicate with one another. A gene perturbation restricted to one population can be used to investigate whether its receptor activity is required within that population or whether signals from neighboring cells are sufficient.

Experiments involving receptors require special caution. An unliganded thyroid hormone receptor can actively regulate transcription. A receptor mutant unable to respond to hormone may remain bound to regulatory DNA and repress targets, whereas a receptor deletion can remove some of that repression. These two mutations are not necessarily equivalent. Students should distinguish a dominant-negative receptor, a complete receptor knockout and a reduction in hormone concentration. All can disrupt normal metamorphosis, but the direction of change in an early target gene and the developmental timing of the phenotype can differ.

Measurements of thyroid hormone concentration through development provide a valuable descriptive framework, but a rise in hormone accompanying a morphological change is not alone proof of necessity. Both could respond to a third signal. Source removal, hormone replacement, organ culture and receptor manipulation each address a different part of the causal chain. Together they link a systemic endocrine factor to local developmental mechanisms. An examination question may present only one of these forms of evidence; the conclusion should not exceed the inference supported by the actual design.

9. Thyroid hormone availability and target-cell responses

The thyroid hormones thyroxine, abbreviated T4, and triiodothyronine, abbreviated T3, are iodine-containing derivatives of tyrosine. T4 contains four iodine atoms; T3 contains three. T3 usually has greater activity at nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, so conversion of T4 to T3 can enhance local signaling. The two molecules should not be treated as unrelated messengers. Their concentrations and interconversion contribute to the total endocrine response. Iodine availability, thyroid synthetic capacity, transport and peripheral metabolism can all influence the active signal.

Hypothalamic factors stimulate pituitary release of thyroid-stimulating hormone, abbreviated TSH, which promotes thyroid hormone production by the thyroid gland. Corticotropin-releasing hormone, or CRH, has important functions in larval amphibian thyroid and stress-axis regulation. It acts at a different level from TSH. A mammal-centered description of hypothalamic thyroid control must not simply be copied into an amphibian developmental context. The importance of releasing hormones and feedback relationships depends on stage and species. The endocrine pathway links sensory or internal information to the production of a signal distributed to the body's tissues.

Feedback regulates hormone availability, and the sensitivity of this control system itself changes during larval development. A measured hormone concentration reflects the balance between entry into and removal from the circulation. The fact that a concentration has increased does not necessarily mean that the instantaneous rate of production is high at the time of sampling. For example, a concentration might remain high after a secretory pulse has ceased if clearance is slow. Measurements of the hormone, gland activity and regulatory signals provide different pieces of the endocrine explanation.

The deiodinase D2 can convert T4 into active T3. D3 can inactivate thyroid hormones by removing iodine at other positions, generating reverse T3 from T4 or less active T2 from T3. A tissue with abundant D2 may experience a larger active signal from the same circulating T4 supply than a tissue with little D2. Higher D3 activity may have the opposite effect. However, these relationships also depend on hormone transport, substrate availability and other routes of metabolism. An enzyme-expression measurement is not identical to a measurement of enzyme activity or intracellular free ligand.

Thyroid hormone receptors, TRs, are nuclear receptors. They can interact with retinoid X receptors, RXRs, and associate with regulatory DNA elements. In an important developmental model, unliganded TR recruits corepressors at certain responsive genes. Hormone binding alters receptor interactions, enabling activation of appropriate transcriptional programs through coactivators. Some genes respond directly to the receptor, whereas other changes depend on the products of earlier response genes. This explains why exposure to thyroid hormone may cause an immediate molecular change followed by a delayed morphological event.

TR alpha and TR beta differ in expression and developmental roles. TR beta is itself induced by thyroid hormone in many relevant tissues. The resulting increase in receptor abundance may help amplify or alter subsequent tissue responsiveness. Local positive regulation of the receptor can coexist with negative feedback on systemic hormone secretion because these mechanisms operate at different organizational levels. This distinction is useful when a dataset shows an increasing receptor concentration despite a decline in circulating TSH.

Two tissues exposed to the same amount of circulating T4 may transform on different schedules. One could express D2 and a suitable receptor early; another could initially inactivate hormone or lack the complete downstream response machinery. Later developmental changes might permit the second tissue to respond. In this way, a systemic endocrine signal can coordinate multiple organs without forcing identical responses at identical times. The biological result depends on local signal availability, receptor competence, prior cell identity and the specific genes that the tissue can regulate.

10. Three developmental outcomes of the same thyroid signal

The transformation of Xenopus offers an unusually clear comparison of three outcomes of hormone-dependent development. The growing hindlimb acquires differentiated adult functions, the larval tail is eliminated, and the intestine is extensively reorganized. All three processes depend on thyroid hormone signaling in the normal metamorphic program. It follows that the hormone cannot be understood as a general growth factor or a general agent of tissue destruction. The information specifying whether a particular structure should grow, disappear or change its organization is distributed among the cell populations that constitute the structure. Hormone availability controls when an appropriate program can be carried out, but the anatomical character of the program depends on the developmental history of the target tissue.

Hindlimb growth begins relatively early in the metamorphic sequence. Limb buds have already been established by processes of embryonic positional specification. They contain populations capable of producing skeletal elements, muscle, connective tissue and other components of a functional appendage. As thyroid-dependent development advances, changes in cell proliferation, differentiation and organization allow the limb to acquire more of its adult architecture. A circulating endocrine signal need not repeat the earlier specification of anterior and posterior digit identities. Instead, its function includes regulation of progression through later developmental states. The distinction between positional information and developmental timing helps explain why a treatment that accelerates limb differentiation need not generate an extra limb or change the order of the digits.

The timing of tail regression presents the complementary problem. A tadpole depends on its tail for effective swimming during much of larval life. Premature elimination of this structure would be disadvantageous before other locomotory mechanisms are available. The tail's responsiveness to thyroid hormone is relatively restricted during earlier stages and changes near climax. Local hormone metabolism and receptor expression contribute to this changing response. The tail is composed of several interacting tissues, including muscle, epidermis, connective tissue and elements supporting movement. Its removal requires more than death of a single type of cell. Changes in extracellular matrix, tissue integrity and cell survival act together as its architecture is dismantled.

One component of tail regression involves programmed cell death. In this context, death is part of normal development because it eliminates a structure whose function belongs to an earlier life-history stage. A reduction in the length of the tail is a macroscopic consequence of several cellular processes; it cannot by itself reveal which of them is rate limiting. A drug that interferes with a cell-death pathway might preserve some cells without maintaining a normally organized tail. Conversely, weakening extracellular support could accelerate structural collapse even if all cell populations have not yet completed their characteristic death programs. A carefully designed study should examine cell number, cell viability, matrix structure and tail dimensions as distinct readouts.

The intestine illustrates a third possibility, in which an existing organ undergoes extensive reconstruction. During Xenopus metamorphosis much of the larval intestinal epithelium is eliminated, while adult epithelial cell populations develop and expand. Some adult epithelial stem cells arise from cells of the earlier epithelium that change their developmental state. Muscle and connective tissue also mature and reorganize, producing the folded structure characteristic of the adult organ. The organ retains continuity through metamorphosis even though substantial portions of its cellular organization are replaced. It is therefore misleading to describe it as either an unchanged larval intestine or an entirely independent organ that appears elsewhere.

The interaction between intestinal epithelium and surrounding mesenchyme is particularly instructive. The epithelium provides the cellular interface that faces the intestinal contents, while neighboring connective and muscular tissues provide support and signals. Thyroid hormone can alter gene expression in more than one compartment. If hormone responsiveness is selectively disrupted in the surrounding tissue, epithelial differentiation may be affected even if epithelial receptors remain functional. This result would support the importance of intercellular communication in remodeling. Conversely, disrupting receptors in epithelial cells could directly alter their ability to respond and indirectly influence the development of their neighbors.

To distinguish these possibilities experimentally, imagine separating the genotypes of the epithelial and mesenchymal compartments in a conceptual tissue recombination study. A preparation with both compartments responsive provides a positive reference. A preparation with neither responsive provides a negative comparison. Two additional preparations retain response capacity in only one compartment at a time. If successful adult epithelial differentiation requires competence in both compartments, neither single-compartment rescue will fully restore it. Such a pattern is more informative than a general failure of remodeling following whole-animal hormone removal. It distinguishes an organ-level response from the individual contributions of its cell populations.

The biological meaning of programmed death in the intestine differs from that in the tail. Tail regression removes a predominantly larval structure, whereas intestinal remodeling must establish a functional adult organ in the location occupied by its larval predecessor. If elimination of larval cells proceeded normally but replacement populations failed to expand, the result could be a poorly functioning intestine rather than a normal organ with fewer larval cells. Therefore, a successful developmental transformation cannot be judged solely by disappearance of an earlier marker. New cell identity, appropriate tissue organization and organ function provide additional criteria.

This comparison is supported by studies using thyroid receptor perturbations in Xenopus. Different receptor subtypes have different stage-specific contributions, and removing both receptors does not necessarily prevent the appearance of every adult-like feature. Relief of repression by unliganded receptors can permit some processes to occur prematurely. Yet failure of hormone-dependent elimination of larval structures can prevent the animal from completing the normal transition. The unusual combination of prematurely developing and persistent structures illustrates why it is necessary to distinguish regulation before hormone exposure from activation by liganded receptors. A single phrase such as development blocked is inadequate when different organs have different phenotypes.

A useful way to interpret such a mutant is to establish a matrix of outcomes for representative tissues. Record whether limb differentiation starts at the expected stage, whether intestinal larval epithelial markers disappear, whether adult epithelial markers increase, and whether tail resorption is completed. The data can then be compared with hormone concentrations and receptor expression. This approach tests whether a mutation affects the organism's overall endocrine timing or the execution of particular local programs. It also avoids an important statistical mistake: treating several measurements from the same animal as independent individuals when they are actually correlated outcomes of its developmental state.

11. Nutrition, developmental checkpoints and insect body size

A larva that begins metamorphosis before storing adequate resources may be unable to support the transformation, whereas one that postpones metamorphosis indefinitely may lose opportunities to reach adulthood. A developmental checkpoint allows progression to depend on internal conditions rather than only on elapsed time. Checkpoints are especially useful in insects whose larval feeding period provides much of the material used to build the adult. The physiological problem is to match the schedule of differentiation with the capacity to complete it. Nutrient intake is therefore important not only as a supply of material but also as information about whether developmental progression is appropriate.

The critical weight is an operational concept in the study of insect developmental timing. In a familiar experimental definition it is the approximate mass or developmental condition beyond which removal of food no longer causes a delay in metamorphosis relative to suitable fed controls. This definition must be distinguished from the minimum viable weight. The latter refers to the size or nutritional state at which an individual has accumulated enough resources to survive a specified transition without additional feeding. The two thresholds need not be equal. An animal can have enough reserves to survive an interval while still altering its developmental timing in response to food deprivation.

To estimate a critical weight experimentally, investigators can select larvae at different sizes during the last larval instar, withhold food at defined times and record the interval until pupariation or an equivalent metamorphic event. Fed controls must be observed under the same temperature and staging conditions. Earlier food removal often delays progression or prevents some animals from completing development. After the relevant checkpoint, the effect on timing is much smaller, although subsequent growth may be reduced. Plotting the response to starvation against starting mass can reveal a transition between these regimes. The estimate depends on the population and conditions and should not be treated as a fixed universal number for all larvae.

The experimental endpoint needs to be specified particularly carefully when mortality is high. If the average time to pupariation is calculated only for survivors, the animals most severely affected by food deprivation may be excluded from the analysis. A short mean time among the surviving subset does not necessarily indicate acceleration in the entire population. Recording the proportion that survives, the stage reached by animals that do not complete the transition, and the time distribution among those that do is more informative. The distinction is important when interpreting a hypothetical question in which a treatment appears to produce faster development but also substantially reduces survival.

Nutritional information can influence endocrine signaling through pathways sensitive to the internal supply of nutrients and energy. In Drosophila, insulin-like signals and TOR-associated nutrient sensing contribute to regulation of growth and developmental timing. These pathways affect tissues in different ways. In a peripheral tissue such as an imaginal disc, nutrient-dependent signals can modify growth and proliferation. In the steroid-producing endocrine system, nutrient-sensitive processes can influence the timing or magnitude of ecdysteroid output. Thus, one nutritional condition can alter both the rate at which the animal increases in size and the interval over which growth is permitted to continue.

The distinction between Manduca and Drosophila is scientifically important. In the tobacco hornworm Manduca sexta, classical endocrine studies associate attainment of the developmental checkpoint with changes in juvenile hormone availability, followed by endocrine events leading to metamorphosis. In Drosophila, the role of JH in determining the critical-weight transition is not simply identical. Nutritional inputs and the timing of ecdysteroid signaling play prominent roles, and some manipulations of JH do not produce the same timing effects predicted from the hornworm model. The correct comparative inference is that both insects coordinate nutritional condition with metamorphosis, but the relative contribution of particular endocrine inputs has diverged.

A developmental delay can provide an opportunity to compensate for slow growth under some circumstances. Suppose two larvae begin an instar at the same mass. One receives adequate nutrients and grows rapidly, whereas the other grows slowly. If their developmental transition depends partly on attaining an internal size-related condition, the slower-growing larva may remain in the feeding stage longer. It can therefore achieve a size nearer to that of the rapidly growing larva than would be possible under a fixed chronological schedule. This compensation has limits. If food is so scarce that continued feeding cannot supply enough material, prolonged larval life may not restore normal growth or survival.

After commitment to metamorphosis, a delay in developmental progression may no longer be available as a compensation mechanism. Food deprivation during this later interval can reduce the material added before feeding ceases. The adult may consequently be smaller even if it emerges on a schedule similar to that of fed individuals. This contrast explains why nutritional manipulations at different stages can produce different relationships between adult size and developmental time. A smaller adult need not imply a longer larval period, and a longer larval period need not imply a smaller adult.

Tissue coordination adds another layer to the checkpoint problem. An imaginal primordium that is injured or develops abnormally slowly may provide signals that modify the overall developmental schedule. In Drosophila, work on imaginal tissues demonstrates that delaying organ development can delay maturation of the animal. Such communication is biologically useful because the organism should not initiate a major transition before essential future structures are sufficiently prepared. However, a delay caused by tissue injury is not automatically identical to a delay caused by nutritional restriction. Both may converge on an endocrine output, while their initiating signals differ.

An examination-level model of growth and timing can therefore be written in words as two interacting processes: nutrient availability affects the rate of tissue growth, while endocrine regulation determines the duration and developmental context of that growth. Neither rate nor duration alone determines the outcome. To interpret adult mass, measurements of larval mass over time and developmental stage are needed. To interpret a hormone concentration, the developmental state and nutritional condition must also be considered. The integration of these measurements is the central lesson of the critical-weight experiments.

The connection between nutrition and endocrine timing has been tested directly by manipulating nutrient-sensitive signaling in the steroid-producing gland of Drosophila. Work on the TOR pathway showed that the gland participates in the coupling of nutritional inputs to ecdysone output. The experimental logic is important: altering the ability of a particular endocrine tissue to respond to nutrient information allows the effect on developmental timing to be compared with the effect of changing growth signals in peripheral tissues. A gland-specific change in timing is consistent with a role for the gland as an endocrine checkpoint, but does not imply that every nutritional response occurs in the gland. Both tissue growth and systemic timing contribute to the integrated phenotype.

An additional clue comes from experiments examining wing-disc patterning after food deprivation at different stages. Before critical weight, nutritional deprivation can delay acquisition of later patterning features. After the checkpoint, parts of the differentiative program become less dependent on continued feeding. The stage-dependent switch implicates ecdysteroid-dependent regulation of the developing tissue as well as the endocrine production of the steroid itself. A growth measurement alone could miss this change: a disc may become smaller under restricted nutrition while still acquiring particular positional markers on a relatively normal schedule. Measuring marker expression at the same time after the molt into the last instar, together with stage and organ size, helps resolve the two outcomes.

12. Temperature and developmental rate

In ectothermic animals, the temperature of the surroundings strongly influences physiological processes. Enzyme-catalyzed reactions, membrane properties and metabolic expenditure respond to temperature, and these changes can alter the pace of developmental events. Within a moderate viable range, warming often shortens the time needed for an insect or amphibian to reach a specified stage. The relationship is not unlimited. Extreme temperatures disrupt molecular and cellular functions, and rates may decline or become highly variable near upper or lower tolerance boundaries. A short developmental period at one temperature cannot justify extrapolation to arbitrarily high temperatures.

A developmental rate can be defined as the reciprocal of the duration of a specified interval. If a group takes ten days to pass from hatching to a defined larval stage, its average rate for that interval is one tenth per day. A group taking five days has twice this average rate. This quantity is an operational measure rather than a direct measurement of any particular molecular reaction. Different organs may progress at somewhat different rates, and the rate for one life-history interval need not equal the rate for another. The stage boundaries and the biological endpoint must be the same when comparing groups.

In many applications, a simple linear approximation is used over a limited range of temperatures. Let r be the measured developmental rate in inverse days and let T be the temperature in degrees Celsius. If r is approximately proportional to the difference between T and a lower developmental threshold T0, then r equals a times the quantity T minus T0. The coefficient a has units of inverse days per degree Celsius. Taking the reciprocal gives a duration proportional to one divided by T minus T0. The product of duration and temperature above the threshold is approximately constant under the model and is often called a thermal sum or degree-day requirement.

The lower threshold derived from a fitted straight line is an estimated parameter. It does not necessarily represent the exact temperature below which every biochemical process in the animal stops. Similarly, a degree-day requirement describes an empirical developmental interval under specified conditions; it is not a universal thermodynamic law. A nonlinear relationship at high or low temperatures may arise because the underlying biochemical and physiological processes do not all share the same temperature dependence. An equation used for forecasting in a moderate range may have no validity outside that range.

The thermal-sum approximation is especially useful for distinguishing changes in chronological time from changes in developmental progress. An insect that reaches a stage after fewer days at a warm temperature need not be developmentally abnormal. If its structural sequence and functional outcomes are appropriate, the difference may be a normal temperature-dependent change in rate. On the other hand, exposure during a sensitive interval can disrupt the proper ordering of developmental processes, producing malformed structures. The fact that a temperature treatment accelerates one observable event does not establish that the whole developmental program is proceeding normally.

Temperature can influence final body size through its effects on growth rate, endocrine timing and the developmental checkpoint that marks the transition to later stages. In many ectothermic species, warmer rearing conditions within a suitable range are associated with smaller adult size, but this relationship is not a universal rule. If warming increases growth rate while reducing the length of the feeding interval, the outcome depends on the balance between these changes. The environment can also affect food quality, oxygen availability and water balance. Isolating a temperature effect therefore requires maintaining other relevant conditions as consistently as possible.

A well-controlled developmental temperature experiment uses several temperatures, not merely an extreme treatment and a control. Individuals are assigned to conditions with comparable food supply and starting stage. Developmental landmarks, body dimensions and survival are recorded, ideally at more than one point. Replicate containers are needed if individuals within a container share a microenvironment. Observed temperature responses can then be separated into changes in the rate of progression, changes in the size attained at a given stage and changes in the frequency of abnormal development. Each has a different biological meaning.

A stage-matched transfer experiment can reveal whether temperature acts throughout development or during a restricted sensitive window. Animals can be moved between otherwise identical thermal environments at specific morphological stages. If a particular structure develops abnormally only when exposed during an early interval, the exposure may have disrupted a developmental decision or the initial formation of a tissue. If the same treatment later changes growth without altering morphology, it acts on a different developmental process. This approach is more informative than comparing two groups raised at different temperatures for their entire life cycle.

13. Photoperiod, diapause and seasonal developmental decisions

A change in temperature is a direct physical condition experienced by an animal, but some environmental signals function mainly as predictors of future conditions. The duration of light and darkness within a day changes regularly with the seasons outside equatorial regions. Many insects use photoperiod to anticipate conditions that may be unsuitable for development or reproduction. A predictable seasonal cue allows preparatory physiological changes to begin before the unfavorable period arrives. This temporal organization differs from an immediate reduction in activity caused directly by cold or lack of water.

Diapause is a regulated developmental arrest or interruption adapted to recurring unfavorable conditions in many species. It is commonly initiated by environmental information acting at a particular sensitive stage, and it can involve preparatory changes in metabolism, resource storage and endocrine regulation. The arrested stage is species-specific. Some insects enter embryonic diapause, others enter larval or pupal diapause, and others suspend reproductive maturation in the adult. Diapause is therefore not synonymous with the pupa. A pupa that continues the ordinary sequence of metamorphosis is not necessarily in diapause.

Quiescence is often distinguished from diapause by its more direct dependence on immediate unfavorable conditions. If an animal simply reduces activity or developmental rate while the temperature is unsuitable and resumes when the condition improves, the response may be quiescence rather than a programmed seasonal arrest. Diapause can persist for a period even after the initiating cue has been removed, because the internal developmental state must progress through the conditions required for termination. Nevertheless, terminology and physiological details vary among species. A single observation of slow or absent growth is insufficient to identify the type of arrest.

Photoperiodic control is often stage-sensitive. An insect may detect day length during a juvenile stage but enter diapause later, or maternal conditions may influence the developmental outcome of offspring in certain species. In a controlled study, light and dark durations must be specified while temperature, food and total developmental time are considered. If a short-day treatment increases the fraction entering diapause in one population, it does not establish that short days induce diapause in all insects. Other species or geographic populations may respond differently because their seasonal ecology differs.

A useful experiment measures diapause incidence across a range of photoperiods. The proportion of individuals entering diapause can be plotted against hours of light per twenty-four-hour cycle. A transition from high to low incidence may allow an estimate of a critical photoperiod for the tested population. If a second population has a different transition point, possible explanations include genetic differences in cue sensitivity and differences in previous experience. Rearing both populations under comparable conditions over appropriate generations can help distinguish environmental carryover from inherited differences. Such common-environment comparisons are fundamental for interpreting developmental plasticity.

The endocrine mechanism of diapause depends on which stage is arrested. In some larval or pupal diapauses, reduced activity of the endocrine system that produces or responds to ecdysteroids helps maintain the absence of a normal metamorphic transition. In adult reproductive diapause, juvenile hormone can be involved in control of reproductive maturation in many models. These examples do not establish one universally low hormone level that defines every diapause. The required physiological outcome differs between an embryo, a pupa and an adult. An appropriate interpretation must identify the developmental process that is suspended and the hormonal system relevant to that process.

Diapause termination is also distinct from the conditions that originally induced entry. A long period of low temperature, an altered photoperiod or internal progression through the diapause program may be important depending on the species. If the first favorable day does not immediately restore ordinary development, the delay is compatible with a regulated state. It could also reflect injury or inadequate nutrition, so further controls are needed. A study should determine whether animals resume development in an organized manner and whether the timing of resumption follows a characteristic physiological pattern rather than merely assuming that inactivity proves diapause.

The cabbage butterfly Pieris melete provides an instructive exception to a one-directional response to day length. Studies of this species found that shorter days preferentially induce winter pupal diapause, while longer days can induce summer diapause. Intermediate photoperiods permit direct passage through the pupal stage in a proportion of the animals, depending on temperature. The same species can therefore use different seasonal information to enter an arrested state at the same anatomical stage of its life cycle. This example warns against inferring the direction of a photoperiodic response from the name of the developmental process. A plot with two regions of high diapause incidence separated by a region of lower incidence is biologically possible.

In a factorial photoperiod experiment, researchers can compare short, intermediate and long days at several controlled temperatures. The resulting pattern can distinguish a main effect of photoperiod from modification of that effect by temperature. A shift in the day-length response between temperatures implies that photoperiod alone is not sufficient to describe the developmental decision. If all animals are measured only at one temperature, that interaction cannot be detected. For an organism whose seasonal cues include both variables, a broad response surface may be more informative than a single estimated critical photoperiod.

14. Environmental information, stress and amphibian plasticity

An aquatic tadpole faces a particularly important environmental problem: the habitat that supports its larval life may disappear before it is ready to leave. Some amphibians breed in temporary ponds, where water levels decline as the season progresses. A fixed developmental schedule could be unsuitable in years when the pond dries rapidly. In several species, larvae can alter the rate of metamorphosis in response to environmental cues associated with declining water availability. This response provides a model for understanding how a normal developmental program can remain flexible without losing its organized sequence.

The endocrine response to declining water availability has been studied in spadefoot toads. Experiments in western spadefoot toads show that reduced water levels can accelerate metamorphic development and are associated with changes in thyroid hormone and corticosterone signaling. Corticosterone is a corticosteroid produced by the amphibian interrenal tissue. It is not a thyroid hormone and does not simply replace T3 at its receptor. Instead, stress-axis signaling can interact with thyroid-dependent programs, including modification of target-tissue sensitivity. The physiological interaction helps explain why a response to habitat change may involve more than increasing the output of the thyroid gland alone.

In larval amphibians, hypothalamic CRH-related signaling can influence both thyroid and interrenal endocrine axes. Environmental information can thus change the hormonal context in which tissues develop. If the thyroid-dependent signal increases at the same time as a corticosteroid-related influence on competence, metamorphic progression may accelerate more strongly than expected from either change considered in isolation. Synergy means that the joint effect exceeds an appropriately defined additive expectation on a specified measurement scale; it should not be asserted merely because two hormones both have detectable effects. A factorial treatment design is useful for testing this distinction.

Environmental acceleration has costs. A tadpole that leaves a drying pond earlier may have less time to accumulate resources or grow. Consequently, the juvenile produced after an accelerated transition can be smaller than one that develops under favorable conditions. Whether the response is adaptive depends on the balance between surviving the aquatic stage and the consequences of the resulting juvenile condition. A smaller size at metamorphosis is not automatically a malformation. It may represent an organized alternative developmental outcome within the animal's reaction norm, though it may have consequences for subsequent performance.

A response to water reduction is not universal among amphibians. Some species exhibit a pronounced acceleration under tested conditions, whereas others show a weaker response or little detectable change. Even in a responsive species, a larva must have reached a state in which successful transformation is possible. An early-stage animal without adequate resources or competent tissues may not respond by completing metamorphosis. Severe or prolonged stress can inhibit development or cause death. The distinction between an informative environmental cue and damaging physiological stress depends on intensity, developmental stage and species-specific regulation.

To examine a water-level effect, replicate groups of stage-matched tadpoles can be assigned to stable and declining water conditions. Food availability, animal density, water temperature and water chemistry must be controlled or measured because changes in water volume can alter all of these variables. Hormone concentrations and stage progression should be recorded at comparable intervals. If the low-water groups progress more rapidly, the result supports a response to the experimental conditions but does not isolate the sensory cue unless competing changes are addressed. This is why water reduction experiments benefit from careful control of the concentration of food and waste products as well as the physical water level.

A hormone measurement made only at the end of such an experiment can be misleading. If low-water animals are at a later developmental stage than controls, their hormone concentrations may differ simply because thyroid activity changes during ordinary metamorphosis. Sampling animals at matched times detects differences in the trajectory of the population, whereas stage-matched sampling helps test whether endocrine differences exist beyond the normal association with stage. Ideally, a series of early measurements made before major morphological divergence can identify candidate regulatory changes that precede the response. Temporal precedence strengthens causal reasoning, although it does not replace perturbation experiments.

A conceptual factorial design can compare four groups: vehicle control, thyroid hormone exposure alone, corticosteroid exposure alone and the two treatments together. If the combined condition produces a larger developmental response than expected from the separate conditions, an interaction is supported on the measured scale. If the corticosteroid has little effect alone but enhances a thyroid-dependent response, it may influence competence or another necessary component of the metamorphic pathway. This experiment would not establish the molecular identity of the target gene responsible. Measuring receptor abundance, early transcriptional markers and hormone metabolism could help localize the interaction.

Food restriction and crowding also influence amphibian development, but their effects vary with ecology and stage. Restricted resources can slow growth and delay development before competence is attained. In some competent larvae, an unfavorable environment may instead favor earlier metamorphosis. High density can reduce food per individual, alter chemical conditions or provide social information. A density treatment therefore does not identify a specific mechanism unless these alternatives are examined. The experimental logic is to ask which environmental variables affect the endocrine system and which affect the capacity of tissues to respond, rather than treating stress as a single developmental input.

Density-dependent effects can be examined by varying the number of larvae per container and separately varying the amount of food provided per individual. If increasing density slows development only when food per individual falls, competition for food is a plausible explanation. If the effect persists when each animal receives comparable nutrition, crowding-associated chemical conditions, oxygen availability or interactions among individuals become alternative hypotheses. The latter observation would not by itself identify a specific crowding signal. It is important to measure available food, water quality and individual stage rather than equate the number of animals in a container with a precisely defined endocrine input.

The same experimental reasoning can identify limits to environmentally accelerated metamorphosis. Compare stage-matched cohorts subjected to moderate water reduction, severe reduction and stable water. If moderate treatment speeds the normal sequence but severe treatment causes high mortality and incomplete tissue remodeling, these outcomes cannot be treated as points on a single curve describing beneficial acceleration. There may be a threshold beyond which physiological capacity is exceeded. A normal plastic response and a damaging exposure may involve the same initial environmental variable but differ in intensity and in the consequences for developing cells.

15. Developmental plasticity, sensitive periods and malformation

The genome of an animal does not encode a single inevitable value for every characteristic. It encodes a set of processes whose behavior depends on conditions within and outside the organism. The adult mass attained by a particular genotype, the duration of its larval period and the season in which metamorphosis occurs may differ among individuals raised under different conditions. Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. Developmental plasticity refers particularly to differences that arise through changes in the course of development. This concept provides a framework for interpreting the nutritional, thermal and endocrine examples in the preceding sections.

A reaction norm describes the range or pattern of phenotypes expressed by a genotype across environments. Imagine measuring the time to metamorphosis for animals from a genetically defined group in several controlled temperatures. Plotting the average duration against temperature produces an estimate of one aspect of the group's reaction norm. The plot can be nonlinear, and an individual point contains uncertainty because individuals within a group differ. A reaction norm does not represent the chronological growth trajectory of one animal. Its horizontal axis is an environmental variable and its vertical axis is a defined phenotypic endpoint. The distinction prevents confusion between a time course of metamorphosis and a comparison of metamorphic outcomes under different conditions.

Genotypes can differ in the shape of their reaction norms. Suppose one population of tadpoles undergoes metamorphosis much earlier in declining water conditions than in stable water, while a second population changes its timing only slightly. If the two groups have been raised in comparable conditions and the experimental environments are adequately replicated, the result is consistent with inherited differences in environmental responsiveness. A genotype-by-environment interaction occurs when the magnitude or direction of the effect of environment depends on genotype. A difference in average development time between groups does not by itself establish an interaction; the environmental response must be compared between them.

A common-environment comparison is important because individuals can carry physiological consequences of their earlier experiences. Larvae obtained from different habitats may differ in nutrient reserves, pathogen exposure or maternal provisioning even before the intended experimental treatment begins. Raising their descendants under comparable conditions can reduce some of these environmental differences. It does not prove that every remaining difference is caused by a specific DNA sequence. Maternal effects and other inherited or persistent influences can still contribute. To identify a genetic cause, additional breeding, genetic analysis or targeted perturbation is needed.

An environmentally produced phenotype is not automatically a mutation. A mutation changes the DNA sequence, whereas a plastic response can occur because an unchanged genome is expressed differently in altered conditions. Environmental agents can cause mutations, and environmental changes can produce phenotypes resembling those of certain mutants. A phenocopy is an environmentally produced phenotype that resembles a genetically caused phenotype. Similar appearances therefore do not establish identical mechanisms. For example, a developmental delay after poor nutrition and a delay produced by defective endocrine signaling may look similar while originating from different causal changes. Hormone measurements, genetic analysis and rescue experiments can help distinguish them.

Plasticity also does not imply that a phenotype must reverse when the original environment returns. Developmental decisions can have persistent consequences. If larval feeding ends early and the adult emerges at a smaller size, later improvement in food supply may not restore the size of a hardened adult exoskeletal structure. In this case the environmental effect was mediated through a limited developmental interval. Persistence of a difference does not establish a DNA mutation. The question is whether the altered outcome resulted from a change in inherited sequence or from the activity of a developmental program in its environmental context.

A sensitive period is an interval during which an environmental influence can alter a particular developmental outcome. The biological basis may be transient receptor expression, a limited period of tissue competence, a checkpoint that has not yet been passed, or the temporary presence of cells whose fates are being determined. Sensitivity need not be all or none; the same treatment may have progressively smaller effects as the developmental program advances. Different tissues may have different sensitive periods even when they share a circulating hormone. This is why treating larvae simply as a single homogeneous developmental stage can lead to contradictory experimental results.

A transfer design tests this temporal dependence. Rear several cohorts in a reference environment, expose each cohort to the same altered environment during a different defined stage, and return the animals to comparable conditions. A continuously exposed group and an unexposed control help interpret the results. If a treatment changes the final phenotype only during an early interval, the relevant developmental process may have become insensitive after that interval. Exposure durations and total dose must be considered: a treatment that lasts longer at one stage might appear more effective for reasons unrelated to competence. Where possible, measuring an early molecular response as well as the later phenotype helps identify what changed during exposure.

Normal developmental plasticity should be distinguished from developmental disruption. A larva that metamorphoses earlier into a viable but smaller juvenile may be expressing an organized alternative trajectory. By contrast, an abnormal organ arrangement, severe loss of function or failure to complete a necessary transformation may indicate damage or dysregulation. These are not always mutually exclusive categories at the level of an entire animal. An environmental condition can induce an adaptive timing response while simultaneously damaging one organ. The interpretation should be based on actual stage, structure, viability and function rather than the assumption that all environmentally induced changes are either beneficial or pathological.

The concept of adaptation requires an additional level of evidence. A response may appear useful under the conditions that induce it, but whether it increases fitness depends on survival and reproduction across the relevant environments. Early metamorphosis can reduce the danger of death in a drying pond while producing a smaller juvenile. If the smaller juvenile later has reduced survival, the net advantage depends on the relative risks in both stages. A developmental experiment demonstrating altered timing does not automatically establish that the alteration evolved because it improves fitness. Comparative and ecological evidence is needed for that evolutionary claim.

Developmental robustness, sometimes discussed in terms of canalization, is the ability of a developmental system to produce relatively consistent outcomes despite certain perturbations. Robustness and plasticity are compatible. An animal may maintain the correct arrangement of its organs over a range of nutritional conditions while altering the time required to produce them. Under more severe conditions that buffering may fail. The biologically significant question is which traits are buffered, which remain adjustable and why the balance differs among species. In examination problems, distinguish a stable anatomical sequence with adjustable timing from a disrupted sequence with malformed structures.

16. Quantitative interpretation of developmental responses

Quantitative models are useful when they clarify a causal question. They need not reproduce every biochemical detail of a developing animal. A model should specify the biological endpoint, the quantities being measured, their units and the assumptions that permit them to be related. Several simple relationships are particularly useful in postembryonic development: the product of growth rate and growth duration, the reciprocal relationship between stage duration and developmental rate, the fractional occupancy of a receptor, and the interaction between two experimental treatments. Each model describes a different aspect of the process and should not be substituted indiscriminately for another.

Consider the simplest representation of larval mass gain. Let M be body mass at the end of a specified feeding interval, M0 the starting mass, g the average net mass gain per unit time, and t the duration of the interval. If g is constant, integration of the constant rate gives M equal to M0 plus g multiplied by t. The dimensions are consistent: multiplying milligrams per day by days yields milligrams. The model illustrates why adult precursor mass depends on both growth rate and the timing of the endocrine transition that ends larval feeding. A treatment that changes the two variables in opposite directions can produce a final mass greater than, equal to or less than that of the control.

This equation is a model of net mass gain, not a conservation law for the complete animal. Feeding rate is not identical to tissue growth rate because ingested material may be unassimilated, used for maintenance or converted into storage and structural material. Water content may also affect wet mass. The mass recorded at the end of feeding need not equal the mass of the adult after metamorphosis. Nonfeeding intervals can involve substantial loss of stored material through respiration and elimination. The simplified equation is useful for comparing larval growth under controlled conditions, provided that the quantities being compared refer to the same stage and measurement method.

A proportional or exponential growth model would instead predict that the rate of mass gain depends on current mass. Its solution has a different shape from the constant-rate model. There is no reason to select an exponential model simply because the data describe a living organism. A larva can have different growth regimes during different instars, and nutritional limitation can change the relationship over time. A graph of mass against time, together with a comparison of local slopes, can reveal whether the constant-rate approximation is reasonable during the chosen interval. The developmental interpretation of a slope remains distinct from the interpretation of an endocrine checkpoint.

Hormone signaling introduces another kind of quantitative relationship. In a simple equilibrium model, a receptor R binds a ligand L to form a complex RL. The dissociation constant Kd is defined as the product of free receptor and free ligand concentrations divided by the concentration of complex. Total receptor concentration equals free receptor plus complex. Replacing free receptor by total receptor minus complex in the equilibrium expression and rearranging gives the fraction of occupied receptors equal to free ligand concentration divided by the sum of that concentration and Kd. At a free ligand concentration equal to Kd, half the receptors are occupied in this idealized model.

This relationship applies only under the assumptions of a single class of independent sites, equilibrium binding and an appropriate value of free ligand concentration. It does not mean that half the developmental response must occur at half receptor occupancy. Transcription may require interactions with other proteins, and cell responses can exhibit thresholds, delays or amplification. Increasing the abundance of receptor can increase the absolute amount of ligand-bound receptor at a fixed fractional occupancy, without changing the intrinsic affinity measured by Kd. Two tissues can therefore have identical occupancy fractions but different amounts of active receptor complex and different outputs.

Local conversion of T4 to T3 provides a useful application of this principle. Suppose two tissues have the same receptor affinity for T3, but one produces a higher intracellular concentration of T3 from a shared circulating T4 supply. Its receptor occupancy may be greater even though the receptor itself has not changed. Conversely, increasing receptor abundance can change the total amount of bound ligand without necessarily changing fractional occupancy. Real thyroid signaling involves transporters, metabolism, receptor subtypes and regulatory interactions that cannot be represented by this single equilibrium. The purpose of the model is to separate local ligand availability from receptor number and affinity.

A factorial experiment addresses a different quantitative question: whether the combined effect of two factors exceeds an additive expectation. Suppose the endpoint is the increase in an appropriate developmental score during a specified interval. Let Y00 be the outcome in the control group, Y10 the outcome with hormone A alone, Y01 the outcome with hormone B alone, and Y11 the outcome with both hormones. The effect of hormone A without B is Y10 minus Y00. The effect of A in the presence of B is Y11 minus Y01. The difference between these effects is the interaction contrast, equal to Y11 minus Y10 minus Y01 plus Y00. A positive contrast on this scale means that the observed joint effect exceeds the simple additive expectation.

A positive interaction contrast from sample means alone is not proof of a reproducible physiological synergy. Replicate variation, uncertainty and the scale of measurement must be considered. A bounded endpoint such as the fraction of animals completing metamorphosis cannot increase indefinitely, and an additive model can predict values outside the possible range when the separate effects are large. For such outcomes, a different statistical model may be appropriate. A developmental score built from ordered stages may also have unequal intervals between successive categories. For this reason, hypothetical exam problems should identify whether a score can reasonably be treated as quantitative or whether it is merely an ordinal rank.

Rate comparisons require similar care. If a treatment reduces the duration of a developmental interval from twenty days to ten, the duration falls by half but the average developmental rate doubles. A percentage reduction in time is not numerically the same as the percentage increase in rate. When comparing treatments, the baseline must be stated explicitly. If the observed time to metamorphosis is calculated for only the individuals that finish during a fixed observation period, the average may omit slower or noncompleting individuals. Recording survival, the proportion completing the event and the observation window prevents the rate estimate from being misinterpreted.

Worked example 1 — growth and timing

Hypothetical larvae begin at 20 mg. Control growth is 5 mg/day for 8 days, giving 20 + 5 × 8 = 60 mg. Treatment growth is 4 mg/day for 12 days, giving 20 + 4 × 12 = 68 mg. The treatment group grows more slowly yet finishes 8 mg heavier because feeding lasts longer. Assume constant net growth; the result describes end-of-feeding mass, not adult mass.

Worked example 2 — degree-days

In a hypothetical constant-temperature study, development takes 10 days at 20 °C and 5 days at 30 °C. Rates are 0.10 and 0.20 day⁻¹. The slope is (0.20 − 0.10)/(30 − 20) = 0.01 day⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Setting rate to zero gives T0 = 20 − 0.10/0.01 = 10 °C. The thermal sum is 1/0.01 = 100 degree-days. At 25 °C the predicted duration is 100/(25 − 10) = 6.67 days. This interpolation assumes the linear model applies; extreme temperatures require a different model.

Worked example 3 — occupancy and tissue competence

Assume independent equilibrium binding, free T3 concentrations of 2 and 8 nM, and Kd = 2 nM in both tissues. Occupancies are 2/(2+2) = 0.50 and 8/(8+2) = 0.80. At equal total receptor concentration the second tissue has 1.6 times as much bound receptor. If the first tissue has twice the total receptor abundance, it instead has 1.0 relative units of bound receptor compared with 0.8 in the second. Actual transcription may differ from this equilibrium prediction.

Worked example 4 — endocrine interaction

For the hypothetical data in Figure 18, the control is 2, A is 5, B is 3, and A+B is 9. Additive prediction = 5 + 3 − 2 = 6. Interaction contrast = 9 − 5 − 3 + 2 = +3 score units. A alone increases the score by 3 units; A with B increases it by 6 units. Replicates and an appropriate measurement scale are needed to assess statistical support.

Worked example 5 — developmental duration versus rate

Control animals complete a stage in 16 days; treatment animals in 12 days. Duration decreases by (16−12)/16 × 100 = 25%. Rate increases from 1/16 = 0.0625 day⁻¹ to 1/12 = 0.08333 day⁻¹, an increase of (0.08333/0.0625−1) × 100 ≈ 33.3%. Faster completion does not prove normal morphology; record survival and stage criteria.

17. Experimental inference, applications and comparative synthesis

The insect and amphibian examples demonstrate the importance of separating a circulating signal from the competence of its targets. In an insect, ecdysteroid-dependent transcription helps initiate and coordinate molts and metamorphic transitions; juvenile hormone helps regulate the identity of the resulting stage in the relevant species and tissues. In an amphibian, thyroid hormone has a central role in orchestrating changes to existing and developing structures. The fact that both systems use nuclear receptor-dependent transcription does not make their hormones chemically equivalent. Their ligands, endocrine sources and developmental histories differ. The conserved principle is the conversion of information about time and physiological state into regulated gene expression in particular cells.

An endocrine perturbation is best interpreted by placing it in a causal chain. Begin with the initiating information, such as nutrient condition, developmental stage or a seasonal cue. Identify the signal-producing tissue, the circulating product, any local conversion or clearance, the receptor and the tissue-specific response machinery. A phenotype at the end of the chain may arise from a defect at any earlier step. For example, a delayed molt may reflect inadequate steroid production, failure of receptor-dependent transcription or abnormal execution of the physical molt. Tail persistence in an amphibian may reflect an inadequate thyroid signal or failure of the specific regression program despite otherwise advancing development. Similar terminal outcomes do not prove that the defective steps are equivalent.

Measurements at several levels help distinguish these hypotheses. If the circulating concentration is low and provision of active ligand restores a characteristic target-gene response, a defect before receptor activation is plausible. If the concentration is normal but receptor-dependent transcription fails, a target-cell defect becomes more plausible. If transcription is normal while the morphological endpoint is abnormal, the lesion may affect a later effector or tissue interaction. These are hypotheses, not automatic conclusions. A hormone assay may fail to capture a transient pulse, the measured concentration may differ from the local free concentration, and the chosen gene marker may not represent all relevant receptor outputs.

Rescue experiments should include an appropriate untreated control and a vehicle comparison. A rescue treatment that itself changes developmental timing in normal animals can be difficult to interpret in mutants. Ideally, its effects are compared in both genetic backgrounds. If a mutant becomes more similar to the control after treatment, assess whether the rescue restores the normal sequence of events or merely compensates for one abnormal endpoint. Artificially activating a downstream process may bypass an earlier defect without reconstituting the normal regulatory system. This distinction is useful in questions about genetic epistasis, where a double perturbation is used to infer the order of components in a pathway.

Environmental studies introduce an additional challenge: the experimental unit. Several tadpoles sharing a single tank experience common water conditions and can influence one another. If a water-level treatment is applied to the tank, the tank is a relevant independent replicate for estimating the treatment effect. Counting every animal as an independent environmental replicate can underestimate uncertainty. The same logic applies to insect larvae sharing food in a container. A design with several independently assigned tanks or containers allows the variation between environmental replicates to be assessed. Individual observations remain useful but should not be confused with independent assignment of the treatment.

The regulation of insect metamorphosis has practical relevance in agriculture and public health because immature insects depend on precisely coordinated developmental transitions. Insect growth regulators can interfere with normal growth or development, including compounds that mimic juvenile hormone action or affect ecdysteroid-related processes. Their effects depend on the target species, exposed life stage and physiological pathway. A developmental disturbance may reduce the production of viable adults even if the exposed larva does not die immediately. The examination-relevant lesson is the mechanistic distinction between a direct acute toxic effect and disruption of a hormone-dependent developmental transition. It does not imply that such compounds are harmless to other organisms or suitable for uncontrolled use.

Amphibian metamorphosis is also a valuable biological model for investigating environmental influences on endocrine-dependent development. Substances that alter thyroid hormone synthesis, metabolism or receptor-dependent responses may change the timing or organization of metamorphosis. An observed delay after exposure does not alone demonstrate a thyroid-specific mechanism, because illness, nutritional changes or general toxicity can also slow development. Measurements of thyroid-related endpoints, developmental stage, viability and appropriate controls provide stronger evidence. The conceptual relevance lies in using a well-characterized developmental transition as a readout of environmental effects, rather than in treating every abnormality as evidence of the same endocrine disruption.

Developmental timing has an ecological consequence even when the underlying anatomical program is unchanged. An insect emerging at an appropriate season may encounter food or suitable conditions, while a larval amphibian leaving a pond before it dries can avoid mortality in the aquatic stage. However, the benefits of timing flexibility depend on the particular life history. A fixed rapid developmental schedule can be advantageous in some environments and costly in others. Different species can therefore evolve different degrees of plasticity in related endocrine pathways. The fact that a pathway is conserved does not establish that its timing, sensitivity or ecological role is identical.

The relationship with earlier developmental chapters can now be made explicit. Embryonic induction and competence explain why a tissue acquires the potential to respond to a particular signal. Pattern formation establishes the spatial organization on which later morphogenesis acts. Organogenesis produces tissue primordia whose later differentiation depends on local and systemic conditions. Postembryonic development extends these ideas to changes that occur across life-history stages. The central problem is the coordination of developmental time with tissue identity and environmental opportunity. The next topic, sex determination, asks how genetic or environmental inputs establish particular developmental pathways; its details are separate from the mechanisms of larval metamorphosis considered here.

Original practice questions; not previous-year questions. Select the best answer, then open the explanation.

1. Which observation best distinguishes indirect from direct development?

  1. All development stops at hatching.
  2. A distinct larval organization precedes the adult-like form.
  3. The young animal never grows after hatching.
  4. Every cell differentiates only after birth.
Answer and explanation

B. A distinct larval organization precedes the adult-like form.

A separate larval form and subsequent transformation distinguish indirect development. Direct developers still grow and differentiate; hatching does not stop development, and embryonic differentiation occurs in both routes.

2. What is an instar?

  1. The act of shedding the old cuticle.
  2. A developmental interval between successive molts, or the form during that interval.
  3. The entire pupal life of every insect.
  4. The separation of epidermis from old cuticle.
Answer and explanation

B. A developmental interval between successive molts, or the form during that interval.

Instar describes a stage, not the physical shedding event (ecdysis), the separation event (apolysis), or exclusively a pupa.

3. Which stage sequence characterizes typical holometabolous insects?

  1. Egg → nymphs → adult without pupa.
  2. Egg → adult → larva.
  3. Egg → larval instars → pupa → adult.
  4. Egg → pupa → feeding larva → adult.
Answer and explanation

C. Egg → larval instars → pupa → adult.

A pupal interval follows larval growth in complete metamorphosis. Nymphs followed directly by adults describe hemimetaboly; the other two sequences reverse developmental order.

4. What is the puparium of Drosophila?

  1. The adult wing primordium.
  2. An endocrine gland inside the brain.
  3. A protective case derived from the final larval cuticle.
  4. The same structure as a newly secreted adult cuticle.
Answer and explanation

C. A protective case derived from the final larval cuticle.

The hardened last larval cuticle encloses the developing pupa. Wing primordia are imaginal discs, endocrine glands are separate organs, and adult cuticle is subsequently formed.

5. Which pairing is correct?

  1. Corpora allata — juvenile hormone.
  2. Prothoracic glands — juvenile hormone.
  3. Corpora cardiaca — principal juvenile hormone synthesis.
  4. Corpora allata — principal larval ecdysone synthesis.
Answer and explanation

A. Corpora allata — juvenile hormone.

The corpora allata produce JH. Prothoracic glands principally synthesize ecdysteroids in larvae; corpora cardiaca participate in neuroendocrine release and energy regulation, rather than being the primary JH source.

6. Which process describes conversion of ecdysone to 20-hydroxyecdysone?

  1. Peripheral hydroxylation of an ecdysteroid precursor.
  2. Removal of iodine from a thyroid hormone.
  3. Translation of a peptide prohormone.
  4. Binding of JH to Met.
Answer and explanation

A. Peripheral hydroxylation of an ecdysteroid precursor.

The conversion adds a hydroxyl group to ecdysone. Deiodination concerns thyroid hormones, translation produces proteins, and Met mediates a separate JH pathway.

7. Which receptor complex mediates canonical ecdysteroid-responsive transcription?

  1. TR–RXR in amphibian cells.
  2. Met–Taiman alone.
  3. EcR–Ultraspiracle.
  4. A membrane acetylcholine receptor.
Answer and explanation

C. EcR–Ultraspiracle.

EcR and USP are the insect nuclear receptor complex for ecdysteroid signaling. TR–RXR is thyroid-related, Met-associated signaling is JH-related, and acetylcholine receptors mediate a different process.

8. What is a major developmental role of Kr-h1 in many insects?

  1. Maintain juvenile identity and restrain premature adult differentiation.
  2. Directly convert ecdysone into 20E.
  3. Activate adult identity independently of JH.
  4. Function as the ligand-binding JH receptor.
Answer and explanation

A. Maintain juvenile identity and restrain premature adult differentiation.

Kr-h1 is a JH-induced transcriptional regulator that restrains premature adult differentiation; E93 promotes adult identity, Met is a JH receptor, and hydroxylating enzymes convert ecdysone into 20E.

9. Which factor is particularly associated with pupal specification in holometabolous models?

  1. E93 as the principal pupal identity factor.
  2. Met as the pupal-specific ecdysone receptor.
  3. Kr-h1 as an exclusive pupal differentiation signal.
  4. Broad-Complex transcription factors.
Answer and explanation

D. Broad-Complex transcription factors.

Broad contributes to pupal identity in holometabolous models; E93 is associated with adult differentiation, Met responds to JH rather than ecdysone, and Kr-h1 supports juvenile identity rather than being an exclusive pupal factor.

10. Which statement about imaginal discs is correct?

  1. They appear only after all larval tissues have dissolved.
  2. They arise earlier in development, proliferate in larvae and contribute to adult structures.
  3. They produce every adult tissue, including all remodeled larval neurons.
  4. They are circulating endocrine hormones.
Answer and explanation

B. They arise earlier in development, proliferate in larvae and contribute to adult structures.

Imaginal primordia grow and pattern before metamorphosis. Some adult tissues have other origins, including histoblasts and retained or remodeled cells; discs are organized tissues rather than hormones.

11. Which stage typically includes the major regression of a frog tadpole's tail?

  1. Early cleavage.
  2. Fertilization.
  3. Metamorphic climax.
  4. Formation of the first blastula.
Answer and explanation

C. Metamorphic climax.

Tail regression is a postembryonic metamorphic event. Cleavage, fertilization and blastula formation occur before larval life.

12. Which source–product relationship is correct for a typical amphibian?

  1. Pituitary — TSH.
  2. Thyroid gland — TSH.
  3. Interrenal tissue — T3 as its principal hormone.
  4. Pituitary — CRH as the upstream hypothalamic signal.
Answer and explanation

A. Pituitary — TSH.

Pituitary TSH stimulates thyroid hormone production. The thyroid produces thyroid hormones, interrenal tissue produces corticosteroids, and hypothalamic CRH can regulate pituitary activity.

13. What is a major function of type II deiodinase?

  1. Convert T4 into T3.
  2. Inactivate T3 by inner-ring deiodination.
  3. Convert T4 predominantly into reverse T3.
  4. Bind T3 at a nuclear response element.
Answer and explanation

A. Convert T4 into T3.

D2 generates active T3 from T4; D3 contributes to T3 inactivation and reverse T3 formation. A nuclear thyroid hormone receptor, not D2, mediates DNA-associated transcriptional responses.

14. Which statement about unliganded thyroid hormone receptors is most accurate?

  1. They are always absent from target-cell nuclei.
  2. They necessarily activate every thyroid-responsive gene.
  3. They can repress certain responsive genes through corepressor recruitment.
  4. Their only role is producing circulating T4.
Answer and explanation

C. They can repress certain responsive genes through corepressor recruitment.

Unliganded receptors can have regulatory activity at DNA, including repression. They are not merely absent or constitutively activating, nor are they thyroid synthetic enzymes.

15. What is paedomorphosis in the axolotl example?

  1. Pupal formation in a salamander.
  2. A new genome appearing during metamorphosis.
  3. Sexual maturity with retention of larval-like somatic characteristics.
  4. The universal loss of all thyroid receptors.
Answer and explanation

C. Sexual maturity with retention of larval-like somatic characteristics.

Axolotls can reproduce while retaining external gills and other larval traits. Salamanders do not have insect pupae, and the example does not imply genomic replacement or universal receptor loss.

16. Which definition describes critical weight in a standard insect experiment?

  1. The minimum mass sufficient for eventual survival after starvation.
  2. The approximate size above which food removal no longer delays metamorphic progression relative to fed controls.
  3. The maximum mass attained before feeding ceases.
  4. The mass at the beginning of the final larval instar, regardless of starvation response.
Answer and explanation

B. The approximate size above which food removal no longer delays metamorphic progression relative to fed controls.

Critical weight is operationally defined through the timing response to starvation. Minimum viable weight is a survival endpoint, maximum mass is a growth endpoint, and instar onset does not itself establish nutritional independence.

17. Which environmental variable measures astronomical day length, independently of short-term weather?

  1. Daily mean temperature.
  2. Photoperiod.
  3. Food availability.
  4. Population density.
Answer and explanation

B. Photoperiod.

Photoperiod is the astronomical duration of light per day. Temperature, food and density may also affect seasonal development but do not directly measure day length.

18. Which description best distinguishes diapause from simple quiescence?

  1. Diapause is a regulated developmental state that may persist after the initiating condition changes.
  2. Diapause is every short-term slowing of enzyme activity in cold.
  3. Diapause is always an adult stage.
  4. Quiescence can only occur in an embryo.
Answer and explanation

A. Diapause is a regulated developmental state that may persist after the initiating condition changes.

Diapause includes programmed regulation and often preparatory responses. Immediate condition-dependent slowing may be quiescence; either state can occur in multiple life stages.

19. Which is an example of developmental plasticity?

  1. A chromosome breaks and permanently changes DNA sequence.
  2. A genotype develops at different rates under two nutritional conditions.
  3. An allele segregates during meiosis without an environmental comparison.
  4. A stage is renamed without any biological change.
Answer and explanation

B. A genotype develops at different rates under two nutritional conditions.

Plasticity describes environmentally dependent phenotypes of a genotype. A sequence alteration is mutation; segregation and renaming alone do not demonstrate plasticity.

20. Which outcome demonstrates that metamorphosis is not synonymous with growth?

  1. Larval mass increases while larval organization remains unchanged.
  2. A larva reaches a higher feeding rate without a stage transition.
  3. Tail mass decreases while adult limb function develops.
  4. Allometric changes occur within a growing larval instar.
Answer and explanation

C. Tail mass decreases while adult limb function develops.

Tail resorption and limb differentiation demonstrate major developmental transformation despite local mass loss. The alternatives describe growth or feeding changes without establishing a metamorphic transition.

  1. Supply an appropriate active ecdysteroid at the relevant stage and score both molt and identity.
  2. Supply TSH and measure adult wing length only.

  1. A has an upstream ligand defect; B may have defective JH response machinery.

  3. B necessarily has greater JH synthesis.
  4. A must lack functional Met entirely.

A. A has an upstream ligand defect; B may have defective JH response machinery.

A responds to replacement ligand, consistent with a source defect. B's failure suggests receptor or downstream defects, not necessarily increased synthesis. Complete absence of functional Met would be inconsistent with ordinary Met-dependent rescue in A.

The timing dependence is consistent with a changing developmental state. Uptake should be measured rather than assumed absent, and neither hormone conversion nor complete receptor loss follows from the result.

Lineage tracing measures normal descendants, not unlimited potency. The other claims confuse cell tracking with hormone measurement or contradict the marked starting tissue.

28. Animals in group X lack endogenous thyroid output; group Y has a tissue-specific nonresponsive TR. Both show delayed tissue remodeling. What result distinguishes them?

  1. T3 necessarily restores both groups fully.
  2. JH restores thyroid receptor function in Y.

D. Appropriate T3 restores receptor-dependent responses in X more readily than in Y.

29. Two Xenopus tissues receive equal T4. Tissue A has active D2, tissue B high D3. Which prediction is most reasonable, all else equal?

  2. B must synthesize more TSH.

Answer and explanation

D. A is likely to have greater active local T3 signaling than B.

D2 activates T4 and D3 reduces active thyroid hormone availability. Systemic equality does not imply local equality, and neither enzyme is the source of TSH.

30. Why may a dominant-negative TR produce a different phenotype from a TR deletion?

  2. Deletion creates unlimited circulating T3 by definition.
  3. A dominant-negative TR is an insect juvenile hormone.
  4. The mutant receptor may remain on DNA and repress targets, whereas deletion can remove repression.
Answer and explanation

D. The mutant receptor may remain on DNA and repress targets, whereas deletion can remove repression.

A retained nonresponsive receptor can interfere with regulation. Loss of the protein can release repression at certain genes. Ligand concentration and insect JH do not follow from either mutation.

31. An isolated tail explant regresses with T3 but not vehicle. What is the strongest inference?

  1. Every individual cell responds directly and independently.
  2. T3 is sufficient for all aspects of whole-animal metamorphosis.
  3. The intact animal never uses pituitary hormones.
  4. The explant contains machinery sufficient for a local response without an intact central endocrine axis.
Answer and explanation

D. The explant contains machinery sufficient for a local response without an intact central endocrine axis.

An organ response can occur locally; intercellular signals within it may still be required. The experiment does not test every whole-animal transformation or exclude systemic regulation in vivo.

32. A perturbation eliminates larval intestinal epithelium normally but prevents adult epithelial expansion. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Normal disappearance of larval cells proves successful complete remodeling.
  2. Removal of old cells and construction of a functional replacement are separable requirements.
  3. The intestine is equivalent to a wholly discarded tail.
  4. Only systemic hormone concentration can be abnormal.
Answer and explanation

B. Removal of old cells and construction of a functional replacement are separable requirements.

The adult organ requires replacement cells and organization. Old-cell disappearance alone is insufficient, and a specific downstream or compartmental defect is possible.

33. A tadpole has adult-like limbs but persists with a larval tail. What does this phenotype show?

  1. Every tissue has completed metamorphosis.
  2. Different organ-specific developmental responses can be uncoupled.
  3. The animal must have lost all developmental pattern information.
  4. Its age alone proves reproductive maturity.
Answer and explanation

B. Different organ-specific developmental responses can be uncoupled.

The discordant structures reveal separable timing and response programs. Limb differentiation alone does not establish complete metamorphosis or reproductive maturity.

34. A cohort is starved before the critical-weight checkpoint and another afterward. Both show reduced mass gain, but only the early group has a large delay. What is the best inference?

  1. Starvation improves growth in both groups.
  2. The checkpoint is identical to the largest attainable adult size.
  3. The later group has necessarily acquired a DNA mutation.
  4. Nutritional control of developmental timing changes across the checkpoint.
Answer and explanation

D. Nutritional control of developmental timing changes across the checkpoint.

The timing response changes with developmental state even when growth is reduced in both groups. Neither greater growth, maximum mass nor mutation is demonstrated.

35. Fed control larvae reach pupariation at 48 h. Starved larvae reach it at 75 h when starved at 1.2 mg, and at 49 h when starved at 2.1 mg. What is supported?

  1. Critical weight is exactly 1.2 mg.
  2. Starvation universally accelerates pupariation.
  3. The operational critical-weight transition lies somewhere between these tested masses under these conditions, subject to replication.
  4. Every larva at 2.1 mg survives to adulthood.
Answer and explanation

C. The operational critical-weight transition lies somewhere between these tested masses under these conditions, subject to replication.

The earlier mass shows a 27-hour delay and the later only 1 hour, so a transition is bracketed rather than exactly located. Survival and generality require additional data.

36. Control larvae begin at 15 mg and gain 6 mg/day for 5 days. Treated larvae gain 4 mg/day for 9 days. What are their end-of-feeding masses under constant net gain?

  1. 30 mg and 36 mg.
  2. 45 mg and 36 mg.
  3. 75 mg and 81 mg.
  4. 45 mg and 51 mg.
Answer and explanation

D. 45 mg and 51 mg.

Add starting mass to rate × duration: 15+30=45, 15+36=51. The 30/36 mg pair omits starting mass; 45/36 mg includes it only for the control; 75/81 mg overcounts gain.

37. For a hypothetical insect with thermal sum 120 degree-days and threshold 12 °C, how long is development at constant 27 °C in the valid linear range?

  1. 4.44 days.
  2. 8 days.
  3. 15 days.
  4. 120 days.
Answer and explanation

B. 8 days.

Duration is 120/(27−12)=8 days. Dividing by absolute temperature gives 4.44, 15 is the temperature excess rather than time, and 120 is the thermal sum rather than duration.

38. A treatment changes completion time from 20 to 15 days. What happens to average developmental rate?

  1. It decreases by 25%.
  2. It increases by exactly 25%.
  3. It does not change.
  4. It increases by about 33.3%.
Answer and explanation

D. It increases by about 33.3%.

Rate is reciprocal duration: (1/15)/(1/20)=4/3. Duration decreases 25%, but rate increases 33.3%; the other choices confuse these quantities.

39. A receptor has Kd = 3 nM. Free ligand rises from 3 to 9 nM, with receptor abundance fixed. What happens to occupancy in the simple model?

  1. It rises from 0.50 to 0.75.
  2. It rises from 0.33 to 1.00.
  3. It rises from 0.25 to 0.50.
  4. It remains 0.50 because Kd is unchanged.
Answer and explanation

A. It rises from 0.50 to 0.75.

Using L/(Kd+L), the values are 3/6 and 9/12. Affinity can remain unchanged while ligand concentration and occupancy change. The other values do not follow the binding equation.

40. A 2 × 2 experiment gives response scores 4 (control), 7 (TH), 6 (corticosterone) and 12 (both). What is the additive interaction contrast?

  1. +5 units.
  2. −1 unit.
  3. +3 units.
  4. +12 units.
Answer and explanation

C. +3 units.

Interaction = 12−7−6+4=3. The additive expectation is 9, not 7, and 12 is the combined response rather than the contrast. Statistical significance still needs replicates.

41. Declining-water tadpoles and controls are sampled on day 12, but treated animals are at a later stage and have more TH. Which addition best addresses stage confounding?

  1. Exclude all controls and sample only treated animals.
  2. Assume day-matching removes every biological difference.
  3. Measure only the final adult mass.
  4. Add stage-matched comparisons and earlier time points before morphological divergence.
Answer and explanation

D. Add stage-matched comparisons and earlier time points before morphological divergence.

Stage and chronological time need separate comparisons. Earlier measurements can establish whether endocrine changes precede visible divergence. Removing controls, ignoring stage or recording final mass alone cannot resolve confounding.

42. A low-water experiment uses one tank per treatment and 40 tadpoles in each. What is the main inferential problem?

  1. Eighty tadpoles guarantee 80 independent environmental assignments.
  2. A tank cannot be an experimental unit.
  3. Treatment is confounded with tank; there is no independent tank-level replication.
  4. Water availability cannot influence development.
Answer and explanation

C. Treatment is confounded with tank; there is no independent tank-level replication.

The tank receives the treatment and shares environmental conditions. Animals within it are subsamples, not independent tank assignments. The design cannot reliably separate treatment and tank-specific effects.

43. Genotype A metamorphoses in 30 days in stable water and 20 in declining water. Genotype B takes 25 and 23 days respectively. What pattern is present?

  1. Identical environmental effects in the two genotypes.
  2. B accelerates by 10 days.
  3. A larger environmental acceleration in A, consistent with a genotype-by-environment interaction.
  4. Only genetic main effects are possible.
Answer and explanation

C. A larger environmental acceleration in A, consistent with a genotype-by-environment interaction.

A changes by −10 days; B changes by −2 days. The difference in environmental response is 8 days. Replicated common-environment data are required to support an inherited interaction.

44. Short-day insects enter an arrest and remain arrested briefly after transfer to favorable light and temperature. Which additional evidence would support diapause over simple quiescence?

  1. Immediate recovery in every animal after warming.
  2. An irreversible random injury in all animals.
  3. Proof that all arrested individuals are pupae.
  4. A stage-specific induction window and a reproducible regulated termination sequence.
Answer and explanation

D. A stage-specific induction window and a reproducible regulated termination sequence.

Programmed induction and termination support diapause. Immediate recovery is more compatible with quiescence, irreversible injury suggests damage, and diapause is not confined to pupae.

45. An insect growth regulator causes normal feeding larvae but severely reduces viable adult emergence. What is the best next measurement?

  1. Only immediate larval mortality, with no stage observations.
  2. Only the adult sex ratio, irrespective of survival.
  3. Stage-specific hormone-responsive markers, molt outcomes and survival through each transition.
  4. Assume a specific receptor is affected because every developmental toxicant has the same target.
Answer and explanation

C. Stage-specific hormone-responsive markers, molt outcomes and survival through each transition.

Developmental disruption can emerge later than exposure. Multiple readouts can separate endocrine signaling, physical molting and survival. Neither immediate death nor sex ratio alone identifies the mechanism.

Advanced integrated practice

Original practice questions; not previous-year questions. Select the best answer, then open the explanation.

46. Assertion–reason: A: A tadpole can lose tail tissue while developing a functional limb. R: Different tissues can interpret a common thyroid signal through different regulatory states. Choose the correct relationship.

  1. Both are true, but R is unrelated to A.
  2. Both are true, and R explains A.
  3. A is true and R is false.
  4. A is false and R is true.
Answer and explanation

B. Both are true, and R explains A.

Growth and regression can coexist because tissue-specific responses differ. R directly explains the observed contrast, so the alternatives deny either the relation or one of the established facts.

47. Multiple statements: I. JH is a sesquiterpenoid. II. EcR–USP mediates canonical ecdysteroid signaling. III. All larval tissues become a homogeneous liquid during pupation. IV. T4 can be activated locally by D2. Which statements are correct?

  1. I and III only.
  2. I, II and IV.
  3. II and III only.
  4. III and IV only.
Answer and explanation

B. I, II and IV.

I, II and IV describe distinct endocrine or molecular facts. III incorrectly denies tissue continuity, so every option including III is incorrect.

48. Matching: 1. Met; 2. EcR; 3. TR; 4. D3. Match to a. JH response; b. ecdysteroid response; c. thyroid hormone transcriptional response; d. thyroid hormone inactivation.

  1. 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d.
  2. 1-b, 2-a, 3-d, 4-c.
  3. 1-c, 2-d, 3-a, 4-b.
  4. 1-d, 2-c, 3-b, 4-a.
Answer and explanation

A. 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d.

Met mediates JH, EcR ecdysteroids, TR thyroid-responsive transcription, and D3 inactivation. The other mappings swap receptors or confuse enzymatic metabolism with receptor activity.

49. Interpret Figure 17: at a free ligand concentration of four times Kd, what conclusion is warranted?

  1. The modeled receptor occupancy is 0.8; the developmental output is not specified.
  2. Exactly 80% of tadpoles metamorphose.
  3. The receptor affinity is four times greater than before.
  4. Every gene is transcribed at 80% of its maximum.
Answer and explanation

A. The modeled receptor occupancy is 0.8; the developmental output is not specified.

4/(1+4)=0.8. This describes equilibrium occupancy, not the fraction of animals transforming, altered intrinsic affinity or a universal transcriptional response.

50. Integrated scenario: identical-genotype larvae reared on low food emerge smaller, with normal organ arrangement; a receptor mutant on adequate food emerges with abnormal cuticle identity. Which distinction is justified?

  1. Both must be DNA mutations caused by food.
  2. The first result is consistent with developmental plasticity; the second involves a genetic signaling perturbation.
  3. Both are automatically adaptive.
  4. Neither involves developmental regulation.
Answer and explanation

B. The first result is consistent with developmental plasticity; the second involves a genetic signaling perturbation.

The environmental effect occurs within a genotype and preserves organization, whereas a specified receptor mutation changes signaling. Similar size or timing effects cannot establish identical causes, and adaptation requires fitness evidence.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All postembryonic development is metamorphosis

Correction: Postembryonic growth and maturation can occur without a distinct larval transformation. Why the error occurs: The terms describe overlapping, not identical, intervals. Examination cue: Classify a chick and a tadpole by their young body forms.

2. Every molt changes stage identity

Correction: A juvenile-to-juvenile molt replaces cuticle while preserving the life-history form. Why the error occurs: Ecdysis is a conspicuous event and can be confused with metamorphosis. Examination cue: Separate physical molt from identity after the molt.

3. A pupa is metabolically inactive

Correction: Differentiation, metabolism and tissue remodeling continue even when feeding stops. Why the error occurs: External immobility is mistaken for cellular inactivity. Examination cue: Predict effects of inadequate larval reserves.

4. All insect larvae are nymphs

Correction: Nymph describes the juvenile stage in typical hemimetabolous development; holometabolous larvae precede a pupa. Why the error occurs: Both are immature insects. Examination cue: Identify the stage sequence rather than rely on size.

5. Ecdysone and JH are identical

Correction: Ecdysteroids and juvenile hormones are chemically and mechanistically distinct. Why the error occurs: Both affect metamorphic timing. Examination cue: Match hormone to source and receptor.

6. A single universal JH concentration code determines every stage

Correction: The outcome depends on developmental state, species, tissue and steroid timing. Why the error occurs: Simplified high/low/absent diagrams are read as universal laws. Examination cue: Compare a Manduca model with Drosophila imaginal tissue.

7. Every adult tissue derives from an imaginal disc

Correction: Histoblasts and retained or remodeled tissues also contribute. Why the error occurs: The wing-disc example is overgeneralized. Examination cue: Interpret fate tracing without assuming an exclusive source.

8. The entire larva dissolves

Correction: Selective cell removal, tissue persistence and precursor differentiation coexist. Why the error occurs: Extensive remodeling is equated with total destruction. Examination cue: Trace a surviving neural lineage.

9. T3 and T4 are interchangeable at equal concentration

Correction: Local conversion, inactivation and receptor potency differ. Why the error occurs: Both are thyroid hormones. Examination cue: Predict a D2 or D3 perturbation.

10. TR deletion and dominant-negative TR always have the same effect

Correction: Unliganded receptors can repress, so retained nonresponsive receptors and deletion differ. Why the error occurs: Both are called loss of function loosely. Examination cue: Compare early gene expression and later tissue loss.

11. A single hormone dictates an identical tissue response

Correction: Competence and tissue-specific downstream programs determine the outcome. Why the error occurs: Circulation is confused with uniform response. Examination cue: Explain simultaneous limb growth and tail regression.

12. Critical weight equals minimum viable weight

Correction: Timing insensitivity and capacity for survival are separate operational endpoints. Why the error occurs: Both are measured in mass units. Examination cue: Score survival separately from time to pupariation.

13. Warmer is always faster

Correction: Moderate-range rate relationships fail near physiological limits. Why the error occurs: Linear thermal models are extrapolated beyond their fitted range. Examination cue: Check the range before using degree-days.

14. Diapause means any inactive pupa

Correction: Diapause is a regulated arrest and can occur in different life stages. Why the error occurs: Many familiar diapausing insects are pupae. Examination cue: Distinguish programmed induction from immediate quiescence.

15. Plasticity is a DNA mutation

Correction: One genotype can express different phenotypes under different environments. Why the error occurs: Persistent changes are mistaken for sequence changes. Examination cue: Interpret a common-genotype environment comparison.

16. Earlier metamorphosis necessarily improves fitness

Correction: Its potential benefits and costs depend on the environment and later performance. Why the error occurs: Adaptive-looking timing is mistaken for demonstrated fitness. Examination cue: Compare survival in larval and juvenile habitats.

17. Same chronological age means same developmental stage

Correction: Temperature and endocrine timing can change the stage attained at a given age. Why the error occurs: Time and developmental progress often correlate. Examination cue: Use both age-matched and stage-matched comparisons.

18. A faster surviving subset proves whole-population acceleration

Correction: Selective mortality can remove the slowest or most affected individuals. Why the error occurs: Only completers enter a reported average. Examination cue: Inspect completion fractions and mortality.

19. The combined effect of two hormones proves synergy

Correction: Compare the joint response with a defined additive expectation and uncertainty. Why the error occurs: A larger combined response alone appears persuasive. Examination cue: Calculate the interaction contrast.

20. A large sample within one tank guarantees independent replicates

Correction: Treatment assignment and shared environment determine the experimental unit. Why the error occurs: Individual counts are confused with independent environmental assignment. Examination cue: Recognize pseudoreplication in tank experiments.

Revision and mastery

Postembryonic morphogenesis coordinates developmental stage, local tissue competence and environmental information. The insect steroid system promotes molts and differentiation; JH contributes to maintenance of juvenile identity in a species- and stage-dependent manner. Amphibian thyroid hormone coordinates growth of new structures, removal of larval structures and reconstruction of existing organs. Local ligand metabolism, receptor expression and downstream cell programs explain tissue-specific outcomes. Nutritional checkpoints, temperature, photoperiod, density and stress can alter developmental timing without necessarily changing inherited DNA. Evaluate experimental evidence by separating chronological age, stage, mass, viability, hormone production and target response.

Essential quantitative relationships

  • Constant net larval mass gain: M = M0 + gt.
  • Developmental rate: r = 1/t; linear thermal model: r = a(T − T0); thermal sum = 1/a.
  • Single-site equilibrium receptor occupancy: θ = L/(Kd + L).
  • Factorial interaction: Y11 − Y10 − Y01 + Y00.
  • Reaction norms compare phenotypes across environments; a nonparallel response may indicate genotype-by-environment interaction.
