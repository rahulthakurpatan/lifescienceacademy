1. Why an RNA transcript needs splicing

Imagine a cell that must manufacture a protein whose amino acid sequence is already specified in its chromosome. Transcription by RNA polymerase II can copy the relevant region into RNA, but copying is not necessarily the last operation needed to produce a usable message. In many interrupted eukaryotic genes, the nucleotide sequences that will be adjacent in the finished messenger RNA are separated from one another in the chromosome. Transcription copies intervening sequences as well as the sequences ultimately retained. A second molecular operation must recognize the correct boundaries, remove particular RNA segments and join the retained pieces in the correct order. This operation is RNA splicing. For most introns in nuclear protein-coding genes, it is performed by a ribonucleoprotein machine called the spliceosome.

Splicing is therefore an operation on the RNA copy of genetic information. It does not ordinarily excise DNA from the chromosome or change the inherited sequence of the gene. The same DNA can be transcribed again, and the next RNA molecule can undergo a similar or different processing decision. This distinction between a permanent change to the genome and a choice made during RNA processing underlies both the classical experiments on interrupted genes and many examination questions about tissue-specific expression. A chromosome may remain completely unchanged while its corresponding messenger RNAs differ in length, composition and capacity to encode a protein.

The term precursor messenger RNA, or pre-mRNA, refers to an RNA that still requires maturation before it can serve as the appropriate messenger. In a typical animal cell, RNA polymerase II produces the precursor, a cap is attached to its emerging 5′ end, introns are removed, and the 3′ end is generated through cleavage and, for many transcripts, polyadenylation. These operations can overlap in time. An RNA molecule need not remain untouched until the polymerase reaches the end of the entire gene. Splice-site recognition may begin while later portions of the precursor are still being synthesized. The earlier chapters on transcription and end processing provide the context; this chapter concentrates on the removal and joining of internal RNA regions.

The distinction between exon and intron describes the fate of a segment in a particular RNA processing pathway. In the usual description of a protein-coding gene, exons are the segments retained in the mature transcript and introns are the intervening segments excised during splicing. Exons do not necessarily consist entirely of protein-coding information. The portion of an exon preceding the translation initiation codon can be part of the 5′ untranslated region, and the portion beyond the termination codon can be part of the 3′ untranslated region. A whole exon may be untranslated. Conversely, an intron can contain sequences important for the regulation of its own removal or the choice of neighboring exons. The statement that an intron is removed is not equivalent to the claim that it has no biological function.

Consider a gene whose transcribed region contains three exons separated by two introns. For a particular fully spliced product, the polymerase copies exon 1, intron 1, exon 2, intron 2 and exon 3. If all three exons are retained, the mature RNA contains exon 1 immediately next to exon 2, followed immediately by exon 3. The junction between exons 1 and 2 is a new adjacency in the RNA, although the sequence of each exon itself need not have changed. The transcript still has a 5′ to 3′ polarity. Splicing neither reverses the exons nor randomly shuffles their order. Ordinary cis-splicing connects regions of the same precursor in their existing orientation. A distinct phenomenon called trans-splicing joins regions derived from different RNA molecules and is not the general rule for mammalian nuclear pre-mRNAs.

To appreciate the demand for precision, imagine that the first exon ends with the first nucleotide of a codon. Its partner exon can begin with the remaining two nucleotides. After ligation the ribosome reads a complete codon across the exon junction. A shift of even one nucleotide at either splice boundary can change the grouping of all subsequent nucleotides into codons. Depending on the location, the resulting protein may contain a different amino acid sequence and terminate prematurely. Nevertheless, not every splicing error creates a frameshift, and not every change in the reading frame is equally consequential. The location of the affected segment, whether it lies in the coding region, its length and the position of the new stop codon all matter.

We must also distinguish the introns removed from conventional nuclear pre-mRNA from intervening sequences found in other biological settings. Some ribosomal or organellar RNA introns can splice through catalytic RNA structures; certain transfer RNA introns are removed by a different enzymatic pathway. Bacterial transcripts generally do not require the nuclear spliceosome, although particular bacteria and their mobile genetic elements can contain self-splicing introns. An organism that lacks the conventional spliceosomal apparatus is therefore not necessarily incapable of any RNA splicing. Mechanism rather than the word intron alone determines which processing pathway is involved.

For an examination, a useful first task is to identify the molecular stage at which a proposed event occurs. If a change is detected in genomic DNA, transcription and subsequent processing may both transmit its consequences. If the DNA remains unchanged but mature RNAs differ in their exon junctions, a processing choice becomes a plausible explanation. If RNAs have identical junctions yet differ in individual nucleotides, RNA editing may instead be relevant. If proteins differ despite apparently identical messenger RNA sequences, the explanation may lie at translation or post-translational modification. These are distinct possibilities, and experiments must distinguish them rather than simply assign every difference in gene output to splicing.

2. How interrupted genes were discovered

Before the discovery of split genes, much molecular reasoning about gene expression had developed from bacteria and their viruses. For many familiar examples, the DNA region specifying a protein corresponded continuously to the RNA message. That correspondence provided a reasonable starting hypothesis for eukaryotic genes as well. A mature transcript was expected to align continuously with the DNA region that encoded it. The discovery that this alignment was interrupted was important precisely because it challenged a straightforward prediction of the earlier view rather than merely supplying an additional detail about RNA chemistry.

Adenovirus offered a productive experimental system because its genome is relatively compact, its RNA products can be studied in infected cells, and its DNA can be mapped. In 1977, Susan Berget, Claire Moore and Phillip Sharp examined the relationship between adenovirus type 2 late messenger RNA and its DNA template. A parallel line of work associated with Richard Roberts and colleagues also established the discontinuous nature of adenovirus messenger RNA sequences. The observations became central to the discovery of split genes, for which Sharp and Roberts received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The scientific point is more important than memorizing a pair of names: sequence correspondence between the mature RNA and its genomic template can directly test a hypothesis about how an RNA was formed.

In a DNA–RNA hybridization experiment, complementary sequences in the two molecules can form a hybrid double helix. If a mature RNA contains a continuous copy of one uninterrupted genomic segment, its complementary DNA strand can pair over the corresponding continuous region. If instead the mature RNA contains two adjacent segments whose corresponding DNA regions are separated by an additional sequence, the DNA lying between those regions has no partner in that mature RNA. Under conditions allowing the relevant strands to hybridize, the unpaired region can form a loop. Electron microscopy can reveal the resulting architecture. The exact appearance depends on the DNA preparation and hybridization arrangement, so a student should not interpret every loop in every nucleic acid preparation as proof of splicing.

In the adenovirus work, the position of sequences complementary to the late hexon messenger RNA was not consistent with a single uninterrupted genomic stretch. Parts of the RNA near its 5′ end corresponded to separate regions upstream of the main coding portion. A model in which a longer nuclear transcript was processed to join distant segments could account for this pattern. The paper by Berget, Moore and Sharp reports that the leader segments mapped to multiple upstream locations. Their interpretation explains why the final RNA contains sequence neighbors that are not immediate neighbors in the chromosome. The structural evidence made it necessary to rethink how the primary transcription product becomes a mature message.

It is useful to reconstruct the reasoning as a hypothetical experiment. Suppose a DNA fragment contains region A, a 900-nucleotide intervening region and region B. A purified RNA contains sequences complementary to A and B but lacks the intervening region. The hybridization model predicts that the DNA corresponding to the 900 nucleotides cannot participate in the same RNA–DNA duplex and will be displaced from it. If the same DNA is hybridized with an unprocessed precursor that includes the intervening region, a larger extent of continuous pairing becomes possible. Comparing the two preparations helps distinguish a genuine RNA composition difference from an artifact caused by one particular DNA structure. Appropriate controls include known uninterrupted RNA–DNA pairs, verification of the RNA and DNA identities, and attention to partial degradation or incomplete hybridization.

Hybridization alone establishes a relationship between sequences; it does not directly identify the chemical bond that was broken during maturation, the enzyme that performed the reaction or the order in which the exons were joined. Those are mechanistic questions that require additional evidence. For instance, mapping the ends of the mature RNA could establish the precision of the junctions, whereas detecting a longer precursor and shorter product in a time-dependent assay could support a precursor–product relationship. Isolating a reaction intermediate could disclose the topology of the excised intron. Each experiment addresses a different link in the explanation. A strong answer separates the observation of discontinuous sequence correspondence from the later discovery of the detailed spliceosomal mechanism.

Modern sequence-based approaches make the same logical distinction easier to see. Complementary DNA synthesized from mature RNA can be compared with the genomic sequence. If a single cDNA molecule contains an exon–exon junction that is absent from the continuous genomic sequence, the intervening genomic region is a candidate intron. Sequencing a precursor containing both boundaries strengthens the interpretation that the mature junction arose during processing. A genomic DNA contamination control matters in amplification experiments because genomic DNA naturally contains the intervening region. A no-reverse-transcriptase control can reveal whether DNA contamination is contributing to what is supposed to be a measurement of RNA. The method does not require the examiner to accept that every computationally detected junction is genuine; independent validation can remain important.

The discovery of interrupted genes changed the relationship between genes and gene products. A gene could now be understood as a source of a precursor that is interpreted by an additional molecular apparatus. The apparatus must recognize the right sites even when many superficially similar sequences occur elsewhere in the transcript. It also creates an opportunity for regulated variation, because different subsets of a precursor's segments may be retained. To explain either fidelity or diversity, we first need to identify the information in the precursor that makes a boundary recognizable.

Historical evidence: Berget, Moore and Sharp (1977); Nobel Prize historical context.

3. Reading the signals at an intron boundary

An intron cannot be recognized simply by measuring its length or by identifying a stretch that fails to encode a protein. Lengths vary greatly, and a retained exon need not be translated at all. The splicing machinery must instead use molecular signals, meaning particular sequence and structural features that interact with recognition factors. For a typical major-spliceosome intron in a mammalian precursor, the principal signals comprise the 5′ splice site, the branch-point region, a pyrimidine-rich tract near the 3′ end and the 3′ splice site. Their organization provides information about the direction, approximate location and chemical readiness of the sites that will participate in splicing.

The 5′ splice site is the boundary at the beginning of an intron when the RNA is read from 5′ to 3′. It is often called the donor site. In many major-class introns, the first two intronic nucleotides are GU. The 3′ splice site is the boundary at the end of that intron, often called the acceptor site. The final two intronic nucleotides are usually AG. A sequence diagram conventionally written as exon 1 | GU … AG | exon 2 places both GU and AG on the intron side of their respective boundaries. Neither is retained merely because it marks a splice site. The phosphodiester bonds at the two vertical bars, rather than the bonds between G and U or between A and G, are the ones replaced during the standard reaction.

Recognition extends beyond the two terminal nucleotides. One familiar representation of a mammalian major-class 5′ site is MAG|GURAGU, where M means A or C and R means A or G. This is a consensus pattern, not an obligatory exact sequence. The nucleotides in the neighboring exon and the following intronic region contribute to recognition; the importance of an individual base depends on its position and the surrounding interactions. At the acceptor end, a typical arrangement contains a branch-point adenosine followed downstream by a region enriched in U and C and then an AG near the exon boundary. The branch point is commonly located tens of nucleotides upstream of the acceptor in mammalian introns, but its precise distance and sequence vary. It is not correct to impose one universal number of intervening bases on all organisms or introns.

A consensus is a statistical description of sequences that tend to be favored at comparable positions. It does not guarantee that every instance of a short pattern is a functional splice site. The GU dinucleotide occurs many times in a long RNA molecule just by chance. Under a simplified model in which all four nucleotides occur independently at equal frequency, the probability of GU at a particular adjacent pair is one sixteenth. A sequence of 1,601 nucleotides contains 1,600 adjacent pairs, giving an expected count of 100 GU occurrences by linearity of expectation. The overlapping pairs need not be independent for this expected count to be valid. This calculation is illustrative rather than a model of an actual gene, because real base composition and sequence dependence vary. It nevertheless demonstrates why the first two nucleotides of the intron alone cannot locate a unique donor among many candidates.

The branch-point adenosine supplies a functional chemical group rather than merely serving as an address label. RNA nucleotides contain ribose with a hydroxyl group at the 2′ carbon. In the branch-point reaction, the 2′ oxygen of an internal adenosine acts as the attacking nucleophile. The adenosine is already part of the normal RNA backbone, so its existing 3′ and 5′ connections remain while an additional linkage forms at its 2′ oxygen. This produces a branched molecule. The ability to position an otherwise internal hydroxyl group at a distant phosphodiester bond is a central achievement of the spliceosome. Adenosine is strongly preferred in standard examples, but branch-point selection and unusual branch nucleotides should be considered in the context of the particular system rather than treated as chemically impossible exceptions.

The polypyrimidine tract contributes to recognition of the downstream region, especially in the mammalian system. Its interaction with protein factors helps coordinate the branch point and acceptor. Replacing several pyrimidines with purines can impair recognition even when the AG dinucleotide is completely unchanged. Conversely, strengthening one recognition interaction may sometimes compensate for a weak neighboring signal. This is why mutation experiments are often interpreted in pairs: changing a single site reveals a defect, and a carefully chosen second change can test whether the defect arose from a lost molecular interaction. The branch region, tract and terminal AG must be considered in their positional context, not as three unrelated words in a memorized list.

Although GU–AG describes the dominant canonical arrangement, there are genuine noncanonical terminal combinations. GC–AG introns can be processed by the major machinery, and some introns of the minor spliceosome possess AU–AC ends. However, the minority U12-type pathway also processes introns with GU–AG termini. Consequently, terminal dinucleotides do not provide a reliable classification of all introns into major or minor pathways. The wider splice-site and branch-point consensus, and the specific recognition apparatus required, distinguish the classes. These variants are particularly useful in examination statements that misuse words such as all, always or exclusively.

The labels 5′ and 3′ do not identify the two ends of the entire gene. A multi-intron transcript contains repeated pairs of 5′ and 3′ splice sites. The intron's 5′ end is downstream of its preceding exon, and its 3′ end is upstream of its following exon. On a DNA sequence printed in the opposite orientation, the apparent order of motifs may be reversed and complemented. A student should first convert the sequence to the RNA orientation used by the machinery. The next task is to understand how small nuclear RNAs and proteins interpret these distributed recognition signals and bring them into one catalytic center.

4. The components of the spliceosome

The spliceosome is unusual among cellular enzymes because it is assembled from several pre-existing RNA–protein particles and many additional proteins onto its substrate. Its components can be reused, but the active arrangement is not a rigid structure waiting to cut every intron in an identical manner. Different interactions form, disappear and are replaced as the precursor advances toward the first and second chemical reactions. This dynamic organization solves a difficult problem: the initial recognition of a splice site requires one kind of RNA–RNA pairing, whereas catalysis requires a different arrangement in which reactive groups on the precursor face one another. A system in which all components remained fixed in their initial positions would have difficulty accomplishing both functions.

The small nuclear RNAs involved in the major pathway are conventionally named U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6. The letter U reflects their historical identification as uridine-rich small nuclear RNAs, rather than indicating that every nucleotide is uridine. Each RNA acts in association with proteins, forming a small nuclear ribonucleoprotein, abbreviated snRNP. Pronunciation of this abbreviation as snurp is common, but the important distinction is structural: an snRNA is the RNA molecule, whereas an snRNP includes the RNA and its associated proteins. The spliceosome includes several snRNPs plus factors that are not permanent constituents of a single snRNP. It is consequently inaccurate to describe five naked RNAs acting independently in solution as the complete spliceosome.

U1 is an early reader of the donor site. A sequence near the 5′ end of U1 snRNA forms complementary interactions with the region surrounding the 5′ splice boundary. The pairing is antiparallel, as are the complementary strands of nucleic acid helices. If a donor region becomes less complementary to U1, the initial interaction may weaken or be lost. Proteins in and around the particle stabilize recognition and cooperate with other factors, so a simple count of complementary bases does not completely determine the fate of a splice site. The interaction nevertheless provides a particularly clear example of how a short RNA can guide a biological machine to a sequence. The guide sequence and the guided reaction site are not the same molecule: U1 is supplied in trans, whereas the site being recognized lies within the precursor.

U2 recognizes the branch region at a later stage of assembly. Its sequence pairs with portions of the precursor around the branch-point adenosine in a way that leaves the reactive nucleotide protruding from the local helix. This unpaired or bulged adenosine can present its 2′ hydroxyl group to the catalytic environment. A perfectly uninterrupted helix at this position would not necessarily be desirable. In this mechanism, correct recognition involves arranging a controlled imperfection in base pairing, not maximizing pairing everywhere. The difference helps answer a common reasoning problem: a mutation that creates more complementary bases could impair rather than improve splicing if the mutation causes the essential branch nucleotide to be sequestered in an unsuitable arrangement.

U4 and U6 initially form a paired RNA system within a U4/U6 di-snRNP. Their association masks regions of U6 that will later take part in the catalytic RNA architecture. The inactive arrangement is compatible with transport and assembly of the spliceosomal particles without premature formation of the catalytic core. During activation, remodeling separates U4 from U6, and U4 leaves the assembling complex. U6 can then form the interactions required for the active state. This change explains why the absence of U4 from an active catalytic spliceosome is not evidence that U4 has no role in splicing. A component needed to construct or control an enzyme can depart before its chemical reaction begins.

U5 has a different task. It helps hold and position exon sequences so that the appropriate termini remain aligned during catalysis. At the first reaction, the upstream exon is separated from the intron, yet it must not diffuse away or be lost before the second reaction. Interactions involving U5 and associated proteins help maintain the useful relationship between the exons and the catalytic site. A defect in exon positioning can therefore affect the ligation step even if initial splice-site recognition and the first cleavage have occurred. Because the same apparatus participates in both stages, actual mutations may disturb several steps, and the stage of failure must be inferred experimentally.

U6 contributes centrally to the active RNA structure. During activation, the donor site is transferred from its initial U1-associated environment to interactions involving U6. U6 also interacts with U2, forming a configuration that positions catalytic metal ions and aligns the substrate. U6 is not simply a replacement copy of U1: U1 primarily assists in early recognition, whereas U6 is a central part of the activated catalytic organization. A useful way of remembering the difference is to ask which RNA identifies the site initially and which RNA participates in arranging the site during chemical conversion. The answer changes with the assembly stage, even though the substrate's original nucleotide sequence remains the same.

Proteins are essential to the overall reaction. Some stabilize the small nuclear RNAs, others recognize precursor sequences, some hold the complex together, and others use nucleotide hydrolysis to drive changes in interactions. The Sm proteins associated with several spliceosomal snRNPs contribute to particle organization; U6 is instead associated with a different related set of proteins. Large scaffold proteins such as Prp8 help organize the active environment and contact components of the reaction machinery. The distinction between an RNA-based catalytic center and a protein-containing enzyme is therefore not contradictory. Both descriptions can be correct when they refer to different levels of the system. The biological machine is a ribonucleoprotein, and the chemistry at its active site is supported by a specific RNA architecture.

Additional recognition proteins help interpret the 3′ portion of the intron. In a standard mammalian model, SF1 can associate with the branch region during early assembly, and U2AF helps recognize the polypyrimidine tract and the neighboring acceptor region. The larger U2AF subunit is commonly described as interacting with the pyrimidine tract, and the smaller subunit as contributing to recognition near the AG. These names are helpful only if attached to a mechanism. Without the recognition of downstream features, a donor site could be coupled to an unsuitable acceptor farther along the RNA. Coordinating signals from both ends reduces the number of acceptable combinations. Yeast and other organisms use related machinery but differ in the relative contributions of these features and factors.

The inventory of snRNPs should be interpreted dynamically rather than as a list of obligatory simultaneous active-site residents. If one isolates an early complex, U1 may be present and the U4/U6 pair may not yet have joined. If one isolates an activated complex, U1 and U4 may already have departed. Both results are consistent with the five major snRNAs being required for the normal cycle. In an experimental question, therefore, the answer to whether a component is present depends on the assembly state being investigated. This is the conceptual preparation needed to follow the progression from an uncommitted precursor to a catalytically competent machine.

5. Assembly: from splice-site selection to an active complex

Assembly begins with the unprocessed precursor, but the precursor is not simply a straight strand displayed on a page. It folds locally and is associated with RNA-binding proteins. Sites become available as the polymerase synthesizes the RNA, and the accessibility of a potential donor or branch region can affect recognition. An exam-level mammalian model divides assembly into early recognition, stable recruitment of U2, addition of the remaining major particles and activation. These stages are often denoted E, A, B and activated B complexes, followed by catalytic and post-catalytic states. The names are useful for ordering observations, not for implying that every intron in every organism traverses exactly the same set of experimentally isolated complexes.

At the early stage, U1 recognizes the 5′ splice site, while factors near the other end help identify the branch region, pyrimidine tract and acceptor. These interactions establish a preliminary relationship between regions that can be separated by hundreds or thousands of nucleotides. In the simplest isolated intron, this preliminary relationship is easy to draw as an arc connecting its boundaries. In a real multi-exon RNA, several possible interactions may coexist or compete. Early splice-site recognition is a commitment-building process rather than the moment of covalent cleavage. A mutation that prevents formation of this early assembly is predicted to reduce the abundance of all later intermediates that depend on it.

Stable association of U2 with the branch region marks the classical A complex. Remodeling of earlier interactions permits U2 to establish its branch-site pairing, replacing the initial branch recognition arrangement. The branch adenosine is left positioned for its later chemical function. ATP-dependent events contribute to this transition, even though no covalent RNA cleavage occurs simply because ATP has been hydrolyzed. This distinction is useful when interpreting experiments in which a complex accumulates in the absence of a nucleotide energy source. If the system cannot make a necessary conformational transition, recognition may take place without progressing to the eventual product.

The U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP joins the assembling complex. This particle brings the U4–U6 paired system and U5 together with their associated proteins. At this stage, all five major snRNA species may be represented in a pre-catalytic arrangement. Yet the catalytic configuration has not been fully established. It would be chemically risky to allow an active RNA catalytic center to act before the intended substrate positions were selected. Assembly establishes the necessary contacts first; remodeling subsequently permits the reactive groups to approach in a productive geometry. In a sequence of gel-separated or purified complexes, addition of these components can be monitored without assuming that the appearance of all five RNAs is equivalent to product formation.

Activation requires major changes in RNA–RNA interactions. The initial U1–donor association is disrupted and the 5′ splice site becomes associated with the U6-containing catalytic environment. The U4–U6 interaction is dismantled, permitting U6 to form new intramolecular and intermolecular interactions, including pairing with U2. U4 departs. U5 helps position the exon ends, and proteins organize the newly formed active architecture. In this progression, recognition and catalysis are linked by an ordered handoff rather than by the permanent binding of one guide particle. An RNA helicase or related ATP-dependent remodeling factor can facilitate the exchange of pairing partners, showing how energy input helps direct a chemical system through different conformational states.

The distinction between helicase-like remodeling and chemistry is important. ATP-dependent RNA remodeling factors alter RNA–RNA or RNA–protein contacts. In many cases they do not behave like simple helicases moving along an unlimited nucleic acid substrate; instead, their activity can rearrange a local ribonucleoprotein structure. For NET-level reasoning, it is sufficient to understand that ATP hydrolysis can help release an interaction, change the relative positions of components or drive progression through a checkpoint. The exact named remodeling factor is not needed for every problem unless it is specified. If a question describes a mutation that prevents U4 release, the expected direct consequence is an activation defect, not failure to recognize the original 5′ site by U1.

An activated complex must place the first scissile phosphodiester bond near the branch-point nucleophile. This requires bringing together parts of one RNA that were previously far apart in the linear sequence. The chemical reaction depends on spatial geometry, so a site can be recognized correctly yet remain catalytically unavailable if the complex cannot arrange it. Conversely, a molecule that is capable of phosphotransfer chemistry is not necessarily an efficient splicing catalyst if it lacks the means to recognize and align the appropriate substrate. This separation of recognition and reaction provides a powerful way to interpret mutant phenotypes. Instead of asking only whether splicing decreased, one asks whether substrate binding, assembly, activation or one of the actual chemistry steps was disrupted.

After the first transesterification, the resulting complex contains two RNA products in an intermediate relationship: an upstream exon with a newly exposed 3′ hydroxyl and a branched intron still attached to the downstream exon. Remodeling helps establish the second-step arrangement. The upstream exon is brought into a productive relationship with the acceptor bond. After exon ligation, the mature exon-containing RNA must be released, the excised intron must be removed, and the snRNP components must be returned to a usable state. Splicing is a cycle in the sense that the machinery can be recycled, not in the sense that an excised intron is normally reinserted into the same messenger RNA.

A useful experiment can distinguish between failure of initial assembly and failure of catalytic activation. Suppose a researcher supplies a normal precursor to a nuclear extract under conditions that allow the spliceosome to function. Protein or RNA association assays indicate that U1 and U2 bind, and an assay for the tri-snRNP indicates that it is also recruited. The unprocessed RNA accumulates, however, with no detectable first-step intermediates. A mutation affecting an activation factor is consistent with the observations. A defect in the initial donor signal would be less consistent because normal recruitment of U1 was observed. Nevertheless, failure of the chemical first step after successful activation would also be possible. A further measurement of U4 association or of the active configuration would help discriminate between those alternatives.

Consider another case in which a normally processed transcript contains two introns and a mutation disrupts the first donor site. One should not automatically conclude that the second intron will remain unspliced. Assembly for different introns can be at least partly independent; it is possible to recover a precursor that retains the first intron but has lost the second. The processing of one intron may also influence recognition of its neighbor, especially when splice sites cooperate across an exon. The result depends on transcript architecture and assembly timing. A diagram that always removes introns in strict order from the 5′ end is an oversimplification unless the question explicitly establishes that order.

6. Splicing in a transcribing nucleus

The nuclear environment brings transcription and RNA processing into proximity. The carboxy-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II consists of repeated sequences that undergo changes in phosphorylation state during transcription. Proteins associated with RNA processing can interact, directly or indirectly, with the transcription machinery or nascent RNA. This organization makes it possible for a newly synthesized donor region to acquire recognition factors before the polymerase has completed the downstream portion of the gene. Once the branch and acceptor regions become available, additional assembly events can occur. Splicing can also be completed after transcription, so co-transcriptional processing is a common organizational principle rather than an absolute rule for every intron.

The direction of polymerase travel introduces an important constraint. The upstream portion of a precursor becomes available first. A downstream acceptor cannot be used before it has been synthesized and is accessible. Yet a long intron may require considerable time to transcribe, and a weak early site may compete with a stronger alternative after further RNA emerges. The rate of transcription can thus influence which choices become available at different times. In some contexts a slower polymerase gives additional time to recognize a weak site before a competing downstream site appears; in other contexts it has a different effect because regulatory binding, RNA folding or local sequence organization changes the competition. No universal equation relates slower elongation to increased exon inclusion in all transcripts.

In organisms with long introns and relatively short exons, recognition may initially occur across an exon. Protein contacts and RNA recognition on its upstream and downstream sides can cooperate to define it as a segment to retain. This mode is called exon definition. The precursor ultimately undergoes chemical reactions across introns, so the initial cross-exon recognition must be reconciled with productive cross-intron assembly. In organisms whose introns tend to be short and whose exons are not the convenient small units, recognition across an intron can be particularly important. This distinction explains why weakening a donor immediately downstream of an exon can sometimes lead to skipping of that exon, rather than only to retention of the downstream intron.

For example, suppose exon 2 is flanked by two long introns and requires recognition of its upstream acceptor and downstream donor to be efficiently defined. A mutation in its downstream donor reduces recruitment of U1. If cooperative exon recognition fails, the upstream exon may be joined directly to exon 3, bypassing exon 2. The same mutation in a different architecture might instead cause accumulation of a transcript retaining the downstream intron or activation of a nearby cryptic donor. All three outcomes are mechanistically possible. A prediction in an examination should take account of the information given about the available competing sites and the method used to identify the product.

Splicing therefore cannot be understood merely as the conversion of a completed string of RNA letters into a shorter string. It is a spatially and temporally organized process involving a changing substrate, multiple recognition partners and a catalytic machine assembled in stages. This framework explains why the next question, how the chemical bonds are actually exchanged, can be addressed independently from how the sites were first identified. The chemical mechanism has an invariant logic even though the upstream selection of a particular site is subject to variation.

7. The chemical foundation of the two reactions

The chemistry of an RNA backbone provides the basis for understanding both splicing reactions. Consecutive nucleotides within an ordinary RNA chain are connected through a phosphate group that forms ester bonds with two ribose sugars. One connection involves the 3′ oxygen of an upstream nucleotide, and the other involves the 5′ oxygen of the downstream nucleotide. The phosphate lies between them, and the conventional description is a 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. During splicing, two specific phosphodiester linkages are replaced. Instead of cleaving every bond within the unwanted sequence and reconstructing the exons from separate nucleotides, the machinery exchanges the oxygen attached to each target phosphate. Most bonds within the exons and intron remain untouched.

A transesterification is a reaction in which an ester linkage is exchanged for another ester linkage. In the RNA context, a suitably positioned hydroxyl-derived oxygen attacks a phosphorus center and displaces an oxygen previously attached to that phosphorus. A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor, whereas the phosphorus center is electrophilic. This description should not be mistaken for the claim that the free hydroxyl group reacts rapidly with the backbone under ordinary cellular conditions. The molecular environment must permit the reaction to occur at a useful rate, and the correct attacking oxygen, target phosphate and departing oxygen must be arranged appropriately. Catalysis improves the accessibility and chemical suitability of this transition.

A good mechanistic representation follows the identity of the atoms rather than memorizing two arrows without knowing their meaning. Suppose the RNA contains upstream exon E1, intron I and downstream exon E2. The phosphate at the E1–I junction is the first target. It joins the 3′ oxygen of the last nucleotide of E1 to the 5′ oxygen of the first nucleotide of I. The phosphate at the I–E2 junction is a different target and joins the 3′ oxygen of the last intronic nucleotide to the 5′ oxygen of the first nucleotide of E2. After both reactions, the phosphate originally present at the second junction becomes the phosphate connecting E1 to E2. The phosphate originally present at the first junction becomes part of the branch linkage inside the excised intron.

These reactions do not require the introduction of an external nucleotide triphosphate to make each new phosphodiester connection. At the level of the net chemical transformations, one ester bond is replaced by another, so the reaction differs fundamentally from nucleotide polymerization. In transcription, an existing 3′ hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphate of an incoming nucleotide triphosphate and pyrophosphate is released. In pre-mRNA splicing, the reacting phosphorus centers are already part of the precursor backbone. The spliceosome nevertheless uses ATP during its assembly, activation, quality control, rearrangements and disassembly. The distinction is between energetic expenditure by the complete catalytic cycle and the direct chemical requirements of each of its two bond exchanges.

Metal ions play an important role in phosphoryl transfer. The active architecture organizes divalent metal ions, normally magnesium in the biological context, in a way that helps position reacting groups, stabilize charges and facilitate the departure of leaving groups. The splicing reactions are consistent with a two-metal-ion catalytic framework. The fact that the active center is an RNA-based structure does not imply that free RNA in solution will automatically reproduce the entire reaction. RNA folding, ion binding and stabilization by proteins are indispensable features of the intact machine. It is also not valid to infer that magnesium only serves as a nonspecific salt: changing ion identity or the coordinating groups can have effects on the chemistry that are distinct from changes in gross ionic strength.

When considering a chemical reaction at a charged phosphate, think of the activation barrier as the energetic difficulty of reaching a reactive arrangement of atoms. Catalytic interactions can lower the effective barrier without being consumed in the final product. Energy input to remodel the enzyme can change how frequently the proper arrangement is reached, while the catalytic groups make the bond exchange itself possible. These are cooperating processes, not mutually exclusive explanations. This distinction helps resolve a common apparent paradox: a spliceosome can be an ATP-consuming machine even though its fundamental phosphoryl-transfer reactions are not driven by direct ATP cleavage at each junction.

8. First transesterification: creating the lariat intermediate

The activated spliceosome arranges the 5′ splice site and the branch-point adenosine near the catalytic center. The branch adenosine is internal to the intron and already joined to its neighboring nucleotides through the normal RNA backbone. Its 2′ hydroxyl is not the same as a 3′ hydroxyl at the end of an RNA molecule. This internal 2′ hydroxyl provides the nucleophile for the first step. Its oxygen attacks the phosphorus at the donor boundary, the phosphate connecting the upstream exon with the first nucleotide of the intron. Productive alignment brings together these two distant positions in the folded and protein-associated precursor.

As the reaction proceeds, the bond between that phosphate and the upstream exon's 3′ oxygen is replaced by a bond between the phosphate and the branch adenosine's 2′ oxygen. The upstream exon is released from its original covalent connection to the intron and acquires an available 3′ hydroxyl at its end. It remains associated with the spliceosome, because it will supply the attacking group in the next reaction. The intron's first nucleotide is now connected through its 5′ phosphate to the 2′ oxygen of the internal branch adenosine. Since the ordinary backbone between the intron's first nucleotide and the branch point remains intact, this additional connection closes a loop.

The branched RNA has the general shape of a lasso, or lariat. The loop extends from the beginning of the intron along the ordinary backbone to the branch region. The remaining downstream part of the intron forms a tail that continues into the downstream exon until the second reaction takes place. In a conventional diagram, the branch adenosine lies at the point where the new 2′–5′ linkage meets the normal 3′–5′ RNA backbone. It therefore has three backbone connections rather than the two normally encountered at an internal nucleotide. A lariat is not simply a covalently closed circular RNA. It has a branch and a tail, and the chemistry at its branch is distinct from an ordinary 3′–5′ backbone junction.

The first reaction generates two covalently separate RNA molecules, even though both can remain within one ribonucleoprotein complex. One consists of the upstream exon, or the already processed exon-containing portion of the RNA upstream of the cut. The other consists of the lariat-form intron still covalently attached to the downstream exon-containing portion. A question that asks what is released at this step must distinguish release from a covalent bond from release into solution. The exon has been separated from the intron chemically, but the spliceosome retains it for the second reaction. A question asking whether the intron has been fully excised should be answered in the negative: the 3′ junction has not yet been replaced.

It is possible to design a hypothetical precursor in which the normal branch adenosine has its 2′ hydroxyl replaced by a hydrogen atom, leaving much of the surrounding recognition sequence unchanged. If this is the only usable branch site, the first chemical reaction should be strongly impaired because the intended attacking oxygen is missing. This prediction follows from the chemical mechanism and does not depend on a memorized association between adenine and splicing. It also illustrates why an adenosine base alone is not sufficient; the ribose group is part of the functional nucleophile. If alternative branch sites are available, however, they might support residual splicing, so the absence of a particular intermediate cannot be interpreted without considering substrate design.

Replacing the donor region with a sequence that cannot be recognized is a different kind of perturbation. It may also block the appearance of the lariat intermediate, but it does so before the active arrangement has been formed. If the two mutations have identical effects on the final mature RNA concentration, a simple endpoint measurement will not distinguish their causes. Observing assembly intermediates or testing a compensatory change in a recognition RNA would provide additional evidence. This principle is common in biochemistry: a change in final product can originate from more than one step, and identification of the limiting step requires intermediate or binding measurements.

The topology of the intermediate itself provides mechanistic evidence. Branched molecules often migrate anomalously on denaturing electrophoresis relative to ordinary linear molecules of equal nucleotide count. That property can help identify a candidate lariat intermediate, but the mobility of a band alone is not an unambiguous structural identification. A branch-specific cleavage or debranching reaction and mapping of the branch junction provide stronger evidence. It is also useful to consider which exon-associated sequences are present in the branched intermediate. Detection of an intron together with the downstream exon but not the upstream exon matches the predicted result of the first reaction much more closely than an observation of a short intron-only RNA.

9. Second transesterification: precise ligation of the exons

The first reaction generates a new reactive terminus on the upstream exon. During the next stage the spliceosome rearranges its substrate and catalytic contacts so that this 3′ hydroxyl can approach the phosphate at the acceptor boundary. This is the phosphate linking the final intronic nucleotide to the first nucleotide of the downstream exon. Unlike the first step, which uses an internal 2′ hydroxyl and produces an unusual linkage, the second step uses a terminal 3′ hydroxyl and restores the familiar arrangement of the RNA backbone in the mature message.

The 3′ oxygen of the upstream exon attacks the acceptor phosphate. The bond between the phosphate and the 3′ oxygen of the intron's last nucleotide is replaced by a bond between the phosphate and the upstream exon's 3′ oxygen. The downstream exon remains connected to its original 5′ phosphate, but that phosphate is now connected to the upstream exon rather than to the intron. The new exon–exon junction is therefore a normal 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. The branch within the intron survives this reaction. The excised intron retains its unusual internal linkage and now has a free 3′ end where it was formerly attached to the downstream exon.

Notice that the newly produced exon junction does not require a DNA template to direct its ligation. The relevant RNA sequences are already present, and the machinery holds their ends in the correct spatial arrangement. Nor does the normal spliceosomal mechanism require a separate ATP-dependent RNA ligase to attach the exons after an independent hydrolytic intron cleavage. Both removal and joining are accomplished by the coordinated phosphotransfer reactions. The two chemical events preserve the identity and orientation of the retained nucleotides, while changing which nucleotide neighbors lie next to one another.

Consider a lesion that selectively prevents the upstream exon's new 3′ hydroxyl from participating in the second reaction after the first has completed. A normal mature product cannot form at that junction under the stated assumptions. The upstream exon and the branched intron–downstream-exon intermediate are expected to accumulate if they are stable and other processing pathways do not remove them. This is fundamentally different from the result of a complete first-step block, which would prevent both intermediate species from appearing. In practice, a mutation at the 3′ splice region can influence earlier assembly as well as the second reaction, so an observed mutation cannot be assigned a second-step-specific effect merely because it is located near the acceptor.

Another possible perturbation is a change in the exon-positioning activity of U5. If this change specifically compromises the productive alignment of the two exon ends, the second reaction can be inefficient despite initial recognition and first-step chemistry. The prediction is conditional: U5 interacts with the substrate through multiple stages, so a severe disruption can have additional consequences. A researcher would compare the amounts of intact precursor, first-step products and mature RNA, preferably over time, while checking whether overall levels of the splicing apparatus remain comparable. A defect in final product accompanied by substantial accumulation of first-step products is more informative than a decrease in mature RNA alone.

In both reactions, the phosphate that remains attached to the downstream side of the original bond is transferred to a new upstream oxygen. The attacking oxygen in the first reaction belongs to an internal intronic nucleotide, whereas in the second it belongs to the end of the first exon. This parallelism helps students derive the lariat structure without memorizing a complicated image. Draw the starting chain, mark the two boundary phosphates, identify the attacking hydroxyl for step one, preserve all other connections, and then identify the free exon hydroxyl for step two. If the resulting drawing contains a newly created 2′–5′ bond in the mature exon–exon junction, the construction is wrong. The exceptional linkage belongs to the removed intron.

The completed spliceosomal reaction is followed by product release and machinery recycling. The removed lariat is often debranched by an enzyme that hydrolyzes the special 2′–5′ phosphodiester linkage. Once linearized, the intron can be degraded by cellular RNA-processing and degradation pathways. Certain introns can contribute to stable functional RNAs or other processing products, but those outcomes should not be generalized to all excised introns. The reason debranching is relevant is structural: the RNA topology created by the first reaction poses a distinctive problem for ordinary linear RNA turnover. Debranching is not required to create the normal exon–exon linkage itself; it acts on a product after the two core reactions.

An instructive conservation exercise tracks nucleotide counts. Suppose a precursor consists of a 140-nucleotide upstream exon, a 260-nucleotide intron and a 190-nucleotide downstream exon. Before splicing there are 590 nucleotides in one RNA chain, ignoring terminal modifications. After the first step, the free upstream exon contains 140 nucleotides and the branched intron–exon intermediate contains 450. After the second step, the ligated exons contain 330 nucleotides and the excised intron contains 260. The total is still 590 immediately after the reactions, before degradation. The chemistry rearranges connectivity; it does not destroy 260 nucleotides as individual monomers at the moment of splicing. This distinction is useful when interpreting mass conservation in a cell-free reaction.

10. What makes the spliceosome a catalytic RNA system?

Historically, discovering small nuclear RNAs associated with the splicing machinery suggested that they might do more than guide substrate recognition. Base pairing explains how some regions of the precursor are selected, but it does not establish which molecular groups lower the barrier to the actual reaction. Distinguishing a structural or guide RNA from a catalytic RNA requires evidence that the RNA contributes directly to the active chemistry. The fact that removal of an snRNA abolishes splicing is not sufficient by itself, because the RNA might be required only to assemble the catalytic protein or position the substrate.

Several lines of evidence support the central role of the spliceosomal RNAs. U2 and U6 form an RNA architecture with features reminiscent of catalytic group II introns. The U6-containing active site is closely associated with the catalytic metal ions. A 2013 study by Fica and colleagues investigated metal-ion coordination during nuclear pre-mRNA splicing and provided direct chemical evidence supporting catalytic roles of U6 RNA. The important inference was not merely that an RNA could bind a metal, but that particular RNA groups contribute to positioning metals involved in the reaction. Structural and biochemical evidence together therefore support a ribonucleoprotein enzyme whose catalytic center is RNA-based. A full discussion of RNA catalysis belongs to the later node on ribozymes.

One approach to testing metal-dependent chemistry uses a change from an oxygen-containing ligand to a sulfur-containing group. The sulfur substitution alters preferences for metal-ion interactions. If a suitably chosen metal restores activity in the modified system relative to an appropriate control, this can support a direct interaction between the metal and the substituted site. Such reasoning requires controls because a metal ion might also influence assembly, folding or other features of the reaction. The examination-level lesson is how a chemical perturbation, together with a specific rescue pattern, can discriminate direct participation from a merely correlative observation. It does not require mastering the detailed experimental construction of the modified substrates.

Modern structural observations are complementary to such perturbation experiments. A structural model may place the relevant RNA groups, substrate phosphates and metal ions near one another in an active complex. That arrangement is consistent with a proposed reaction mechanism, but a static structure cannot by itself reveal every transient interaction or prove that each nearby group contributes to catalysis. Functional changes and chemical rescue help make that connection. Conversely, a biochemical experiment may establish the importance of a group without fully specifying the three-dimensional arrangement. Integrating the two forms of evidence yields a stronger mechanistic explanation than treating either alone as a complete proof.

The central catalytic role of RNA also has an evolutionary implication. Group II introns can carry out related chemistry using folded RNA, and their active architecture has meaningful similarities to parts of the spliceosome. These correspondences support models relating the evolution of spliceosomal splicing to ancient self-splicing introns. They do not constitute direct observations of the historical evolutionary transition, and the detailed route remains an evolutionary reconstruction. In exam reasoning, distinguish the experimentally supported similarity of chemical mechanisms and active-site features from a hypothesis about how one ancestral system may have given rise to another. This prepares us to examine how the same major spliceosomal chemistry can produce alternative mature messages.

11. Constitutive splicing and alternative splice-site choices

The reactions we have studied explain how two selected RNA regions can be connected. They do not yet explain why those regions are chosen. When all relevant introns are removed according to the usual arrangement and the same set of exons is regularly joined, the process is described as constitutive splicing. When a precursor can be processed using different acceptable combinations of sites, the resulting variation is called alternative splicing. These terms describe the choices made by the processing system; the underlying spliceosomal chemistry can remain the same. The spliceosome does not need a completely new catalytic mechanism for each possible transcript, because its ordinary catalytic center acts on whatever donor and acceptor are properly selected and brought together.

Consider a gene whose RNA contains four exons, E1, E2, E3 and E4. Suppose E1 and E4 are always retained and E2 can be included or omitted. If E3 is also always retained, two products are possible under this simple rule: E1–E2–E3–E4 and E1–E3–E4. In the first RNA, there is a junction between E1 and E2 and a separate junction between E2 and E3. In the second, a new E1–E3 junction directly joins the flanking regions. Exon 2 is a cassette exon in this example. Its absence from one mature product does not mean that it was absent from the gene or that transcription skipped the genomic region. The excluded region was transcribed but was not retained in that particular product.

A cassette exon is sometimes described as being removed together with its neighboring introns. This is a convenient description of the final connectivity, but it must not be interpreted as a separate enzyme simply deleting a completed exon from a previously finished messenger RNA. If the spliceosome chooses the donor after E1 and the acceptor before E3, the intervening region comprising the intron after E1, E2 and the intron before E3 can behave as one excised region for that processing event. In the inclusion pathway, separate connections are made across the two flanking introns. The different products arise because different boundaries are paired, and a drawing should identify those boundaries rather than merely erase a rectangular block.

Not all alternative splicing involves the inclusion or omission of a complete predesignated exon. If a region contains two usable donor sites associated with the same downstream acceptor, the choice determines how far the upstream exon extends. Selecting a donor farther toward the 3′ end can retain additional nucleotides in that exon compared with choosing an upstream donor. Similarly, two usable acceptor sites paired with the same donor can alter where the downstream exon begins. Selecting an acceptor farther downstream removes a longer region and shortens the retained downstream segment. These relationships follow directly from the direction in which RNA is read. They are more reliable than attempting to memorize which end of an exon changes without drawing the relevant boundary.

Mutually exclusive exons illustrate another type of constraint. Imagine two neighboring candidate exons, X and Y, between constitutive exons A and B. A regulated pathway might produce A–X–B or A–Y–B, while avoiding products that contain both candidates or neither candidate. Such a constraint may arise through competition, incompatible local processing arrangements or coordinated regulation. The name mutually exclusive describes the resulting pattern, not one universal molecular explanation. When counting products, the restriction changes the calculation: two mutually exclusive exons contribute two choices, whereas two independently optional exons could contribute four combinations, including both and neither.

Intron retention occurs when a particular intervening region remains in an RNA product that would otherwise be expected to lose it. It must be interpreted carefully. A nuclear preparation containing incompletely processed RNA can appear to show abundant intron retention even when those RNAs are transient precursors rather than intentionally retained mature products. To distinguish regulated retention from a processing delay, one may examine the cellular compartment, the processing status of other introns, the transcript's ends and the persistence of the retained form. A retained intron can introduce an early termination codon, change the coding sequence, alter the untranslated region or prevent an RNA from reaching the cytoplasmic translation machinery. No single outcome follows from the label alone.

Changes in transcript ends can also contribute to the diversity of mRNAs derived from a locus. An alternative promoter may change which first exon is present, and alternative cleavage and polyadenylation may change the final RNA region. These events can interact with splicing, for instance when selection of a last exon is coupled to selection of a cleavage site. Nevertheless, the underlying operations must be kept distinct. A change in 3′ end formation alone is not proof of a change in internal splice-site choice. Likewise, two RNAs beginning at different transcription start sites do not necessarily establish alternative splicing. Sequence comparison and knowledge of the primary transcripts are needed to assign the mechanism.

Alternative splicing challenges an overly simple version of the relationship between gene number and protein diversity. A single gene can give rise to several mature RNA isoforms, and some of these RNAs may encode different proteins. Other differences may change RNA stability, localization or translation without changing the encoded amino acid sequence. Some isoforms may not give rise to stable proteins at all. Thus the number of observed RNA isoforms is not automatically the number of protein isoforms, and the number of theoretically possible splice combinations is not necessarily the number found in a living cell. Biological constraints and selective processing determine which potential products are actually made and maintained.

12. How RNA sequence and regulatory factors determine splice-site choice

The presence of consensus splice-site signals does not guarantee identical use of those sites in every transcript or every cell. Initial recognition is influenced by the affinity and accessibility of the RNA sequences, the concentrations and activities of RNA-binding proteins, and cooperation or competition among nearby sites. A strong donor and acceptor may support efficient assembly without extensive additional assistance. A weaker site may depend more strongly on auxiliary recognition. This dependence allows sequence features outside the core splice-site motifs to influence the final processing decision. The regulated result need not involve a change in the catalytic center; a change in which substrate arrangement reaches that center can be enough.

Auxiliary regulatory sequences are commonly classified by their positions and effects. Exonic splicing enhancers are sequences within an exon that promote its recognition in a particular context. Exonic splicing silencers have the opposite effect. Related enhancing and silencing sequences can occur in introns. These labels refer to observed functional effects rather than an immutable chemical property of a short sequence. A motif that enhances exon inclusion when placed at one position might not produce the same response in a different transcript. The regulatory proteins present, the neighboring recognition sites and the structure adopted by the RNA all influence the outcome.

Members of the serine/arginine-rich, or SR, protein family often promote recognition through interactions with RNA elements and other splicing factors. For example, a protein bound within a weak exon may help stabilize a recognition factor at a neighboring site, thereby supporting exon definition. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins, commonly referred to as hnRNP proteins, can act as regulators and frequently repress particular splicing events in appropriate contexts. It would be incorrect to turn these common tendencies into an absolute rule that every SR protein always enhances all splicing and every hnRNP protein always suppresses it. Effects depend on the protein, sequence, position and competing interactions. For the current node, the useful principle is that local RNA-binding proteins influence site choice by changing the probability of productive assembly.

A regulatory sequence can be distinguished from a trans-acting factor using genetic reasoning. A mutation in an enhancer sequence lies within the same precursor whose processing it influences; it is a cis-acting change. A mutation in the gene encoding an RNA-binding protein may affect many transcripts on different chromosomes because the protein can act on multiple precursor molecules. This is a trans-acting change. If two differently marked reporter transcripts are introduced into the same cell and only the reporter carrying a mutated enhancer changes its splicing pattern, that result is consistent with a local cis effect. If both reporters change when a regulatory protein is depleted, a shared trans influence is more plausible. Indirect effects remain possible, so rescue experiments and controls improve the inference.

RNA folding adds another layer of interpretation. A donor sequence may possess favorable primary-sequence complementarity to a recognition RNA but become partly hidden within an intramolecular helix. Changing a nearby sequence could expose the donor or conceal it without altering the donor itself. In a hypothetical compensatory mutation experiment, two sequence changes that individually disrupt a proposed intramolecular stem but restore pairing when combined could test the structural explanation. If both single mutants expose a site and the double mutant restores its concealment, the pattern supports a role for the structure rather than an effect of one individual nucleotide. Alternative explanations such as overlapping protein-binding motifs must still be considered.

Tissue-specific splicing can result from the same precursor encountering different regulatory environments. A factor abundant in one tissue may promote use of a weak site, while another factor present elsewhere may favor the competing choice. The underlying genomic sequence need not differ. Developmental changes in factor expression or activity can likewise change the isoform composition over time. Splicing variation is therefore a means of interpreting a gene in a cellular context. A useful biological comparison considers both the shared gene and the differing processing environment, rather than treating tissue-specific proteins as necessarily encoded by different genes.

Competition between sites is especially important when interpreting variants. Suppose a donor normally supports strong inclusion of an exon and a nearby cryptic donor has little use. A mutation weakening the normal donor may allow the cryptic site to be selected more frequently. A cryptic site is a potential site that is normally unused or used at a low level but can become more prominent under altered conditions. It need not have been created by the mutation itself. Conversely, a mutation can create a new candidate recognition motif in a region that previously lacked one. These are two distinct routes to abnormal junctions: revealing a pre-existing competitor and generating a new competitor. Sequencing the resulting RNA junction helps identify which boundary was actually used.

It is useful to distinguish a reduction in splicing efficiency from a redistribution among products. If a perturbation reduces all mature isoforms and increases unprocessed RNA, an impairment of a shared processing step is plausible. If total mature RNA remains approximately constant but the fraction containing a particular exon changes, altered choice is more plausible. Both patterns can occur together, and measured abundance also depends on RNA degradation. A well-designed comparison should therefore measure multiple species and avoid treating a single band or a single quantitative signal as sufficient evidence of the complete pathway.

13. What a splice choice means for the encoded product

The biological effect of a splice choice is determined not only by the RNA sequence removed or retained but also by its relationship to translation. In a protein-coding transcript, the ribosome interprets codons in a reading frame established at translation initiation. A change in the number of coding nucleotides before a downstream region can preserve that reading frame or alter it. When an internal coding segment of length divisible by three is removed, and the same initiation site and downstream coding region are used, the downstream reading frame is preserved. If the removed length is not divisible by three, the reading frame generally changes unless another event compensates for the difference. These are arithmetic consequences of triplet decoding rather than special rules invented for exon boundaries.

Suppose a cassette exon contributes exactly ninety coding nucleotides and lies entirely between the initiation and termination codons. Omitting it changes the length of the coding region by thirty triplets. The downstream frame remains the same. The resulting protein need not be functionally normal: removing thirty amino acid positions can eliminate a structural element, binding region or regulatory sequence. At the novel exon junction, a codon may also be reconstructed from nucleotides that were formerly separated by the skipped region, so one should not assert that the protein always differs only by a simple uninterrupted thirty-residue deletion with no junctional change. The frame calculation predicts the continuity of downstream decoding, not every detail of the new amino acid sequence.

If instead the omitted exon contributes ninety-one coding nucleotides, the difference leaves a remainder of one when divided by three. Under the same assumptions, decoding downstream of the new junction occurs in a changed frame. A premature stop may arise, but its exact location depends on the nucleotide sequence. It is not possible to infer the number of amino acids in the altered protein from the exon length alone. One must identify the initiation codon and translate the actual reconstructed coding sequence until the relevant stop codon. When an exon includes both untranslated and coding sequence, it is especially important not to apply the divisibility rule to its total length without checking which part contributes to the translated region.

Intron phase offers a compact way to reason about the relationship between splice boundaries and codons. A phase-zero intron separates two complete codons. A phase-one intron interrupts a codon after its first nucleotide, and a phase-two intron interrupts after its second nucleotide. These are properties of the position of an intron relative to a specified coding frame. For an internal coding exon bounded by two introns of the same phase, the contribution of that exon to the coding sequence has length divisible by three. To see why, represent positions of boundaries by their remainders upon division by three. The difference between two positions with equal remainder is a multiple of three. Consequently, matching flanking phases can favor a frame-preserving exclusion of a whole internal coding exon under the stated assumptions. The phase rule does not independently establish that the exclusion occurs or that the resulting protein is functional.

Some splice variants primarily alter untranslated regions. A changed 5′ untranslated region can influence translation initiation without changing the protein's coding sequence, and a changed 3′ untranslated region can alter binding of regulatory factors or affect the lifetime and location of the RNA. In other cases, altered processing generates a premature termination codon and the RNA becomes susceptible to surveillance pathways such as nonsense-mediated decay. The precise response depends on transcript architecture and cellular context. RNA export and detailed quality control are assigned to a later node, but this connection explains why an isoform's abundance is not determined by splice-site choice alone. Its lifetime after processing can change how frequently it is detected.

A meaningful application of the same principle is the comparison between genomic DNA and complementary DNA for expression of a eukaryotic protein in bacteria. If a genomic fragment contains introns that require a nuclear spliceosome, a bacterial expression system generally cannot perform the appropriate processing. A cDNA representing the mature, correctly spliced RNA can supply a continuous coding region instead, provided the other requirements of the expression system are satisfied. This is not a claim that every cDNA automatically produces a functional protein in bacteria, since initiation signals, folding and post-translational requirements can still limit expression. It isolates the contribution of splicing to one practical difference between the genomic sequence and the sequence used for expression.

14. Quantitative reasoning about transcript diversity

Counting possible splice products requires a description of the allowed biological decisions before the calculation begins. The simplest counting problem contains constitutive terminal exons and a specified number of independent cassette exons between them. For each cassette exon there are two states, included and excluded. If all states are compatible with every other state and the order of the exons is fixed, the number of different exon-selection patterns for n independent cassette exons is 2 raised to the power n. The exponent counts decisions, not the total number of exons. The result follows from the multiplication principle: every choice for the first cassette can be combined with either choice for the second, and the same multiplication continues for each additional independent cassette.

For instance, take a precursor with six exons. Let the first and last be compulsory and the four internal exons independently optional. The maximum number of products under these assumptions is 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. There is exactly one product with no optional exons, and exactly one with all four. To count products containing exactly two of the four optional exons, choose two positions from four: the binomial coefficient 4 choose 2 is 4! divided by 2!2!, equal to 6. One does not multiply this value by the number of arrangements of the selected exons, because their order remains the genomic order. Counting all allowed inclusion numbers gives 1 + 4 + 6 + 4 + 1 = 16, an independent way of checking the original calculation.

More complex rules change the count. If a gene has three independently optional cassette exons, followed by a cluster from which precisely one of five exons must be retained, there are 2³ × 5 = 40 products, provided all those choices can coexist. If the cluster can also be omitted entirely, the factor is six instead of five and the count becomes 48. If the first optional exon must be present whenever the fifth member of the cluster is selected, the unrestricted calculation overcounts. Four of the eight cassette patterns exclude that first exon. Each of those four is incompatible with the fifth member. The resulting number under the new restriction is 40 − 4 = 36. This example demonstrates why the biological assumptions must be stated before an arithmetic shortcut is used.

One well-known biological illustration is the Drosophila melanogaster Dscam1 gene. Its variable exon clusters are conventionally described by 12, 48, 33 and 2 alternatives, respectively. The processing pattern selects one member from each cluster, while the constant exons supply the remainder of the transcript. If the four cluster choices are combined independently, the potential diversity is 12 × 48 × 33 × 2 = 38,016 combinations. The number is a combinatorial repertoire, not a claim that every fly cell simultaneously produces all 38,016 transcripts or that all products have equal abundance. Expression patterns and biological constraints can restrict actual usage. The example illustrates how structured, mutually exclusive choices can generate a large potential repertoire without requiring an equally large number of separate genes.

The biological consequence in Dscam1 is more than a large number. Different exon choices can specify variants of extracellular recognition regions or of a membrane-associated segment. The resulting family of related cell-surface proteins is important in neuronal recognition and wiring. This is a useful example of the difference between combinatorial potential and a set of proteins with a shared structural framework. It also contrasts with a cassette exon whose inclusion disrupts a reading frame and causes its transcript to be degraded. In the Dscam1 illustration, compatible alternatives are arranged in a way that can preserve a broad protein-domain organization, showing how exon architecture and splice regulation can cooperate.

Another quantity commonly used in interpreting alternative splicing is the fraction of measured molecules that include a selected cassette exon. For an ideal experiment that counts individual mature isoforms without bias, let I denote the number of molecules containing the exon and S the number lacking it. The inclusion fraction is I divided by the sum I + S. Multiplying by 100 expresses the value as a percentage, often termed percent spliced in or PSI. The two categories must be mutually exclusive and together exhaust the population being analyzed. If other isoforms cannot be unambiguously assigned, the denominator must be defined explicitly rather than treating every RNA measurement as belonging to one of the two classes.

In a hypothetical experiment, the control sample contains 180 inclusion molecules and 60 skipping molecules, while a treated sample contains 90 inclusion and 150 skipping molecules. Total measured molecules are 240 in both samples. The control inclusion percentage is 180 divided by 240 times 100, or 75%. The treated value is 37.5%. The difference is a decrease of 37.5 percentage points, while the relative decrease in inclusion fraction is 50% of the control value. The two quantities answer different questions. A decrease of 37.5 percentage points should not be described as a 37.5% relative reduction. Since the total mature count is unchanged in this idealized dataset, redistribution of isoform proportions is especially apparent, although the measurements do not alone establish a direct action on the spliceosome.

When junction reads rather than intact molecules are counted, the calculation needs care. Inclusion of a cassette exon creates two flanking junctions, whereas skipping creates one junction between the constitutive exons. Simply adding both inclusion-junction counts and comparing the sum with the skipping-junction count can give the inclusion isoform two opportunities to contribute. In an idealized dataset with equal sampling of junctions, a common simple approach is to average the two inclusion-junction counts before comparing with skipping. Suppose counts for the two inclusion junctions are 100 and 120 and the skipping junction count is 90. Their average is 110, and the estimated inclusion fraction is 110 divided by 200, or 55%. This calculation assumes comparable junction detectability; real sequencing analyses may account for effective lengths and other biases. If the two inclusion junction counts differ substantially, complex isoforms or measurement artifacts may require investigation.

Steady-state RNA abundance also depends on degradation. For a simple model with a production flux f in molecules per minute and first-order loss constant d in inverse minutes, the number N of molecules changes at rate f − dN. At steady state, production equals loss, so N = f/d. If two isoforms are produced at equal rates of 20 molecules per minute but one has a loss constant of 0.1 per minute and the other 0.2 per minute, their expected steady-state abundances are 200 and 100 molecules. A measurement of those abundances would suggest an inclusion fraction of two thirds if the first isoform were the inclusion form. Yet the production fractions were exactly equal. A steady-state abundance measurement cannot be equated with a direct measurement of processing flux unless degradation differences are excluded or accounted for. The model assumes a constant source, first-order losses and enough time to reach steady state.

The rate of splicing of a defined precursor can be treated with a simple kinetic model when the assumptions are suitable. In a hypothetical pulse experiment, a population of labeled precursor RNA is followed after labeling stops, and its only significant loss is a first-order conversion to product. If k is a constant effective conversion rate per minute, then the precursor fraction remaining after time t is exp(−kt). For a precursor fraction of 0.25 after 10 minutes, rearranging gives k = −ln(0.25)/10 = 0.1386 per minute. Its effective half-time is ln(2)/k, approximately five minutes. This value is an empirical description of the observed population under the model, not the rate constant of every individual assembly or catalytic transition. A multistep pathway, parallel degradation or a mixture of fast and slow precursors can invalidate a single-exponential interpretation.

15. Experiments that distinguish recognition, catalysis and splice-site choice

A mechanistic experiment is strongest when it is designed to distinguish two plausible explanations. A decrease in the abundance of a mature RNA could reflect slower transcription, slower splicing, faster degradation or defective export followed by turnover. To attribute the observation specifically to splicing, the investigator must look for the processing intermediates or junction changes predicted by that hypothesis. Experiments often compare the normal transcript with a defined altered transcript, or compare the same transcript in a normal and altered processing environment. A useful control should make an alternative explanation less likely, not simply add another sample to an experiment.

Compensatory base-pair changes provide a classical way to test direct recognition by U1. The prediction is that a mutation in the donor sequence which disrupts its pairing with U1 should reduce use of that donor, and a complementary mutation in U1 could restore the interaction. Zhuang and Weiner examined this principle in adenovirus E1A alternative donor sites in 1986. Their findings included strong suppression of one donor mutation but much weaker suppression of another. This difference was informative: direct base pairing is important, but restoring complementarity alone does not necessarily repair every feature of recognition. A mutation may disrupt other contacts or later interactions that are not restored by modifying U1. The experiment is a useful example of evidence for a specific interaction accompanied by evidence against an overly exclusive model of that interaction.

In a simplified hypothetical reporter experiment, wild-type precursor with normal U1 yields 90% correctly spliced RNA. Mutant precursor with normal U1 yields 15%, mutant precursor with a complementary U1 variant yields 72%, and mutant precursor with an unrelated U1 variant yields 17%. These figures are educational data, not results from the historical paper. The contrast between the two U1 variants supports sequence-specific rescue rather than a general effect of supplying additional U1. However, restoration is incomplete compared with the wild type. Measuring U1 variant abundance and testing whether it is assembled into functional particles would help rule out differences in expression. Testing the normal precursor with the complementary variant would additionally assess whether the changed U1 has a broad nonspecific effect on processing.

Cell-free splicing assays can reveal the order of the chemical transformations by following labeled precursor and products over time. A normal reaction should initially contain mostly precursor, then develop intermediate species, and eventually accumulate ligated exons and released intron. If a short-lived intermediate is rapidly converted, its abundance can remain low even if every precursor molecule passes through that state. Therefore, absence of a prominent band in a final-time sample does not demonstrate that the intermediate never existed. Early time points, appropriate labeling and conditions that selectively slow a later step can reveal a species that is otherwise difficult to detect. This logic is transferable to many enzyme pathways in which intermediate abundance depends on both formation and consumption rates.

Imagine a precursor with two exons of 100 and 150 nucleotides separated by an intron of 200 nucleotides. The unmodified transcript has 450 nucleotides; first-step products contain 100 and 350 nucleotides; second-step products contain 250 and 200 nucleotides. A label confined to the first exon detects the precursor, free first exon and mature RNA, but not the intron-containing intermediate that lacks the first exon. A label confined to the intron detects the precursor, branched intermediate and excised intron, but not the mature exon product. In a real gel the branched species may not migrate according to their nucleotide counts. Combining differently located labels with appropriate structural tests is consequently more informative than assigning every band exclusively on the basis of its apparent length.

A minigene reporter provides a convenient model for studying alternative exon usage. A cloned segment containing a cassette exon, its flanking constitutive exons and sufficient neighboring intronic sequences can be transcribed in a suitable cell. Investigators can change candidate regulatory motifs and compare the junctions formed. If deleting an enhancer reduces the proportion of inclusion RNA while leaving the relevant donor and acceptor intact, the enhancer is implicated in exon recognition. Nevertheless, a deletion may change spacing or RNA folding as well as remove a binding motif. Smaller targeted substitutions, preservation of length where feasible, restoration of the original sequence and comparisons in the same cell context help refine the conclusion. A minigene also lacks some features of its native chromosomal setting, so its behavior need not perfectly reproduce all regulation of the full endogenous gene.

Reverse-transcription PCR is particularly useful for interpreting simple cassette-exon reporters. Primers placed in constitutive exons flanking the variable exon can produce products of two lengths. Suppose the flanking portions included in the amplicon total 160 nucleotides and the alternative exon contributes 84 additional nucleotides. The skipping product is 160 base pairs and the inclusion product is 244 base pairs. A product of intermediate length could reflect use of an alternative internal site rather than mere partial inclusion in a population of molecules. Sequencing the product across its junction identifies the actual boundaries used. Endpoint PCR band brightness may not reliably equal molecule number, because shorter fragments can amplify differently and reactions can approach saturation. Quantitative claims require an appropriately calibrated measurement or a method that counts isoforms more directly.

Northern analysis using probes complementary to selected RNA regions can also help identify intermediates or isoforms. A probe against a constitutive exon may detect multiple products that share that exon. A probe against a variable exon detects only transcripts containing that region, subject to appropriate sequence specificity. An intronic probe may detect a retained-intron transcript, an unprocessed precursor or a released intron. Thus two signals at similar positions on a gel do not by themselves establish that the corresponding sequences occur in the same molecule. A targeted junction assay, or another method that determines connectivity directly, can answer that question. The distinction between detecting a sequence and demonstrating its neighbors is central to interpreting RNA processing evidence.

When testing an inhibitor or depletion of a spliceosomal component, a further difficulty is that the affected machinery may act on a large number of transcripts. A delayed cellular response could reflect secondary effects of altered gene expression rather than the immediate molecular consequence. An acute, carefully controlled cell-free experiment on a defined substrate can isolate a processing step more directly, while an intact-cell experiment can show the physiological importance of the perturbation. Neither replaces the other. In examination problems, note the timescale of an observed response, the specificity of the assay and whether normal transcription and RNA integrity have been checked. These observations help distinguish direct processing defects from indirect changes in RNA abundance.

16. Comparative splicing pathways and the connection to catalytic RNA

The major spliceosome accounts for the conventional processing mechanism described throughout this chapter, but it is not the only means of removing RNA introns. A minor spliceosome acts on a distinct class of introns in organisms that retain the corresponding machinery. It uses several different small nuclear RNAs, U11, U12, U4atac and U6atac, while sharing U5 with the major system. U11 and U12 have recognition roles analogous in broad outline to those of U1 and U2. The minor system illustrates how related functional tasks can be accomplished by distinct sets of recognition and catalytic components. Some of its substrates have AU–AC boundaries, but others have GU–AG termini, so the sequence context is essential to identifying the pathway.

Group I and group II introns offer a different comparison. Certain representatives can catalyze their own excision in suitable conditions because the intron RNA itself folds into a structure capable of arranging the reacting groups. In a conventional group I example, a free guanosine species supplies the first attacking 3′ hydroxyl, and the guanosine becomes attached to the intron's 5′ end. The released upstream exon then supplies its 3′ hydroxyl for exon ligation. The primary excised group I intron product is linear, although subsequent reactions can produce other forms. In a conventional branching pathway of group II introns, an internal adenosine supplies a 2′ hydroxyl for the initial reaction, forming a lariat analogous to that seen in the spliceosome. Some group II introns can use alternative hydrolytic pathways, so lariat formation should be understood as the characteristic branching mechanism rather than an unconditional property of every possible reaction.

Self-splicing introns and spliceosomal introns differ in how the catalytic structure is supplied. A self-splicing intron's own sequence can encode much of the architecture required for its excision. The spliceosome instead assembles a catalytic arrangement from reusable small nuclear RNAs, proteins and the substrate. Many group I and II introns require or benefit from proteins in living cells to reach an active fold efficiently, despite the ability of certain representatives to carry out chemistry in protein-free laboratory systems. The term self-splicing therefore refers to an intrinsic catalytic capacity of the RNA under appropriate conditions, not an assertion that all naturally occurring introns in these classes function entirely without proteins. This comparison is a bridge to the later discussion of ribozymes and should not be confused with the unrelated cleavage and ligation pathways used to process many transfer RNA introns.

Differences between organisms provide context for the biological role of splicing. In many bacterial genes, a continuous coding region can be transcribed and translated without removal of conventional spliceosomal introns. In eukaryotic nuclei, RNA processing creates a separate level at which a gene's output can be modified before the transcript reaches the translation machinery. Introns and alternative processing are distributed unevenly among eukaryotes, however, and their relative frequency or importance cannot be inferred solely from organismal complexity. Many yeast genes lack introns, whereas numerous animal and plant genes contain them. Comparing the mechanisms rather than assigning a simple ranking of biological sophistication provides the more scientifically useful conclusion.

Splicing also contributes to the interpretation of genetic variants. A change within an intron may be far from a protein-coding triplet yet alter the amount or identity of functional messenger RNA. A change within an exon that appears synonymous when translated in isolation may disrupt a regulatory motif or splice-site recognition. Thus a sequence variant can affect gene expression through processing even when its direct codon-level effect is small or absent. A prediction of this kind should be tested by observing the actual RNA products rather than inferred solely from the variant's location. The principle connects molecular processing to inheritance and explains why knowledge of genomic sequence is necessary but sometimes insufficient to predict a gene's functional output.

Finally, place the entire process back into the flow of genetic information. A cell transcribes its DNA, selects processing sites through interactions among RNA and proteins, rearranges the precursor's connectivity, and releases products whose future depends on their composition and cellular handling. Splicing is simultaneously a problem of sequence recognition, enzyme assembly, chemical catalysis and selective information retention. Its two fundamental chemical reactions explain how a chosen intron is removed; regulated site selection explains how the same precursor can yield distinct products. The next processing mechanisms, RNA editing and maturation of ribosomal and transfer RNAs, alter RNA in different ways and must be distinguished from this exon-joining mechanism.

Part B — conceptual practice

Original examination-style questions; numerical and experimental datasets are hypothetical unless stated otherwise.

1. Which RNA species contains an exon–exon junction absent from the corresponding uninterrupted genomic sequence?

  1. Genomic DNA alone
  2. A correctly spliced RNA
  3. A free intron alone
  4. An incoming NTP
Answer and explanation

B. Splicing creates a new adjacency between retained RNA regions. Genomic DNA still contains the intervening sequence; neither an isolated intron nor an incoming nucleotide contains the mature junction.

2. Which statement correctly defines an exon?

  1. It is necessarily translated
  2. It is always exactly three bases long
  3. It is retained in the specified mature RNA
  4. It is a promoter
Answer and explanation

C. An exon can include untranslated sequence; its defining feature is retention in the relevant mature RNA. The promoter directs transcription and is not generally part of the mature transcript.

3. For an RNA drawn E1 | GU…AG | E2, which bases are usually intronic?

  1. GU only
  2. AG only
  3. Both GU and AG
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

C. Both consensus dinucleotides lie on the intronic side. The boundary phosphates are at the two bars, not between the bases of either dinucleotide.

4. Which group attacks the donor phosphate during the first canonical reaction?

  1. Branch A 2′-OH
  2. Branch A 3′-OH
  3. E2 5′-OH
  4. Free ATP
Answer and explanation

A. The internal adenosine supplies a 2′ oxygen; it creates a 2′–5′ branch. E1's new 3′-OH acts in the second reaction, and ATP is used for remodeling rather than as the nucleophile.

5. Which molecule initially recognizes many major-class donor sites through base pairing?

  1. U6 only
  2. U1 snRNA
  3. U4 snRNA
  4. 5S rRNA
Answer and explanation

B. U1 participates in early donor recognition. U6 takes part in the activated complex, U4 associates with U6 before activation, and 5S is a ribosomal RNA.

6. What is a distinctive property of the branch nucleotide in the canonical U2 interaction?

  1. It is always removed before catalysis
  2. It becomes a DNA nucleotide
  3. It is bulged out of the recognition helix
  4. It is the RNA 5′ cap
Answer and explanation

C. Leaving the branch A unpaired positions its ribose for chemistry. It remains in the intron and is neither DNA nor a cap.

7. Which snRNA helps position exon termini during splicing?

  1. U5
  2. U4 alone
  3. U1 alone
  4. 18S
Answer and explanation

A. U5 participates in holding and aligning exon sequences. U4 has a preactivation role, U1 initially recognizes the donor, and 18S is ribosomal.

8. Which association is dismantled during major-spliceosome activation?

  1. U4–U6
  2. The covalent DNA–RNA bond of a gene
  3. The mature E1–E2 linkage before it forms
  4. The cap's methyl group
Answer and explanation

A. Separating U4 and U6 permits U6 to form its active configuration. The other alternatives do not describe an activation transition.

9. Which RNAs are central to the major spliceosomal RNA-based catalytic core?

  1. U1 and U4
  2. U2 and U6
  3. U4 and 5S
  4. U11 and 18S
Answer and explanation

B. U2–U6 interactions and the U6-containing active architecture organize the reacting groups and metals. U1 and U4 principally have earlier roles.

10. What is the second-step nucleophile?

  1. E1 3′-OH
  2. A second branch A 2′-OH
  3. A free guanosine in every spliceosome
  4. DNA 3′-OH
Answer and explanation

A. The first step exposes the upstream exon 3′ hydroxyl. It attacks the acceptor phosphate; free guanosine is characteristic of group I self-splicing initiation, not canonical nuclear splicing.

11. What remains covalently joined immediately after step one?

  1. E1 and E2
  2. Lariat intron and E2
  3. E1 and intron at the original junction
  4. Neither exon to any RNA
Answer and explanation

B. The donor boundary has been exchanged; the acceptor boundary is still intact. E1 is covalently separate but may remain bound to the machinery.

12. Where is the unusual 2′–5′ linkage in the final canonical products?

  1. Exon–exon junction
  2. 5′ cap only
  3. Excised intron branch
  4. Every backbone bond
Answer and explanation

C. The lariat contains the branch linkage. The exon–exon junction is a regular 3′–5′ phosphodiester.

13. What does ATP hydrolysis accomplish in the spliceosomal cycle?

  1. Supplies the phosphate of every exon junction
  2. Drives assembly/remodeling and recycling
  3. Copies the DNA template
  4. Directly methylates the cap
Answer and explanation

B. The junction phosphates already belong to the precursor. ATP-dependent conformational changes assist progression through the machine; ATP is not consumed as the direct chemical donor at each transesterification.

14. Which early mammalian recognition factor is most closely associated with the pyrimidine-rich region?

  1. U2AF large subunit
  2. DNA helicase DnaB
  3. Ribosomal 23S RNA
  4. Poly(A) polymerase
Answer and explanation

A. U2AF contributes to downstream intron recognition; its large subunit recognizes the pyrimidine tract. The other factors act in different molecular processes.

15. What distinguishes a GC–AG major-class intron from an AU–AC minor-class example?

  1. The former cannot splice
  2. Terminal bases alone always identify the machinery
  3. Splice-site context and pathway determine classification
  4. Both require no RNA recognition
Answer and explanation

C. Some major-class introns have GC–AG termini and some minor-class introns have GU–AG termini. The broader signals and required machinery are decisive.

16. Which result is an example of exon skipping?

  1. E1–E2–E3 becomes E1–E3
  2. DNA E2 is deleted permanently
  3. A is edited to I in E2
  4. The cap is added
Answer and explanation

A. Skipping creates a different exon junction in RNA. A DNA deletion and RNA editing are distinct mechanisms, and capping does not alter internal exon selection.

17. Two alternative donors share an acceptor. Which choice normally retains more upstream RNA?

  1. The more upstream donor
  2. The more downstream donor
  3. Both always retain identical sequence
  4. The acceptor must move
Answer and explanation

B. The more downstream donor places the cut later in the 5′→3′ sequence, retaining the intervening segment in the upstream exon.

18. Which group I self-splicing feature differs from the usual group II branching pathway?

  1. It uses a free guanosine species to initiate
  2. It must use U1
  3. It requires ribosomal translation
  4. It creates genomic DNA
Answer and explanation

A. Group I commonly uses an external guanosine nucleophile; group II commonly uses an internal branch A. Both can use sequential phosphotransfer chemistry.

19. What does a debranching enzyme act on?

  1. The new 3′–5′ exon junction
  2. The special linkage within an excised lariat
  3. The genomic promoter
  4. The translation start codon
Answer and explanation

B. Debranching linearizes the removed intron by hydrolyzing its unusual bond. It is not the enzyme that ligates exons.

20. Which statement about splicing timing is correct?

  1. All splicing must wait for termination
  2. It can occur during transcription
  3. It always precedes synthesis of the acceptor
  4. It occurs only in ribosomes
Answer and explanation

B. Co-transcriptional recognition and processing are common. A site cannot participate before it becomes available, and some splicing completes after transcription.

21. Assertion: U1 can leave the major spliceosome before catalysis. Reason: The donor undergoes a handoff to the U6-containing arrangement during activation.

  1. Both true, reason explains
  2. Both true, unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false
Answer and explanation

A. The handoff is part of activation and explains why U1 is not required to remain at the active donor during phosphotransfer.

22. Assertion: Exon skipping always causes a frameshift. Reason: A coding segment of length divisible by three can be removed without changing downstream frame.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

C. Skipping an in-frame coding segment can preserve downstream decoding; skipping untranslated sequence need not affect frame at all.

23. Assertion: An intron-retaining read from nuclear RNA proves a stable cytoplasmic isoform exists. Reason: Nuclear RNA can contain incompletely processed precursor.

  1. Both true
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

C. The read may derive from a transient precursor. Compartment, transcript completion and stability must be checked before inferring a mature exported product.

24. Match U1, U2, U4 and U5 to their principal roles: i. early donor recognition; ii. branch recognition; iii. paired with U6 before activation; iv. exon alignment.

  1. i, ii, iii, iv
  2. ii, i, iv, iii
  3. iv, iii, ii, i
  4. iii, iv, i, ii
Answer and explanation

A. These are distinct roles in an ordered assembly model. They do not mean each particle has only one interaction or stays bound throughout the cycle.

25. Statements: I. GU–AG is a common terminal arrangement. II. The lariat has a 2′–5′ branch. III. Every exon encodes protein. IV. The spliceosome directly hydrolyzes ATP for each phosphodiester exchange. Which are correct?

  1. I and II
  2. II and III
  3. I, III and IV
  4. All
Answer and explanation

A. I and II describe canonical features. Untranslated exons disprove III, and the distinction between remodeling and transesterification disproves IV.

Original examination-style questions; numerical and experimental datasets are hypothetical unless stated otherwise.

26. A precursor has E1=120 nt, I=360 nt, E2=180 nt. What nucleotide counts are expected after step one, before any degradation?

  1. 120 and 540
  2. 300 and 360
  3. 480 and 180
  4. 120 and 360
Answer and explanation

A. Step one separates E1 (120) from branched I–E2 (360+180=540). Option B describes final products; C would imply removal at the opposite boundary; D loses E2.

27. For the same precursor, a label restricted to E2 detects which species after step one?

  1. E1 only
  2. Branched I–E2
  3. Free intron only
  4. Neither
Answer and explanation

B. E2 remains covalently attached to the intron until step two. E1 lacks the label, and free intron has not yet been produced.

28. A step-one reaction proceeds, but exon ligation is selectively blocked. What accumulates in a stable cell-free assay?

  1. Mature RNA exclusively
  2. E1 and lariat–E2 intermediate
  3. Only free intron
  4. Genomic DNA
Answer and explanation

B. The products of the first step form but cannot be consumed by step two. A free intron normally appears after the acceptor bond is exchanged.

29. A precursor has only one usable branch A. Its 2′-OH is chemically replaced with H without altering other recognition features. What is the direct prediction?

  1. First-step chemistry impaired
  2. Only cap methylation blocked
  3. Exon alignment necessarily improved
  4. DNA replication halted
Answer and explanation

A. The intended nucleophile is absent. Recognition could still occur, but the conventional first transesterification cannot use that group.

30. U1, U2 and tri-snRNP bind normally, but U4 remains paired to U6 and no first-step intermediates form. Which step is most directly implicated?

  1. Donor transcription
  2. Catalytic activation
  3. Debranching
  4. Translation elongation
Answer and explanation

B. Persistent U4–U6 pairing is incompatible with the normal active arrangement. Binding data argue against a simple failure of initial recruitment.

31. A mutant produces low mature RNA. First-step intermediates accumulate while initial complexes appear normal. Which additional experiment best tests a second-step block?

  1. Measure genomic copy number alone
  2. Follow labeled intermediates and exon ligation over time
  3. Count ribosomes only
  4. Measure unrelated ATP levels alone
Answer and explanation

B. A time course directly tests conversion of first-step intermediates into ligated exons. DNA copy number or ribosome count cannot identify that processing transition.

32. Hypothetical reporter: mutant donor + normal U1 gives 12% correct product; mutant donor + complementary U1 gives 81%; mutant donor + unrelated U1 gives 14%. What is supported?

  1. Direct sequence-specific recognition contributes
  2. All U1 mutations enhance splicing
  3. U1 is the sole catalyst
  4. DNA was repaired
Answer and explanation

A. The complementary change specifically rescues processing, supporting pairing. It does not demonstrate that U1 supplies the catalytic center or that genomic DNA changed.

33. A second donor mutation is not rescued by a complementary U1 variant. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. U1 never recognizes donors
  2. Additional interactions or later steps may be defective
  3. The intron must be absent
  4. RNA editing is proven
Answer and explanation

B. Rescue tests a particular interaction. Failure may reflect effects beyond pairing or deficient mutant U1; it does not invalidate direct recognition at other sites.

34. A gene has five internal cassette exons, all independently optional, with two mandatory terminal exons. How many selection patterns are possible?

  1. 10
  2. 25
  3. 32
  4. 128
Answer and explanation

C. Each of five decisions has two possibilities: 2⁵=32. The terminal exons introduce no decisions; exon order is fixed.

35. For that gene, how many patterns contain exactly three optional exons?

  1. 6
  2. 10
  3. 15
  4. 60
Answer and explanation

B. The binomial count is 5!/(3!2!)=10. Permutations are not allowed because normal cis-splicing preserves order.

36. Two independent cassette exons and a mandatory one-of-four exon cluster are available. How many products?

  1. 8
  2. 12
  3. 16
  4. 64
Answer and explanation

C. There are 2²×4=16 choices. The four cluster members are mutually exclusive, not independently optional.

37. A three-member exclusive cluster follows two optional exons A and B. Cluster member 3 requires A. How many allowed products?

  1. 10
  2. 12
  3. 9
  4. 6
Answer and explanation

A. The unrestricted count is 4×3=12. Two patterns lacking A conflict with member 3, leaving 10.

38. A Dscam-like model has 12, 48, 33 and 2 choices. If the second cluster is reduced from 48 to 12, what is the theoretical repertoire?

  1. 9,504
  2. 19,008
  3. 38,016
  4. 792
Answer and explanation

A. 12×12×33×2=9,504. This is one quarter of 38,016 because only the second factor changed; it is not a measured number of expressed isoforms.

39. A cassette exon contributes 75 coding nucleotides. Assuming identical initiation and downstream sequence, what follows from its exclusion?

  1. Frame shifts by one
  2. Frame shifts by two
  3. Downstream frame preserved; coding length decreases by 25 triplets
  4. No possible protein effect
Answer and explanation

C. 75/3=25 exactly. A junctional codon or protein domain may change despite preserved frame, so D is not justified.

40. An internal coding exon is bounded by phase-one and phase-two introns, respectively, in a single reading frame. What is its length modulo three?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. Unknowable under these assumptions
Answer and explanation

B. The downstream boundary remainder is 2 and the upstream is 1. Their difference is 1 modulo 3. Skipping the whole coding exon generally changes the downstream frame.

41. Two alternative acceptors are 12 nt apart, with a shared donor; both are within the coding region. Choosing the downstream acceptor changes retained length by what amount?

  1. Adds 12
  2. Removes 12, downstream frame preserved
  3. Removes 12, necessarily frameshifts
  4. Adds 4 nucleotides
Answer and explanation

B. The downstream acceptor removes the intervening 12 nt, equivalent to four triplets. Frame preservation does not establish protein functionality.

42. A control has 240 inclusion and 160 skipping molecules. Treatment has 120 inclusion and 280 skipping molecules. What is the change in PSI?

  1. +30 percentage points
  2. −30 percentage points
  3. −50 percentage points
  4. −20 percentage points
Answer and explanation

B. Control PSI=240/400=60%; treated=120/400=30%. Difference=−30 percentage points (a 50% relative reduction).

43. Idealized inclusion junction counts are 80 and 100, skipping junction count is 60. Estimate PSI using the average inclusion count.

  1. 60%
  2. 75%
  3. 33.3%
  4. 45%
Answer and explanation

A. Average inclusion count=90. 90/(90+60)=0.6. Summing both inclusion junctions produces an overestimate under the stated equal-detection model.

44. Two isoforms are produced at 30 molecules/min each. Their loss constants are 0.15 and 0.30 per min. At steady state, what are their abundances?

  1. 200 and 100
  2. 4.5 and 9
  3. 30 and 30
  4. 100 and 200
Answer and explanation

A. N=f/d gives 30/0.15=200 and 30/0.30=100. Equal production does not imply equal abundance when lifetimes differ.

45. In a first-order precursor-conversion model, 12.5% of labeled precursor remains after 15 min. What is the half-time?

  1. 3 min
  2. 5 min
  3. 7.5 min
  4. 15 min
Answer and explanation

B. 0.125=1/8=(1/2)³. Three half-times elapsed in 15 min, so each is 5 min. This is an effective model, not a measured rate of a particular snRNP.

46. A gel amplicon between constitutive exons is 180 bp when a cassette exon is skipped. The cassette contributes 96 nt. What is the expected inclusion amplicon?

  1. 84 bp
  2. 180 bp
  3. 276 bp
  4. 288 bp
Answer and explanation

C. 180+96=276 bp. The amplicon contains the same flanking primer-to-junction segments plus the cassette.

47. A reporter gives a new 200-bp band between predicted skipping 180 bp and inclusion 276 bp. Which result would identify the mechanism most directly?

  1. Sequencing the new junction
  2. Counting total cells
  3. Measuring total protein only
  4. Ignoring the band
Answer and explanation

A. The intermediate size could result from a cryptic donor or acceptor or another RNA product. Sequence connectivity identifies the actual boundaries.

48. An enhancer mutation changes only the marked reporter bearing that motif in a cell with two reporters. What is the strongest preliminary inference?

  1. Cis-dependent effect
  2. All spliceosomal proteins destroyed
  3. All genes have changed DNA copy number
  4. Direct catalytic metal loss proven
Answer and explanation

A. Both reporters experience the same trans environment, but only the mutated RNA changes. This supports a local sequence-dependent effect; folding or spacing changes remain alternatives.

49. A candidate structure is disrupted by either single mutation, which increases exon inclusion. A compensatory double mutation restores pairing and low inclusion. What does this pattern support?

  1. RNA secondary structure contributes
  2. Every enhancer is a protein
  3. All introns are self-splicing
  4. The coding frame determines donor recognition alone
Answer and explanation

A. Restoring pairing with altered primary sequence supports the proposed structural contribution. Overlapping regulatory motifs remain a possible confounder.

50. A nuclear intronic probe detects an abundant RNA, but a mature exon-junction assay does not detect a corresponding cytoplasmic retained form. What is justified?

  1. Stable exported retention proven
  2. Unprocessed or retained nuclear RNA is possible
  3. The intron must have been deleted from DNA
  4. The protein is certainly abundant
Answer and explanation

B. An intronic signal cannot establish a mature exported product. Nuclear retention, processing delay and free introns can contribute depending on the detected species.

51. Two tissues have equal measured total mature RNA but PSI values 80% and 20%. Which explanation fits without requiring genomic differences?

  1. Different local splicing-factor environments
  2. Mandatory permanent deletion of the exon in one tissue
  3. Identical isoform ratios
  4. No splice-site selection
Answer and explanation

A. The observed proportions differ despite equal totals. Trans-acting environments and cis-context interactions can alter site selection, but differential isoform stability should also be considered.

52. An intron has GU–AG termini but depends on U11 and U12 for processing. What is the best interpretation?

  1. It cannot be minor class
  2. It may be a U12-type intron
  3. It is necessarily group I
  4. It must lack a branch point
Answer and explanation

B. Minor-class identity depends on recognition machinery and broader sequence signals. GU–AG termini are compatible with some U12-type introns.

53. A branched RNA isolated after reaction is converted to a linear RNA by a debranching enzyme. Which claim is supported most directly?

  1. The RNA has a debranchable branch linkage
  2. All RNA splicing is protein-free
  3. ATP never participates
  4. The RNA came from a specific gene without sequencing
Answer and explanation

A. The structural response supports a lariat-like linkage; it does not identify all catalytic components or establish the sequence origin.

54. A DNA–RNA hybrid made with mature RNA shows an unpaired DNA loop. With precursor RNA, the same region pairs continuously. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. The intervening genomic sequence is present in precursor but absent from mature RNA
  2. The experiment identifies U6 metal ligands
  3. DNA was necessarily deleted
  4. All exons encode protein
Answer and explanation

A. The paired comparison supports processing-dependent RNA discontinuity. It cannot identify the chemistry or catalysts without additional mechanistic evidence.

55. In a minigene assay, total RNA decreases after treatment, and inclusion and skipping isoforms both fall by half. What should be measured before claiming selective exon skipping?

  1. Relative isoform fractions, precursor levels and RNA stability
  2. Only the inclusion band
  3. Only DNA mass
  4. Nothing further
Answer and explanation

A. Both mature products decreased proportionately, so the data do not indicate redistribution by themselves. Precursor and stability measurements help distinguish transcription, processing and degradation.

Formula and assumption sheet

  • One intron: Lprecursor = LE1 + LI + LE2. Immediately after step one: LE1 and LI + LE2; after step two: LE1 + LE2 and LI. Ignore end modifications and degradation.
  • Independent optional exons: 2n products; exactly k included: n!/[k!(n−k)!]. Fixed order; all counted combinations must be allowed.
  • Independent mandatory exclusive clusters: multiply their numbers of choices. Subtract incompatible combinations when choices are conditional.
  • PSI = 100I/(I+S), where I and S are inclusion and skipping molecule counts. Use a justified count model for junction reads.
  • Coding-frame preservation for an internal coding deletion: removed nucleotide count ≡ 0 (mod 3), assuming unchanged start and downstream context.
  • Simple first-order conversion: P(t)/P(0)=e−kt; t1/2=ln(2)/k. Applies to constant effective rate, no competing losses.
  • Steady-state abundance N=f/d for constant production flux f and first-order loss constant d; equal flux does not imply equal abundance.

Twelve misconceptions to eliminate

  1. Exon means protein coding. Exons can contain untranslated regions and noncoding RNA sequences. Retention, not translation, defines their role.
  2. Splicing removes DNA. Ordinary pre-mRNA splicing rearranges RNA connectivity, leaving the genomic sequence intact.
  3. GU and AG are sufficient recognition signals. Short motifs recur by chance; broader signals and cooperative factors distinguish productive sites.
  4. All introns start GU and end AG. Noncanonical terminal patterns exist; minor-class recognition cannot be inferred from terminal dinucleotides alone.
  5. U1 is the central chemical catalyst. U1 helps early recognition; U6 and U2 participate in the active RNA architecture.
  6. All five major snRNAs remain together at catalysis. U1 and U4 depart during activation; their earlier functions remain essential.
  7. Branch A attacks through its 3′ hydroxyl. Its internal 2′ hydroxyl attacks in the first canonical reaction.
  8. Step one releases the complete intron. The intron is still attached to the downstream exon until step two.
  9. ATP supplies the exon-junction phosphate. The junction phosphate originates in the precursor; ATP-dependent remodeling assists the overall cycle.
  10. Every skipped exon frameshifts the protein. Coding length, position and reading frame determine the effect; untranslated exons may not change amino acid sequence.
  11. Every predicted splice combination is expressed. Combinatorial counts depend on assumptions; biological regulation and stability restrict observed products.
  12. A lariat band migrates like a linear molecule of equal length. Branch topology alters mobility; identification requires appropriate controls or structural evidence.
