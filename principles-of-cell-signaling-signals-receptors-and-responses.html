1. Why cells need signals

A liver cell can contain abundant glycogen and all the enzymes required to break it down without continuously releasing glucose. A muscle cell can contain the machinery required for contraction without remaining permanently contracted. A plant cell can possess the molecules necessary for growth while changing its growth rate when illumination changes. These observations identify a problem distinct from the synthesis of cellular machinery: when should existing machinery operate, and when should it stop? A cell must match its internal activity to changing conditions outside and inside itself. The processes that connect detectable changes to appropriate changes in cellular behavior are called signaling.

Consider an animal that has not eaten for several hours. The nutritional requirements of its tissues are not identical to the amount of glucose recently supplied by digestion. Some cells must alter the rates of pathways that mobilize or conserve fuel. Each liver cell cannot independently inspect every tissue in the animal to determine its requirements. A change in circulating chemical signals can provide information about the physiological state of the organism. The liver cell uses that information to regulate its own enzymes. The chemical signal is therefore different from the glucose that is eventually released: the first is an instruction in a molecular form, while the second is a material supplied through metabolism.

The distinction between information and material is especially important when interpreting a small amount of an active chemical. If a very small concentration of an extracellular molecule causes the release of a large quantity of glucose, the extracellular molecules are not being converted into the glucose. The glucose was already stored in the cell in a polymeric form. The signal alters the rate at which a pre-existing store is mobilized. This explains why relatively few signaling molecules can produce a large biological change without violating conservation of mass. Energy for the response also comes from the cell or its pre-existing gradients, not necessarily from chemical energy supplied by the original signal.

The same problem occurs in a single-celled organism, although there is no endocrine system. A bacterium in a changing environment must adjust movement or metabolism in response to nutrients, osmolarity and other variables. A cell that moves toward an attractant does not need the attractant to serve directly as the fuel for every movement. It needs a mechanism that detects the relevant property of its surroundings and couples that detection to its motility machinery. The biochemical details of bacterial chemotaxis will be studied later; its value here is to show that receiving and interpreting environmental information is a fundamental cellular property rather than an invention of multicellular organisms.

A useful signaling mechanism must solve several distinguishable problems. The stimulus must reach or influence a suitable sensor. The sensor must distinguish the relevant stimulus from unrelated changes. Detection must alter something in the cell that can affect a downstream process. The downstream activity must be appropriately sized, localized and timed. Finally, the system must be capable of resetting or adapting if conditions change again. A failure at any one of these stages can leave a cell unresponsive even when the other stages are normal.

The term signal is functional. A change in a molecule's concentration may be a signal in a cell containing the appropriate sensing machinery but may have no regulatory significance for a neighboring cell lacking that machinery. The existence of a molecule outside a cell is not enough to establish that it is a signal for that cell. Similarly, a molecule detected inside a cell may simply be a metabolite rather than a signaling intermediate. Evidence of a connection between the detected change and a regulated response is necessary before assigning these roles.

Cells can distinguish relatively constant conditions from changes within those conditions. A cell normally exists in a chemical environment containing many ions, nutrients and extracellular proteins. Against this background, it may detect a small increase in one particular signal. A pathway that responded indiscriminately to every molecule would be incapable of selective regulation. Selective molecular recognition is the starting point, but downstream biochemical organization is equally necessary: recognizing an extracellular molecule does not specify by itself which of hundreds of possible cellular processes will change.

Signaling is often introduced as communication between a sending cell and a receiving cell. This is a useful starting picture, but not every signal originates from a cell that intentionally releases a molecule. Photons, changes in membrane tension and changes in the local ionic environment can provide detectable information. A receptor may therefore recognize a chemical ligand or a physical change. The common feature is that the sensing element undergoes a change that can be coupled to other cellular processes. A biochemical binding equation for ligand recognition must not automatically be applied to a physical stimulus such as illumination.

A cell that receives a growth-promoting signal does not invariably divide. Its response may depend on whether nutrients are available, whether its DNA is damaged, and whether other regulatory systems permit progression through the cell cycle. Earlier study of cell-cycle checkpoints established that proliferation is an integrated decision rather than an isolated enzyme reaction. Signaling provides inputs to such decisions. Understanding a pathway therefore requires distinguishing the immediate molecular effect of an input from a final behavior that depends on multiple conditions.

The following sections develop the logic of reception, transduction and response using tractable binding and enzyme models. These models are deliberately simple enough to calculate and test. Their limitations will matter as much as their predictions, because a student who mistakes a model assumption for a universal biological law will make incorrect inferences from realistic experimental data.

2. Signals, stimuli and the availability of a cue

An extracellular chemical signal must be present in a form that can interact with a sensing molecule. Chemical identity alone does not determine its effect. A signal bound tightly to an extracellular carrier, trapped in a separate compartment or degraded before reaching its target may have a different effective availability from an equal total quantity of freely accessible signal. When a question specifies a ligand concentration, the first task is to determine which concentration it means: the concentration supplied to the preparation, the concentration in the extracellular medium, or the free concentration available at the receptor.

Concentration describes amount relative to volume. One nanomolar means one nanomole per liter, or ten to the power minus nine moles per liter. This is not the same as one molecule in a cell. In a small compartment even a concentration that seems large in laboratory units can correspond to a surprisingly small number of molecules. Conversely, a small concentration in the bloodstream can represent a substantial total number of molecules because the accessible volume is large. Receptor binding depends on the local conditions at the receptor, not the total number of molecules present throughout the organism.

A chemical signal can be a peptide, an amino acid derivative, a lipid-derived molecule, a nucleotide or a small gas. These categories help predict whether the molecule can readily cross a lipid bilayer, whether it is likely to remain dissolved in an aqueous environment and whether it requires transport or specialized access. They do not identify the response without further information. A peptide's inability to diffuse through the bilayer does not prevent it from changing nuclear transcription: the membrane can contain receptors that initiate an intracellular series of events reaching the nucleus.

The physical separation between a cell's environment and cytoplasm is one of the major reasons that transduction is needed. Recall that the interior of the plasma membrane is hydrophobic. Many water-soluble substances cannot simply equilibrate across it on the timescale of signaling. A receptor spanning the membrane can bind a substance on one side and change the state of a domain on the other side. The stimulus is not necessarily transported across the membrane; rather, a change in molecular state is conveyed across the membrane. This is a particularly clear example of a change in the form of the information.

Some signals can reach a receptor within the cell. In such cases, a transmembrane receptor is not a prerequisite for signaling. A molecule that enters a cell can interact with an intracellular protein and alter its activity. Even here, sensing is not the same as the final response. Entry, binding and functional regulation remain separable stages. The detailed relationship between hormone chemistry and receptor location belongs to the next chapter. At present the important distinction is between a barrier to molecular transport and a barrier to information transfer, which are not equivalent.

Physical cues illustrate another version of this distinction. A photoreceptor responds when light alters the state of a light-sensitive molecular system. A mechanically sensitive channel can respond to mechanical changes by altering the probability of channel opening. In neither case is it essential to imagine an extracellular ligand colliding with a binding pocket. A photon has energy and wavelength; membrane deformation has a physical magnitude and direction. The nature of the input determines the appropriate measurement of stimulus intensity. Comparing a light-response curve with a ligand-binding curve requires care because the underlying input variables differ.

The source of a chemical signal can be local or distant. In autocrine signaling, a secreted factor acts on the producing cell, or on cells of the same type that produce and respond to it. In paracrine signaling, a locally released factor affects nearby targets. Endocrine signals in animals reach distant targets through circulation. These terms describe patterns of delivery rather than a molecular classification of receptors. A molecule does not acquire a fundamentally different binding chemistry simply because it traveled farther. A particular signaling substance may participate in more than one delivery pattern depending on biological context.

Spatial delivery changes what a cell can infer from a signal. A rapidly removed local signal can create a concentration difference across a tissue. A circulating signal can reach many locations, but only cells with the appropriate receptor and response machinery are expected to respond through the specified pathway. A local gradient and a widespread circulating signal therefore represent different distributions of input. Neither distribution guarantees a particular output; cells may differ in receptor abundance, accessibility and internal regulatory state. The full principles of intercellular communication are developed in the next subunit.

Timing of delivery is as important as concentration. Imagine a signal supplied for ten seconds and another supplied for ten minutes at the same concentration. A receptor might reach the same instantaneous activity during both exposures, yet one exposure might be too brief to generate enough of a downstream product to change gene expression. Alternatively, both exposures could initiate a rapid effect that terminates even while the signal remains present. These possibilities arise because the pathway has its own timescales, and exposure duration must be distinguished from the lifetime of the receptor state and the lifetime of the response.

A change in stimulus can be quantified in absolute or relative terms. Doubling a concentration from one to two nanomolar and doubling it from one hundred to two hundred nanomolar are equal fold changes but not equal changes in concentration. If the receptor is already almost saturated at one hundred nanomolar, the second doubling may cause a negligible change in receptor occupancy. If the relevant detector operates near its sensitive range at one nanomolar, the first change may have a substantial effect. This anticipates why receptor affinity helps determine the range over which a pathway can detect changes in its input.

The medium in which a signal is measured can also change the interpretation of a result. In a cell-culture experiment a researcher may add a ligand to a defined volume. If the ligand is rapidly degraded, adsorbed to a surface or taken up by cells, its measured concentration later may no longer equal the initial value. A dose-response experiment conducted for five minutes and one conducted overnight may therefore expose cells to different effective concentrations despite identical initial additions. Recording exposure conditions and measuring the relevant free concentration where possible help separate the chemistry of delivery from the behavior of the intracellular pathway.

3. Receptor structure, molecular recognition and selectivity

A receptor is a cellular sensing component whose interaction with a stimulus is coupled to a change in activity that can regulate downstream processes. Many receptors are proteins. Their amino acid sequences determine three-dimensional structures containing regions that interact with ligands and regions that transmit a change in activity. A receptor's binding region may lie within a folded domain, in a pocket between membrane-spanning segments, or at an interface between subunits. The ligand is the molecule that interacts with the binding site; it need not resemble the substrate of the enzyme that ultimately performs the response.

A ligand-binding surface is not a rigid hole with a perfectly matching shape in every situation. Both ligand and receptor undergo thermal motion, and flexible groups can adopt different arrangements. Binding can stabilize one ensemble of conformations relative to another. Thus a change in receptor shape after binding need not imply that the ligand pushes a mechanical lever in a literal way. Molecular interactions can shift the probabilities of accessible states, favoring a conformation capable of changing catalytic activity or interacting with a partner. This view helps reconcile specific recognition with the reversibility of binding.

Noncovalent interactions are central to ordinary receptor-ligand association. An appropriately positioned hydrogen-bond donor may interact with an acceptor. Charged groups can attract or repel depending on sign and local conditions. Hydrophobic groups may be favorably buried away from water, and closely packed atoms contribute short-range interactions. The overall free-energy change reflects not only contacts in the bound complex but also changes in solvation and conformational freedom. A mutation in a binding site can therefore weaken recognition even if the receptor remains folded and reaches the correct cellular location.

Specificity describes preferential recognition or activation by one stimulus compared with alternatives. Affinity describes the tendency of a particular ligand and receptor to associate under stated conditions. They answer different questions. A receptor might bind two structurally related ligands with affinities of ten nanomolar and one hundred nanomolar, respectively. It prefers the first in an equilibrium binding comparison, but the second is not necessarily excluded. At sufficiently high concentrations of the second molecule, substantial occupancy can occur. Describing specificity as absolute exclusivity would be incorrect in this case.

An additional distinction is required between recognition and activation. Some ligands bind a receptor and promote an active signaling state. These are agonists in the relevant response system. Other ligands occupy the recognition site without activating it and can prevent an activating ligand from reaching that site. A receptor can also have basal activity before agonist addition, making the exact effect of a bound ligand dependent on which receptor states it favors. For the simple models in this chapter, a neutral competitive antagonist is taken to occupy the same site without changing basal activity; this restricted definition is sufficient for interpreting elementary experiments.

The ability to bind is therefore not proof of the ability to signal. Consider a mutant receptor that retains the extracellular binding domain of a normal membrane receptor but loses the intracellular region required to engage a downstream partner. A labeled ligand may still associate specifically and reversibly with the mutant. Nevertheless, adding that ligand may not change the downstream reporter. This is a powerful counterexample to the assumption that a receptor-binding assay is equivalent to a functional signaling assay. The two measurements report different stages of the pathway.

Conversely, receptor activation can occur without a newly added extracellular ligand in some experimental or pathological circumstances. A mutation may favor an active conformation, or an artificial intervention may activate an intracellular component directly. Observing a downstream response in a ligand-free sample therefore does not establish that ligand binding was missed by the measurement. Basal signaling and ligand-independent activation are alternative explanations that should be tested, for example by comparing cells lacking the receptor, cells carrying a normal receptor and cells carrying the mutant under matched conditions.

Receptor abundance must be distinguished from receptor affinity. Increasing the number of identical receptors on a membrane increases the maximum quantity of ligand that can bind at saturation. It does not by itself change the dissociation constant of each independent binding site. An altered receptor concentration can nevertheless change the biological sensitivity of the cell, because the number of active receptors at a given fractional occupancy can change. Later sections develop this difference quantitatively. For now, a binding curve's plateau and its horizontal position should be treated as separately interpretable features.

Specificity also has an intracellular dimension. Activated receptors may engage particular partners because their intracellular interaction surfaces recognize appropriate protein regions. A ligand can selectively bind its receptor yet fail to produce a particular output if the necessary downstream protein is absent. Conversely, two different receptors can converge on a common intracellular activity. The identities of signal, receptor and effector should therefore be recorded separately in a pathway diagram rather than compressed into a phrase such as the hormone activates the cell.

A simple way to explore the distinction is to compare two receptors that bind the same chemical but activate different effectors. In one cell the effect might be a rise in an intracellular messenger; in another cell the effect might be a change in ion permeability. Both are legitimate responses to the same extracellular signal. The chemical does not physically encode a unique tissue behavior in isolation. The responding cell's expression of receptors and downstream molecules determines how the detected information is used.

A useful prediction follows from the chemistry of binding. If a mutation weakens a ligand contact but leaves the activation machinery unchanged, a greater ligand concentration might restore occupancy and response. If a mutation instead destroys the coupling interface, increasing ligand concentration may saturate the receptor without restoring signaling. The two defects can produce superficially similar reductions in response at one concentration. Measuring both binding and function over a concentration range allows them to be distinguished. This reasoning will be central to analytical questions in which an unknown mutation must be placed in a pathway.

4. Reception, transduction, effectors and cellular response

Reception is the initial detection of a stimulus by a suitable receptor or sensing system. In a chemical signaling pathway this often involves formation of a ligand-receptor complex followed by a change in the receptor's functional state. Detection alone is not the completed response. In a cell where an extracellular growth factor activates a membrane receptor, a measurement of receptor phosphorylation reports an early signaling event, whereas a measurement of subsequent DNA synthesis reports a much later outcome. Both can be relevant, but they cannot be substituted for one another without evidence.

Transduction is the conversion and transmission of information through changes in cellular components. A receptor may change the activity of a membrane enzyme, an ion channel, an intracellular molecular switch or a transcriptional regulator. These changes can then influence other molecules. The term transduction emphasizes that the form of the signal may change: an extracellular chemical concentration becomes a receptor state, which becomes a catalytic rate, which becomes a concentration of an intracellular messenger or a modification state of a protein. The chemical identity of the original ligand need not be preserved along this chain.

A downstream effector is a component that carries out a regulated action in the context of the pathway being described. The term is relational, so its meaning depends on the position in the diagram. An enzyme that produces a messenger is an effector of an upstream receptor-associated switch, while the messenger subsequently regulates a different effector. A kinase may be an effector of an intracellular messenger and simultaneously act upstream of another protein. Specifying the reference point prevents ambiguity. A final physiological response is not always a single molecular event; it may be the collective result of several effectors.

A familiar illustration is the regulation of glycogen metabolism by an extracellular hormone. The hormone binds a receptor on a responsive cell. Receptor activation changes intracellular signaling activity, which can increase the concentration of cyclic AMP. Cyclic AMP regulates a protein kinase, and downstream phosphorylation events change the activity of enzymes involved in glycogen metabolism. Glycogen mobilization is the metabolic response. The receptor does not cleave the glucose polymer, and cyclic AMP is not the original hormone. The individual components of this pathway will receive detailed treatment in the subsequent cyclic nucleotide chapter.

A second illustration is a chemical signal that regulates a ligand-gated ion channel. Ligand binding can directly alter channel gating, allowing ions to move according to their electrochemical gradients. The change in ion movement can alter electrical properties of the cell. This pathway may require fewer intermediates than the metabolic example. Reception and the first transduction event can occur in the same protein. The existence of this case prevents us from defining signaling as necessarily requiring a long chain of enzymes or the production of a soluble second messenger.

The distinction between an intracellular signal and an enzyme substrate is particularly helpful here. ATP serves as a substrate for enzymes that make cyclic AMP and for protein kinases that transfer phosphate groups to proteins. Cyclic AMP, by contrast, can regulate the activity of proteins by binding to them. Some ATP outside cells can act as a signaling molecule at appropriate receptors, so chemical identity is not sufficient to assign a fixed role in every context. A molecule's location, interaction and functional consequence establish whether it is acting as a signal, substrate or product in the particular situation.

A chain of arrows in a diagram usually summarizes causal influences rather than physical movements of the same molecule. An arrow from a receptor to an enzyme indicates that receptor activity affects the enzyme, not that the receptor travels to the enzyme or is chemically converted into it. An arrow from a kinase to a substrate can indicate phosphorylation. An inhibitory line indicates a decrease in the activity of a downstream component. Reading a pathway requires identifying what each arrow means; otherwise it is easy to infer an impossible material flow or incorrectly predict the effect of an inhibitor.

A pathway can branch after a single reception event. One receptor may activate two downstream processes that regulate different cellular properties. Branching differs from amplification: two branches are two distinct routes, whereas amplification means a larger number or magnitude of downstream events relative to an upstream event under specified conditions. A branch can amplify its output, but branching alone does not demonstrate a measured gain. A pathway can also converge when two distinct inputs influence a common effector. This structure creates opportunities for cooperation or opposition between inputs.

The time between reception and response depends on the processes that intervene. Opening a pre-existing ion channel can produce a change in ion flow rapidly. Modifying an existing enzyme can alter metabolism without waiting for gene expression. A response requiring newly synthesized proteins must allow time for transcription, RNA processing where relevant, translation and protein accumulation. These are relative expectations rather than absolute time limits. Rapid signaling may eventually change transcription, and a receptor usually associated with transcription can have other effects. The observed endpoint and its required steps determine the interpretation of timing.

The four stages provide a practical experimental framework. If ligand binds normally but the early intracellular signal is missing, the likely defect lies in receptor activation or coupling rather than signal availability. If the intracellular signal is generated normally but the final response is absent, the defect may lie after that signal. If a directly activated effector restores the response despite loss of reception, the downstream machinery is at least capable of producing the measured response under the intervention. Such rescue experiments are informative because they test the consequences of bypassing a stage rather than relying only on correlation.

Finally, a response may involve an increase or decrease in activity. Signaling is not synonymous with stimulation of every component. Inhibiting an inhibitor can increase a final output, while activating an inhibitor can reduce it. Negative responses are just as meaningful as positive responses. For example, reducing an enzyme's activity can conserve a substrate, and reducing movement can change how long a bacterium remains in a favorable environment. A student should learn to follow the signs and the specific molecular states in a diagram, not assume that an activating ligand increases every downstream quantity.
