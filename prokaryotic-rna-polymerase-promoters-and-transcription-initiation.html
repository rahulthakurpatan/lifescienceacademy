1. The problem of selective transcription

A bacterial chromosome is simultaneously a store of genetic information and a physical substrate for molecular reactions. Its DNA contains many thousands of potential positions at which a complementary RNA could begin. Yet the cell does not normally copy every stretch of DNA into RNA at the same frequency. It directs RNA synthesis towards selected transcription units, and it can change those selections when the physiological demands of the cell change. This is possible because the beginning of transcription is not merely the first chemical step in polymerization. It is a recognition and commitment problem. An enzyme has to find a suitable DNA sequence, establish the correct orientation, make a local region of the double helix available for copying and enter a productive cycle. Each of these events creates a distinct opportunity for the cell to influence its RNA output.

Consider a bacterium that is growing in an environment containing a readily metabolizable carbon source. Many genes needed for central metabolism are actively expressed. When environmental conditions change, some of these genes become less important, whereas genes that support adaptation become more valuable. The DNA sequence of the chromosome has not changed simply because the environment has changed. What changes is the use of that sequence. A major part of this flexibility resides in the molecular events that determine which promoters are recognized and how frequently their RNA polymerase complexes begin productive transcription.

A promoter is a DNA region that directs initiation of transcription. It should not be pictured as a universal punctuation mark with an identical nucleotide sequence in every gene. It is a functional arrangement of sequence information, spacing, DNA shape and local interactions with proteins. A promoter can be recognized efficiently by one form of the transcription machinery but poorly by another. Even a promoter that is bound efficiently can fail to produce RNA if subsequent steps are unfavorable. The useful definition of a promoter therefore concerns a sequence's ability to support correctly positioned initiation, not simply its ability to attract a protein in a binding experiment.

The RNA polymerase that performs bacterial transcription is a large protein complex. Its catalytic center can link ribonucleotides into a chain, using one DNA strand as a template. The information needed to select a particular starting point comes in substantial part from an accessory protein called a sigma factor. The association of the catalytic machinery with a sigma factor is a powerful example of division of labor at the molecular level. A cell can retain the same fundamental polymerization chemistry while varying the way in which that chemistry is directed to different regions of the genome. The advantages of such organization become clearer when one compares the functions of the catalytic core, the promoter and the specificity factor separately.

It is useful to distinguish the making of a correct chemical product from the making of a product at the correct place. If a polymerase joins nucleotides complementary to a DNA fragment, it may be performing accurate template-directed catalysis. However, the resulting RNA may lack a biologically meaningful start site. Conversely, if a protein recognizes a promoter with exquisite specificity but cannot join ribonucleotides, it does not itself make a transcript. Cellular transcription requires both properties, coordinated within a suitable molecular assembly. This distinction is foundational for interpreting the experimental evidence that revealed the organization of bacterial RNA polymerase.

The discussion here uses Escherichia coli as its principal model because many classical experiments and familiar examination conventions were developed in this organism. The basic roles of a catalytic core and promoter-specific initiation factors extend widely across bacteria, but the exact repertoire of sigma proteins and promoter features varies. The term prokaryotic is broader than bacterial: archaea also lack a nucleus, yet their transcription apparatus is not simply the bacterial sigma-dependent system. Their multisubunit polymerase and initiation factors share important similarities with eukaryotic machinery. Thus an answer about the alpha, beta, beta-prime, omega and sigma subunits should be identified as a bacterial answer rather than an assertion about every prokaryote.

The central thread of this chapter follows a single enzyme from its unbound form to its first stable, elongation-competent transcript. Later chapters deal with the full elongation cycle, the recognition of termination signals and the integrated regulation of gene expression. Keeping this boundary makes it possible to examine initiation in considerable depth without confusing a promoter mutation with a terminator defect or a change in translation efficiency.

2. How the sigma factor was discovered

The discovery of a specificity factor depended on separating components that normally work together. A mixture containing bacterial RNA polymerase could synthesize RNA in vitro when supplied with DNA and ribonucleoside triphosphates. An investigator measuring incorporation of labeled ribonucleotides into RNA could establish that the mixture contained an active polymerase. Such an experiment alone would not reveal how many protein components were necessary or whether the enzyme selected biologically meaningful transcription start sites.

Purification creates a useful experimental opportunity because distinct proteins sometimes separate according to their charges, sizes or affinities for a chromatographic matrix. If a function declines following separation, the missing activity can be investigated by mixing individual fractions again. The important logical step is reconstitution: the recovery of a function after the separated components are reunited. This approach distinguishes a genuinely necessary accessory component from a protein that merely happens to accompany an enzyme during isolation.

In 1969 Richard Burgess, Andrew Travers, John Dunn and Ekkehard Bautz reported a separable component that stimulated transcription by E. coli RNA polymerase [1]. Work with bacteriophage T4 DNA was especially informative because activity on a natural double-stranded template depended strongly on the associated component. The protein later became known as sigma. Comparing the purified fractions demonstrated that the capacity to carry out RNA synthesis could be experimentally separated from efficient initiation on a biologically relevant DNA substrate. Addition of the isolated factor restored the missing function. The experiment did not require the investigators to assume in advance that the polymerase contained a separate promoter-recognition module.

A modern classroom reconstruction of the logic would use several kinds of reaction. A complete polymerase preparation with DNA and nucleotides would provide a positive reference. A catalytic core preparation would test whether removal of the associated component altered transcription. A mixture of the core and the separated sigma factor would test whether the deficit could be rescued. Sigma alone and a reaction lacking DNA would serve as important negative comparisons. To distinguish loss of polymerization from loss of promoter specificity, an investigator would also compare initiation from a recognizable promoter with extension on a substrate that already presents an appropriate RNA and DNA arrangement. These are conceptual controls for understanding the evidence, not a claim that each was performed in exactly this form in the original short report.

There are several possible outcomes of a separation experiment. Suppose both core and sigma fractions are inactive independently, but their mixture restores activity. That would establish cooperation, although it would not by itself establish which component carries the catalytic center. Suppose instead that the core synthesizes RNA on a permissive artificial substrate but cannot initiate efficiently at a natural promoter. The distinction would be much stronger: the core retains the capacity to make phosphodiester bonds while the missing factor is particularly important for positioning the initiation complex. An experiment that measures the positions of the RNA start sites is more discriminating than a bulk incorporation assay, because total incorporation can include RNA that begins at inappropriate locations.

Subsequent work by Travers and Burgess demonstrated cyclic use of sigma during transcription [2]. The concept of recycling resolved an important physiological problem. If the specificity factor had to remain permanently attached to a polymerase throughout the synthesis of every RNA, the number of promoter-recognition complexes would depend closely on the number of polymerases engaged in long transcription units. The possibility of dissociation and reuse allows specificity factors to participate repeatedly in initiation. Nevertheless, the early idea of sigma release must not be turned into a rigid rule that every molecule leaves at one exact RNA length. Later evidence supports persistence of sigma in at least a fraction of transcription complexes under some conditions [5].

The historical significance of these results extends beyond the discovery of an additional subunit. They provided a general strategy for explaining specificity in a biochemical system: purify the parts, measure separate functions, restore the complete assembly and test whether the restored activity has the expected biological properties. When a CSIR-NET question presents a series of lanes showing core polymerase, sigma and reconstituted holoenzyme, its central issue is usually this separation of catalysis from promoter selection. The most defensible interpretation follows the measured property rather than merely counting which fraction makes more total RNA.

3. The bacterial polymerase core as a molecular machine

The standard E. coli RNA polymerase core is represented as alpha-two beta beta-prime omega, written α₂ββ′ω. The subscript two denotes two copies of the alpha polypeptide; the other symbols each represent one copy in the classical core. This gives a total of five polypeptide chains belonging to four subunit types. Association with a sigma factor produces a holoenzyme, which in this simplified stoichiometric description contains six chains. A student should be able to move between a symbolic formula, a count of chains and an account of their molecular roles without treating the symbol beta-prime as a modified beta molecule. Beta and beta-prime are distinct proteins encoded by distinct genes.

In E. coli, rpoA encodes alpha, rpoB encodes beta, rpoC encodes beta-prime and rpoZ encodes omega. The two alpha chains participate early in the assembly of the polymerase. Their amino-terminal regions support core organization, whereas their more flexible carboxyl-terminal regions can contribute to contacts with upstream DNA and regulatory proteins. The latter role makes alpha important for transcriptional efficiency even when the basic catalytic chemistry is retained. For example, a mutation restricted to an upstream-contacting surface might affect promoters that make extensive use of upstream interactions more than promoters that do not.

The beta and beta-prime subunits are the largest constituents of the bacterial enzyme and together form much of its catalytic and nucleic-acid-handling architecture. Their association creates the cleft that accommodates DNA and the growing RNA–DNA hybrid. The catalytic center involves conserved residues, particularly acidic residues within beta-prime that coordinate metal ions. The beta subunit contributes to the organization of substrates and products near the catalytic region and is the target of rifampicin, a classical bacterial transcription inhibitor whose detailed consequences are examined in the next node. The contributions of beta and beta-prime overlap structurally; an examination model that attributes every catalytic function to one subunit and every DNA contact to the other is unnecessarily rigid.

Omega is a relatively small component that helps support assembly and stability of the enzyme, including interactions with beta-prime. Its small molecular mass should not be confused with absence of biological importance. A complex machine can depend on a small component for maintaining its conformation. However, the effect of losing omega varies with organism and experimental conditions, so one should not claim that omega is always an independently essential catalytic subunit in every bacterial system. The broad principle is that proper organization of the catalytic core matters for both initiation and later chain extension.

The architecture is often compared to a clamp surrounding nucleic acids. This is useful because transcription involves a physical problem: DNA must be held and locally separated while one strand is fed into the catalytic center. The nontemplate strand must be accommodated away from the copying path. The nascent RNA must eventually separate from its DNA complement and leave the complex. A polymerase is therefore not just an exposed catalytic pocket on a compact sphere. Its channels and movable structural elements organize the entering DNA, the template strand, incoming nucleotide substrates and the departing RNA.

The core can catalyze synthesis even in the absence of sigma if provided with a substrate suitable for catalysis. In biochemical experiments such substrates may include a preassembled transcription elongation complex or permissive DNA structures. This statement does not imply that core initiates correctly at ordinary promoters without assistance. A convenient comparison is an active enzyme that has lost its targeting module. Its catalytic center may still work when an appropriate substrate is physically supplied to it, but the normal process of finding and positioning a genomic substrate becomes inefficient.

An enzyme-subunit mutation can therefore have multiple interpretations. A defect in catalytic metal coordination is expected to impair nucleotide addition broadly, including addition in complexes already assembled on a template. A defect that selectively impairs sigma binding may produce a much stronger failure of promoter-dependent initiation than of extension on an already assembled scaffold. An alpha mutation that changes upstream contacts could have promoter-selective effects. These predictions are more informative than the simple statement that all mutations in RNA polymerase reduce transcription.

A further complication is the distinction between loss of activity and loss of enzyme abundance. A mutant beta-prime protein may fail to assemble, lowering the amount of intact core rather than directly slowing the chemistry of each assembled enzyme. A careful experiment would compare amounts of properly assembled enzyme as well as activity per enzyme molecule. Such normalization is a recurring theme in molecular biology. When an experimental graph reports reduced RNA production, the first mechanistic question should be whether fewer active polymerases are present or whether each active polymerase behaves differently.

4. Sigma association and the formation of holoenzyme

A holoenzyme is the form of bacterial RNA polymerase containing the catalytic core associated with an initiation specificity factor. In the familiar E. coli housekeeping system the factor is σ⁷⁰, encoded by rpoD. Its name refers to its approximate molecular mass convention, not to the number of nucleotides recognized or the number of subunits in the enzyme. The sigma factor contributes to selective recognition of the promoter and helps establish an initiation-competent configuration of DNA and polymerase.

One way to think about the association is as a reversible molecular equilibrium. An unbound core and a free sigma factor can form a holoenzyme when their interacting surfaces encounter each other. Noncovalent forces stabilize the association, and the balance between assembly and dissociation is influenced by the concentrations and affinities of the partners. This is why a change in sigma availability can alter the amount of holoenzyme even when the total amount of core is unchanged. It is not necessary to synthesize a new catalytic center every time a bacterium changes which promoters it uses.

The sigma factor has multiple regions with different structural functions. In the σ⁷⁰ family, regions associated with domain two contribute to contacts with the minus ten region and help stabilize exposed nontemplate bases during promoter melting. Domain four is important for recognition of the minus thirty-five region in classical promoters. Domain three can contribute to recognition of an extended minus ten element, and a linker extending towards the active-center region influences early RNA synthesis. These functions explain why different mutations in the same sigma polypeptide can generate very different phenotypes. A mutation in a sequence-recognition region may alter promoter preference; a mutation in a core-interacting region may reduce formation of a stable holoenzyme; and a mutation in a region influencing early RNA can change the balance between abortive and productive initiation.

The primary sigma factor also has structural features that help prevent isolated sigma from behaving simply as a constitutive sequence-specific DNA-binding protein. Association with core changes the context in which its DNA-recognition surfaces operate. This matters when interpreting biochemical studies using purified sigma alone: the behavior of an isolated protein need not reproduce the behavior of the same protein inside holoenzyme. The binding of sigma to a short DNA sequence is not by itself sufficient evidence that the normal initiation complex has assembled.

Bacteria frequently encode more than one sigma factor. In E. coli, alternative factors such as σ³², σ³⁸ and σ²⁸ are associated with characteristic sets of genes involved, respectively, in responses to heat-related protein stress, stationary-phase or general stress adaptation, and flagellar gene expression. These examples establish the principle of alternative promoter specificity without requiring the learner to memorize every regulon. The precise set of promoters recognized by different sigma factors is not always mutually exclusive. Related factors may recognize overlapping sequences, and promoter context can influence which factor acts efficiently.

A sigma switch can affect expression through competition for the same core. If two factors are capable of associating with a limited pool of enzyme, increasing the abundance of one factor can shift the relative distribution of holoenzymes. However, competition cannot be predicted solely by comparing total cellular sigma concentrations. Binding affinity, sequestration, access to core, promoter kinetics and ongoing transcription also contribute. The distinction between a simple equilibrium model and a whole-cell prediction should be made explicit. The equilibrium model is valuable for teaching direction of change, but it does not replace measurements in the living bacterium.

In a mutant carrying an altered sigma factor, transcription of some genes may decline while other genes remain active. Such a phenotype is consistent with a change in promoter selection, especially when unaffected transcription is mediated by another sigma factor or when an affected promoter has a particular recognition sequence. The observation alone does not prove altered sequence recognition. Loss of the mutant sigma through protein instability or reduction of its core-binding affinity could yield a similar pattern. Reconstitution with controlled concentrations and comparison of promoter variants provide a means to distinguish these possibilities.

5. Promoter maps, strand orientation and coordinate conventions

Before examining promoter motifs, the learner needs a reliable coordinate system. In standard transcription maps, the first DNA position represented in the RNA is designated +1. Positions upstream are assigned negative numbers, and positions downstream of +1 receive positive numbers. There is no nucleotide numbered zero in this usual convention. Consequently the nucleotide immediately upstream of +1 is −1, and a sequence extending from −3 to +3 contains six positions rather than seven. This simple detail is a common source of avoidable errors in sequence-based problems.

Upstream and downstream are defined relative to the direction of transcription, not by a fixed left or right side of the chromosome. A transcription unit drawn from left to right has upstream DNA to the left of +1. Another gene located on the opposite strand may be transcribed from right to left, so its upstream region is physically to the right on the same chromosome diagram. The choice of template strand follows the selected direction because the polymerase reads DNA in the 3′ to 5′ orientation while synthesizing RNA in the 5′ to 3′ orientation.

For a forward-facing bacterial promoter, sequence motifs are commonly written on the nontemplate or coding strand in its 5′ to 3′ direction. The familiar minus thirty-five and minus ten consensus sequences are therefore not sequences that are automatically read off whichever strand happens to be placed at the top of a figure. If an exercise supplies the template strand in the 5′ to 3′ orientation, it must be reverse-complemented before comparing its promoter sequence with the standard coding-strand consensus. Merely complementing without reversing produces a sequence written in the wrong orientation.

Imagine a coding strand containing the six bases AGTCCA immediately after an indicated transcription start. The RNA beginning at that position is 5′-AGUCCA-3′, assuming the strand is displayed in the direction of transcription. The paired DNA template is 3′-TCAGGT-5′ over the same positions. The sequence of the RNA corresponds to the coding strand except for replacement of thymine by uracil, but the physical template used by the enzyme is the opposite strand. This relationship explains why the word coding can be misleading: it describes a sequence correspondence, not the strand that physically guides incorporation of incoming ribonucleotides.

A promoter can be located in a region containing several regulatory binding sites. Such sites are not automatically part of the minimal promoter required for recognition by RNA polymerase. The promoter, an operator, an activator-binding sequence and the first translated codon are distinguishable functional features even when they overlap in a compact bacterial genome. The position of a feature may be expressed relative to the transcription start, but that convention does not mean that it is included in the RNA. Most familiar minus ten and minus thirty-five elements lie upstream of +1 and are not copied into that transcript. A promoter can also contain functional positions at and downstream of the start site, so the statement that every promoter nucleotide is necessarily upstream is too broad.

Start-site mapping is therefore important when identifying a promoter. One can align DNA sequences relative to experimentally determined transcription start sites and then examine the frequencies of bases at upstream positions. Without a reliable +1 position, apparent distances between motifs may be misleading. In a mutation study, a change in promoter sequence might alter initiation efficiency without shifting the transcription start, or it might alter which start site is preferred. A measurement of only RNA concentration would not distinguish those outcomes.

The start codon of a protein-coding message belongs to translation, not transcription. It may lie downstream of a leader sequence, and its distance from +1 can vary. In some bacterial messages the transcription start occurs at or very close to the translation start, but this does not make the two processes identical. A promoter mutation can eliminate the entire RNA even if the downstream start codon remains intact. A start-codon mutation can preserve the RNA while interfering with protein synthesis. The directionality and coordinate conventions developed here allow these different genetic effects to be distinguished without confusing DNA recognition with translation.

6. The classical minus thirty-five and minus ten elements

A promoter can be understood initially through two sets of sequence-specific contacts made at different positions along DNA. In a frequently used model of an E. coli σ⁷⁰ promoter, the upstream hexamer is TTGACA and the downstream hexamer is TATAAT, written on the nontemplate strand in the direction of transcription. The former is known as the −35 element and the latter as the −10 element or Pribnow box. Their names are approximate positional conventions: a typical coordinate assignment places the six bases of the −35 element at −35 through −30 and those of the −10 element at −12 through −7. Notice that the numbers name regions rather than the distance between their centers in a strict mathematical sense. These sequences are consensus sequences derived from comparisons among promoters, not fixed sequences that every promoter must possess.

A consensus sequence summarizes a set of aligned biological sequences. For example, if eight out of ten promoters have thymine at an aligned position and the other two have cytosine, the consensus at that position is ordinarily written as thymine. The result describes the most frequent base, not a guarantee that thymine is required for activity. Furthermore, a consensus sequence constructed one position at a time need not correspond to any actual member of the alignment. Certain substitutions may be tolerated only when other positions have complementary properties, so frequency at one position cannot fully capture the relationships among positions.

The historical identification of a conserved promoter region was enabled by the ability to isolate DNA associated with RNA polymerase and determine sequence information. In 1975 David Pribnow reported the sequence of a protected RNA polymerase binding region at an early bacteriophage T7 promoter [3]. The observation linked a physically recognized region to its primary DNA sequence. Comparing multiple promoter sequences allowed recurring features to be identified. The reasoning differs from simply finding short nucleotide words in a genome: the sequences were connected to measured biological behavior. A motif becomes meaningful because promoter recognition or activity has been demonstrated, not merely because it resembles a familiar pattern.

Why do two recognition elements help to locate a starting point? A sequence of six nucleotides is short enough to occur elsewhere by chance. Recognition of two spatially separated features provides additional constraints. The holoenzyme can use the upstream sequence to help orient its binding and the downstream sequence to position the region that must open. In a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter, sigma region four is associated with the −35 element, whereas region two interacts with the −10 region. The physical separation of the domains means that successful recognition depends on their relationship along the double helix. A promoter with individually favorable sequences can function poorly if those sequences are placed at an unsuitable distance.

A simplified probability calculation illustrates why additional constraints are useful. Assume DNA is an independent random sequence with equal probabilities of the four bases. The chance of observing any specified six-base word at an assigned position is 1 divided by 4⁶, or 1/4096. In a hypothetical genome of four million bases, roughly 977 occurrences of that word would be expected in one chosen orientation, ignoring edges. The probability of two specified six-base words at predetermined nonoverlapping positions is 1/4¹², approximately one in 16.8 million. Their expected number in the same simplified genome is about 0.24. These numbers are not estimates of the true number of bacterial promoters, since real promoters tolerate multiple sequences, DNA composition is unequal and occurrences are not always independent. The calculation demonstrates the statistical value of combined recognition constraints rather than providing a literal model of bacterial transcription.

A promoter with one substitution in the −35 hexamer may retain activity if the other recognition contacts remain favorable. Similarly, a promoter with a weak −35 element can be functional when additional elements or regulatory proteins compensate. Some promoters with a near-consensus pair of hexamers nevertheless initiate inefficiently because the subsequent transition to an open complex or promoter escape is unfavorable. Thus promoter prediction from sequence alone involves a hierarchy: identifying candidate recognition sites is a useful first step, evaluating their geometry and context is a second, and measuring correctly initiated RNA provides the functional test.

The AT-rich nature of the familiar −10 sequence is connected to its role in the region of DNA opening. However, the biological significance is not explained simply by saying that adenine and thymine form two hydrogen bonds while guanine and cytosine form three. Base stacking, the DNA sequence context and interactions with the polymerase also affect the energetics. Sigma recognition of particular exposed bases contributes sequence specificity after the strands have separated. The chemical composition of the DNA influences the ease with which opening occurs, while protein recognition determines whether the opened state is stabilized in the appropriate orientation. These are related but distinguishable properties.

7. Spacer length, helical geometry and start-site positioning

The spacer is the DNA segment between the two recognition hexamers. With a −35 element extending from −35 through −30 and a −10 element extending from −12 through −7, the intervening positions are −29 through −13. Counting these inclusively gives seventeen base pairs. This is the basis for the common description of a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter as containing a seventeen-base spacer. The distance between the first nucleotide of one hexamer and the first nucleotide of the other is twenty-three base positions, not seventeen. The examination question must be read carefully to determine which distance is being requested.

Spacer length influences the ability of sigma to contact the two recognition elements simultaneously. DNA is a double helix rather than a straight ribbon. As the number of intervening base pairs changes, the next recognition surface is both displaced along the helical axis and rotated around it. The preferred geometry of a protein complex may therefore become less favorable when bases are inserted or deleted between otherwise intact elements. The presence of two consensus hexamers is not enough to establish that their relative placement is suitable.

For a rough geometric model, B-form DNA has about 10.5 base pairs per helical turn and an axial rise of approximately 0.34 nanometres per base pair. An insertion of one base pair changes the relative phase of the downstream element by roughly 360/10.5 degrees, or 34 degrees, and changes the axial separation by about 0.34 nanometres. An insertion of two bases doubles these estimates to about 69 degrees and 0.68 nanometres. Actual promoter DNA can bend, twist and deform in the complex, so these values describe ideal free DNA and should not be confused with measured distances between protein domains. Nevertheless, the model explains why small spacing changes can have substantial functional consequences even without changing any nucleotide within a recognition hexamer.

An insertion of approximately one helical turn restores much of the original rotational relationship but adds considerable axial distance. It would therefore be incorrect to assume that insertion of eleven bases automatically restores wild-type activity merely because the motifs again face nearly the same direction. The sigma regions must still reach their respective DNA elements, and the rest of the promoter must still fit the catalytic cleft. In some regulatory systems long-range protein contacts are influenced by helical phasing, but the core bacterial promoter is additionally constrained by the short distance over which different parts of one holoenzyme must interact.

The spacer is not necessarily biologically inert. Its sequence affects DNA flexibility and can influence the relative ease of forming an initiation complex. An insertion may also introduce a new recognition element or change a local sequence-dependent bend. To investigate length specifically, it is useful to compare several insertion variants of the same length but different base sequences. If their activities are similar to each other and different from the original, a length-related interpretation becomes stronger. If variants of equal length behave differently, sequence-dependent effects must also be considered.

The distance from the −10 element to the transcription start site creates another constraint. When the downstream DNA enters the active center, the template base that occupies the initiating position determines the first nucleotide incorporated into RNA. The geometry of the opened DNA, sequence-dependent nucleotide preference and the concentrations of initiating substrates all help influence the selection. The +1 position is often a purine on the nontemplate strand, corresponding to initiation with ATP or GTP, but the use of a pyrimidine as the first nucleotide is possible. Thus the statement that every bacterial transcript begins with adenine is false.

Start-site selection can be investigated by changing the position of a preferred initiating nucleotide without changing the upstream recognition motifs. An experiment might reveal two RNA products that differ in their first few bases but share the remainder of the transcription unit. This outcome is consistent with use of alternative transcription starts. It is distinct from the presence of two separate promoters, because the same binding arrangement may support initiation at neighboring positions. Additional mapping is needed before concluding which explanation is correct.

Consider a hypothetical promoter with a normal spacer and a start at a defined adenine. A two-base insertion upstream of the −10 element, between the two hexamers, can impair simultaneous recognition without changing the sequence surrounding the start. A two-base insertion between the −10 element and +1 can instead alter the relationship of the start region to the binding site. The two mutations insert the same number of bases but change different geometrical relationships. This distinction is why a mutation description must specify its position rather than merely reporting its size.

8. Additional promoter elements and natural exceptions

Although the classical two-hexamer picture is an excellent introduction, some important bacterial promoters use additional recognition surfaces. These extend the range of sequences capable of supporting initiation. An upstream promoter element, known as the UP element, is a region often enriched in adenine and thymine that can interact with the carboxyl-terminal domains of the RNA polymerase alpha subunits. It lies upstream of the usual −35 element, commonly in a region approximately between −40 and −60, although the exact functional boundaries depend on the promoter.

The alpha subunit therefore participates in more than core assembly. Its flexible connection between the amino-terminal assembly domain and the carboxyl-terminal DNA-contacting domain allows it to contribute to promoter recognition at positions upstream of the principal sigma contacts. This arrangement can stabilize the promoter-bound complex or help establish favorable geometry. A promoter that includes an effective UP element may recruit polymerase much more efficiently than an otherwise identical promoter without it.

A classical example comes from studies of the E. coli ribosomal RNA promoter rrnB P1. Ross and colleagues reported in 1993 that an upstream AT-rich element could greatly stimulate transcription and that the alpha subunit participated in recognition [4]. Their findings established an additional direct RNA polymerase–DNA interaction rather than requiring a separate transcription activator for the effect. The distinction between direct recognition by alpha and regulation through an independent DNA-binding protein is important. Both can increase polymerase recruitment, but a mutation removing alpha's DNA-contacting surface and a mutation removing an activator do not necessarily produce identical results.

A second important variation is the extended −10 element. Some σ⁷⁰ promoters contain a short additional sequence, often represented by a TG motif immediately upstream of the −10 hexamer. This feature interacts with another region of sigma and may allow efficient recognition despite a weak or absent recognizable −35 sequence. The familiar elements should therefore be treated as a family of compatible contacts rather than an obligatory checklist. A sequence lacking TTGACA is not automatically disqualified from functioning as a bacterial promoter.

The discriminator is located between the −10 element and the transcription start region. Its sequence can influence the stability and lifetime of the open complex and therefore how initiation responds to changing conditions. This feature is particularly useful for distinguishing initial DNA binding from later kinetic events. Two promoters with identical −35 and −10 motifs may have different discriminator sequences and different lifetimes in the open state. An investigator who measures binding but not productive RNA formation can miss this difference.

The initially transcribed region is also relevant. Once transcription starts, those nucleotides determine the sequence of the short RNA and the nature of its contacts with the active center, the DNA template and other parts of the initiating complex. Changing this region can influence abortive-product formation and escape even if the upstream recognition elements are unchanged. Consequently, the functional boundary of an initiation region extends beyond the two most familiar upstream motifs. A region that is copied into RNA can still affect the initiation process that produces the RNA.

A conceptual example makes the modular nature of promoters clearer. Promoter A has recognizable −35 and −10 elements and no strong additional contacts. Promoter B has a weak −35 element but a favorable extended −10 region. Promoter C contains both conventional elements and an effective UP element. It is reasonable to predict that their binding mechanisms will not be identical, but it is not safe to rank their complete transcriptional output solely from these descriptions. Each promoter still has to open, choose a start, synthesize its first nucleotides and release its upstream contacts. The relative effectiveness of each additional interaction depends on the remaining steps.

Promoter modularity also provides an experimentally useful strategy. One can compare wild-type polymerase and a derivative with altered alpha upstream-contacting ability on templates that differ only in their UP elements. If removal of an UP element reduces transcription strongly with wild-type polymerase but has little additional effect with the alpha mutant, the two changes may affect the same functional interaction. This is a form of genetic or biochemical interaction reasoning. The absence of an additional effect is not absolute proof of direct physical contact, because both mutations might independently drive a process to a floor below which it cannot be measured. Supporting evidence comes from DNA-binding and structural studies, but the comparison itself is a valuable examination-level prediction.

9. Alternative sigma factors and the sigma-54 exception

The same catalytic core can function with different sigma factors, allowing a bacterium to direct transcription toward different classes of promoters. Members of the σ⁷⁰ family share broad structural and functional principles but differ in their recognition surfaces and other properties. A housekeeping sigma factor usually supports a large set of genes required for routine growth, while alternative factors direct the transcription machinery toward subsets of genes relevant to specific developmental or environmental conditions. These categories are useful descriptions of dominant physiological roles, not strict divisions that prevent overlap.

In E. coli, σ³² provides a classic example of a factor involved in responses to heat-associated protein damage. σ³⁸, commonly called RpoS, participates in stationary-phase and stress-associated expression, and σ²⁸ contributes to expression of flagellar genes. In Bacillus subtilis, the housekeeping sigma factor is commonly called σᴬ, and alternative factors participate in the temporal and cellular organization of sporulation. These examples demonstrate how a targeting principle is reused in different organisms. They do not imply that all bacteria possess E. coli's particular set of sigma proteins.

Some examination questions present a strain in which a particular alternative sigma is inactivated. If a promoter is recognized mainly by that sigma, its output is expected to decline more strongly than that of an otherwise comparable housekeeping promoter. However, residual transcription may occur through another factor with partially overlapping specificity. The phenotype can be conditional because a promoter that is used only under a particular stress may contribute little under standard laboratory growth. A lack of phenotype in one environment therefore does not prove that a sigma factor is biologically irrelevant.

A distinctive family is represented by σ⁵⁴, encoded by rpoN in E. coli. Its promoter recognition is commonly associated with elements near −24 and −12 rather than the canonical −35 and −10 arrangement of the σ⁷⁰ family. The functional difference extends beyond sequence preference. The σ⁵⁴ holoenzyme ordinarily forms a stable closed promoter complex that requires remodeling by a specialized activator using ATP hydrolysis to achieve the open state. This is a standard exception to the general description of bacterial promoter opening as occurring through the binding and conformational behavior of the σ⁷⁰ holoenzyme without an obligatory separate ATP-driven initiation motor [6].

It is important to distinguish ATP as an initiating nucleotide from ATP as the fuel for an activator. ATP can serve as one of the four ribonucleoside triphosphates used for polymerization, in which case its alpha phosphate becomes part of RNA during an addition reaction or the initiating ATP retains its original triphosphate at the RNA's 5′ end. An ATPase activator, by contrast, hydrolyzes ATP in a separate reaction to support conformational remodeling. The latter can be required even when the RNA sequence does not begin with adenosine. These two roles concern different chemical events and should not be conflated simply because ATP appears in both.

Imagine an assay with an intact σ⁵⁴ holoenzyme and a suitable promoter. A mutation in the activator's ATP-hydrolysis machinery leaves binding of the holoenzyme to DNA largely intact but prevents efficient formation of the melted initiation complex. In a separate extension assay, a preassembled catalytic core may still extend a short RNA normally. This combination of observations identifies the problem as initiation-specific remodeling rather than general RNA synthesis chemistry. A mutation in the active center of core polymerase would be expected to compromise extension itself.

The comparison with σ⁷⁰ also helps clarify what an activator does in other bacterial systems. A transcription activator may improve recruitment, stabilize an intermediate or increase the rate of opening without necessarily using ATP hydrolysis directly. It is inaccurate to infer that because one family uses an ATP-driven remodeling mechanism, every bacterial activator must work the same way. Likewise, the absence of a separate ATPase requirement in a classical σ⁷⁰ promoter does not make initiation energetically costless: association and DNA opening involve changes in free energy, and subsequent polymerization uses energetic nucleotide substrates.

The term alternative sigma factor should not imply a second type of catalytic RNA polymerase. Changing sigma changes the initiation specificity module while retaining the core catalytic architecture. This organization differs from the division of labor among the major nuclear RNA polymerases in eukaryotes, which are distinct multisubunit enzymes. The comparison is useful for preventing a common conceptual transfer error between bacterial and eukaryotic transcription.

10. From promoter search to the closed initiation complex

Before any DNA strand can be copied, holoenzyme must encounter a promoter. Molecular motion in the cytoplasm is influenced by diffusion, interactions with DNA, the presence of other macromolecules and the physical organization of the chromosome. RNA polymerase can associate transiently with nonpromoter DNA, and these interactions need not lead to transcription. Encounter with an appropriate sequence allows additional contacts to form and can increase the probability that the complex persists long enough to proceed toward initiation.

It is tempting to imagine RNA polymerase systematically reading every base in the chromosome until it finds the exact consensus sequence. This picture is too mechanical. Proteins recognize DNA through physical and chemical interactions with accessible molecular surfaces, and promoter search involves stochastic encounters rather than an obligatory sequential inspection of all bases. Different search pathways and DNA contexts can influence encounter efficiency. At the level needed here, the central distinction is between nonspecific interactions and formation of a promoter-specific recognition complex. Neither requires the production of an RNA molecule at the moment of initial contact.

The first conventional state in the simplified initiation pathway is the closed complex, often denoted RPc, where R represents polymerase holoenzyme and P represents promoter DNA. The designation closed refers primarily to the base-paired state of the promoter DNA in the starting region, not to the absence of any interaction between the polymerase and the DNA. The holoenzyme has located and associated with the promoter, but the template around the transcription start site is not yet exposed in the arrangement necessary for initiation.

This distinction allows a binding assay to be interpreted without making an unwarranted claim about RNA synthesis. If purified holoenzyme causes a promoter DNA fragment to migrate more slowly during electrophoresis, it may have formed a complex. Such an observation can reveal binding under appropriate controls. It does not, however, directly establish that a transcription bubble has formed or that the first nucleotide has been incorporated. Additional tests are needed to distinguish a closed complex, an open complex and a complex that has already synthesized a short RNA.

The initial recognition event is generally reversible. If the complex dissociates before the next transition, the promoter becomes available for another encounter. Binding therefore competes with the conformational changes that commit the enzyme to subsequent steps. A promoter mutation that increases the rate of dissociation can reduce the probability of opening even if the rate at which polymerase first arrives is unaffected. Conversely, a change that slows opening can reduce productive initiation even when the binding equilibrium appears favorable.

A simple two-state equilibrium provides a starting quantitative description. Let free holoenzyme concentration be R and let promoter occupancy by closed complexes be θ. For binding of one holoenzyme to one independent promoter site, an equilibrium dissociation constant Kd is defined by the product of free concentrations divided by the bound-complex concentration. Under the assumption that free holoenzyme is effectively fixed, the fractional occupancy is θ = R/(Kd + R). Both R and Kd must use the same concentration units. This relationship follows from dividing the bound promoter amount by the sum of bound and unbound promoter amounts.

For a hypothetical promoter with Kd equal to 20 nM and free holoenzyme concentration equal to 20 nM, θ equals 0.5. Increasing the enzyme concentration to 80 nM raises θ to 80/100, or 0.8. A mutant with Kd equal to 80 nM at the original 20 nM enzyme concentration has θ equal to 0.2. These values predict the fraction of sites in a bound state under the simplified equilibrium assumptions. They do not directly predict the fraction of sites producing full transcripts, because the rates of opening and productive escape have not been specified.

This is a useful place to compare affinity with transcriptional flux. High affinity means that the bound state is favored relative to the unbound state at equilibrium. High flux means that many productive transcription events occur per unit time. An enzyme that binds tightly but progresses slowly may occupy a promoter for a long time without producing many RNAs. Another enzyme may spend less time in the bound state but move efficiently through initiation. The distinction between occupancy and flux appears throughout biochemistry and is central to interpreting promoter strength.

The closed complex is best regarded as an ensemble of possible molecular arrangements rather than an inflexible statue. Some complexes contain downstream DNA that has begun to enter the active-site cleft, while others may still be reorganizing interactions. Detailed studies of bacterial initiation support multiple intermediates rather than a universal single closed-to-open elementary event [7]. The two-state representation remains useful at examination level when it is explicitly recognized as a simplified description of a pathway that can contain additional steps.

11. Open-complex formation: making a template accessible

The DNA bound by RNA polymerase is initially a double helix. The bases that carry the genetic information are predominantly paired and stacked within the helix. For nucleotide selection during transcription, the enzyme needs access to the hydrogen-bonding surfaces of individual template bases. The next essential transition is therefore not simply tighter binding to the same double-stranded DNA. It involves local rearrangement of the polymerase and DNA to expose an appropriately positioned single-stranded template.

In a classical σ⁷⁰ initiation pathway, promoter-bound polymerase bends and rearranges the DNA, facilitating entry of the downstream region into the catalytic cleft. Multiple contacts with the promoter and the polymerase stabilize a sequence of intermediate configurations. DNA then becomes locally unwound near the transcription start, and the template and nontemplate strands adopt different paths through the enzyme. In a commonly studied E. coli model, a region approximately from −11 through +2 is open. This is thirteen base pairs when the absence of position zero is taken into account: eleven positions upstream and two downstream. The boundaries vary with the promoter and the stage of initiation, so the figure should be understood as a representative scale rather than a constant.

A particularly important aspect of opening is recognition of exposed nontemplate bases by sigma. At an ordinary double-stranded DNA-binding site, a protein can recognize features of bases accessible in the grooves without separating the strands. At the melted promoter, individual exposed bases can make different interactions with the protein. Favorable contacts with the nontemplate strand can stabilize the open state while the complementary template strand is organized in the active center. Thus sigma participates in both initial recognition and the transition to a state in which DNA can be copied.

The energetic explanation requires care. Breaking interactions between paired and stacked bases costs free energy under the usual conditions in which the double helix is stable. However, formation of new protein–DNA contacts can provide favorable interactions that compensate for this cost. DNA bending and structural changes in the polymerase are also part of the free-energy balance. The overall reaction depends on the sum of these contributions, not on the cost of disrupting base pairing considered in isolation. An enzyme can favor a locally open configuration without possessing a separate helicase motor that moves processively along the chromosome.

This is a useful contrast with DNA replication. The replication fork employs a dedicated helicase to separate parental strands over an extended region as DNA is copied. A bacterial transcription initiation complex ordinarily melts a comparatively small promoter region through the concerted interactions of the holoenzyme and DNA. During transcription elongation the bubble moves along the template, with DNA opening ahead and reannealing behind. Local melting at initiation is therefore not equivalent to replication-fork unwinding. The distinction also explains why bacterial RNA polymerase does not ordinarily require an externally supplied primer or a separate helicase for its classical σ⁷⁰ initiation reaction.

An open complex, denoted RPo, is an initiation-competent polymerase–promoter assembly in which the template is exposed near the active center. It can form before the nucleotide substrates required for RNA synthesis are supplied. For example, a purified holoenzyme and promoter can be incubated without ribonucleoside triphosphates under conditions that favor open-complex formation. If chemical probing reveals unpaired DNA around the expected start site, opening has occurred even though no RNA product has been made. Addition of the appropriate substrates can then test whether that configuration supports initiation. This order of observations distinguishes structural readiness from chemical activity.

Open complexes do not all have the same lifetime. Some are relatively stable and can survive an experimental challenge that removes weakly bound enzyme, whereas others dissociate or reclose rapidly. A promoter with an open complex that lasts only a few seconds may still function effectively if the first nucleotide addition occurs quickly. A promoter that forms a long-lived open complex may nevertheless initiate slowly if subsequent RNA synthesis or escape is unfavorable. Stability is therefore meaningful only in relation to the rates of competing events. A long-lived intermediate is beneficial when it prevents unproductive reversal, but it can become a kinetic trap when transition out of it is slow.

A hypothetical mutation that changes a favorable −10 base into a base that is poorly accommodated by sigma may affect both recognition and opening. If binding falls only slightly but the chemical signal for unpaired DNA falls sharply, the primary defect may concern the isomerization process rather than initial recruitment. If the mutant is equally defective on a promoter substrate with a deliberately exposed template region, an additional problem may exist in the catalytic configuration or start-site positioning. Such comparisons transform descriptive knowledge of the open complex into testable molecular reasoning.

12. The physical chemistry of opening and DNA topology

For a fixed set of conditions, promoter opening can be discussed as an equilibrium between ensembles of closed and open states. The standard free-energy difference ΔG° between them is related to a dimensionless equilibrium constant K by ΔG° = −RT ln K. Here R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and the natural logarithm is used. For a simplified intracomplex transition, K is the ratio of the concentrations of open and closed complexes at equilibrium. This treatment does not describe a complete actively transcribing system, in which substrates are consumed and products are released. It is a model for understanding the relative favorability of two promoter-bound states.

At 310 K, RT is approximately 2.58 kJ per mole when the gas constant is expressed as 0.008314 kJ per mole per kelvin. Suppose a hypothetical promoter has an open-to-closed equilibrium ratio of 10. Its standard free-energy difference for opening is approximately −2.58 times ln 10, or −5.93 kJ per mole. A different promoter has a ratio of 0.1, corresponding to +5.93 kJ per mole. The difference in the free energies is therefore about 11.86 kJ per mole even though both systems use the same catalytic enzyme. The example shows how relatively modest changes in interactions can produce large differences in state populations.

If only these two bound states are considered, the fraction of bound complexes that are open is K/(1 + K). For K equal to 10 this is about 0.909, while for K equal to 0.1 it is about 0.091. The denominator in this calculation includes only bound complexes. It does not include free promoter unless the model is expanded to include the initial binding equilibrium. Therefore it would be wrong to interpret the fraction 0.909 as the fraction of all promoter molecules that are open if only a small proportion are bound. An examination problem may intentionally distinguish these two denominators.

The rate of reaching the open state is a different property from the equilibrium fraction. A favorable open state may be reached slowly if an intermediate configuration has a high activation barrier. The rate constant for a transition depends on that barrier and on the molecular pathway, whereas the equilibrium ratio concerns the free-energy difference between endpoints. A mutation may lower the activation barrier without substantially changing the final equilibrium. Such a mutation could accelerate initiation under conditions in which opening is rate limiting, even if measurements made after a very long incubation detect similar proportions of open complexes.

DNA topology supplies another physical contribution. In a covalently closed circular DNA molecule, linking number cannot change without strand breakage and resealing or an equivalent topological intervention. The familiar relation Lk = Tw + Wr states that linking number is the sum of twist and writhe. A negatively supercoiled DNA molecule can have an underwound tendency relative to relaxed DNA, which can favor local strand separation in suitable sequence contexts. Formation of a transcription bubble changes the local twist and requires compensating changes in the rest of a constrained DNA molecule. The resulting energetic effects can influence initiation.

The topology relationship does not imply that negative supercoiling always increases transcription from every promoter. The response depends on which initiation step is limiting, sequence-dependent DNA properties and interactions with the surrounding DNA. Some promoters are more responsive than others, and excessive or altered supercoiling may favor alternative conformations or change regulatory interactions. A useful examination-level statement is that negative supercoiling can facilitate promoter melting, all else being equal, while the actual change in productive output is promoter dependent.

A classroom calculation illustrates the scale of the change without implying that DNA behaves as a perfectly rigid helix. Opening thirteen base pairs would remove roughly 13/10.5, or 1.24 turns of local helical twist relative to a simple B-DNA reference if the opened region were assumed to contribute no duplex twist. The remaining DNA of a closed circular molecule would have to accommodate the topology change. The calculation is only a geometric approximation: the separated strands and protein-bound DNA can adopt complex paths, and the transcription complex itself changes the distribution of twist and writhe. This is why the same number should not be identified with a universal experimentally measured change in supercoiling.

To evaluate topology experimentally, one can compare initiation on identical promoter sequences presented in DNA molecules of different topological states. It is important that template amount and polymerase concentration be matched. A change in transcription may otherwise reflect the amount of DNA available rather than its physical state. If the negatively supercoiled form exhibits a stronger opening signal and more initiation than the relaxed form, the observations support a contribution of topology to the formation of the open complex. The experiment still does not establish that topology acts solely on opening, because initial binding and escape may also be affected.

The distinction among affinity, free-energy differences, kinetic barriers and topological constraints is not an unnecessary mathematical extension. These ideas explain why superficially similar promoter sequences can behave differently and why apparently contradictory measurements may both be correct. They also connect the mechanism of transcription to foundational principles of thermodynamics and nucleic acid structure. A learner who understands the distinction can interpret an unfamiliar dataset without memorizing every individual promoter.

13. Beginning RNA synthesis without a primer

Once the template is exposed and the transcription start site is positioned in the active center, the next problem is forming the first phosphodiester bond. DNA polymerases generally require an existing primer with a suitable 3′ hydroxyl group. The bacterial RNA polymerase can instead begin a new chain de novo. It accommodates an initiating ribonucleoside triphosphate and a second complementary ribonucleoside triphosphate at adjacent positions, bringing their reactive groups into a configuration appropriate for bond formation.

The first nucleotide is selected largely through complementarity with the template base at +1, together with properties of the active center and the local initiation sequence. For example, a template thymine at +1 corresponds to adenine in the nascent RNA. If the next template nucleotide in the direction of reading is guanine, the second RNA nucleotide is cytidine. The first product is therefore 5′-AC-3′. The direction of reading is crucial: identifying the next base on a template written from 5′ to 3′ may require moving toward its left rather than its right.

The first initiating nucleotide already has a 3′ hydroxyl group in its ribose. The formation of the initial phosphodiester bond uses that hydroxyl as the nucleophile attacking the alpha phosphorus of the second nucleotide triphosphate. Metal ions in the active center, commonly magnesium, support appropriate geometry, charge stabilization and catalysis. The beta and gamma phosphates of the second nucleotide leave together as pyrophosphate. A new bond is formed between the 3′ oxygen of the first nucleotide and the 5′ phosphate of the second nucleotide. The resulting RNA dinucleotide has a new terminal 3′ hydroxyl group available for the next addition.

An important distinction concerns the 5′ end of the transcript. The first nucleotide was not added to a preexisting RNA hydroxyl, so its original triphosphate is normally retained in an RNA made by ordinary de novo initiation with a nucleotide triphosphate. By contrast, each later incorporated nucleotide contributes one phosphate to the growing phosphodiester backbone and releases its other two phosphate groups as pyrophosphate. Subsequent cellular processing can modify the 5′ chemical state, and alternative initiating substrates occur in some biological contexts. The standard examination answer about a primary bacterial RNA transcript is nevertheless that conventional de novo initiation produces a 5′ triphosphate.

The reaction can be represented schematically as pppN₁ + pppN₂ → pppN₁pN₂ + PPi for the first bond. The symbols ppp and p indicate the triphosphate of the initiating nucleotide and the single phosphate participating in the internucleotide linkage. For each subsequent addition, the generic reaction is RNA of length n plus an incoming NTP yielding RNA of length n + 1 and pyrophosphate. If a conventional unprocessed transcript is made from twenty nucleotides, nineteen internucleotide bonds are formed and nineteen pyrophosphate molecules are released directly by polymerization. The initiating triphosphate is not counted as a released pyrophosphate.

Hydrolysis of pyrophosphate by cellular pyrophosphatases can favor the overall synthesis process by reducing the concentration of a product of nucleotide addition. This does not mean that a separate ATP molecule is hydrolyzed for every phosphodiester bond. The incoming nucleotide itself supplies chemical energy through its triphosphate group. Nor does it imply that polymerization is absolutely irreversible under all conditions; the reverse reaction can be significant in appropriate biochemical systems. The physiological direction depends on reactant and product concentrations and the coupling to other reactions in the cell.

The concentrations of initiating NTPs can be particularly important because the first short RNA has few stabilizing interactions with the template and polymerase. If the preferred initiating nucleotide is scarce, a promoter may bind polymerase and form an open complex but initiate less frequently. The magnitude of this effect depends on the promoter and the stability of the open complex. A long-lived open complex has more time to encounter an initiating substrate; a short-lived complex can be lost before the relevant nucleotide is captured. The same change in nucleotide concentration can therefore have unequal effects on different promoters.

A useful conceptual assay exploits the sequence of the initial transcript. If a promoter is designed or selected such that its first two RNA positions require ATP and CTP, a reaction lacking CTP may allow binding and opening but cannot produce the normal dinucleotide through the ordinary templated addition reaction. Supplying CTP should restore formation of the dinucleotide if the rest of the machinery is intact. This observation localizes the block downstream of promoter recognition. However, a very short RNA product detected under a restricted substrate mixture must be interpreted carefully, since it may reflect artificial stalling rather than the natural frequency of abortive initiation.

Finally, one should not mistake primer independence for a difference in basic synthesis polarity. Both RNA polymerase and ordinary DNA polymerases extend the growing strand at its 3′ end, producing chains in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The distinctive property at initiation is the ability of RNA polymerase to position the first two substrates without relying on a preexisting primer strand. Once a short RNA has formed, the fundamental rule of addition to an existing 3′ hydroxyl applies just as it does during later RNA chain extension.

14. Initial transcription and the mechanical origin of DNA scrunching

The initiation complex cannot become a productive elongation complex simply by making its first bond. At the beginning of RNA synthesis the enzyme is still held by interactions with the upstream promoter. However, its active center must access successive template bases to add nucleotides to the growing RNA. This creates a geometrical problem: if the whole polymerase were to move downstream immediately, it would have to relinquish the promoter contacts that helped establish the initiation complex. If it remained entirely static with no internal rearrangement, it could not bring new template bases into the catalytic center. The mechanism of initial transcription resolves this apparent contradiction.

The term DNA scrunching describes a process in which downstream DNA is drawn into a promoter-anchored polymerase as the short RNA grows. In this picture the upstream promoter contacts remain largely maintained during early additions, whereas extra downstream DNA enters the enzyme and additional DNA is locally unwound. This allows the active center to use successive template bases without requiring the whole promoter-binding arrangement to translate along the DNA by an equivalent distance at each early step. The DNA accommodated within the complex occupies a progressively altered configuration.

The conceptual model can be distinguished from two alternatives. In one alternative, sometimes represented as transient forward movement, the entire enzyme would shift downstream as RNA grew and would subsequently return to the start site if the short product were released. In another, conformational changes of polymerase would extend its reach downstream without the additional DNA unwinding expected from a scrunching mechanism. Scrunching predicts that the amount of unwound DNA increases with the length of the initial RNA while upstream contacts remain relatively fixed. Experimental tests that can distinguish DNA movement from enzyme movement were therefore important in establishing the model.

In 2006, Revyakin and colleagues reported single-molecule observations supporting DNA scrunching during abortive as well as productive initiation [8]. Changes in the physical configuration of DNA linked to its unwinding could be monitored as RNA polymerase initiated transcription. The results supported a stressed intermediate in which additional DNA is accommodated before the polymerase escapes the promoter. The significance of this work was not simply that another DNA conformation had been observed. It provided a mechanistic link between the synthesis of a short RNA and the eventual disruption of promoter contacts.

One can understand the experiment at a conceptual level using a torsionally constrained DNA molecule. Local opening changes the number of paired helical turns and therefore changes the distribution of twist and writhe in the DNA. When the molecule is held under appropriate physical constraints, these changes can be converted into a measurable change in its extension. Observing the changes over time allows transient opening events to be distinguished from a stable open state. The researchers could compare the amount of unwinding associated with different stages of transcription. A student does not need specialist instrument design to understand the prediction: more DNA unwinding during synthesis of a longer initial RNA supports accumulation of additional DNA within the initiation complex.

A hypothetical sequence of observations might begin with a promoter opened in the absence of NTPs. On allowing formation of short RNAs, additional transient unwinding would be detected. If the complexes repeatedly release their products and return to an open state, the additional unwinding signal would repeatedly disappear. When a productive complex forms, the promoter-associated configuration is lost and the enzyme enters the next phase of transcription. The relationship between RNA length and extra unwinding distinguishes these events from random DNA melting that occurs independently of RNA synthesis.

Scrunching stores stress in the nucleic acid and protein arrangement. It is reasonable to understand this stress as a contribution to the free-energy balance that may favor either release of the short RNA or disruption of upstream promoter contacts. It should not be pictured as a literal coil spring with a universal force constant, because the molecular details vary with sequence, enzyme and initiation state. The useful causal principle is that synthesis progressively changes the balance of interactions holding the initial complex together.

The nontemplate strand also has to be accommodated as extra DNA is unwound. The template strand provides bases for RNA synthesis, while the separated nontemplate strand follows another route. Both contribute to the geometric constraints of the scrunched complex. As the short RNA grows, the RNA itself occupies space near the active center and begins to encounter structural elements of sigma that were useful for positioning the initial substrate but may obstruct continued growth. Thus the transition from initial synthesis to elongation depends on a coordinated rearrangement of DNA, RNA and proteins, not simply on the attainment of one predetermined RNA length.

15. Abortive initiation: why short RNA products are released

A promoter-bound polymerase can begin transcription repeatedly without completing a full transcript. It may synthesize a short RNA, release it and remain in or return to a promoter-associated state capable of beginning again. This behavior is called abortive initiation. The term refers to unsuccessful completion of the transition into productive elongation, not to the normal termination of a full-length RNA at the end of its transcription unit.

The early RNA is relatively short and has limited interactions with the DNA template. Its stability depends on its sequence, its length and its contacts with polymerase. As it grows, it may encounter structural constraints arising from the arrangement of sigma and the scrunched DNA. There are competing possibilities: the RNA may be retained while promoter contacts are reorganized, or the short RNA may be released and the enzyme may reset. Some promoter–polymerase combinations undergo numerous abortive cycles, whereas others escape efficiently with relatively few released short RNAs.

A short RNA product is often less stable in a complex than a longer RNA–DNA hybrid. However, length alone does not determine the probability of release. Sequence affects base pairing and stacking, the identity of the incoming nucleotide affects the next chemical transition, and the strength of upstream promoter interactions influences the energetic cost of escape. A mutation in a region not transcribed into RNA may still influence abortive initiation by changing the promoter contacts that must be overcome. Conversely, a mutation in the initially transcribed sequence may affect abortive output without significantly changing holoenzyme recruitment.

The distinction can be demonstrated by comparing the RNA products from two hypothetical promoters with identical −35 and −10 motifs but different initially transcribed sequences. Suppose both show equal binding and similar open-complex formation, yet one yields abundant two- to eight-nucleotide products and relatively few long RNAs. The other produces many more run-off transcripts and fewer short products. These observations identify a difference in the early transcription pathway rather than a simple failure of promoter recognition. The data do not by themselves prove that a particular sigma–RNA steric contact caused the difference, but they constrain the explanation to events after opening and before successful elongation.

In a simplified probability model, let p be the probability that a given initial transcription attempt becomes productive. Assume that an unsuccessful attempt returns the enzyme to an equivalent state, that it remains associated with the promoter, and that successive attempts are independent. The number of attempts until the first success then follows a geometric distribution. Its mean is 1/p. The expected number of unsuccessful attempts before a successful one is (1 − p)/p. These results follow because the expected number of attempts E satisfies E = 1 + (1 − p)E: one attempt always occurs, and after failure the same expected sequence begins again. Rearranging gives pE = 1.

For a hypothetical promoter with p equal to 0.20, the expected number of attempts per successful transcript is five, of which four are unsuccessful on average. If a mutation changes p to 0.50 under otherwise identical conditions, the average number of attempts becomes two and the average number of unsuccessful attempts becomes one. This is an example of why an increase in productive RNA can arise even when the rate of arrival of polymerase at the promoter is unchanged. More of the polymerase's repeated early efforts are converted into successful transcription events.

The model omits several real possibilities. A failed attempt may result in loss of polymerase from DNA rather than complete reset. The probabilities may change as the complex undergoes conformational rearrangements. RNA products may have different lengths, and some may be degraded or retained in a way that changes the relationship between observed product abundance and the number of failed attempts. Thus the ratio of short products to full transcripts can approximate an abortive-to-productive ratio only under carefully specified conditions. A gel band of greater intensity is not automatically evidence of a greater number of molecules if the detection method labels different lengths or sequences differently.

The energetic cost of abortive initiation is real even though the genomic DNA remains intact. Each released RNA contains chemical bonds made using ribonucleotide triphosphates, and its synthesis occupies time during which the promoter may be unavailable for another productive initiation event. Under a simplified model in which four abortive RNAs of length five precede one productive RNA of length one hundred, the abortive products account for twenty incorporated nucleotides and sixteen internucleotide bond-forming events. The productive product accounts for one hundred nucleotides and ninety-nine bond-forming events. The expenditure attributable to unsuccessful attempts is therefore not captured by measuring only the full-length transcript.

Abortive transcription is not necessarily pathological. It is an intrinsic outcome of competition among possible early initiation states, and its frequency can be affected by normal regulatory conditions. What matters biologically is the relationship between those short cycles and the flux of productive RNA. This perspective also prevents the misconception that transcription initiation is a single all-or-nothing event. The recognition of a promoter is followed by a sequence of reversible and partially committed steps, and the synthesis of a few nucleotides does not guarantee successful completion.

16. Promoter clearance and commitment to elongation

Promoter clearance, also called promoter escape, is the transition in which the initial transcription complex relinquishes the upstream interactions that hold it at the promoter and begins movement along the transcribed DNA as an elongation complex. The word clearance refers to the departure of the polymerase from its initiation arrangement, not the removal of the promoter DNA from the chromosome. The promoter remains a functional sequence and can participate in further initiation cycles once it becomes accessible again.

Several events favor this transition. As RNA grows, a longer RNA–DNA hybrid can help stabilize the transcription complex. The additional DNA accommodated during early synthesis contributes stress to the initiation arrangement. The growing RNA also conflicts with portions of sigma that occupy or approach the RNA path at earlier stages. To continue transcription, interactions among sigma, polymerase, DNA and RNA have to change. Upstream contacts are broken, the initiation configuration is reorganized and the enzyme is able to progress downstream. The detailed timing of these changes is not identical at every promoter.

Textbooks often associate promoter clearance with an RNA length of roughly ten nucleotides. This is a useful order-of-magnitude guide to the transition in many classical bacterial examples, not an exact molecular switch. At some promoters, escape occurs efficiently over a relatively narrow range of RNA lengths; at others, intermediate states persist longer or short products are released repeatedly. A learner should interpret a diagram labeled ten nucleotides as a representative stage of initiation rather than a universal rule that a nine-nucleotide RNA must abort and a ten-nucleotide RNA must escape.

An important historical simplification concerns sigma release. In the classic transcription cycle, sigma is shown dissociating from core as polymerase enters elongation, making it available to form new holoenzyme molecules. This picture correctly emphasizes the reuse of the initiation factor, but promoter escape and complete sigma dissociation are distinguishable events. The essential feature of escape is the loss or reorganization of initiation-specific promoter contacts. Sigma can remain associated with some complexes for a time after escape, and certain downstream sequence features can allow retained sigma to influence early elongation behavior [5]. It is therefore inaccurate to define escape as instantaneous and obligatory loss of every sigma interaction.

The transition can be studied by monitoring the location of polymerase relative to the promoter and the RNA products produced. Suppose an experiment uses a short linear DNA fragment with a defined promoter and a downstream end. An RNA that reaches the end of the template has a predictable length determined by the mapped start position and the template boundary. Such an RNA is called a run-off transcript. Detecting the expected run-off product supports productive initiation followed by successful extension to the template end. It is not the same as observing physiological termination at a natural terminator, and a failure to produce the run-off RNA could result from either a problem at initiation or a defect during later extension.

A complementary experiment compares preassembled elongation complexes with promoter-dependent reactions. A polymerase mutant that produces few run-off transcripts from an intact promoter but extends RNA normally when provided with a suitable preassembled scaffold may have a defect before or during escape. If that mutant also exhibits normal promoter binding and opening but releases many short RNAs, the evidence localizes the defect more specifically to early synthesis or escape. No one experiment has to reveal every step of the mechanism; the combination of assays progressively narrows the possibilities.

Promoter clearance influences the availability of polymerase and promoter DNA in the cell. An enzyme that escapes rapidly can permit another holoenzyme to initiate at the same promoter after the relevant DNA region becomes accessible. A complex that remains trapped near the start can obstruct subsequent initiation even if many free polymerases are present. Therefore a promoter's capacity to recruit polymerase cannot be considered independently of the duration of promoter occupancy. A system that binds efficiently but clears slowly may use the available polymerase and DNA inefficiently.

The end product of successful initiation is an elongation-competent complex containing the polymerase, the DNA template and a nascent RNA. The subsequent elongation cycle must repeatedly add nucleotides, handle DNA reannealing and eventually respond to termination signals. These processes are examined separately in 3.B.03. For the present chapter the important causal transition is from a promoter-bound, initiation-specific complex to a moving polymerase that has established a stable RNA synthesis pathway.

17. Quantitative reasoning across the complete initiation pathway

A simple kinetic scheme helps bring together promoter recognition, opening and escape. Let R be free holoenzyme and P be free promoter. The first reversible event forms a closed complex C with association rate constant k₁ and dissociation rate constant k₋₁. The units of k₁ are inverse concentration per unit time because the rate depends on two free species. The units of k₋₁ are inverse time. A subsequent transition converts C to an open state O with rate constant k₂, and an effective productive transition from O to an escaped complex E occurs with rate constant k₃. The actual pathway contains several intermediates and competing abortive events; these symbols describe a deliberately reduced model suitable for reasoning about bottlenecks.

Suppose a newly formed closed complex has only two available transitions: opening at rate k₂ or dissociating at rate k₋₁. If both are independent first-order processes with constant rates, the probability of opening before dissociation is k₂/(k₂ + k₋₁). This result can be derived by considering the time-dependent probability of remaining in the closed state. The probability that neither transition has occurred by time t is exp[−(k₂ + k₋₁)t]. The probability density for opening at that time is k₂ multiplied by this survival term. Integrating from zero to infinity gives the stated ratio. A larger opening rate increases the chance of progress; a larger dissociation rate decreases it.

For a hypothetical complex with k₂ equal to 0.30 s⁻¹ and k₋₁ equal to 0.10 s⁻¹, the probability of opening is 0.75. A mutation that lowers k₂ to 0.05 s⁻¹ while leaving dissociation unchanged reduces this probability to one third. The average waiting time for either transition changes from 1/0.40, or 2.5 seconds, to 1/0.15, or about 6.67 seconds. The mutant not only progresses less often but also remains in the closed state for longer on average before either opening or dissociating. These predictions concern the two-event model; reverse transitions and additional intermediates would require an expanded scheme.

If the open complex can either proceed productively or revert to an earlier state, its probability of a successful transition can be described similarly. For a single pass through an idealized sequence, suppose 75 percent of closed complexes open and 40 percent of those open complexes escape without failure. The chance of success along that uninterrupted route is 0.75 × 0.40 = 0.30. This is not necessarily the eventual success probability, because complexes that fail may try again. The problem must specify whether it concerns first-pass behavior or the outcome after repeated attempts. Confusing these interpretations can produce incorrect answers even when the individual multiplication is mathematically correct.

A second way to model initiation is to consider the time consumed by consecutive stages. Suppose recruitment takes an average of two seconds, opening takes three seconds and early RNA synthesis plus escape takes five seconds. In a simplified serial model with no reversals, the mean time per completed initiation event is ten seconds. A single isolated promoter that becomes available immediately after this cycle can therefore support approximately six initiation events per minute. If opening is accelerated from three seconds to one second, the mean cycle time falls to eight seconds and flux rises to 7.5 per minute. The increase is 25 percent, not threefold, because opening is only one of several time-consuming steps.

This observation explains the limited benefit of accelerating a step that is not the dominant bottleneck. If escape alone requires twenty seconds, improving binding from two seconds to one second would produce only a small increase in total flux. If recruitment requires twenty seconds and escape is rapid, increasing promoter affinity or enzyme availability may be much more effective. In a real cell, promoter occupancy, polymerase availability and interactions with other transcription complexes complicate such simple arithmetic. The serial model remains useful when its assumptions are stated and the rates are interpreted as average cycle times.

Sigma competition provides another instructive model. Suppose two sigma factors S₁ and S₂ bind mutually exclusively to the same free core C. Let K₁ and K₂ be their dissociation constants. At equilibrium the amount of each holoenzyme relative to free core is the free concentration of its sigma divided by its dissociation constant. If the free sigma concentrations are held fixed, the fraction of total core associated with S₁ is ([S₁]/K₁)/(1 + [S₁]/K₁ + [S₂]/K₂). The term one represents free core. The analogous fraction for S₂ has [S₂]/K₂ in the numerator.

In a hypothetical example, [S₁]/K₁ is four and [S₂]/K₂ is one. The fractions of core associated with S₁ and S₂ are 4/6 and 1/6, respectively, with 1/6 remaining free. If the second ratio increases to five while the first remains four, the new fractions are 4/10 and 5/10, with 1/10 free. The shift illustrates how increasing one specificity factor can reduce the fraction of core available to another without changing the total amount of catalytic machinery. It does not establish that transcript levels change in the same numerical proportions: promoter kinetics and other regulatory interactions must still be considered.

These models share a useful principle. A single measurement of final RNA abundance cannot uniquely determine which rate constant changed. The same reduction in output could be explained by slower association, faster dissociation, slower opening, frequent abortive initiation or inefficient escape. Quantitative analysis becomes mechanistic when additional measurements independently constrain these alternatives.

18. Experimental strategies for separating recognition, opening and RNA synthesis

An effective experimental strategy begins by asking which molecular state an assay actually detects. A measurement of bound DNA, a measurement of local strand separation and a measurement of RNA production are not interchangeable. These assays provide different windows into the initiation pathway and can be combined to distinguish defects that would look identical if only final transcript yield were measured.

An electrophoretic mobility shift assay uses the different migration of free DNA and DNA associated with a protein complex. A slower migrating DNA band can indicate association of RNA polymerase with a promoter fragment. Suitable comparisons include DNA without protein, a promoter mutant and an unrelated DNA fragment. The latter is useful for evaluating whether binding is selective under the tested conditions. The technique does not provide a direct picture of the DNA strands within the bound complex. A mobility shift may therefore occur even if the polymerase has failed to expose the template or has bound in an unproductive arrangement.

Footprinting techniques provide information about the regions of DNA affected by a bound protein. In a conventional DNase I footprint, a protein-bound DNA segment may be less accessible to cleavage than DNA without protein. Comparison of the resulting fragment patterns reveals a protected region. Protection can arise because the protein physically covers the DNA or because it changes the local conformation and accessibility of the DNA. Changes in cleavage intensity therefore should not be treated as a direct atomic map of every protein–base contact.

The landmark study by Pribnow illustrates the historical utility of protecting DNA through its interaction with RNA polymerase [3]. A region associated with an early T7 promoter could be identified and its nucleotide sequence investigated. The logical bridge from physical protection to promoter sequence helped establish the molecular basis of bacterial initiation. Modern interpretation still requires care: a protected DNA region can extend beyond the subset of bases that make the most important sequence-specific contacts. The size of the footprint is not the number of nucleotides in the transcription bubble.

Chemical probing can distinguish a promoter-bound complex with locally exposed bases from an entirely double-stranded promoter region. Potassium permanganate reacts preferentially with accessible thymine residues in unpaired or distorted DNA. The resulting pattern can therefore report on particular positions that have changed accessibility during opening. A strong signal at appropriate positions near the start is consistent with open-complex formation. Absence of a signal at a position lacking thymine cannot establish that the DNA at that position is paired, because the probe's chemical selectivity limits what it detects. Other chemical probes and controls can help interpret the pattern.

Time-resolved footprinting studies have helped distinguish rearrangement of promoter-bound DNA from the later formation of exposed bases. Work on the E. coli polymerase at the λ PR promoter, for example, supported a pathway involving entry of downstream DNA into the enzyme before the mature open state was formed [9]. This is a more informative picture than a single cartoon in which the DNA spontaneously separates outside the enzyme and is then captured. The experiment concerns a particular system, so exact intermediate lifetimes or footprint boundaries should not be generalized to every promoter.

RNA product analysis addresses a different question. Short products can be separated from longer transcripts and compared under conditions in which the identity of the initiating and extending nucleotides is known. An assay that measures both early RNA and run-off products is particularly useful because an increase in short products with a decrease in full-length RNA suggests a changed balance of early initiation outcomes. It remains essential to account for detection sensitivity. If RNA is labeled throughout its length, a long molecule may contribute more signal than a short molecule. If only its initiating nucleotide is labeled under an appropriate scheme, the interpretation of band intensity may be closer to molecule number. The exact labeling design determines the correct normalization.

A single-round transcription experiment is intended to measure the output of a defined population of initiating complexes without permitting unrestricted reinitiation by fresh polymerase. This can help separate the behavior of preformed complexes from the repeated recruitment and escape cycles of a multiple-round experiment. A competitor that binds free polymerase can be useful in some designs, but the experimental conditions matter: a challenge that destabilizes the complexes of interest will distort interpretation. The distinction is conceptual rather than a demand for one universal protocol.

Consider a hypothetical dataset in which wild-type promoter binding is normalized to 100 units, opening to 100 units and run-off RNA to 100 units. Mutant A produces 25, 20 and 18 units, respectively. Mutant B produces 95, 15 and 12 units. Mutant C produces 98, 92 and 20 units, with a marked increase in short products. Mutant A is consistent with impaired recruitment, although downstream defects cannot be excluded. Mutant B binds almost normally but fails to generate the opening signal efficiently, suggesting an isomerization problem. Mutant C forms bound and open complexes nearly normally but yields far fewer productive RNAs, implicating early synthesis or escape. None of these synthetic numbers comes from a published experiment; they are designed to demonstrate the causal logic of assay comparison.

The strongest inference arises when a second perturbation specifically rescues the proposed defect. An altered recognition motif that is recognized by a compensatory sigma variant provides one example. If the altered sigma restores transcription from the mutant promoter while reducing activity at the original promoter, the pattern supports a change in specificity. Conversely, a nonspecific increase in total polymerase concentration may partly restore transcription from many weak promoters without proving restoration of a particular molecular contact. The distinction between selective and nonspecific rescue is fundamental to genetic and biochemical reasoning.

19. Promoter strength in the cell and its biological consequences

A promoter's strength is commonly described in terms of how efficiently it directs transcription under defined conditions. This description must specify the measured quantity. Initiation events per promoter per unit time, total RNA produced per unit time, steady-state RNA abundance and reporter protein abundance are related but different outcomes. A promoter can be described as strong in a particular assay yet behave differently in a cell with a different concentration of polymerase or a different complement of regulatory factors.

A useful mathematical connection concerns RNA abundance. Let M be the number or concentration of RNA molecules, J be their rate of production and δ be a first-order effective loss constant that may include degradation and dilution. The simple model dM/dt = J − δM reaches steady state when production balances loss. Thus Mss = J/δ. For two hypothetical promoters producing ten and five RNA molecules per minute, respectively, with equal loss constants of 0.2 min⁻¹, the expected steady-state RNA numbers are fifty and twenty-five. If instead both promoters produce ten molecules per minute but one transcript has a loss constant of 0.4 min⁻¹, its steady-state abundance is also twenty-five. Identical changes in RNA abundance can therefore arise from different mechanisms.

This distinction is especially important when promoter output is measured through a reporter gene. A transcriptional reporter places an identifiable RNA or protein output under the control of the promoter being studied. A protein reporter additionally depends on translation and protein stability. If a promoter mutation changes the transcription start site and therefore the 5′ leader of the reporter RNA, the mutation may affect translation or RNA stability as well as initiation. Measuring reporter fluorescence alone cannot separate those contributions. Comparison with direct RNA measurements and appropriate controls strengthens the inference about promoter function.

In bacteria, promoter selection has broad consequences because the same core machinery is shared among many transcription units. The availability of a particular holoenzyme can influence the expression of an entire class of genes. A shift in sigma availability can support adaptation without requiring a change in the genome sequence. The relevant principle is not that every gene in a physiological pathway possesses an identical promoter but that several promoters can respond to common molecular specificity or regulatory mechanisms. Their individual responses can differ because of differences in promoter architecture and initiation kinetics.

Promoter mutations also illustrate the difference between regulatory and coding mutations. A change upstream of a transcription start may reduce the amount of RNA produced while leaving the coding sequence of the associated protein unchanged. The protein molecules that are still made can retain their normal amino acid sequence, yet the organism may display a phenotype because too little protein is available. A coding mutation, by contrast, can alter the structure or activity of the protein even if its RNA is produced normally. Some mutations have mixed consequences, particularly when promoter features overlap transcribed sequence. Mapping the mutation and measuring the intermediate products are therefore more informative than assigning a category solely from the final phenotype.

Promoter variants are also useful in biotechnology, where expression of a reporter or desired protein can be adjusted by changing recognition elements or using an appropriate host polymerase. The design goal is not universally maximal initiation. Excessive expression can impose a cellular burden, exhaust available resources or alter growth, while moderate expression may produce a more useful balance. A promoter chosen in one bacterial species may not be recognized equally well in another because their initiation factors and regulatory contexts differ. These principles explain why promoter characterization is necessary even when a sequence is already known to support expression in a model organism.

The final connection is between molecular mechanism and examination reasoning. Given a promoter sequence, first establish the transcription orientation and positions of the recognition elements. Given a mutation, ask which molecular contact or transition it is most likely to influence. Given an experimental result, identify the state actually detected by the assay. Given a change in full-length RNA, consider recruitment, opening, early synthesis, escape and RNA stability as distinguishable possibilities. This reasoning framework allows knowledge of bacterial RNA polymerase to be transferred to unfamiliar promoter systems without assuming that every detail of the E. coli model is universal.

  2. 1-A, 2-D, 3-B, 4-C
  3. 1-D, 2-A, 3-C, 4-B
  4. 1-B, 2-C, 3-A, 4-D
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 1-B, 2-C, 3-A, 4-D

Binding precedes opening, abortive initiation releases a short product, and escape reorganizes upstream contacts. The other mappings confuse the order or properties of the states.

Part C practice

Original analytical and numerical problems. All numerical assay datasets are hypothetical educational examples.

37. Numerical

A −35 hexamer occupies −35 through −30 and a −10 hexamer occupies −12 through −7. How many bases lie strictly between them?

  1. 16
  2. 23
  3. 17
  4. 24
Answer and scientific explanation

C. 17

The intervening coordinates are −29 through −13 inclusive: −13 − (−29) + 1 = 17. Twenty-three is the distance between motif starts, not the spacer length.

38. Numerical

In ideal B-DNA with 10.5 bp per turn, a 3-bp spacer insertion changes relative phase by approximately how much?

  1. 34°
  2. 103°
  3. 69°
  4. 180°
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 103°

The phase change is 3 × 360/10.5 ≈ 102.9°. The first two values correspond to one and two bases; 180° corresponds to about half a turn. Protein-bound DNA may deform.

39. Numerical

At free holoenzyme 30 nM, two independent promoters have Kd values 10 nM and 90 nM. What are their equilibrium bound fractions?

  1. 0.25 and 0.75
  2. 0.33 and 0.11
  3. 0.75 and 0.25
  4. Both 0.5
Answer and scientific explanation

C. 0.75 and 0.25

Using θ = R/(R + Kd), the fractions are 30/40 = 0.75 and 30/120 = 0.25. The other choices reverse the affinities or use incorrect denominators. This is binding, not output.

40. Numerical

At 310 K, an open-to-closed equilibrium ratio is 0.01. Using RT = 2.58 kJ/mol, calculate ΔG° for opening.

  1. +11.88 kJ/mol
  2. −11.88 kJ/mol
  3. −5.94 kJ/mol
  4. +2.58 kJ/mol
Answer and scientific explanation

A. +11.88 kJ/mol

ΔG° = −RT ln K = −2.58 ln 0.01 ≈ +11.88 kJ/mol. A positive value agrees with the open state being less favorable; −11.88 reverses the sign.

41. Numerical

A closed complex opens at 0.4 s⁻¹ or dissociates at 0.6 s⁻¹. What fraction opens first and what is the mean time to either event?

  1. 0.6; 2.5 s
  2. 0.4; 2.5 s
  3. 1; 1 s
  4. 0.4; 1 s
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 0.4; 1 s

Probability = 0.4/(0.4 + 0.6) = 0.4; mean competing-event waiting time = 1/(0.4 + 0.6) = 1 s. Using only the opening rate gives 2.5 s, incorrectly ignoring dissociation.

42. Numerical

An independent initiation attempt has productive probability 0.125; failures reset to the same bound state. What is the expected number of abortive attempts before a success?

  1. 1
  2. 7
  3. 4
  4. 8
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 7

Mean total attempts = 1/0.125 = 8, of which one is the productive attempt. The expected failed attempts are seven, not eight. The model assumes independent identical attempts.

43. Numerical

A promoter makes a 120-nt run-off RNA and three 6-nt abortive RNAs, all by conventional de novo initiation. How many PPi molecules are released by polymerization?

  1. 138
  2. 134
  3. 137
  4. 120
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 134

The 120-nt RNA forms 119 bonds, and three 6-nt RNAs form 3 × 5 = 15 bonds. Total PPi = 134. Counting all 138 incorporated NTPs ignores the four initiating triphosphates.

44. Numerical

For two competing sigma factors, free [S₁]/K₁ = 2 and [S₂]/K₂ = 5. Under the simple fixed-free-sigma model, what fraction of total core is bound to S₂?

  1. 5/7
  2. 2/8
  3. 1/8
  4. 5/8
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 5/8

The denominator includes free core: 1 + 2 + 5 = 8. The fraction for S₂ is 5/8. A denominator of seven excludes free core.

45. Experimental / analytical

A hypothetical mutant gives binding 96%, opening 18% and full RNA 15% of wild type. Which interpretation is best?

  1. A selective defect in opening is supported
  2. It cannot bind DNA at all
  3. The data prove a translation defect
  4. It necessarily lacks β′
Answer and scientific explanation

A. A selective defect in opening is supported

Near-normal binding with sharply reduced opening supports a defect between recognition and opening. It does not uniquely identify the affected protein and says nothing directly about translation.

46. Experimental / analytical

A mutant produces 94% normal open-complex signal but only 12% run-off RNA, with abundant short initial products and normal scaffold extension. What is the most likely affected stage?

  1. Initial promoter binding
  2. DNA replication
  3. Early synthesis or promoter escape
  4. RNA export
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Early synthesis or promoter escape

Opening and scaffold extension are largely retained, while abortive output is high. This localizes the defect before stable elongation and after opening; replication and export are unrelated.

47. Experimental / analytical

A sigma mutant restores activity on a particular altered −35 motif but loses activity on the original motif. Core abundance is unchanged. Which conclusion is best?

  1. The mutant acquired altered promoter preference
  2. All catalytic activity was eliminated
  3. The promoter is now a terminator
  4. The sigma mutation changed the RNA genetic code
Answer and scientific explanation

A. The mutant acquired altered promoter preference

Selective reciprocal changes suggest altered recognition specificity. General catalytic failure would not selectively restore the altered promoter; termination and codon interpretation are separate.

48. Experimental / analytical

Two promoters have identical −10/−35 elements. Replacing only promoter A's initially transcribed sequence with B's sequence changes the short-to-long RNA ratio without changing opening. What does this support?

  1. Only −35 can affect initiation
  2. The DNA sequence no longer matters
  3. Opening must be completely abolished
  4. The initially transcribed region can affect early RNA synthesis or escape
Answer and scientific explanation

D. The initially transcribed region can affect early RNA synthesis or escape

The substitution changes a feature encountered after opening, consistent with altered short RNA stability or escape. Normal opening excludes complete loss of that transition; the data do not show one unique atomic mechanism.

49. Experimental / analytical

An αCTD-defective RNAP and deletion of an UP element each strongly reduce promoter output. Combining the two changes has little further effect. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The two proteins are proved identical
  2. UP DNA is a translated codon
  3. They may disrupt the same functional interaction; a floor effect remains possible
  4. The result demonstrates zero binding at all promoters
Answer and scientific explanation

C. They may disrupt the same functional interaction; a floor effect remains possible

Nonadditivity is consistent with both changes impairing αCTD–UP interaction, but measurement floors or other shared bottlenecks can mimic it. The other claims are not established.

50. Experimental / analytical

An Eσ⁵⁴ complex binds promoter DNA in the absence of an activator, yet a probe shows little melting. Adding an ATPase-defective activator does not restore melting. Which step is most directly implicated?

  1. Translation elongation
  2. ATP-driven remodeling of the closed complex
  3. DNA ligation
  4. Termination after a complete RNA
Answer and scientific explanation

B. ATP-driven remodeling of the closed complex

The intact binding step and failed opening point to activator-mediated remodeling. None of the other steps explains a promoter-bound, unmelted σ⁵⁴ complex.

51. Experimental / analytical

A promoter gives a strong DNase I footprint but lacks a permanganate signal. The candidate melted interval contains several thymine bases, and the wild-type control gives a clear signal. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. It is certainly a mature elongation complex
  2. It cannot bind RNA polymerase
  3. DNA association occurs, but opening is impaired or below detection
  4. The sequence has been deleted
Answer and scientific explanation

C. DNA association occurs, but opening is impaired or below detection

Protection demonstrates altered accessibility and the comparative chemical signal supports impaired opening. The result does not exclude every possible open conformation or directly show elongation.

52. Experimental / analytical

A protein reporter decreases fourfold after a change that also shifts +1 downstream. Direct run-off RNA yield stays similar. What is the best next interpretation?

  1. A leader-dependent RNA stability or translation effect should be tested
  2. Initiation necessarily decreased fourfold
  3. The promoter lost all polymerase binding
  4. Protein output is a direct measure of only k₁
Answer and scientific explanation

A. A leader-dependent RNA stability or translation effect should be tested

A changed 5′ leader may influence RNA lifetime or translation while RNA synthesis remains similar. Reporter protein integrates several processes; it cannot alone quantify recruitment.

53. Experimental / analytical

A long RNA band becomes twice as intense as a short band in a uniformly labeled nucleotide incorporation assay. What can be concluded about molecule numbers?

  1. There are exactly twice as many long RNAs
  2. There are exactly equal numbers
  3. Short RNA molecules are never labeled
  4. The relative molecule count requires correction for label incorporation and detection
Answer and scientific explanation

D. The relative molecule count requires correction for label incorporation and detection

Signal depends on incorporation and detection, not necessarily one signal unit per molecule. Length and labeled nucleotide composition must be accounted for before estimating molecule ratios.

54. Experimental / analytical

A DNA fragment bears an intact σ⁷⁰ consensus promoter, but insertion of eleven bases in its spacer nearly eliminates activity. Why is restored approximate helical phase insufficient?

  1. The DNA no longer contains the consensus motifs
  2. The insertion also changes axial spacing and domain reach
  3. Eleven bases eliminate all DNA hydrogen bonding
  4. Polymerase now requires a ribosome
Answer and scientific explanation

B. The insertion also changes axial spacing and domain reach

One turn approximately restores rotational phase but increases axial separation. The two recognition regions may no longer fit the holoenzyme geometry despite unchanged motifs.

55. Experimental / analytical

A single-molecule assay shows additional DNA unwinding as initial RNA length rises while upstream promoter contacts remain stationary; the signal reverses when short RNA is released. Which model best fits?

  1. Entire polymerase translocates without additional unwinding
  2. DNA scrunching followed by reset
  3. DNA degradation at +1
  4. RNA synthesis without any template
Answer and scientific explanation

B. DNA scrunching followed by reset

Additional unwinding with stationary upstream contacts is the prediction of scrunching; reversal upon abortive release fits reset. The alternatives do not account for the coupled DNA and RNA changes.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

Core versus holoenzyme

Core retains catalytic capacity; sigma enables effective specific promoter initiation. Core activity on an artificial substrate is not proof of normal initiation.

Consensus versus invariant sequence

TTGACA and TATAAT are statistical consensus motifs, not mandatory identical words at every bacterial promoter.

Spacer versus motif-start distance

For −35…−30 and −12…−7, the spacer contains 17 bases; motif starts are separated by 23 positions.

Upstream versus left

Upstream is defined relative to transcription direction, so it can be on either side of a chromosome drawing.

Start codon versus start site

+1 marks the first RNA nucleotide, while AUG usually marks the start of a translated reading frame.

Closed versus open complex

Binding does not prove melting. An open complex can exist without RNA synthesis when NTPs are absent.

AT content versus stability

Hydrogen bonds are only one contribution to duplex stability; stacking, topology and protein contacts also matter.

ATP as fuel versus substrate

σ⁵⁴ activator ATP hydrolysis is distinct from ATP incorporation as an RNA nucleotide; classical σ⁷⁰ opening lacks an obligatory separate ATP motor.

Affinity versus flux

A stable bound complex can be slow to open or escape. Occupancy is not productive initiation rate.

Short RNA versus termination

An abortive product is released before productive elongation; termination releases a transcript after elongation.

Escape versus sigma release

Escape entails disengaging initiation-specific promoter contacts. Sigma can remain associated in some elongation complexes.

RNA abundance versus transcription rate

Steady-state RNA depends on synthesis and loss, while protein reporter output also depends on translation and protein stability.

Bacteria versus all prokaryotes

Archaeal transcription machinery is not the bacterial α₂ββ′ω–sigma system.

Melted length versus RNA length

A representative open bubble includes upstream bases; its size is not the length of a nascent RNA.

Footprint versus bubble

Protection from nuclease cleavage indicates altered accessibility and is not a direct measurement of the number of melted base pairs.

Active recall

Why is sigma called a specificity factor?

It contributes promoter recognition and initiation-complex formation, allowing a common catalytic core to select different promoters.

What is the normal chain count of core and holoenzyme?

Five core chains, six with one sigma factor in the canonical E. coli model.

Which way does polymerase read DNA?

3′ to 5′ on the template; RNA grows 5′ to 3′.

What changes during open-complex formation?

Local DNA base pairing is disrupted and the strands are organized in distinct paths, exposing the template.

Why does σ⁵⁴ provide an exception to the σ⁷⁰ model?

Its closed complex ordinarily requires an ATP-driven activator to remodel it for opening.

What happens to the first NTP's 5′ phosphates?

They normally remain at the primary RNA 5′ end in conventional de novo initiation.

How does scrunching accommodate RNA growth?

The initiation complex retains upstream contacts while drawing additional downstream DNA into the enzyme.

How can a promoter mutation affect escape without altering RNA sequence?

It may change the strength or geometry of upstream contacts that must be disrupted.

What assay combination can separate binding from opening?

A mobility shift or footprint, combined with suitable chemical probing of exposed bases.

Why can increased promoter binding fail to raise full-length RNA?

Opening, nucleotide addition, abortive cycling or escape may remain limiting.

What does a run-off product reveal?

Successful RNA synthesis to the defined end of the linear template, not necessarily physiological termination.

What is the basic downstream hand-off?

The escaped complex contains polymerase, template DNA and nascent RNA ready for productive elongation.
