1. From a successfully initiated transcript to a complete RNA

A bacterial cell faces two distinct informational problems each time it transcribes a gene. It must locate the appropriate starting point in an enormous number of potential DNA sequences, and it must then copy the selected information into an RNA molecule of an appropriate length. A promoter solves the first problem, but a promoter cannot by itself specify when the copy is complete. The second problem requires an enzyme capable of maintaining its contact with DNA while incorporating many nucleotides, a mechanism for responding to obstacles during this journey, and a mechanism that releases the finished RNA. These are the subjects of elongation, pausing and termination. They are causally connected rather than independent stages in a diagram. An elongating enzyme is stable enough to copy a long gene, but the same stability creates a problem at its end: a signal must selectively destabilize an otherwise productive complex.

Imagine two neighboring bacterial genes, each with a different promoter. A polymerase that initiates the first gene must not automatically express the second simply because its DNA lies downstream. Sometimes several adjacent genes are intended to be transcribed together as an operon, but even an operon has boundaries. A well-positioned terminator limits unnecessary RNA synthesis and helps separate adjacent transcription units. A terminator that fails may allow the production of RNA extending beyond the normal boundary. Such an RNA is called a readthrough transcript. Depending on the arrangement of genes, readthrough can increase expression of downstream information, interfere with transcription arriving from another promoter, or generate RNA that is degraded without producing useful protein. Thus a change that leaves the coding sequence of every protein intact may still have a substantial effect on gene expression.

RNA polymerase is a DNA-directed enzyme, not an enzyme that makes a duplicate DNA chromosome. It follows one DNA strand in the direction from its 3′ end toward its 5′ end and extends RNA at the RNA's 3′ end. The emerging 5′ region contains information copied earlier, while the growing 3′ region represents the latest information acquired. Once transcription begins, the polymerase does not need a succession of new promoters at every codon. It remains associated with the nucleic acids and repeatedly carries out the same chemical reaction. A start codon is interpreted by a ribosome, not by bacterial RNA polymerase as a general instruction to begin elongation. Likewise, a stop codon does not by itself cause RNA polymerase to release RNA. Transcription signals and translation signals are interpreted by different molecular machines.

This distinction becomes especially important in bacteria, where there is no nuclear envelope separating the usual site of chromosomal transcription from the cytoplasmic translation machinery. A ribosome can engage a suitable ribosome-binding region of an emerging messenger RNA before its 3′ end has been produced. The RNA therefore becomes a shared molecular object: its upstream part may be translated while its downstream end remains in RNA polymerase. The availability of the intervening RNA to other proteins depends partly on whether it is folded or occupied by ribosomes. This connection helps explain why the movement of a protein called Rho can be influenced by the translation status of an RNA even though Rho belongs to the transcription termination machinery. Ribosomal translation is neither required for every bacterial transcription reaction nor possible on every transcript, because many bacterial RNAs are functional without being translated.

A useful learning approach is to distinguish the events by their physical consequences. Elongation adds a covalently attached nucleotide to the RNA chain. Pausing decreases or temporarily interrupts the rate of addition but can leave the RNA and DNA securely associated with polymerase. Termination releases the transcript and dismantles the elongation complex. Inhibition is a perturbation that decreases transcription by interfering with one or more stages; it need not reproduce physiological termination. A drug-treated polymerase that has stopped making nucleotides may remain attached to DNA. The absence of further RNA synthesis is therefore insufficient evidence that termination occurred. This distinction underlies many experimental reasoning questions.

2. How experimental evidence separated elongation from initiation and termination

Early biochemical work on transcription established that RNA can be synthesized by an enzyme supplied with DNA and ribonucleoside triphosphates. The crucial observation is not simply that radioactive label enters an acid-insoluble fraction, but that the product depends on the appropriate substrates and a DNA template and behaves as RNA. An experiment without DNA tests whether an observed signal depends on the intended template. An experiment without RNA polymerase establishes the background associated with the remaining reagents. Removal of an essential nucleotide prevents productive synthesis beyond positions that require that substrate. These controls distinguish template-dependent RNA formation from nonspecific association of label with a protein or precipitated material.

A bulk incorporation assay, however, does not tell us which DNA position the enzyme has copied. It reports a summed measure of incorporation. If one reaction produces ten molecules of RNA of one hundred nucleotides and another produces a hundred molecules of ten nucleotides, the total number of incorporated residues may be similar. A measurement proportional to incorporated radioactivity could fail to distinguish them. Electrophoretic separation of RNA products under denaturing conditions resolves this ambiguity by revealing the lengths of the products. With suitable labeling, size markers and reaction design, short products near a promoter can be distinguished from longer intermediates, full-length transcripts and RNA ending at an internal position. The assay thus connects a population measurement to the progress of individual molecular complexes.

It became possible to ask whether termination required a cellular component other than RNA polymerase. In 1969, Jeffrey W. Roberts reported a protein factor from Escherichia coli that promoted specific termination and RNA release in a cell-free transcription system. This finding, published as Termination factor for RNA synthesis in Nature, established that at least some RNA ends depend on a separate protein activity. The protein was named Rho. An important conceptual consequence was that neither the termination position nor the ability to form a short RNA can always be predicted solely by identifying the promoter and the polymerase. An additional factor can participate in determining the final length of the transcript.

The logic of the factor-addition experiment remains useful. Suppose purified bacterial RNA polymerase is supplied with a suitable template and all four substrates. Without an added protein fraction it produces predominantly long RNA. When an active fraction is added, a shorter, reproducible RNA species appears. The obvious hypothesis is that the fraction promotes termination at a specific region. A competing explanation is that the fraction contains an RNase that cuts an already synthesized long RNA. Merely observing the short RNA does not exclude this alternative. Investigators must establish whether RNA was released from an elongation complex in a transcription-dependent manner and whether the short product reflects cessation of chain extension rather than cleavage after synthesis. An RNA degradation control using previously synthesized RNA helps distinguish the possibilities. The result becomes much more convincing when purified factor and defined transcription complexes reproduce the behavior.

The discovery of factor-dependent termination does not imply that all bacterial termination requires the factor. Some sequences allow RNA polymerase to release its transcript without added Rho or other dedicated termination machinery. This is why experiments using a defined set of components are especially instructive. A transcript that terminates efficiently when the only enzyme is bacterial RNA polymerase provides evidence for an intrinsic route; comparison of alternative DNA sequences then identifies features that contribute to its activity. Conversely, factor-dependent release on a template that is otherwise read through shows that a factor can supply a missing termination activity. The two mechanisms can coexist in the same organism and can operate at different transcription units.

Later structural and biochemical studies helped explain how an enzyme that is neither a conventional replicative helicase nor a ribosome can copy DNA while keeping only a small portion locally open. Structures of elongation complexes reveal where template DNA, growing RNA and incoming nucleotides are arranged. A structure is a snapshot: it identifies spatial relationships but does not directly record how rapidly an intermediate forms or disappears. Kinetic experiments complement it by measuring the timing of nucleotide addition, pausing and release. Students should not treat a single static structure as proof of every transition in a catalytic cycle. The strongest mechanistic conclusions combine biochemical, structural and kinetic observations.

3. The elongation complex: a mobile, locally opened nucleic-acid assembly

At the end of bacterial initiation, RNA polymerase has produced enough RNA to establish a stable association between the nascent transcript and its DNA template. The initiation-specific arrangement of sigma factor and promoter DNA is reorganized during promoter escape. The core polymerase, containing two alpha subunits, beta, beta-prime and omega, can carry out the subsequent elongation chemistry. Sigma factor commonly dissociates during the transition, although its dissociation is not invariably immediate and complete at every promoter. The important functional difference is that the elongation complex is no longer anchored to promoter recognition in the way the initial complex was. Its stability depends substantially on the interactions of core polymerase with DNA and RNA.

An elongation complex includes downstream double-stranded DNA approaching the enzyme, a short region in which the DNA strands are locally separated, an RNA–DNA hybrid in which the recently synthesized RNA is paired with its template, an exiting RNA segment and upstream DNA that has reformed its double helix. The locally separated DNA region is called the transcription bubble. The RNA–DNA hybrid is not the same structure as the entire bubble. The bubble is defined by the DNA strands being apart; the hybrid is defined by pairing between RNA and one of those DNA strands. In conventional bacterial structural models, approximately eight to nine base pairs of RNA–DNA hybrid lie inside a bubble on the order of ten to fourteen opened DNA base pairs, depending on the complex and how boundaries are defined. These values are useful approximate scales, not a universal fixed counting rule.

The template strand enters the active-center region with its orientation suited to reading consecutive bases toward its 5′ end. The other strand, often called the nontemplate strand, is displaced from the hybrid. The newly formed RNA bases pair with template DNA through ordinary complementary base-pairing interactions. The RNA sequence therefore resembles the nontemplate or coding strand when both are written in the 5′ to 3′ direction, with uracil replacing thymine. Consider a short coding-strand segment written as 5′-GACTAC-3′. The complementary template reads 3′-CTGATG-5′ across the same region, and the RNA is 5′-GACUAC-3′. The six-base example describes sequence identity, not the actual full width of the elongation complex.

One should picture the elongation complex as a moving window rather than a growing wedge. If the polymerase has copied a thousand nucleotides, it has not opened a thousand-base-pair bubble. DNA opens near the leading edge, while the template and nontemplate strands re-pair behind the enzyme as RNA is peeled away from the hybrid. Most of the transcript exits the enzyme as a single RNA strand that can fold or interact with proteins. The same relatively short hybrid is continuously renewed. This arrangement conserves the double-stranded character of the genome while allowing temporary access to one strand for information transfer.

The polymerase surrounds portions of the nucleic acids in a cleft and makes many noncovalent contacts with them. A clamp-like region contributes to holding downstream DNA and the RNA–DNA hybrid in a productive configuration. Separate channels provide routes for DNA, RNA and substrates. At the active center, the growing RNA 3′ end is aligned with the template base and a complementary incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate. These physical arrangements explain why a stable elongation complex does not need to detach and rebind after each nucleotide. Breaking all contacts after every chemical step would expose the transcript to premature loss and drastically reduce the efficiency of copying long transcription units.

Stability nevertheless has limits. A transient change in nucleic-acid position can move the RNA 3′ end away from the catalytic site. A distortion of the hybrid, a physical barrier on DNA or an interaction between the emerging RNA and the enzyme can favor an inactive configuration. If the complex retains the transcript and can return to a productive configuration, it has paused rather than terminated. If the transcript is released and the productive arrangement is dismantled, the enzyme has undergone termination or an alternative form of dissociation. The molecular challenge is to balance stable association against sufficient flexibility for movement and controlled release.

The displaced nontemplate strand is also chemically and biologically relevant. It is not the source of sequence information for the RNA during normal synthesis, but it forms part of the nucleic-acid environment through which the polymerase must move. DNA sequences can affect the stability of the bubble, the kinetics of translocation and the energetics of DNA re-pairing. Consequently, synonymous or noncoding sequence changes can affect transcription even when their impact on the final protein sequence is negligible. It is not enough to know that the active site reads one strand; the entire nucleic-acid assembly influences how easily that reading proceeds.

4. The chemistry of extending RNA by one nucleotide

To understand a long RNA transcript, first examine a single chemical step. An incoming nucleotide is not merely a free base. It is a ribonucleoside triphosphate, composed of a base, ribose and three phosphate groups. The four main substrates are ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP. Their bases differ in complementarity to template DNA, but their phosphate-containing chemical groups participate in a shared reaction. A nucleotide that pairs with the template can be positioned with its alpha phosphate near the hydroxyl group at the 3′ end of the existing RNA. The alpha phosphate is the phosphate closest to the ribose; the beta and gamma phosphates lie farther away.

The oxygen of the terminal 3′ hydroxyl participates in a nucleophilic attack on the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphorus. Formation of the new bond connects the previous RNA nucleotide's 3′ oxygen to the phosphate associated with the incoming nucleotide's 5′ carbon. A pyrophosphate group, containing the beta and gamma phosphates, is released. The product is an RNA chain extended by one ribonucleotide residue, with a new 3′ hydroxyl suitable for the next step. In symbolic form, RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi. This equation abstracts away proton transfers and metal coordination but correctly describes the principal substrates and products of chain extension.

Divalent metal ions, normally magnesium in physiological descriptions of RNA polymerase, facilitate catalysis. They help arrange the negatively charged reactive groups, stabilize developing charge in the reaction and assist the departure of pyrophosphate. Acidic residues of the polymerase coordinate metal ions within the active-center environment. The standard two-metal-ion picture is useful because it relates catalysis to electrostatics and geometry rather than attributing the reaction to vague enzymatic energy. The enzyme lowers kinetic barriers; it does not supply a new source of chemical free energy from nothing. Changing a metal-binding residue can therefore strongly impair polymerization even if the protein still associates with DNA.

The incoming triphosphate provides the chemical basis for the net energy change of incorporation. Hydrolysis of released pyrophosphate by cellular inorganic pyrophosphatase helps favor forward synthesis in vivo by decreasing the concentration of a reaction product. This is distinct from saying that RNA polymerase must hydrolyze a separate ATP molecule every time it moves a nucleotide. When a G is incorporated, the substrate is GTP; when a U is incorporated, the substrate is UTP. ATP is required as a substrate specifically where an adenine-containing RNA residue is incorporated. ATP may also be consumed by other proteins in transcription-associated reactions, but their hydrolysis is mechanistically separate from the chemical incorporation reaction.

Unlike DNA polymerase in ordinary chromosome replication, bacterial RNA polymerase can initiate a new chain without an existing nucleic-acid primer. Once a transcript has begun, however, an elongation reaction requires an available 3′ hydroxyl on the growing chain. This is a distinction between the requirements of de novo initiation and those of subsequent phosphodiester formation. A normal growing RNA with a free 3′ hydroxyl can accept another nucleotide; a chain whose end lacks a usable hydroxyl cannot undergo the same extension step. The absence of a primer requirement at the start does not mean phosphodiester bond formation can proceed in the absence of an appropriately positioned nucleophilic group.

RNA polymerase distinguishes ribonucleotides from deoxyribonucleotides and favors correct base pairing. Neither distinction is absolutely perfect. Wrong nucleotides may occasionally be incorporated. A mismatch can distort the geometry of the RNA–DNA hybrid or slow further extension. Some erroneous states are corrected through transcript cleavage, which removes one or more RNA residues and restores a productive 3′ end. Such proofreading does not use the same separate 3′ to 5′ exonuclease mechanism commonly taught for many DNA polymerases. The chemistry of transcription fidelity is integrated with the movement and possible backtracking of the elongation complex. For examination reasoning, distinguish a defective ability to choose a complementary substrate from a defective ability to recover after an incorrect substrate was accepted.

Several experiments can localize the cause of a catalytic defect. A mutant enzyme that binds DNA normally but produces almost no RNA might have a defect in substrate positioning or chemistry. An enzyme that adds a few nucleotides normally but frequently produces shortened products might instead have altered processivity or increased pausing. Measurements of substrate dependence, RNA length, DNA association and reaction time can distinguish these possibilities. No single readout automatically identifies the affected step.

5. Movement after chemistry: translocation and the next addition

Catalysis adds one nucleotide, but it does not by itself prepare the active center for every subsequent reaction. After incorporation, the newly extended RNA 3′ end must be repositioned relative to the next template base. Translocation is the change in relative position of the polymerase and nucleic acids that enables the next addition. In a simplified pre-translocated state, the most recently incorporated nucleotide occupies the position associated with the completed addition reaction. In a post-translocated state, the next template position is aligned for an incoming substrate. The enzyme can fluctuate between nearby positions. Correct substrate binding favors the productive state, and catalysis converts the substrate into a covalently incorporated residue.

The Brownian-ratchet description emphasizes that molecular movement involves thermal fluctuations rather than a large deterministic push that must occur at every step. Proteins and nucleic acids continually undergo thermal motion. A suitable arrangement of binding and chemical reactions biases these fluctuations toward net forward progress. When the next complementary nucleotide binds and is incorporated, a forward configuration becomes part of another catalytic cycle. This model explains why it is misleading to think of every nucleotide addition as requiring a separate mechanical ATPase that pulls the DNA through the enzyme.

The active center is surrounded by flexible protein elements, commonly described in structural accounts as the bridge helix and trigger loop. Their motions help coordinate substrate selection, catalysis and changes in active-center configuration. A student need not memorize individual atomic contacts to understand the principle. A correct nucleotide has to enter, be oriented, react and allow the enzyme to proceed. Conformational flexibility makes the sequence possible, while inappropriate conformations can prevent one of those stages. A mutation that changes the tendency of a flexible element to adopt a catalytic configuration may affect elongation speed and accuracy without directly changing its affinity for promoter DNA.

It is useful to distinguish the instantaneous rate of nucleotide addition at a productive site from the average velocity of a polymerase traveling along a gene. The former describes the chemistry and translocation cycle under particular local conditions. The latter includes pauses and other delays accumulated along the entire sequence. An enzyme might add successive nucleotides rapidly whenever active but have a low average velocity because it frequently pauses. A slower catalytic mutant and a pause-prone mutant may take similar amounts of time to reach a distant position but behave differently when observed at high temporal resolution or when assayed on a short, pause-free template.

Translocation also changes the positions of the RNA–DNA hybrid and upstream bubble boundary. As the next DNA base enters the decoding position, pairing must eventually be broken at the rear of the hybrid to allow the older RNA to leave. The RNA is not covalently attached to the DNA, so this separation requires disruption of noncovalent interactions rather than cleavage of a phosphodiester linkage. Simultaneously the DNA strands behind the active center can re-pair. This is an example of coordinated structural transitions: a single change in position connects substrate chemistry, hybrid renewal, strand opening and strand closure. It is the coordination, rather than four independent enzymes performing four unrelated steps, that permits processive transcription.

For a long transcript, the cost of each additional nucleotide is paid through its own substrate. The number of phosphodiester bonds in a newly made RNA of N residues is N minus one, because the first nucleotide begins the chain and every subsequent addition creates one new linkage. If the problem starts with an already existing RNA of length n and ends at N, the number of new covalent linkages is N minus n. Under the standard net-incorporation model without proofreading or abortive synthesis, this also equals the number of NTP substrates incorporated during that extension. Actual cellular consumption can be higher when nucleotides are incorporated and later removed or when initiation produces discarded short RNAs. The distinction between retained residues and total substrate turnover is important whenever a quantitative problem includes repeated failed events.

6. Processivity, RNA folding and the topological cost of movement

Processivity is the tendency of an enzyme to perform repeated catalytic events while remaining associated with its substrate. A highly processive RNA polymerase can extend a single RNA through many hundreds or thousands of nucleotide positions without releasing it. Processivity should not be confused with speed. A machine can move slowly while remaining attached to its track for a very long time, or it can move rapidly but fall off after only a few steps. In molecular terms, the average rate of addition depends on the time spent in productive and nonproductive states; the probability of continuing depends on the competition between extension, recovery and dissociation. A student who distinguishes these quantities can interpret an apparent contradiction such as normal initial incorporation accompanied by poor production of long transcripts.

Consider a simple model in which each nucleotide-addition cycle has a constant probability q of successful continuation and a probability 1 − q of irreversible premature release. If the events are independent, the probability of continuing through m such steps is q raised to the power m. This relationship is obtained by multiplying the probability of success at the first step by the conditional probability of success at the next, and repeating the multiplication. At q = 0.999, the probability of surviving 1,000 identical steps is approximately 0.368. At q = 0.9999, the corresponding probability is approximately 0.905. These are hypothetical values, not measured probabilities for E. coli. They show why even a small probability of inappropriate dissociation can accumulate into a major loss of long transcripts. Real sequence-dependent pauses and termination signals violate the assumption of a constant independent probability, so the equation is a conceptual tool rather than a universal description.

Processivity becomes especially important as the nascent transcript leaves the hybrid and begins to fold. An RNA strand can pair with a complementary sequence within itself, forming a stem and loop. Unlike the final shape of an isolated mature RNA, cotranscriptional folding is constrained by the order of synthesis: upstream sequences become available before downstream sequences exist. An RNA segment can transiently adopt one pairing arrangement and then adopt another after a complementary region has emerged. This means the time between the emergence of different sequences may influence which structure actually forms. A sequence-based drawing of a hairpin is therefore an indication of possible pairing, not proof that the structure has formed at the moment when polymerase encounters a relevant decision point.

An elongating RNA polymerase also operates on a helical DNA molecule. For B-form DNA, one helical turn corresponds approximately to ten to eleven base pairs. For an enzyme to advance along a strand that winds around its partner, either the enzyme and associated RNA must rotate relative to DNA, the DNA must rotate relative to the enzyme, or the system must redistribute torsional strain. In a constrained chromosomal domain, free rotation may be limited by DNA attachments, nearby proteins or the growing RNA and its associated molecules. The consequence is a topological challenge: the DNA ahead of the moving enzyme tends to become overwound, while DNA behind it tends to become underwound. This is the basis of the twin supercoiled-domain model formulated by Liu and Wang in 1987.

Positive and negative supercoiling are changes in DNA topology relative to an appropriate relaxed reference state. They are not descriptions of positive or negative electric charge. Positive supercoiling in front of polymerase tends to oppose further separation of the DNA strands, while negative supercoiling behind it can influence local strand separation and the tendency of RNA to associate with DNA. The two effects need not accumulate to the same extent if different topoisomerases remove them or if DNA can rotate. Bacterial DNA gyrase can remove positive torsional stress and introduce negative supercoiling through an ATP-dependent mechanism; bacterial topoisomerase I can relax excess negative supercoiling. Inhibition or loss of one of these activities can affect transcription indirectly even though its direct target is not the RNA polymerase active center.

The theoretical number of helical turns encountered while traversing 1,050 base pairs of a DNA helix with 10.5 base pairs per turn is 100. That is a geometric estimate of the amount of relative rotation associated with traversal, not a prediction that an actual plasmid must accumulate exactly one hundred positive and one hundred negative turns. Rotation, topology, other polymerases and topoisomerase activity all affect the observed result. In a closed circular DNA molecule, linking number is constrained unless a strand-passage or strand-break process changes it. The local generation of oppositely signed supercoiling therefore does not imply a simultaneous net creation of one hundred linking-number units of each sign as independent conserved quantities. This connection to DNA topology explains why an elongation problem cannot always be reduced to the chemistry of nucleotide addition alone.

7. Elementary pauses and the distinction between delay and release

A common textbook diagram represents polymerase moving along DNA at a constant speed until a terminator is reached. This is useful as an initial illustration, but actual movement is interrupted by transient pauses. A pause is an interval during which an elongation complex adds nucleotides more slowly than it does at its ordinary productive rate. Its duration may range from a brief kinetic delay to a long-lived arrest-like state. The important criterion is that the nascent RNA remains in a complex from which synthesis can potentially resume. If the enzyme and RNA have separated and the transcript can no longer be extended by the original complex, the event is no longer merely a pause.

An elementary pause can originate when the particular arrangement of DNA and RNA at the active center favors an inactive conformation or makes a translocation step less favorable. Sequence affects the energetics of base pairing, stacking, bubble movement and RNA positioning. The effect does not require that a special regulatory protein bind every pause site. Such sites can be intrinsic features of the nucleic-acid sequence encountered during elongation. Some pauses are brief and resolve without additional factors. Others are stabilized by RNA structures, interactions with proteins or backward movement of the enzyme. These possibilities explain how a common kinetic delay can be converted into states with distinct durations and outcomes.

The term backtracking describes reverse movement of the polymerase relative to the normal direction of transcription along DNA. During backtracking, the recently synthesized RNA is displaced from its productive alignment at the catalytic site. Its 3′ end can protrude away from the position needed for incorporation. Even though complementary substrates are available, they cannot simply be appended to an RNA terminus that is incorrectly positioned. Recovery may occur if the polymerase moves forward again and restores alignment. Alternatively, cleavage of the displaced RNA segment can generate a new 3′ hydroxyl positioned for renewed synthesis. Bacterial Gre factors help stimulate this RNA cleavage activity of RNA polymerase; they do not act as a second DNA-directed RNA polymerase.

The biological importance of this rescue is apparent when a mismatch slows the next addition. A poorly paired RNA residue at the growing end may favor an inactive or backtracked configuration. Cleavage can eliminate the defective terminal region and allow another incorporation attempt from a correctly aligned end. Not every pause is caused by an error, and not every error is necessarily removed by this route. A correctly copied region may pause because of its sequence or because of an obstacle, while some mismatched residues may escape correction. The connection is mechanistic: a pause can provide an opportunity to inspect or restore the productive RNA 3′ end. It is not an instruction that the enzyme must always remove the latest nucleotide whenever it stops briefly.

RNA structure can stabilize particular pauses. A stem-loop emerging near the RNA exit path can interact with the elongation complex or influence its conformational equilibrium. For a hairpin-stabilized pause, changes in the stability or placement of the hairpin can alter the length of the delay. Yet many stable RNA hairpins do not cause termination, because the ability to slow polymerase is not identical to the ability to destabilize the RNA–DNA contacts sufficiently for transcript release. This is an essential distinction before learning intrinsic termination. A pause provides time for other events to occur; it does not guarantee their occurrence.

The consequence of a pause depends on the molecular context. A ribosome following an elongating polymerase may catch up during a transcription pause. A transcription-associated factor may have time to bind the emerging RNA. A structural element may fold while the RNA 3′ end remains at a particular position. Conversely, prolonged stalling can create a barrier to other molecular traffic on DNA. Such conflicts can require removal of a stalled transcription complex, for example through mechanisms connected to transcription-coupled DNA repair. For this node, it is sufficient to recognize that obstacle-induced removal is conceptually different from the two usual end-of-transcription pathways. The detailed repair pathway belongs to the preceding DNA damage and repair curriculum.

One experimental signature of a pause is the appearance of an RNA intermediate at a particular length followed, over time, by loss of that intermediate and accumulation of longer RNA. In a single-round, synchronized reaction, newly initiated RNA cannot continually replenish the same short product. If it nevertheless disappears and longer products increase, the interpretation of a reversible pause becomes much stronger. In contrast, a released short transcript that persists and cannot chase into longer RNA represents a different kind of event. If the intermediate is unstable because of RNA degradation, its disappearance alone is not diagnostic; a corresponding gain of longer RNA and a direct check of RNA association are valuable controls. Examination problems frequently exploit the difference between a position-specific band on a gel and the molecular state that produced it.

8. Quantifying elongation time and pause occupancy

For a transcript whose remaining length is L nucleotides, a polymerase with a constant productive velocity v nucleotides per second would require L/v seconds to finish that portion in the absence of additional delays. If several distinct pauses introduce waiting times t1, t2 and so on, the total time in a simple additive model is L/v plus the sum of those waiting times. This expression assumes the productive velocity excludes the pauses and that the delays do not overlap. If an experimental value for velocity already averages over all the pauses, adding their durations again would count the same time twice. The definition of a measured quantity therefore matters as much as its numerical value.

For example, suppose a polymerase extends 1,800 nucleotides at a productive rate of 60 nucleotides per second, and encounters three pauses of two, four and nine seconds. Productive synthesis occupies thirty seconds, while the pauses contribute fifteen seconds. Total elapsed time is forty-five seconds, and the average velocity over the interval is forty nucleotides per second. Another polymerase with a productive rate of forty nucleotides per second and no pauses would reach the same endpoint in the same time. Yet its pattern of intermediate products could differ substantially. These hypothetical cases show why an endpoint time alone cannot establish whether a mutation reduces catalytic speed or increases pausing.

For a simple reversible pause with one first-order escape process, the fraction of a synchronized population still paused at time t is exp(−kt), where k is the escape rate constant in inverse seconds. The exponential arises because the decrease in the paused population over a short time is proportional to the number of complexes that remain paused. Integrating the differential relation dP/dt = −kP gives P(t) = P(0) exp(−kt). The half-time is ln(2)/k, since P(t)/P(0) equals one half at that time. The mean waiting time for an exponential distribution is 1/k. These two characteristic times are not numerically equal.

Imagine that the fraction remaining at a particular pause falls to one quarter after twelve seconds. Assuming a single first-order recovery process and no new entry into the pause, twelve seconds represent two half-times, giving a half-time of six seconds. The corresponding rate constant is approximately 0.116 per second, obtained by dividing 0.693 by six seconds. If another condition doubles the escape rate while all other assumptions remain valid, the half-time becomes three seconds. If the pause also has an irreversible release pathway, however, the disappearance of the paused state includes both processes. Fitting the decrease with one rate constant would then give the total exit rate, not necessarily the rate of resumed elongation. The identities of the products must be used to distinguish the alternatives.

A particularly useful quantity is the fraction of time spent paused. In the example of forty-five seconds total, fifteen seconds are spent at pauses, so pause occupancy during the observed journey is one third. If a researcher records the positions of many polymerases at a single instant, slow regions may have higher polymerase occupancy because an arriving enzyme remains there longer. This is analogous to a queue: a location that is slow to traverse can contain more machines than a region with a higher throughput. High local occupancy is consequently not automatically evidence of increased initiation or increased RNA production. If initiation rates are equal, increased occupancy at a single pause can instead indicate reduced onward movement.

In a simplified steady-state picture with independent polymerases and no collisions, the expected number of enzymes present at a region is related to the rate at which enzymes enter the region multiplied by their mean residence time. The units support the relation: enzymes per second multiplied by seconds gives enzymes. A mutation that increases residence time tenfold can increase local occupancy without any increase in transcription initiation. The assumptions fail when polymerases obstruct one another or when a substantial fraction of enzymes is lost before reaching the region. Those limitations explain why occupancy-based measurements need complementary measurements of RNA output. An analytical question may give an increased polymerase signal over a terminator region together with reduced downstream RNA. Such a combination supports a delay or release mechanism more strongly than a simple conclusion of increased initiation.

9. Intrinsic termination signals: reading a sequence as an RNA structure

Intrinsic termination is the route in which features encoded by the transcription template and emerging RNA can induce release without the obligatory participation of Rho. A classical intrinsic terminator contains a region of inverted repeats capable of forming a stable RNA stem-loop, followed by a stretch rich in uridine residues. The sequence arrangement must be interpreted in the direction of transcription. A stretch of thymidines on the coding DNA strand yields uridines in RNA, whereas the corresponding template segment contains adenines. If a question shows the template strand rather than the coding strand, a template run of A bases is the expected complement of the RNA U tract. Confusing which strand is displayed is a frequent source of wrong answers.

An inverted repeat differs from a direct repeat. Two copies of the same sequence in the same orientation are not automatically complementary within a single RNA molecule. The stem of a hairpin forms when the first region can pair antiparallel to a later region in the same strand. To examine a proposed sequence, write the RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction, identify candidate stem arms and reverse the orientation of one arm before comparing their bases. A simplified RNA pattern is 5′-GCGCC-AAA-GGCGC-UUUUUUU-3′. The first five residues, GCGCC, can pair with the last five residues of the second arm written antiparallel, whose sequence as written in the complete RNA is GGCGC. The three intervening adenines form a loop in this cartoon. The U-rich segment lies immediately after the second arm toward the RNA 3′ end. This is an illustrative sequence, not a natural terminator whose efficiency has been measured.

The tendency to form a stable stem depends on the base pairs, stacking interactions, loop size and any bulges or mismatches. Guanine–cytosine-rich stems are common in conventional examples because their interactions frequently favor a stable helix. A simplistic explanation that every G–C pair is always stronger than every A–U pair solely because of the number of hydrogen bonds is incomplete. Base stacking and neighboring sequence also contribute to stability, and the surrounding solution influences folding. Thus the common preference for a stable, often G–C-rich hairpin is a useful starting point, not a universal numerical scoring rule. Noncanonical or interrupted terminators can function, particularly with assistance from transcription factors.

The U-rich RNA tract is positioned such that at the termination site several of its residues participate in the RNA–DNA hybrid. In the ordinary arrangement, those U residues pair with template A residues. The resulting region of hybrid often has relatively low stability compared with hybrids of many other compositions. This makes it easier for the remaining contacts to be disrupted when the upstream RNA folds into a terminator hairpin and the polymerase is in a susceptible state. A U tract should not be confused with a polyadenylated tail or with a stretch added after transcription. Its residues are encoded in DNA and incorporated by RNA polymerase during the normal reaction.

The order of the hairpin and U tract matters. As the RNA emerges, the upstream arm of the prospective stem becomes available first. Its downstream partner appears later. When the polymerase reaches a suitable location near the end of the U tract, the upstream RNA is in a position to form or complete a stem while the hybrid is relatively weak. If the U tract occurred before the hairpin in the RNA, the temporal and spatial coincidence would be altered. A sequence with the same base composition but a different order can therefore have a very different termination behavior. The cell does not read the average proportion of G, C or U across the entire gene as a terminator; it responds to a local combination of sequence, structure and polymerase state.

A compensatory mutation experiment can test whether the ability to base-pair, rather than the identity of a particular sequence alone, is central to the function of a hairpin. Start with a functional stem. Change one arm so that pairing with the other arm is disrupted, then make a second alteration in the other arm that restores complementarity without restoring the original sequence. If the first mutation reduces termination and the double mutation restores it, the result supports a role for stem formation. It is not absolute proof that no nucleotide-specific interactions matter, because the altered stem may also change stability or pausing. Nevertheless, rescue by complementary changes is stronger evidence for a structural mechanism than a single disruptive mutation.

This logic is central to many Part C problems. A mutant terminator might produce more full-length readthrough RNA even though the promoter is unchanged. The interpretation should identify the affected step, such as weakened hairpin formation or a changed U-rich region, rather than automatically conclude that initiation increased. If the mutation lies in a translated region, an additional possibility is that ribosome behavior has changed, so a defined transcription-only system can help isolate the intrinsic sequence effect. Correct reasoning moves from the location and molecular effect of the mutation to the expected product distribution.

10. How intrinsic termination dismantles the elongation complex

A productive elongation complex can be highly stable, yet it must disassemble when the appropriate signal is copied. An intrinsic terminator achieves this through a sequence-dependent transition from elongation to release. First, polymerase approaches the U-rich region and enters a susceptible paused state. Second, complementary portions of the upstream RNA fold into a hairpin near the exit region. Third, completion of the hairpin and changes in the weak hybrid and enzyme configuration destabilize the contacts that normally retain the transcript. Finally, the RNA is released and the DNA strands can return to their paired configuration as the elongation complex is dismantled. This sequence of events is a mechanistic framework, not an assertion that every bacterial terminator traverses an identical set of microscopic intermediates.

The hairpin does not act like a pair of scissors cutting the RNA. The backbone remains intact as the transcript is released. Rather, pairing within RNA competes with interactions that helped retain and position the RNA in the transcription complex. A terminator stem can begin forming as the two arms emerge and can continue forming in a region where its completion is coupled to separation of RNA from the DNA template. In some mechanistic descriptions, the growing hairpin exerts an influence on the RNA exit path and draws RNA out of the hybrid; in others, changes in polymerase position and reannealing of DNA contribute to the transition. The physical point common to these descriptions is that RNA is transferred from a stable elongation configuration to a released state without requiring a dedicated Rho-driven translocation event.

The U-rich hybrid and the upstream stem serve complementary purposes. A hairpin can provide a thermodynamically favorable alternative arrangement for a portion of the RNA and alter the local structure of the polymerase complex. A relatively weak hybrid provides a susceptible set of contacts to disrupt. A pause creates a window in which folding and destabilization can compete with addition of the next nucleotide. If polymerase escapes the pause before productive termination can occur, readthrough may result. If the destabilizing transition happens first, a terminated transcript is produced. This explains why terminator strength is usually best viewed as a probability measured under particular conditions rather than a property that is necessarily all or nothing.

To visualize this competition, imagine two hypothetical complexes with the same eventual hairpin stability. In the first, the two arms form quickly as soon as they become available; in the second, an alternative upstream RNA interaction delays pairing of the terminator arms. Even if the thermodynamically preferred final RNA structure is the same in both cases, the first is more likely to form the required structure while polymerase is in the susceptible position. Termination thus depends not only on the equilibrium stability of the final structure but also on folding kinetics and the time available before elongation resumes. An equilibrium calculation on a complete isolated RNA may not capture this temporal competition.

The re-pairing of template and nontemplate DNA behind the active center contributes to restoring a normal duplex at the site of transcription. The fact that DNA returns toward its initial paired state is important: an intrinsic terminator does not ordinarily leave a permanent double-strand DNA break, nor does it require DNA replication to fill a missing segment. The polymerase may undergo several changes in contact with DNA before it is available to initiate another round. In a simple teaching diagram the release of RNA and dissociation of polymerase are often drawn as a single endpoint; experimentally, these events can be distinguishable. A short product can be released before every residual DNA association of polymerase has been lost. The defining consequence for a transcript is its release from the active elongation configuration.

Although the pathway is called intrinsic, accessory proteins can improve its efficiency. NusA is a bacterial elongation factor that can favor the function of certain intrinsic terminators, including those whose RNA structural elements are suboptimal. This does not contradict the definition of the pathway. The classical sequence-based terminator can function with core RNA polymerase in a purified system, but its behavior in a cell can be assisted by proteins and altered by sequence context. Some otherwise weak signals may become effective only in an appropriate factor environment. A student should distinguish the lack of an obligatory Rho requirement from a claim that the cellular process is completely unaffected by proteins.

The timing of a perturbation can also help distinguish an effect on termination from an effect on synthesis. If an enzyme variant fails to reach a terminator because it pauses earlier, the amount of terminated RNA may decrease even though the terminator would function normally if reached. Conversely, a mutant polymerase that arrives at the same rate but passes through the terminator more often likely has a defect in the termination decision. A proper measurement should compare the fraction of complexes that terminate among those that actually reach the relevant region. Comparing absolute quantities of a short band without considering upstream losses can falsely attribute a change to terminator efficiency.

A published study of NusA in Bacillus subtilis provides a useful contrast to the hypothetical mutation examples. Investigators compared cells with NusA available to cells in which its level was reduced, mapped RNA ends across the genome, and examined selected terminators in a defined transcription system. The reduction in NusA was associated with increased readthrough at many weaker termination signals. Terminators with less stable stem structures or interruptions in the U-rich region were particularly informative. The researchers also compared altered terminator sequences and found that NusA could improve the behavior of some weakened signals. The combination of cellular observations and defined reactions helped distinguish a direct effect at a terminator from the many indirect effects that can occur after a broadly important transcription factor is depleted. A limitation is that cellular depletion changes the expression of numerous genes and is not identical to removing one factor from an isolated reaction. The study therefore illustrates both the significance of accessory factors and the value of using complementary experimental approaches. Some particularly weak, noncanonical signals require NusA for effective termination in the tested cellular context; such signals should not be described as strictly factor-independent even though their sequence organization resembles that of classical intrinsic terminators.

11. Rho-dependent termination: an RNA-directed motor

Intrinsic termination solves the problem of releasing RNA through a particular combination of sequence, structure and polymerase behavior. But a bacterium must also control transcription at locations that lack a suitably arranged intrinsic terminator. Rho-dependent termination provides an additional means of recognizing an emerging transcript and influencing the behavior of the elongation complex. Rho is a protein, not an RNA sequence, and it is not one of the permanent catalytic subunits of bacterial RNA polymerase. Its ability to bind RNA and use ATP hydrolysis makes it a separate molecular motor capable of coupling events on an emerging transcript to the termination of further synthesis.

In the conventional description, the process begins at an RNA region called a Rho utilization site, commonly abbreviated rut. The recognition site is part of the newly synthesized RNA rather than a promoter sequence to which sigma factor binds. Many well-studied rut regions contain relatively abundant cytosines, relatively few guanines and enough accessible single-stranded RNA for Rho to engage. There is no simple invariant rut sequence that can be identified merely by finding a short exact motif in every bacterial genome. Base composition, RNA accessibility and the local folding history are all relevant. A cytosine-rich stretch buried in a stable helix or occupied by a protein may behave very differently from a similar stretch that remains exposed.

Rho typically assembles as a hexamer containing six copies of its protein subunit. The arrangement creates a ring-like structure capable of engaging RNA and accommodating it in a central channel. At a useful level of structural description, distinguish the initial RNA-binding surface from the RNA-engaging region associated with movement through the ring. Binding and ATP-driven translocation are different functional steps. A mutation may interfere with initial engagement of the RNA while leaving some ATPase capacity measurable in other conditions. Another mutation may allow binding but prevent productive movement because the coupling between ATP hydrolysis and changes in the protein's configuration is impaired. An assay designed to measure only one of these activities does not necessarily establish that the entire termination pathway is functional.

The polarity of RNA provides the track on which the motor operates. Rho translocates along RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction in the classical model. The polymerase continuously creates new RNA at the 3′ end, so movement of Rho toward that end takes it toward the elongation complex. This is not the same directional description as movement of RNA polymerase on the DNA template, which is read from 3′ to 5′. The two directions are compatible because RNA and template DNA are antiparallel. A diagram with the template and RNA pointing in opposite chemical directions can still show both polymerase and Rho progressing toward the same downstream transcription region.

ATP binding and hydrolysis power changes in Rho that can produce directed movement along the RNA. The ATP molecules used for this motor activity do not become part of the transcript as a consequence of being hydrolyzed by Rho. In contrast, RNA polymerase consumes ATP as a ribonucleotide substrate at template positions where A must be incorporated. This difference is critical when interpreting a purified system: removing ATP may simultaneously impair elongation and Rho movement, so a simple experiment lacking ATP cannot by itself isolate Rho's energy requirement. A more informative comparison changes the motor's capacity for ATP hydrolysis while retaining otherwise productive RNA synthesis or tests ATP-dependent RNA translocation in an assay separate from polymerization.

Rho's recognition region and the site at which RNA polymerase actually releases RNA need not be the same position. Rho can bind an RNA sequence copied earlier, then engage the elongation complex at a downstream region. Thus a mutation that removes a rut sequence can reduce termination at a site many nucleotides away. Conversely, changing the sequence where polymerase normally pauses can alter the termination behavior even if the upstream loading site remains unchanged. This spatial separation is a fundamental distinction from a simplified model in which a single DNA signal determines one exact release coordinate without any intervening factor movement.

A useful way to organize the mechanism is as a series of conditional opportunities. First, the relevant RNA must be synthesized. Second, its accessible sequence must permit Rho to associate productively. Third, the protein must adopt an RNA-engaged state that can use ATP. Fourth, it must interact with a susceptible elongation complex. Finally, the transcript must be released from the polymerase. A defect at any one of these stages can increase readthrough, yet the underlying molecular causes can differ. Restoring RNA binding would not necessarily rescue an ATPase-defective factor, while increasing ATP availability would not necessarily expose a rut region concealed by a ribosome. The ability to locate a failure in this sequence is more valuable for analytical questions than simply recalling that Rho requires ATP.

12. The catch-up model and the fate of a Rho-engaged elongation complex

The textbook catch-up model gives an intuitive account of why RNA polymerase pausing can assist Rho-dependent termination. Suppose a rut region emerges from polymerase and becomes accessible to Rho. The motor begins moving along the transcript toward the growing end. If the motor advances relative to the RNA faster than polymerase creates new RNA, the distance separating it from the RNA's exit region decreases. If polymerase continues rapidly and the motor advances slowly, that distance may increase. A pause in transcription creates an especially favorable opportunity because the polymerase temporarily stops adding track length while Rho can continue moving.

One can illustrate this reasoning with a hypothetical constant-velocity model. Let d be the effective distance in nucleotides between the initial motor position and the target position associated with polymerase. Let vR be Rho's velocity relative to RNA and vP be the rate at which polymerase adds nucleotides, both in nucleotides per second. Ignoring loading delays, RNA folding, motor detachment and changes in geometry, the separation after time t is d + (vP − vR)t. If vR exceeds vP, the separation reaches zero at d/(vR − vP). If d equals sixty nucleotides, vR equals fifty nucleotides per second and vP equals thirty nucleotides per second, the ideal catch-up time is three seconds. The dimensions are consistent because nucleotides divided by nucleotides per second yield seconds.

If the two velocities are equal and the initial separation is positive, the motor cannot close the gap in this simplified model while both move continuously. If Rho is slower, it also cannot catch up during uniform movement. However, either case may permit an encounter during a sufficiently long polymerase pause. For instance, if Rho is forty nucleotides away when polymerase stops and continues moving at twenty nucleotides per second, it would take two seconds to reach the target in the simplified calculation. A pause of one second would be insufficient under those assumptions, whereas a pause of four seconds would provide enough time. These are deliberately artificial numbers used to illustrate relative motion, not experimentally determined speeds or a prediction of termination efficiency.

Even a successful encounter need not guarantee release. A complex may be susceptible or resistant depending on its configuration and associated factors. Rho is traditionally described as disrupting the contacts that retain nascent RNA, either by pulling RNA from the complex or influencing polymerase movement and the hybrid. The exact sequence of structural transitions can depend on the system, and it is not scientifically justified to depict all Rho-dependent events as one universally established mechanical tug. More recent accounts also describe Rho associated with the elongation machinery before an apparent catch-up event, often with the assistance of NusG. Such observations refine rather than eliminate the essential principles taught here: accessible RNA, ATP-dependent Rho activity and a susceptible transcription complex are important determinants of release.

For examination purposes, the catch-up description is best understood as a model whose predictions depend on stated assumptions. If a question explicitly proposes a motor starting on exposed RNA and asks how a slower polymerase affects encounter probability, reason from the relative speeds and waiting times. If a question asks whether Rho must always first load far upstream and physically chase polymerase over a fixed distance in every bacterial system, the correct answer is more cautious. A mechanistic model may be strongly useful without being an exclusive description of all biological cases. Recognizing its scope prevents the familiar error of treating a textbook cartoon as a complete description of microscopic dynamics.

The RNA product of Rho-dependent termination is not necessarily marked by a classical hairpin followed by a long U tract. Because polymerase can pause or become susceptible at a region rather than one perfectly defined nucleotide, a collection of nearby transcript ends can be observed. Their distribution may depend on the RNA sequence, the polymerase, available factors and the reaction conditions. A cluster of short RNA products is therefore compatible with Rho-dependent termination, but the cluster alone is not proof of the mechanism. A change in the pattern when Rho is removed, restored or functionally impaired gives stronger evidence. As with intrinsic termination, RNA cleavage and degradation must be distinguished from authentic release of a nascent transcript.

This reasoning also explains why a simple comparison of Rho protein concentration with final transcript length may be misleading. Adding more motor can increase the probability of productive engagement if motor availability is limiting, but the response can become insensitive when RNA accessibility or polymerase susceptibility is the dominant limitation. If every relevant RNA region is already occupied by active Rho, additional protein need not further shorten the products. If the loading region is inaccessible, increased concentration may not restore normal termination. Experimental curves should therefore be interpreted in relation to the sequence of molecular events rather than assigned a universal linear relationship between factor concentration and termination frequency.

13. Coupling transcription to translation changes the accessibility of nascent RNA

The existence of an RNA-directed termination factor creates an important biological question: what prevents it from prematurely terminating the synthesis of ordinary messenger RNA? One answer lies in the occupancy and structure of nascent RNA. Bacterial ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being synthesized. A ribosome traveling along the RNA physically occupies a region of the molecule. Other proteins cannot always engage the same region simultaneously. If a potential Rho-loading region is covered by a translating ribosome or otherwise inaccessible, productive binding of Rho may be reduced. This does not mean every translating ribosome provides absolute protection against every Rho-dependent terminator; the geometry, spacing and factor interactions matter.

An interruption of translation can change this relationship without altering the promoter. Consider an operon that normally produces a continuous RNA encoding two proteins. If a premature stop codon appears in the first coding region, the translating ribosome may leave earlier than it would on the normal RNA. The newly exposed downstream RNA may then provide an opportunity for Rho to engage. If termination occurs before the second coding region is fully transcribed, the expression of the second protein can fall. This downstream effect of an upstream mutation is called polarity in a genetic context. It is not simply the effect of producing a shortened first protein; it can involve altered transcription of downstream sequences.

To analyze this possibility, compare several conditions rather than only the normal and mutant protein levels. Measure RNA from a region upstream of the mutation and from a region downstream of the suspected termination point. If upstream RNA is retained but downstream RNA is selectively reduced, premature termination becomes a plausible explanation. If a condition that reduces Rho-dependent termination restores downstream RNA without restoring the original first coding sequence, the case becomes stronger. Additional controls are needed because altered translation can also affect RNA stability, and a downstream protein can be lost for translational reasons even when its coding RNA is present. The distinction between protein output and RNA continuity is particularly important for Part C questions.

NusG provides another example of an association between elongation and termination. In well-studied bacteria such as E. coli, this factor interacts with the transcription elongation complex and can participate in contacts with Rho or components of the translation machinery. The functional consequence depends on which interactions are possible in a particular context. At an exam-appropriate level, NusG can be viewed as a factor connecting the behavior of an elongating polymerase with other molecular events occurring on or near the transcript. It is not itself the ATP-powered Rho motor, and it is not a substitute for the catalytic RNA polymerase core. The general lesson is that the transcription complex is part of a changing assembly rather than an enzyme working in isolation inside a cell.

Rho's ability to respond to exposed RNA is also valuable for suppressing unwanted transcription. Bacterial chromosomes contain many sequences that can, in principle, serve as templates for RNA synthesis, including sequences that are not intended to produce a functional transcript under normal conditions. If transcription begins at an unintended site or extends through a normal boundary, it can waste substrates and create interference with other genomic processes. Rho can contribute to limiting such transcription in organisms where the factor is present and active. This role is not restricted to the last codon of a protein-coding gene; it concerns the organization of transcription across the genome. Excessive formation of RNA–DNA hybrids outside the normal protected elongation complex is another potential consequence of failures in transcription control, although not every such hybrid is caused by defective Rho.

An RNA–DNA hybrid in the normal elongation complex must not be confused with a persistent R-loop. During normal synthesis, a short hybrid is retained and renewed in association with RNA polymerase. An R-loop involves RNA pairing with one DNA strand while displacing the other over a region that can extend beyond the normal transient hybrid arrangement. Persistent or inappropriate R-loops can interfere with DNA transactions. Some are biologically regulated, so their existence is not always harmful. The point relevant to termination is that proper management of RNA release and accessibility helps prevent unintended RNA association with DNA. This connects transcription to the preceding topics of replication and chromosome maintenance without requiring detailed treatment of specialized R-loop pathways.

Coupled transcription and translation is a characteristic and useful framework for bacterial gene expression, but it is not a universal description of every transcript or every moment of bacterial transcription. Noncoding RNAs do not have to be translated, and some bacterial transcription and translation events may be spatially or temporally separated. Archaeal transcription uses a multisubunit polymerase and factors with substantial differences from bacterial systems, despite the absence of a conventional eukaryotic nucleus. It is therefore preferable to describe Rho-dependent termination and the classical bacterial coupling examples specifically as bacterial mechanisms rather than claim that every prokaryotic organism has identical transcription machinery.

14. Rifampicin: using a stage-specific inhibitor to understand RNA polymerase

An inhibitor can be valuable scientifically because its effects reveal which stages of a pathway depend on its target. Rifampicin, also called rifampin, is a rifamycin antibiotic whose direct target is bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Its molecular action should not be confused with a compound that destroys all RNA, an inhibitor of the bacterial ribosome or a substance that binds DNA indiscriminately. It binds to a pocket in the beta subunit of the bacterial enzyme, near the active-center region through which the initially growing RNA must pass. The beta subunit is encoded by rpoB in standard bacterial nomenclature.

Structural and biochemical evidence reported by Campbell and colleagues in 2001 explained an important property of the drug: it obstructs the path of the very short RNA as it grows beyond roughly two or three nucleotides. This explains how the enzyme may still participate in formation of an initiation complex and very short RNA products yet fail to generate a normal long transcript. The drug does not need to compete with each of the four NTPs at their normal substrate-binding position to inhibit synthesis. It occupies a region whose physical relationship to the growing RNA makes early extension incompatible with continued productive transcription.

Rifampicin is frequently described as a transcription initiation inhibitor. The shorthand is appropriate when it refers to prevention of productive initiation and transition into established elongation. It does not imply that absolutely no promoter binding, DNA opening or first phosphodiester formation can occur in the presence of the compound. Conversely, describing rifampicin as an inhibitor of RNA-chain elongation without specifying the stage can be misleading, because its characteristic action is on the earliest extension of RNA rather than uniformly on polymerases that already carry a substantial transcript. Established elongation complexes are generally much less susceptible to its canonical inhibitory action. The timing of drug addition is therefore essential to interpreting an experiment.

Imagine a defined single-round transcription experiment with two reaction tubes. In the first, rifampicin is present before productive initiation. In the second, a stable elongation complex has already been formed and is allowed to extend after the drug is added. If the first produces predominantly very short products while the second produces much longer RNA, the contrast supports a stage-dependent action. The experiment must ensure that long RNA in the second tube is not simply RNA made before the drug was added. A pulse of label introduced after formation of the established complex can help distinguish newly added residues from pre-existing material. A no-drug control confirms that both preparations can otherwise support synthesis.

In a cell, blocking new productive initiation does not instantly remove all RNA already present. Messenger RNAs and other cellular RNAs have distinct lifetimes. Polymerases that were already engaged may continue synthesis for some time, and existing mRNA may continue to be translated. Consequently, a measurement of total RNA immediately after rifampicin addition may show a smaller change than a measurement specifically tracking new initiation. After an interval, the abundance of unstable RNA species can decrease as degradation proceeds without adequate replacement. Stable RNAs can persist much longer. Students should distinguish inhibition of the production process from loss of the existing product.

Rifampicin's bacterial target also helps explain its medical significance in treating certain bacterial infections, including tuberculosis. The relevant examination principle is selectivity for a bacterial enzyme and how a change in that enzyme can alter susceptibility. Amino-acid substitutions in the drug-binding region of RpoB can decrease the ability of rifampicin to bind effectively while preserving enough RNA polymerase activity for the bacterium to survive. Such a substitution can cause target-based antibiotic resistance. Not every rpoB mutation causes resistance, and a resistance phenotype can have additional causes involving access to the target or other cellular properties. The topic of antimicrobial resistance in Unit 2.E treats broader classes of mechanisms; here the focus remains on the connection between polymerase structure and inhibition.

A useful biochemical comparison uses a sensitive enzyme and an enzyme isolated from a resistant strain, with their basal activities normalized in a transcription assay. If the resistant enzyme retains much more activity than the sensitive one at the same inhibitor concentration, a target-based difference is supported. If both purified enzymes have similar sensitivity but intact cells differ greatly in susceptibility, altered cellular access or another context-dependent mechanism deserves investigation. It is important not to infer a specific molecular change from a whole-cell growth curve alone. Growth depends on many cellular activities, whereas a defined RNA synthesis assay narrows the range of possible explanations. This distinction between phenotype and direct biochemical mechanism is a recurring theme in antimicrobial reasoning.

15. Actinomycin D and the distinction between a DNA-binding inhibitor and an enzyme-targeted drug

Actinomycin D, also known as dactinomycin, provides an important contrasting example of transcription inhibition. Its characteristic action involves binding to DNA rather than occupying the rifampicin-binding pocket of bacterial RNA polymerase. Its planar chromophore can intercalate between DNA base pairs, while peptide portions interact with the DNA grooves. The compound shows preferences for particular sequence contexts, classically including guanine–cytosine steps, although its binding behavior is not restricted to a single simple sequence in every nucleic-acid context. By altering the properties and accessibility of the template, bound drug can interfere with RNA polymerase progression and reduce RNA synthesis.

The term intercalation describes insertion of a planar chemical group between stacked base pairs. It is distinct from replacing a DNA nucleotide with an abnormal nucleotide during a copying reaction. A bound intercalator can disturb the geometry of the local DNA helix and create a physical obstacle for a moving molecular complex. If RNA polymerase must open a region of DNA and move through it, a stable drug–DNA interaction can alter that process. The inhibitory effect thus involves the interaction of the compound with the template, rather than the same enzyme-specific mechanism established for rifampicin. This is the main distinction to retain for NET and SET questions comparing transcription inhibitors.

Because DNA is a substrate used by many DNA-associated processes, a DNA-binding compound need not act exclusively on bacterial transcription. Actinomycin D can inhibit transcription in eukaryotic cells as well, and it is used in particular medical contexts as an antineoplastic drug. Its effects depend on concentration, cellular conditions, accessibility of DNA and properties of the relevant transcription machinery. At some concentrations or experimental settings, one class of transcription can appear more sensitive than another. Such observations should not be converted into the false universal claim that the drug binds only one RNA polymerase or only bacterial DNA. Its direct interaction with DNA provides a mechanistic explanation for its broader transcriptional effects.

An experimental design comparing rifampicin and actinomycin D can illuminate this difference. Consider two otherwise identical transcription reactions in which a mature elongation complex is stalled immediately before a defined DNA region. The first reaction tests addition of a drug known to interfere primarily with productive initiation, while the second tests a drug that binds to the downstream template. If the first drug has little effect on the already established complex but the second reduces passage through a suitable occupied DNA site, the results are consistent with different stages and targets. This is a hypothetical instructional scenario; actual inhibition depends on binding, timing, concentration and the particular template. A drug may fail to reach a site in the experimental preparation, so an absence of inhibition under one condition is not universal proof of absence of interaction.

To distinguish DNA binding from binding to RNA polymerase, one could conceptually compare independent measurements of association of the compound with purified DNA and purified enzyme. DNA binding alone does not establish that every consequence in a cell is caused directly by transcription inhibition, and an enzyme-binding measurement alone does not establish that the binding inhibits a particular stage. The strongest assignment combines binding evidence with functional effects in defined reactions and appropriate controls. For rifampicin, a binding-site mutation in the enzyme can provide additional mechanistic support. For a DNA-binding drug, sequence and template dependence can provide informative evidence when interpreted with its known chemistry.

Transcription inhibitors must also be distinguished from translation inhibitors. Bacterial ribosomes are the targets of many well-known antibiotic classes, such as tetracyclines and chloramphenicol. A ribosome-directed drug can reduce protein synthesis even if transcription continues, at least initially. The effect on RNA metabolism can be indirect because disruption of translation changes RNA occupancy, ribosome movement and the regulatory environment of transcription. It would therefore be incorrect to identify the direct target of a drug solely from the observation that both RNA and protein production eventually decline. The earliest molecular effects in a defined system, together with target binding, offer more direct evidence than late consequences for cell growth.

A related exam distinction concerns alpha-amanitin, best known for its effects on eukaryotic nuclear RNA polymerases, especially RNA polymerase II. It is not the standard bacterial counterpart of rifampicin and does not share the same target specificity. The detailed comparison of eukaryotic polymerases and their sensitivities belongs to subsequent nodes of this subunit. For now, the learner should be able to classify an inhibitor by its primary target, the affected stage and the immediate biochemical consequence. This approach is much more reliable than memorizing a list of chemical names without understanding the molecular steps they perturb.

16. Measuring termination efficiency rather than counting short RNAs

The central quantitative problem in termination experiments is to measure the fraction of transcription complexes that choose release rather than onward synthesis at a defined region. Suppose one hundred complexes arrive at a terminator and eighty release RNA there while twenty continue. The termination efficiency in that particular population is eighty percent, and the readthrough fraction is twenty percent. These numbers concern the fate of complexes reaching the decision point. They do not directly measure how many RNA molecules a cell produces per unit time, because the number of complexes reaching the region also depends on initiation and earlier losses.

If T denotes the number of terminated transcripts and R denotes the number of readthrough transcripts in an experiment in which all relevant complexes have reached a final state, the termination fraction is T/(T + R). Its percentage is that fraction multiplied by one hundred. The corresponding readthrough fraction is R/(T + R). The two sum to unity under the assumptions that the measured products are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. If some complexes are still paused, some RNA molecules have degraded, or an additional product class is omitted, the relationship may not provide a faithful estimate of the underlying decision. A time course and an accounting of all major products can help establish whether these assumptions are appropriate.

An instructive complication arises in interpreting gels. If each transcript has a single identical label near its 5′ end and the label is detected linearly, band signal can be proportional to molecule number. If the signal instead depends on the number of incorporated labeled residues, a longer RNA can give a larger signal per molecule. Consider a hypothetical assay with a 100-nucleotide terminated product and a 300-nucleotide readthrough product, each having the same labeled nucleotide fraction and uniform detection. Suppose the measured signal intensities are 600 and 900 arbitrary units, respectively. Direct use of signal gives a termination fraction of 600/1500, or forty percent. But if signal per RNA is proportional to length, the molecule-equivalent values are 600/100 = 6 and 900/300 = 3. The corrected termination fraction is 6/9, or about sixty-seven percent. The exact correction would require the actual number of incorporated labeled nucleotides if only one nucleotide type is labeled. It is not always sufficient to normalize by total length.

For a sequence that can be either terminated or read through, a simple kinetic model treats the decision as competition between two independent first-order exit processes. Let kt represent the rate constant for termination and ke represent the rate constant for productive escape. If a population starts entirely in the same susceptible state and both rate constants remain constant, its total remaining population decreases with rate kt + ke. The fraction entering the termination pathway is obtained by integrating the termination flux kt times the remaining population over all time. The result is kt/(kt + ke). The analogous expression for escape is ke/(kt + ke). This derivation is an example of how the probability of a biochemical outcome can depend on competing rates rather than only on the equilibrium stability of the final products.

With kt = 3 per second and ke = 1 per second, the model predicts a termination fraction of three quarters. If a change in sequence increases escape to 3 per second while leaving termination unchanged, the fraction becomes one half. If a factor instead increases kt to 6 per second without altering the initial ke, the fraction becomes 6/7, approximately eighty-six percent. The population's mean residence time in the decision state is the inverse of the sum of the rates, so these changes also influence how long complexes remain at the site. The model cannot distinguish individual structural intermediates, and folding and factor binding may have time-dependent rates. Its value is to show why an alteration in elongation can change apparent termination efficiency even without directly altering the chemistry of release.

There may be more than one terminator in a transcription unit. Suppose an upstream terminator stops seventy percent of arriving complexes, and a downstream terminator stops half of those that reach it. Of one hundred initial complexes, thirty pass the first and fifteen pass the second, provided neither other losses nor new initiations occur between the two sites. The cumulative termination fraction is eighty-five percent, not one hundred twenty percent. The second terminator's efficiency is conditional on arrival. This is the same probability reasoning used in genetics when successive conditional events are multiplied. If the second terminator has an independent downstream promoter feeding it, however, the simple multiplication cannot describe the total downstream transcript population without accounting for the extra input.

These calculations suggest several distinct explanations for increased downstream RNA. The promoter may be more active; the first terminator may be weaker; an upstream block may have been removed; RNA may have become more stable; or transcription may begin from a previously inactive downstream promoter. Measuring only the amount of downstream RNA does not isolate these possibilities. A suitably designed comparison of upstream and downstream nascent RNA, mapped transcript ends and independent controls for initiation can narrow the explanation. A student should be prepared to state which assumptions allow a ratio to represent termination efficiency and which observations would violate those assumptions.

17. Discriminating among mechanisms with a defined transcription system

A defined experimental system is useful because it allows individual components to be varied while maintaining the others. Consider two hypothetical DNA templates containing the same bacterial promoter, a common upstream transcribed region and different internal candidate terminators. Template I contains a plausible stem-loop followed by a U-rich sequence. Template II contains a relatively accessible cytosine-rich RNA segment followed by a region where polymerase pauses. Neither sequence pattern alone proves how termination occurs. The proposed mechanisms must be tested by comparing the behavior of each template with and without active Rho and by examining whether the observed short RNAs have been released.

Suppose Template I yields eighty percent terminated RNA without Rho and eighty-two percent with Rho. Template II yields ten percent short released RNA without Rho but seventy-five percent with it. Under the assumptions that the measurements reflect actual released transcripts and that other reaction properties are controlled, these hypothetical results support substantial intrinsic termination on Template I and a major Rho-dependent contribution on Template II. They do not show that Rho never affects Template I or that all ten percent of the short RNA on Template II reflects a particular intrinsic signal. The background could arise from a weaker intrinsic pathway, another form of premature release or experimental heterogeneity. The data justify comparative mechanistic conclusions, not an exhaustive description of every product.

Now suppose that altering one arm of the proposed hairpin on Template I reduces termination from eighty percent to fifteen percent and that introducing a complementary alteration into the other arm restores termination to sixty-eight percent. The restoration is consistent with an important role for base pairing. Because the double mutant does not exactly recover the original efficiency, one should not assume that the new stem and the old stem are energetically identical. Sequence changes can also influence the rate of stem formation, the propensity of polymerase to pause or the interactions of the emerging RNA with the enzyme. Partial rescue is scientifically informative rather than a failed result: it supports the structural hypothesis while leaving room for additional contributions from sequence and kinetics.

On Template II, imagine that a Rho variant can bind the candidate RNA in a separate binding assay but has severely reduced ATP hydrolysis. If this variant fails to restore the high termination efficiency observed with normal Rho, RNA binding alone appears insufficient for the full termination effect. A complementary control uses a Rho variant with impaired RNA binding but preserved ATPase function in an appropriate biochemical context. Failure of both classes of variant would be consistent with the requirement for both RNA recognition and motor function. However, before assigning a specific molecular cause, one should consider whether the variants are folded, present in comparable active amounts and able to assemble normally. A mutation that broadly destabilizes a protein can reduce multiple activities at once.

A time-resolved experiment can distinguish reversible pausing from release. Imagine labeled elongation complexes initially positioned upstream of a candidate signal. At an early time point, a 120-nucleotide RNA is abundant. After a chase with all required unlabeled substrates, most of that intermediate becomes a 250-nucleotide RNA, while very little remains short. If a direct association measurement shows the short molecules initially remain with the elongation complex, this supports pausing and subsequent resumption. In another reaction, a 120-nucleotide RNA persists as a free product and fails to convert into longer RNA when all required substrates are available. This supports termination at the earlier region. It would be misleading to call both events termination merely because the initial gel images showed the same short band.

RNA-end mapping provides another source of evidence. A strong accumulation of RNA ends near a candidate terminator is consistent with termination but may also arise from RNA processing or selective stability. A measured mature RNA end does not necessarily specify the position at which RNA polymerase released its original transcript. A short RNA produced by cleavage of a longer molecule can have the same mapped 3′ end as a primary termination product. A useful additional measurement asks whether polymerase and newly synthesized RNA extend beyond the candidate end under the same conditions. Combined with factor perturbations and direct release evidence, this can distinguish primary transcription termination from processing events.

The same caution applies to an RNA polymerase occupancy profile. A large peak at a position followed by a sharp reduction downstream may indicate a pause that allows termination, but it is not by itself proof of termination. If polymerase remains trapped there for a long time, high occupancy can result even without immediate RNA release. An increase in short RNA combined with increased free product and decreased downstream nascent RNA provides a more complete picture. Data types are complementary because they observe different aspects of the same process: enzyme position, RNA length, RNA release and flux beyond a particular genomic region. Good examination reasoning connects observations to the appropriate molecular state rather than relying on the name of an experimental method.

18. Inhibitor time courses, synthesis versus stability, and biological integration

A common experimental strategy for studying RNA metabolism is to reduce new RNA synthesis and then follow the disappearance of RNA that already exists. The interpretation requires distinguishing transcript production from degradation. Let M denote the abundance of a particular RNA, s the rate at which complete molecules are produced, and kd a first-order loss constant. In a simple well-mixed model, dM/dt = s − kd M. At steady state, production balances loss and M equals s/kd. This is not an equilibrium between chemical reactants; it is a dynamic steady state maintained by continued production and loss. A stable RNA can be abundant even if production is relatively modest, while a rapidly degraded RNA can have low abundance despite frequent synthesis.

If production is stopped instantaneously and completely, the expression becomes dM/dt = −kd M, with solution M(t) = M(0) exp(−kd t). The half-life is ln(2)/kd. Suppose an RNA decreases from 800 molecules to 200 molecules over four minutes after a hypothetical immediate stop of synthesis. The abundance has fallen by two factors of two, so the apparent half-life is two minutes and kd is about 0.347 per minute. A second RNA that remains at 400 molecules after four minutes has undergone only one half-life, if it began at 800 under identical assumptions. Its loss constant would be approximately 0.173 per minute. The different rates say nothing by themselves about which RNA was initiated more frequently before the block.

Rifampicin is useful as an example because its effect is largely directed toward productive new initiation. In a real transcription shutoff experiment, already initiated complexes can finish copying their RNAs after the compound is added. A long transcript may continue to receive newly synthesized full-length molecules during an initial run-off interval. The time required depends on its remaining length and on the velocities of the complexes already present. If one fits an exponential disappearance curve from the exact moment of drug addition without considering continuing completion of existing transcripts, the resulting decay constant may be biased. It may appear that RNA disappears more slowly than it actually does after the source of completed molecules has ceased.

For a simplified numerical illustration, suppose a gene is 2,400 nucleotides long and an engaged polymerase is 600 nucleotides from its start when productive initiation is blocked. If its remaining productive velocity averages forty-five nucleotides per second, it has 1,800 nucleotides left to synthesize and will take about forty seconds to reach the end, ignoring additional termination delays. A polymerase that was already near the end will finish much earlier. Thus even a perfect block of further initiation creates a distribution of completion times across existing complexes. A measurement of new RNA synthesis in the first few seconds can show residual activity without contradicting a strong block of productive initiation.

The same model helps interpret genetic alterations to termination. If an upstream promoter supplies transcripts at rate s0 and a terminator allows a fraction f of complexes to pass into the downstream region, a simple downstream production rate is s0 f, assuming each surviving complex yields one appropriate RNA and no downstream promoter contributes. A change in f from 0.1 to 0.4 increases this production rate fourfold even though s0 remains unchanged. At steady state, the downstream RNA would also increase fourfold if its loss constant remains the same. If the extended transcripts are degraded more rapidly, their abundance might increase by a smaller factor or not at all. The distinction between transcription flux and mature RNA abundance therefore applies to both drug experiments and termination mutants.

Termination is also important in engineering biological expression systems. When a bacterial transcription unit is constructed to express a desired product, a suitable transcription boundary can reduce unintended readthrough into adjacent DNA. A strong terminator may help isolate the intended expression unit from neighboring units, but its performance is dependent on the polymerase, surrounding sequences and cellular conditions. Two terminators measured under different contexts cannot automatically be ranked by comparing unrelated product abundances. A comparison using the same upstream promoter and compatible measurement conditions is more informative. This application is a direct extension of the biological role of termination in preserving the boundaries of transcription units; it does not require specialized design protocols.

Comparative biology offers a final perspective. Bacterial and eukaryotic multisubunit RNA polymerases share fundamental chemical principles of template-directed chain extension, yet their termination signals and associated factors differ. Eukaryotic RNA polymerase II termination commonly involves RNA processing-linked events that are not equivalent to the standard bacterial hairpin and U-rich tract mechanism. Archaeal transcription has important similarities to eukaryotic transcription machinery, as well as its own termination strategies. Therefore, the word prokaryotic in the chapter title should not license the assumption that all organisms lacking a membrane-bound nucleus use bacterial sigma factors, bacterial Rho or the same inhibitor sensitivities. A bacterial model is powerful when its biological boundary is respected.

The main conceptual conclusion is that accurate transcription requires both controlled continuation and controlled stopping. Stable DNA and RNA contacts support processivity; local conformational flexibility allows substrate selection, pauses and recovery; sequence signals and accessory factors can favor release at appropriate boundaries. Chemical inhibitors reveal the dependence of the system on either enzyme structure or template integrity. In a quantitative or experimental problem, the most reliable starting point is to identify which step a perturbation can affect, what physical product each measurement detects, and whether the measured change can be explained by an alternative process. This reasoning prepares the learner to compare bacterial transcription with the distinct nuclear RNA polymerases and general transcription machinery considered next.

Q1. Which feature distinguishes a productive elongation complex from a promoter-bound closed complex?

  2. A growing RNA paired to template DNA
  3. An obligatory free RNA primer

Answer and explanation

B. A growing RNA paired to template DNA. An elongation complex contains the nascent RNA–DNA hybrid. A closed promoter complex has not opened the DNA; neither a free primer nor a ribosome is a required RNAP subunit.

Q2. Which RNA end receives each new nucleotide?

  1. 3′ end
  2. 5′ end
  3. Both ends alternately
  4. Either end depending on sigma
Answer and explanation

A. 3′ end. The terminal 3′ hydroxyl attacks the incoming NTP alpha phosphate; the 5′ end is not extended.

Q3. What becomes of the beta and gamma phosphates during normal incorporation?

  1. Both remain in the RNA backbone
  2. They become an amino acid
  3. They are hydrolyzed by Rho before incorporation
  4. They are released as pyrophosphate
Answer and explanation

D. They are released as pyrophosphate. The alpha phosphate becomes part of the new phosphodiester bond; beta and gamma leave as PPi. Rho is not needed.

Q4. A DNA coding strand reads 5′-ACGTTG-3′. What RNA sequence is produced across it?

  1. 5′-UGCAAC-3′
  2. 5′-CAACGU-3′
  3. 5′-ACGUUG-3′
  4. 5′-ACGTTG-3′
Answer and explanation

C. 5′-ACGUUG-3′. The coding strand and RNA have the same sequence in the same orientation except T becomes U. The other choices confuse complement, orientation or RNA bases.

  2. It is a local region of strand separation that moves with RNAP

B. It is a local region of strand separation that moves with RNAP. The bubble is renewed as downstream DNA opens and upstream DNA reanneals; only a short hybrid remains inside.

A. It contributes to correct alignment and complex stability. Complementary noncovalent pairing positions RNA and helps stabilize the complex; RNA must eventually be released.

  2. GTP hydrolysis by a ribosome
  3. ATP hydrolysis by Rho at every position
  4. The triphosphate of the incoming NTP
Answer and explanation

D. The triphosphate of the incoming NTP. The incoming NTP supplies the reactive phosphate group and releases PPi; other motors use energy independently.

  1. Number of promoters in the genome
  2. Rate of ATP synthesis

C. Ability to continue repeated incorporation without dissociation. Processivity concerns persistence on the substrate; speed and promoter selection are distinct.

A. The RNA 3′ end leaves productive alignment. Displacement of the 3′ end prevents normal catalysis. Recovery can involve forward movement or RNA cleavage.

D. Stimulating RNA cleavage in backtracked RNAP. Gre factors stimulate transcript cleavage, restoring a usable 3′ end; they are not promoter recognition factors or DNA nucleases.

Part C analytical practice

All numerical values and experimental datasets in these questions are hypothetical educational examples.

Q38. A 2,100-nt region is copied at 70 nt/s, excluding three pauses of 3, 5 and 7 s. Calculate total time and average velocity.

Answer and explanation

30 + 15 = 45 s; 2,100/45 = 46.7 nt/s. The productive rate excludes pause time; dividing length by elapsed time includes it.

Q39. At a pause, 25% remain after 10 s, with single first-order escape and no new entry. Find half-time and escape rate.

Answer and explanation

5 s; ln(2)/5 = 0.139 s⁻¹. Two half-lives reduce the population to one-quarter. If release also occurs, the fitted rate is total exit.

Q40. A Rho motor is 90 nt behind RNAP. Motor speed 60 nt/s, RNAP speed 30 nt/s. Under constant-speed catch-up assumptions, when do they meet?

Answer and explanation

3 s. Relative speed is 30 nt/s, so 90/30 = 3. Meeting is not guaranteed termination.

Q41. Rho is 48 nt away at the start of a pause and moves at 24 nt/s. Does a 1.5-s pause permit catch-up? What about 3 s?

Answer and explanation

No for 1.5 s; yes for 3 s in the model. The distance requires 48/24 = 2 s. Loading delays and conformational states are ignored.

Q42. Termination and readthrough molecule counts are 135 and 45. Calculate both fractions.

Answer and explanation

TE = 75%; readthrough = 25%. Divide each count by 180; their sum is 100% only if all relevant products are counted.

Q43. Terminated 150-nt RNA has signal 900; readthrough 450-nt RNA has signal 900. Uniform signal per residue. Calculate corrected termination efficiency.

Answer and explanation

75%. Molecule equivalents are 900/150 = 6 and 900/450 = 2; 6/(6+2) = 0.75, not 50%.

Q44. A single decision state has kt = 2 s⁻¹ and ke = 6 s⁻¹. What are termination probability and mean residence time?

Answer and explanation

0.25 and 0.125 s. Competing rates give kt/(kt+ke); mean dwell time is 1/(kt+ke).

Q45. Two sequential terminators stop 60% and 75% of arrivals. With no new initiation or other loss, what percentage reads through both?

Answer and explanation

10%. Readthrough = (1−0.60)(1−0.75) = 0.40×0.25 = 0.10; total termination is 90%.

Q46. Under an ideal instantaneous block, RNA drops from 960 to 120 molecules in 9 min. Find apparent half-life.

Answer and explanation

3 min. 960/120 = 8 = 2³, so three half-lives occupy nine minutes. Run-off could bias real measurements.

Q47. A polymerase has 1,500 nt remaining when rifampicin is added. At 50 nt/s and assuming no effect on established elongation, when can it finish?

Answer and explanation

30 s. The block prevents new productive starts, not necessarily ongoing extension; 1,500/50 = 30.

Q48. A mutant stem arm gives termination 12% versus WT 80%; double compensatory mutant gives 73%. What is the strongest inference?

Answer and explanation

Stem complementarity is important; the rescue supports RNA structure rather than a uniquely required original base sequence. The rescue does not prove all sequence-specific effects are absent, and 73% is not identical to WT.

Q49. A candidate site gives 8% short RNA without Rho, 70% with WT Rho, and 9% with an RNA-binding but ATPase-impaired Rho. Explain.

Answer and explanation

A large Rho-dependent component requires functional ATPase activity, assuming variants are stable and active amounts comparable. RNA binding alone is insufficient; short product release and degradation controls are still necessary.

Q50. A 100-nt RNA accumulates at 5 s, then most becomes 300-nt RNA in a single-round chase. What is the interpretation?

Answer and explanation

A reversible pause is supported. Conversion of the labeled associated product into longer RNA distinguishes pausing from irreversible release.

Q51. A mutation reduces downstream RNA but leaves upstream RNA unchanged. Rho impairment rescues the downstream signal. What mechanism is supported?

Answer and explanation

Premature Rho-dependent termination, possibly due to exposed RNA. Alternative effects on stability or internal promoter activity require controls; the rescue strengthens the termination hypothesis.

Q52. Drug X inhibits only new productive starts in a defined assay. Drug Y binds duplex DNA and reduces passage of established complexes. Identify likely examples.

Answer and explanation

X: rifampicin; Y: actinomycin D. The timing and binding evidence distinguish the direct targets. A growth effect alone would not identify the drugs.

Q53. A polymerase mutant doubles occupancy at a locus but halves downstream transcript output. What competing interpretations should be considered?

Answer and explanation

Increased dwell time, stalling or termination may reduce productive flux. Occupancy depends on arrival and residence; it does not establish increased initiation.

Q54. Two RNA ends are identical in sequence; one is produced in vitro by RNAP release, the other by cleavage of a longer RNA. Can 3′-end mapping alone distinguish them?

Answer and explanation

No. Both have the same mapped end. Nascent transcription and release/cleavage evidence are needed.

Q55. An RNA production rate is 100 molecules/min and readthrough is 0.2. A mutant changes readthrough to 0.5, leaving decay constant and initiation unchanged. Predict relative downstream steady-state RNA.

Answer and explanation

2.5-fold increase. Flux rises from 20 to 50 molecules/min; at identical first-order loss the steady-state ratio is 50/20.

Q56. A mutation slows RNAP but leaves Rho motion unchanged; under catch-up assumptions, what is the predicted effect on Rho-dependent termination?

Answer and explanation

More opportunity for Rho encounter and potentially increased termination. The change is conditional on accessible RNA and susceptible RNAP, and does not guarantee release.

Q57. A drug inhibits RNA production in intact cells, but purified RNAP from treated cells functions normally without drug. Is the direct target established?

Answer and explanation

No. Removing drug or cellular context may restore activity; compare direct target binding and defined functional reactions with drug present.

A pause can recover; termination releases RNA.
Hairpin alone
A stem-loop is not always an efficient intrinsic terminator.
U-tract strand
The coding strand is T-rich; the template is A-rich.
Rho recognition
Rut is accessible RNA, not a fixed promoter DNA motif.
Rho direction
Rho moves along RNA 5′→3′, not 3′→5′.
Rifampicin
It blocks productive early synthesis; established elongation complexes may continue.
Actinomycin D
Direct DNA binding is not a selective bacterial RpoB pocket interaction.
RNA end mapping
A mature 3′ end may result from processing rather than RNAP release.
Gel intensity
Intensity per transcript may depend on labeling and length.
High occupancy
Long residence time can raise occupancy without increased flux.
