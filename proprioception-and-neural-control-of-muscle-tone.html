1. The biological problem: knowing where the body is

A motor command does not guarantee a movement. The same neural instruction to an elbow flexor can move an unloaded forearm rapidly, hold a heavy object almost motionless, or fail to prevent extension if an external force is sufficiently large. The difference arises because movement depends on the interaction of muscle force, limb geometry, gravity and external loads. A nervous system that merely sends instructions without receiving information about their consequences would have difficulty adjusting those instructions to unpredictable circumstances. Proprioceptive feedback helps solve this problem by reporting mechanical conditions within the body, allowing neural circuits to refine movement and maintain useful resistance to disturbances.

Consider standing on a firm surface with the eyes closed. The muscles around the ankle do not need to generate maximal force, but their activity must adjust when the body sways. Forward sway can increase the length of some calf muscles, alter the distribution of pressure over the sole, and change the activity of several muscle receptors. These sensory signals participate in corrective motor responses. Vestibular signals and central predictions also contribute. Removing vision does not remove every source of orientation information. Conversely, preserving muscle strength does not guarantee that a person can stand steadily if the information needed for coordination is missing. The distinction between the capacity to produce force and the capacity to regulate it is fundamental.

Proprioception encompasses the sensations and signals related to the configuration and movement of body parts, including aspects of force and effort. The sense of joint position and the sense of movement, often called kinaesthesia, can be investigated separately. A person may identify the direction of passive movement before identifying the exact final angle. Force perception is also not identical to position perception: holding a stationary load may require substantial muscular effort without changing joint position. These are related functions of a distributed sensorimotor system, not three perfectly independent labelled lines.

A proprioceptor is a sensory receptor whose activity carries information about the body's own mechanical state. In the mammalian skeletal muscle system, the muscle spindle and Golgi tendon organ are especially important. Muscle spindles are arranged mechanically in parallel with nearby force-producing muscle fibres and are sensitive to their local length and changes in length. Tendon organs are arranged in series with groups of muscle fibres and respond to the force transmitted through their collagen bundles. The words parallel and series describe mechanical relationships, not the arrangement of electrical synapses. The distinction explains why one receptor can remain active during maintained stretch while another responds strongly during contraction against a load.

Receptors in the skin and joint tissues also contribute to the estimation of limb configuration. Skin on the outside of a bending joint stretches while skin on the inside tends to fold; the pattern provides information that complements muscle receptor signals. Joint receptors can be especially informative in particular parts of the range of motion, but it would be misleading to treat them as the only or universal source of joint angle. The nervous system integrates many measurements with the geometry of the body and information about motor commands. No single muscle receptor directly reports an abstract three-dimensional position of the whole limb.

Feedback can influence movement even when the animal does not consciously recognise the sensory signal. Afferent fibres enter the spinal cord, and some of their branches affect local spinal circuits. Other branches participate in ascending pathways through which the brain receives information. Thus a sudden stretch may evoke a spinal response and also contribute to a conscious sensation; these outcomes do not require identical neural routes or identical processing times. Removing a conscious sensory pathway need not abolish every local reflex, while damage to the peripheral receptor or its afferent may compromise both.

The preceding chapter established that alpha motor neurons activate extrafusal skeletal muscle fibres, which produce force through excitation–contraction coupling. Here the key question is how the firing of those motor neurons is adjusted in relation to what the muscle is actually doing. This requires an examination of mechanical transduction, sensory firing, spinal synaptic organisation, the control of intrafusal contraction and the interaction of local circuits with descending motor pathways. The central selection and coordination of movement will be developed in the following chapter.

2. From observation to the experimental study of feedback

The existence of sensory responses to the deformation of muscles can be understood in the context of classical studies of reflex action. The work of Charles Sherrington in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was particularly influential because it treated the nervous system as an integrative network rather than a collection of independent reflexes. In his 1906 book, The Integrative Action of the Nervous System, Sherrington described relationships between sensory input, motor output and the coordination of antagonistic muscles. His use of the concept of proprioception helped distinguish information arising from the body itself from information about external events. The modern account has been refined by direct recordings of sensory fibres and by experiments in intact moving animals.

One observation that motivates the distinction between different muscle receptors is that passive stretching and active contraction are mechanically different interventions. If a muscle is stretched without voluntary activity, both its length and passive tension may rise. If it is stimulated to contract while its overall length is held nearly constant, its active tension may rise substantially without a comparable increase in whole-muscle length. Recording from a sensory fibre during these interventions allows an investigator to ask whether the fibre is more responsive to length, to force, or to a combination of mechanical variables. The interventions must be compared under specified conditions because active contraction can alter fascicle length even when the muscle–tendon unit is fixed.

Classical preparations in animal physiology allowed experimenters to record impulses in individual afferents while applying controlled length changes to a muscle. A controlled ramp followed by a hold is especially informative. During the ramp, both length and velocity change relative to the initial state. During the hold, length remains increased while velocity becomes approximately zero. The difference between firing near the end of the ramp and firing during the later hold reveals a dynamic component, whereas a maintained difference between the original and final firing rates reveals a static component. These terms describe features of the response, not two separate electrical forms of action potential.

The existence of motor innervation of the spindle leads to a further experimental question. If the receptor were simply a passive strain sensor, its response at a given length and history should not be independently adjustable by the nervous system. Experiments stimulating fusimotor axons demonstrate otherwise. Activation of the contractile ends of intrafusal fibres can increase tension at the central sensory region, increasing firing or sensitivity without requiring the same change in the force-producing fibres. Comparing alpha stimulation alone with combined alpha and gamma stimulation provides particularly clear evidence that the sensory apparatus can remain responsive during active shortening.

The classical description of an isolated reflex is invaluable for identifying connectivity, but it does not by itself specify what the same pathway does during natural movement. The excitability of interneurons and motor neurons changes with behavioural state. A circuit that reduces extensor activation under one set of conditions can participate in promoting load-bearing activity under another. Recording an afferent is therefore not equivalent to recording the final motor response. Evidence about receptor physiology, synaptic connections and functional effects must be interpreted at the appropriate level of organisation.

The experimental reasoning developed in this chapter uses distinctions between mechanical input and electrical output, direct and indirect synaptic pathways, and observations made under controlled conditions versus those made during behaviour. The classical account can explain a tendon-tap reflex under well-defined conditions; the broader evidence is necessary for understanding locomotion, posture and the context-dependent action of force feedback. The sources at the end include the relevant chapters of Neuroscience and reviews of proprioceptive sensation and spinal force feedback.

3. Muscle spindle structure and mechanical transduction

A skeletal muscle contains numerous elongated extrafusal fibres. Their sarcomeres generate force and their attachments transmit force toward the tendons. Embedded within many skeletal muscles are smaller encapsulated receptors known as muscle spindles. Each spindle contains several specialised intrafusal muscle fibres. These are arranged in a connective tissue capsule, and their mechanical attachment places them approximately in parallel with nearby extrafusal fibres. They occupy a small fraction of the muscle's volume and are specialised primarily for sensory regulation rather than for producing appreciable joint torque.

An intrafusal fibre has a central region specialised for sensory endings and peripheral regions capable of active contraction. The central region contains nuclei in characteristic arrangements and has little contractile apparatus compared with the polar ends. When the spindle is elongated, the deformation of this central region changes the mechanical stress on the associated sensory ending. The polar regions are supplied by fusimotor neurons, so the tension experienced by the ending is a consequence of both externally imposed elongation and contraction within the spindle. This structural arrangement explains why the receptor has an adjustable operating point.

The principal morphological classes are nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres. In a bag fibre the nuclei are clustered within a wider central region; in a chain fibre they form a more elongated arrangement. The nuclear bag category includes bag1 fibres, usually associated with dynamic sensitivity, and bag2 fibres, usually associated with static sensitivity. The distinction is useful but must not be exaggerated into a rule that one class detects only velocity and the other only length. The recorded response of an afferent often integrates mechanical contributions from more than one intrafusal fibre type.

Primary sensory endings of group Ia afferents contact the central regions of bag and chain fibres. Secondary endings, supplied by group II afferents, generally contact regions adjacent to the equator, especially on nuclear chain and bag2 fibres. The primary ending is often described as annulospiral and the secondary ending as flower-spray. These morphological descriptions are textbook patterns and can vary across species and individual receptors. A group Ia fibre is a large, rapidly conducting sensory axon, and its conduction speed contributes to the short latency of stretch reflexes.

When the spindle is stretched, mechanical deformation of the sensory terminal opens mechanically sensitive ion channels and generates a graded receptor potential. Piezo2 is a major mechanotransduction channel in mammalian proprioceptive neurons. Ionic currents depolarise the sensory ending; when the depolarisation reaching an excitable region is sufficient, action potentials are generated. Increasing the stimulus usually changes the timing and frequency of spikes rather than the magnitude of each propagated spike. This links the mechanical input to the principles of neural coding established in the earlier chapters.

There is no conventional chemical synapse between the intrafusal fibre and the sensory ending that is required to convert stretch into the receptor potential. The ending is itself the mechanosensitive part of the sensory neuron. By contrast, when that sensory neuron communicates with a spinal motor neuron, transmission at the central synapse is chemical and depends on transmitter release. Confusing these two interfaces leads to a common mechanistic error: the ionic events that initiate the sensory impulse and the ionic events occurring in the postsynaptic motor neuron are not the same event.

A sensory ending may discharge even when the muscle is held at a constant length, provided the spindle remains under sufficient tension. This background discharge is valuable because both an increase and a decrease in firing can signal deviations from the current operating condition. If the spindle becomes completely unloaded, however, its discharge may decline greatly or cease. Under that condition a further shortening cannot produce a proportional decrease in firing below zero. The resulting loss of sensitivity is one reason the nervous system actively controls intrafusal tension.

Muscle length is a useful variable but must be defined carefully. The distance between the muscle's origin and insertion is not necessarily the length of every fascicle, because tendons stretch and fascicles can change their angle of pennation. A spindle lies within local muscle tissue; it does not simply measure the angle between two bones. For example, a person maintaining a fixed ankle angle while increasing calf muscle force can change the relative lengths of tendon and muscle fascicles. A spindle's response may therefore change even though an external angle sensor reports no movement.

The organisation of the spindle is particularly suitable for detecting disturbances in a muscle that is acting to maintain a limb position. Stretching that muscle can stretch its intrafusal elements, increase afferent activity and elicit a compensatory change in motor neuron firing. Yet the magnitude of the correction also depends on the control of the spindle and on the state of the spinal motor circuitry. A muscle spindle should therefore be regarded as an adjustable sensory element embedded within a mechanically active organ, rather than an immutable ruler attached to a muscle.

4. Static and dynamic encoding: reading a spindle trace

Imagine a muscle maintained at its initial length for several seconds. A primary spindle afferent discharges regularly at a baseline rate. The experimenter then lengthens the muscle smoothly over a short interval and maintains the new length. The afferent typically fires more rapidly during elongation. When lengthening ends, the rate decreases from its dynamic peak, but may remain above the original baseline because the spindle is now held at a longer length. Plotting the spike train together with the actual length and velocity traces reveals more than simply counting the total number of spikes.

The static response can be estimated by comparing the mean firing rates during two steady holds at different lengths. Suppose an illustrative primary afferent discharges at 20 impulses per second before a controlled elongation of 2 mm and at 32 impulses per second during the later plateau. If other conditions are unchanged, the local static sensitivity calculated from these two points is (32 − 20)/2 = 6 impulses per second per millimetre. This estimate characterises the measured interval only. A different baseline length, recent history or level of fusimotor activity might produce a different slope. The units indicate that a length increment of one millimetre is associated with approximately six additional impulses per second under the stipulated conditions.

Suppose that during the elongation the firing rate reaches 52 impulses per second just before the hold. The difference between this rate and the later plateau is 20 impulses per second. If the speed of elongation was 4 mm/s, a simple linear velocity coefficient estimated from these values would be 5 impulses per second per (mm/s). This procedure is a simplified teaching calculation rather than a complete model of the primary ending. It treats length and velocity effects as additive, neglects rapid transients and adaptation, and assumes that the end-ramp length is the same as that during the plateau. In an actual preparation, the precise time at which the plateau is measured must be stated.

A secondary spindle ending also responds to maintained stretch, but its transient response to velocity is often less prominent than that of a primary ending. To discriminate the endings experimentally, one should compare both the transient and maintained rates, under equivalent starting lengths and fusimotor conditions. A single high firing rate during one stretch is not sufficient evidence of afferent identity, since a receptor with a high baseline might generate a higher absolute rate than another despite having a smaller change in response to velocity.

A useful local model expresses discharge rate f as f0 + ksΔL + kvv, where f is in impulses/s, f0 is the baseline rate, ΔL is elongation in mm, v is lengthening velocity in mm/s, ks is the local static sensitivity and kv the local dynamic sensitivity. A positive v represents lengthening. This model is a first-order description of small perturbations around an operating point, not a universal equation for the spindle. It omits saturation, history-dependent mechanical effects, fusimotor state, adaptation and the fact that the discharge cannot fall below zero. Its value is that it distinguishes a persistent displacement from a temporary change in velocity.

For example, consider two ramps of identical 3 mm displacement, one at 1 mm/s and another at 6 mm/s. With f0 = 15 impulses/s, ks = 4 impulses/s/mm and kv = 2 impulses/s/(mm/s), the estimated end-ramp rates are 29 and 39 impulses/s respectively. After both ramps end and the velocity becomes zero, the predicted maintained rate is 27 impulses/s in both trials. The difference between the ramps is not a difference in final length; it follows from their different velocities. If the final maintained rates were unequal, possible explanations would include altered history, adaptation, nonlinearity or a change in fusimotor drive.

The static sensitivity of a receptor should not be confused with its absolute firing rate. A manipulation that raises the baseline but leaves the change in rate per millimetre unchanged shifts the operating point without increasing the local slope. Another manipulation can increase slope while leaving baseline almost constant. These different outcomes can be distinguished by measuring responses to several lengths and speeds rather than reporting one number. This distinction is especially important when interpreting the effects of gamma motor neuron activation.

History matters because the intrafusal fibres are mechanical structures, not ideal springs. Recent shortening or contraction can influence internal tension, and a subsequent return to the same length does not necessarily restore the same receptor response immediately. The term thixotropy is used for history-dependent mechanical properties of muscle, including changes in stiffness associated with previous activity and length. At the NET level, the important reasoning is that identical current joint angles do not imply identical receptor activity if the preceding mechanical conditions differ. A controlled experiment should therefore standardise preconditioning, initial posture and the time interval before recording.

The same reasoning applies to interpretation of action potential counts. A high rate early in a movement does not necessarily mean that the final position is extreme. It may reflect rapid lengthening, an increase in gamma activation or a mixture of both. Conversely, a receptor can maintain activity at a stationary posture. The nervous system receives population signals over many muscles and can compare their changing patterns with the motor task. Position estimation emerges from integration rather than from a literal reading of one spindle's firing frequency as an angle.

5. Alpha and gamma motor neurons: controlling force and sensor tension

Motor neurons in the ventral grey matter of the spinal cord send their axons through the ventral roots and peripheral nerves to skeletal muscles. Alpha motor neurons supply the extrafusal fibres that produce the major share of skeletal muscle force. Gamma motor neurons supply the contractile portions of intrafusal fibres. Both are somatic efferents; neither is a sensory afferent. Their axons differ in diameter and target, and their physiological roles cannot be inferred merely from the fact that both release acetylcholine at neuromuscular contacts. The target determines whether the resulting contraction contributes primarily to external force or to the mechanical state of a receptor.

To appreciate the gamma pathway, imagine an ordinary elastic cord that contains a small force sensor. If the cord is stretched at both ends, the sensor is loaded. If the ends move closer together, the cord may lose tension. Now imagine a mechanism that can pull the sensor's ends apart from inside the apparatus, even when the external attachment points move closer together. This is a crude analogy for the action of intrafusal contraction. It is limited because the spindle is an active biological structure with different fibre types, viscoelasticity and multiple sensory endings, rather than a sensor attached to a simple ideal spring.

When gamma motor neurons increase their discharge, acetylcholine is released at the motor endings on the polar regions of intrafusal fibres. The contractile apparatus in these regions responds to excitation. Since the central sensory region is mechanically linked to both polar portions, their contraction can stretch or maintain the tension of that region. The resulting mechanical deformation enhances the ability of sensory endings to respond. This means that contraction of one part of the intrafusal fibre can produce elongation of another part, even though the length of the entire spindle may remain approximately constant. There is no contradiction: the lengths of components connected in series can change in opposite directions.

At fixed external length, gamma activation can increase spindle afferent firing. If the spinal circuitry linking the afferent to alpha motor neurons is sufficiently excitable, this can lead indirectly to increased extrafusal activity. This sequence is sometimes called the gamma loop. Gamma activity does not directly produce the principal skeletal muscle force; the force appears through the alpha motor neurons and their extrafusal targets. Consequently, blocking the sensory afferent can remove an indirect mechanical effect of gamma activation while preserving the local intrafusal contraction. This provides a way of separating the direct and reflex consequences of a motor manipulation.

The gamma loop also illustrates an important distinction between functional connectivity and actual activity. A complete anatomical pathway may be present, yet its output can be small if the Ia input is weak, the alpha motor pool is far from threshold, or transmission at the relevant synapses is attenuated. Conversely, a relatively small afferent change can recruit motor neurons that are already close to threshold because of descending or other spinal inputs. It is therefore incorrect to claim that every gamma action potential inevitably produces a corresponding alpha action potential.

The terms static and dynamic are also applied to gamma motor neurons. Dynamic gamma axons predominantly influence bag1 fibres, enhancing the dynamic component of primary spindle responses. Static gamma axons supply bag2 and nuclear chain fibres and enhance tonic length-related signalling by primary and secondary endings. These functional effects are most readily understood from comparisons made while a muscle is subjected to controlled length changes. They are not perfectly independent controls because different intrafusal fibres interact mechanically through the spindle apparatus.

Suppose that a controlled stretch is repeated at identical length and velocity before and during a change in fusimotor stimulation. In one condition the maintained discharge rises substantially and the secondary afferent shows enhanced steady responsiveness; this pattern supports a predominant static gamma contribution. In a second condition the transient firing during the ramp increases disproportionately, while the maintained response changes relatively little; this supports a predominant dynamic gamma contribution. A conclusion based only on a single rate at the end of a ramp would be weaker, because both forms of gamma activity can affect the primary afferent.

Some motor axons, called beta or skeletofusimotor axons, can innervate both extrafusal and intrafusal fibres in certain mammalian preparations. Their existence is a useful exception to an overly rigid division of all motor axons into mutually exclusive alpha and gamma categories. Nevertheless, the alpha–gamma distinction remains a powerful explanatory framework for understanding the control of force and sensor tension. At examination level, beta innervation is best treated as a qualification, not as a replacement for the standard scheme.

A complete account of fusimotor control must also distinguish spindle sensitivity from reflex gain. Spindle sensitivity concerns the change in sensory firing for a specified mechanical change. Reflex gain concerns the magnitude of the resulting motor or mechanical correction relative to the disturbance. Gamma drive can influence reflex gain through the spindle, but presynaptic regulation, interneuronal activity, alpha motor neuron excitability and muscle mechanics can alter the reflex response even at the same spindle sensitivity. To conclude that increased reflex amplitude proves increased gamma firing is therefore an error of causal inference.

6. Alpha–gamma coactivation during shortening

A major difficulty arises when a skeletal muscle is actively shortened. If only its extrafusal fibres contract, the decrease in fascicle length can reduce the tension in its parallel intrafusal fibres. The spindle may become slack and its afferent discharge may fall, even while the animal is producing substantial force. This is not a failure of the action potential mechanism. The sensory terminal is receiving a smaller mechanical stimulus because the geometry of the muscle has changed. Without adjustment, the feedback sensor would become least useful just when an actively shortening muscle needs to report unexpected disturbances.

Alpha–gamma coactivation provides one solution. Descending commands and spinal circuits can activate alpha and gamma motor neurons together. The extrafusal fibres shorten as the alpha motor neurons discharge. Contraction of the intrafusal polar regions counteracts the unloading of the sensory equator. Afferent activity may be maintained, and the spindle remains better positioned to detect a superimposed stretch. The expression coactivation means that the two motor systems can be recruited in a coordinated manner; it does not require identical instantaneous spike frequencies or a fixed one-to-one correspondence between the two neuronal populations.

One can compare three idealised conditions in an isolated preparation. In the first, a relaxed muscle is passively stretched, so spindle discharge increases. In the second, alpha motor neurons are stimulated and the muscle is allowed to shorten, so the spindle discharge may decrease. In the third, the same shortening is accompanied by suitable gamma stimulation, so the loss of afferent firing is reduced. If the joint angle, fascicle length and alpha output are carefully matched between the latter two conditions, the difference in afferent response supports a role for intrafusal tension rather than a difference in the overall movement.

Coactivation does not necessarily preserve the exact original firing frequency. The required balance between extrafusal shortening and intrafusal contraction depends on the velocity of shortening, the starting mechanical state and the types of intrafusal fibres activated. If gamma drive is too small, the receptor may still unload. If gamma drive is sufficiently large, firing can increase even during shortening. The nervous system can therefore modify sensitivity in relation to the task rather than simply maintaining a single fixed sensory value.

Consider reaching toward a cup and then grasping it. In a predictable, unloaded phase of movement, sensory information must help guide the trajectory without opposing every intended change in muscle length. When the cup is lifted, the weight may differ from expectation. Feedback becomes useful for detecting the discrepancy and modifying the force produced. Gamma activity can assist by maintaining spindle sensitivity, while central circuits adjust how strongly particular afferent inputs influence motor output. The action of the gamma system is not equivalent to increasing muscle force indiscriminately.

A model that treats alpha–gamma coactivation as an obligatory invariant rule misses the adaptability observed across tasks. Fusimotor activity can be adjusted according to expected mechanical demands, and sensory pathways can be differently weighted during locomotion or other patterned movements. Some behaviours require muscles to yield to disturbances; others require substantial resistance to changes in limb configuration. Maintaining spindle activity is advantageous only when the resulting feedback is interpreted in the context of the intended movement.

It is possible to distinguish the effects of an ordinary motor command from the feedback that follows it using a simple causal comparison. If motor activity begins before any change in spindle discharge, the initial activation cannot be explained solely as a reflex response to that discharge. Later adjustments may nevertheless depend on sensory feedback. If a perturbation changes spindle activity and is followed by a reproducible alteration of muscle activation after a plausible neural delay, sensory involvement is supported. Additional controls are needed to distinguish a local spinal reflex from longer pathways and to exclude direct changes caused by the original motor command.

The relationship between intended movement and afferent signals is also relevant to conscious perception. During active movements, the brain has access to information associated with its own commands as well as to feedback from peripheral receptors. During externally imposed passive movement, the contribution of a voluntary command differs. The same peripheral firing pattern may therefore be interpreted in different contexts. This is one reason proprioception is not a simple passive reading of muscle length, although muscle spindles are central to its physiological basis.

7. The stretch reflex: a causal sequence in the spinal cord

The stretch reflex is a motor response elicited by elongation of a muscle. It tends to oppose the imposed elongation by increasing activation of that muscle or of functionally related muscles. The most direct mammalian pathway begins at a primary spindle ending and proceeds through a group Ia afferent to alpha motor neurons supplying the stretched muscle. The response is known as the myotatic reflex. Its canonical excitatory pathway contains one central synapse, which is why it is described as monosynaptic.

The word monosynaptic refers to the connection between the afferent and motor neuron in the central nervous system. It does not mean that the whole pathway contains only one physiological transfer or that the muscle contracts without a neuromuscular junction. Mechanical transduction at the receptor, conduction along the sensory axon, chemical transmission in the spinal cord, conduction along the motor axon, neuromuscular transmission and excitation–contraction coupling all contribute to the complete response. The neuromuscular synapse is peripheral and is not counted when naming the spinal reflex as monosynaptic.

The sensory neuron has its cell body in a dorsal root ganglion. Its peripheral process carries impulses from the spindle, and its central process enters the spinal cord through a dorsal root. The central axon branches and establishes excitatory contacts with alpha motor neurons in the ventral horn. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials can summate with other inputs and trigger action potentials in the motor neuron. The motor axon exits via a ventral root and travels toward the extrafusal fibres. When the muscle is activated, it produces force opposing the external lengthening, to an extent determined by load and mechanical conditions.

The afferent neuron does not need to conduct an impulse to its cell body and then cross an additional synapse in the dorsal root ganglion. A pseudounipolar sensory neuron's peripheral and central processes are parts of one neuron. This matters when tracing a reflex circuit in an examination diagram: the ganglion contains sensory cell bodies but is not an extra relay synapse along this pathway. Confusion with autonomic ganglia, where preganglionic neurons synapse on postganglionic neurons, leads to an incorrect synapse count.

The excitatory Ia pathway is particularly useful for rapid local correction because it has relatively few central processing stages and uses rapidly conducting sensory and motor fibres. The reflex latency is not zero. The receptor must transduce the disturbance, impulses must travel along nerves, synaptic transmission must occur, and skeletal muscle must respond. A correction generated by the reflex therefore follows the perturbation rather than preventing its initial occurrence. Anticipatory mechanisms can prepare the muscles before a predictable disturbance, but those mechanisms should not be confused with feedback elicited by the actual stretch.

A tendon tap provides a familiar illustration. Tapping the patellar tendon briefly displaces the extensor apparatus and stretches the quadriceps muscle spindles. The resulting Ia input can excite the quadriceps motor pool, causing knee extension. Although the mechanical stimulus is delivered through the tendon, the principal sensory receptor responsible for the canonical tendon jerk is the muscle spindle, not the Golgi tendon organ. The term tendon reflex describes the manner in which the mechanical stimulus is commonly delivered. It does not identify the receptor that initiates the excitatory stretch-reflex pathway.

The movement produced by a tendon tap is only a visible consequence of the neural and muscular events. If an investigator records surface electromyography, an electrical response can often be detected before the full movement becomes apparent. A force transducer can reveal mechanical tension changes even when joint motion is constrained. These measurements answer different questions. EMG reflects electrical activity in muscle, whereas joint displacement depends on force, inertia, opposing muscles, passive resistance and external support. A lack of visible movement is therefore insufficient on its own to prove a complete absence of reflex motor activity.

A maintained stretch differs from the brief disturbance produced by a tendon tap. A sustained elongation can lead to a maintained change in spindle discharge and a tonic alteration in motor activity, whereas a sudden stretch often produces a prominent transient response. The contribution of dynamic Ia signalling, static spindle responses and other spinal pathways depends on the imposed movement and physiological state. The phasic and tonic components are not entirely separate reflex systems; they are aspects of a feedback system that responds differently to different time courses of disturbance.

The expression homonymous muscle refers to the muscle from which the afferent originates. Excitatory Ia projections also reach some synergistic motor pools, which produce mechanically compatible actions. A synergist need not generate precisely the same force direction as the homonymous muscle at every joint angle, especially for muscles that cross more than one joint. The spinal circuitry coordinates the behaviour of muscles rather than operating as a universal rule that all muscles with a textbook label receive equal activation.

A positive synaptic connection can contribute to negative mechanical feedback. Stretch increases Ia activity, which excites alpha motor neurons, which increase force in the stretched muscle. If that force opposes further elongation, the resulting effect on the original length deviation has a negative sign. The sign of feedback must therefore be determined around the entire mechanical and neural loop, not from whether the first synapse is excitatory or inhibitory. An excitatory neural pathway can form part of a stabilising negative-feedback system.

8. Reciprocal inhibition and coordinated antagonists

At a typical joint, muscles generate forces that can produce opposing movements. An elbow flexor can reduce the angle between the arm and forearm, whereas an elbow extensor can increase it. During a simple flexion response, strong simultaneous activation of an opposing extensor may reduce the resulting movement. Reciprocal inhibition is a neural mechanism that can facilitate coordinated activation by reducing the excitability of antagonist motor neurons when the agonist receives appropriate excitatory input.

In the classical spinal stretch-reflex circuit, a group Ia afferent branches to contact an inhibitory interneuron. This interneuron forms inhibitory connections onto alpha motor neurons of a suitable antagonist. The resulting inhibitory postsynaptic effect can reduce the likelihood of motor neuron firing. The route from the afferent to the antagonist motor neuron includes two central synapses: the excitatory connection to the interneuron and the inhibitory connection from the interneuron to the motor neuron. It is consequently described as disynaptic, in contrast with the monosynaptic excitatory connection to the homonymous motor pool.

The inhibitory neuron is essential to the sign of the response. An excitatory sensory neuron can reduce activity elsewhere by exciting a neuron that releases an inhibitory transmitter. In the spinal cord, glycine is a major transmitter involved in fast inhibition, including classical reciprocal inhibitory pathways. This does not imply that every inhibitory spinal circuit uses glycine exclusively or that every inhibitory response has the same ionic mechanism. The essential principle is that the sign of the final motor effect is determined by the arrangement and physiological properties of the intervening synapses.

A selective interruption of reciprocal inhibitory interneurons should not automatically eliminate the direct Ia excitation of the stretched muscle. These two routes share an afferent input but diverge within the spinal cord. One could therefore observe preserved agonist excitation with reduced antagonist suppression. By contrast, interruption of the relevant Ia afferent before its central branches reach their targets can diminish both components. This comparison is a powerful way to identify where a hypothetical lesion or experimental manipulation acts.

Reciprocal inhibition should not be confused with the broader concept of antagonistic muscles. The mechanical opposition between two muscles is a property of their attachments and actions. Reciprocal inhibition is a particular neural relationship between pathways. Mechanical antagonists may sometimes be coactivated, for example when a person wishes to stabilise the wrist while manipulating a tool. Coactivation can increase joint stiffness because both muscles resist displacement in opposite directions. Under such circumstances, centrally regulated motor commands can permit or promote activity in both pools rather than enforcing complete reciprocal relaxation.

When the antagonist is already active, a reduction in its activation can be observed in an EMG recording, provided the timing and measurement conditions are suitable. If the antagonist is initially silent, there may be no visible decrease in EMG even if an inhibitory synaptic input occurs. A motor neuron that is below firing threshold cannot show a reduction in spike rate below zero. The absence of a measured fall in electrical activity does not therefore prove that the inhibitory pathway is absent. An investigator may need to examine excitability using an appropriate test response.

It is also important to distinguish reciprocal inhibition from recurrent inhibition. In a recurrent inhibitory circuit, a motor neuron sends an axon collateral that excites a Renshaw cell, which can inhibit motor neurons and influence other spinal interneurons. The initiating signal is associated with motor output rather than directly with spindle stretch. Although both mechanisms can regulate the activity of motor pools, their circuit organisation differs. Examination questions often exploit the similarity of their names by asking which neuron initiates the inhibitory route.

The two-synapse reciprocal pathway introduces an additional synaptic delay compared with the direct monosynaptic route, assuming similar conduction distances and physiological conditions. However, it would be unjustified to infer an exact difference in milliseconds without measurements. The overall delay depends on axon length, conduction velocity, synaptic integration and the timing of the motor readout. Counting synapses identifies an important component of the latency, not the total time required for all physiological events.

Reciprocal inhibition demonstrates that spinal reflexes are coordinated network responses, even when a particular component of the response is monosynaptic. The simple drawing of a sensory neuron connecting to one motor neuron is necessary for learning the core stretch reflex, but it is not sufficient for understanding how groups of muscles produce useful movements. The next stage is to examine a different sensory receptor whose input reflects force rather than the lengthening of intrafusal fibres.

9. Golgi tendon organs: a mechanical measure of force

A muscle spindle is not the only source of information needed to regulate skeletal muscle contraction. A muscle can generate greatly different forces at the same external length, and the same external displacement may be associated with different loads. Force feedback is supplied importantly by the Golgi tendon organs, named after the anatomist Camillo Golgi. These receptors are located near the junctions between muscle fibres and tendinous tissue. Their organisation allows them to detect the tensile load transmitted by associated muscle fibres rather than simply the amount by which the muscle has lengthened.

A tendon organ is a small encapsulated receptor associated with bundles of collagen. A group Ib sensory axon enters the capsule and branches among those bundles. When the associated muscle fibres generate tension, the collagen bundles are drawn taut and their changing configuration deforms the sensory branches. Mechanically sensitive channels in the nerve ending respond to this deformation, ultimately generating afferent action potentials. The receptor is in series with the muscle fibres whose force is transmitted through its collagen bundles. Force exerted by those fibres consequently influences the mechanical conditions of the sensory ending.

It is useful to distinguish a tendon organ from the tendon as a whole. A tendon transmits forces generated by a large population of muscle fibres, whereas an individual receptor samples a local subset of the force-transmission system. The force distribution among collagen bundles varies with motor-unit recruitment and muscle architecture. Therefore one tendon organ should not be regarded as a universal dynamometer that reports the total force of an entire muscle without error. A population of tendon organs can provide information about the distribution and magnitude of force across an active muscle.

Active contraction is particularly effective at exciting many tendon organs. When a set of extrafusal fibres contracts, the load they transmit through the associated collagen produces receptor deformation. Passive stretch can also produce sufficient tension to activate tendon organs, especially at longer muscle lengths. The distinction is not that a tendon organ is incapable of responding to passive stretching. Rather, its relevant stimulus is tensile force, and passive stretch often distributes force differently from active contraction of fibres connected to the receptor.

This distinction can be examined in an illustrative comparison of two conditions. In both, the muscle–tendon unit is held at the same overall length. In condition A the muscle is relaxed and its tendon bears relatively little force. In condition B alpha motor neurons activate extrafusal fibres, causing a substantial rise in tensile force while an external apparatus prevents overall shortening. A force-sensitive afferent will generally respond more strongly in condition B. A spindle response is less straightforward because the active fascicles may shorten as the compliant tendon elongates even at constant muscle–tendon length. Measurements of fascicle geometry, force and neural activation are therefore necessary before making a definitive prediction.

Unlike the spindle, the tendon organ does not contain intrafusal fibres with gamma innervation that directly adjusts the tension of its sensory region. This does not mean its output is immune to the action of the nervous system. Alpha motor commands alter the forces transmitted by the fibres attached to the receptor, and central circuits regulate the impact of Ib signals on downstream neurons. The distinction is between direct fusimotor adjustment of the receptor apparatus and regulation of its input or central consequences.

Group Ib afferents and group Ia afferents are both large myelinated fibres of group I. A fast conduction velocity alone is insufficient to distinguish them unambiguously. One must examine their receptor origin and their response to mechanical interventions. Group II spindle afferents have a smaller average diameter and generally slower conduction, but classifications concern characteristic populations rather than rigid speeds applicable to every axon. An exam question that describes only a fast afferent in a muscle nerve does not provide sufficient evidence to identify it as Ia rather than Ib.

Tendon organ firing can encode the intensity and changes of transmitted force. A simple local estimate of force sensitivity is the change in firing rate divided by the measured change in force. For an illustrative receptor with rates of 10 and 34 impulses/s at forces of 4 and 16 N, the estimated slope is (34 − 10)/(16 − 4) = 2 impulses/s/N. It applies only to the experimental range and the force distribution present in those trials. An equal increase in total muscle force produced by a different recruitment pattern might not increase the discharge of the same receptor by precisely the same amount.

A tendon organ has no need to wait until a dangerous force is reached before reporting tension. Its signals are useful during ordinary movement and low-force contractions as well as during strong loading. This observation is crucial for understanding why force feedback can regulate the fine coordination of movement. A receptor that responded only at the point of injury could contribute to emergency protection but could not continuously help the nervous system adjust ordinary muscular forces. The tendon organ's regular participation in the control of movement is a more complete account than the simplified textbook description of a protective switch.

10. Ib spinal circuits and context-dependent force feedback

The classical circuit associated with the Golgi tendon organ is often introduced as the inverse myotatic reflex. In an idealised resting spinal preparation, an increase in muscle tension increases Ib afferent discharge. The afferent can excite inhibitory interneurons that reduce the activity of the alpha motor neurons supplying the same muscle. If the muscle then produces less force, the initial increase in tension tends to be opposed. This is a useful example of negative force feedback. The term autogenic inhibition refers to the suppression of activity in the muscle from which the relevant sensory input arises.

Unlike direct Ia excitation of homonymous motor neurons, the canonical inhibitory Ib pathway requires an intervening inhibitory neuron. Its two central synapses have different signs: excitation of the interneuron and inhibition of the motor neuron. The term inverse refers to the typical contrast in motor consequences with the excitatory stretch reflex, not to reversal of the direction of nerve conduction. Both Ia and Ib sensory impulses travel from receptors toward the central nervous system. Both pathways can affect the same alpha motor pool through distinct routes.

To understand the possible function of the inhibitory route, imagine a muscle holding a load with approximately constant force. A small unexpected increase in its force will increase tension in the associated tendon organs. If the resulting input suppresses motor output, the deviation may be reduced. In a sufficiently simple system this contributes to stabilisation of force. The decrease in motor output need not be a complete cessation of contraction; a modest reduction in firing rate or recruitment may be sufficient. Continuous graded regulation is more plausible in routine behaviour than an all-or-none shutdown of the muscle.

The classical description is incomplete if it is treated as an invariant rule. Ib afferents project to networks of interneurons whose excitability and output depend on inputs from other receptors and descending pathways. In certain phases of locomotion, particularly when a limb bears weight, extensor force feedback can facilitate rather than suppress extensor motor activity. When an animal encounters a greater supporting load, facilitating appropriate extensor force may help prevent collapse of the limb. The same class of receptor can therefore contribute to different behavioural outcomes depending on how its signals are processed.

A change from inhibitory to facilitatory motor action does not require the tendon organ to become a different type of receptor. The mechanical deformation of its sensory ending and the afferent signal may remain broadly similar. The change can occur in the central circuitry that receives the signal. Some interneuronal routes may be favoured during one task and suppressed during another. Motor neurons may also have different background excitability. The effect measured at the muscle reflects the balance of these routes and their interaction with ongoing motor drive.

For example, suppose a brief perturbation increases tendon force by 5 N in two controlled conditions. In an illustrative resting condition, an associated extensor EMG response decreases. During a weight-bearing phase, an equivalent afferent input is followed by an increase in extensor EMG. If baseline activation, stimulus recruitment and mechanical conditions are properly considered, the opposite motor consequences support context-dependent processing. They do not prove that the afferent changes from excitatory to inhibitory transmitter release at its terminal. The overall response can change sign because the relative contributions of central pathways change.

Positive force feedback is not necessarily pathological. If an increase in load elicits greater muscle activation, and that activation helps support the limb, the response may serve the animal's task. Nonetheless, a positive feedback loop that increases its own input without restriction can become unstable. Biological systems contain limits imposed by motor recruitment, inhibitory pathways, muscle mechanics and changes in behaviour. The effect of force feedback must be interpreted within the entire controlled mechanical system rather than by deciding that positive feedback is always harmful or negative feedback is always beneficial.

Force feedback also operates across muscles. An afferent originating in one muscle can influence motor pools whose actions affect other joints or whose forces change the mechanical load on the original muscle. A limb has multiple joints, and muscles often span more than one joint. If a perturbation changes the force of one muscle, the most useful adjustment may involve several muscles rather than an isolated response in the receptor's own muscle. This distributed organisation helps explain why a single fixed two-neuron diagram cannot predict every natural force-related response.

The distinction between recording sensory activity and measuring spinal influence is particularly important for Ib circuits. An experiment may show that a tendon organ's firing follows force, but that observation alone cannot establish whether a particular spinal effect is inhibitory or excitatory. To investigate the motor consequence, it is necessary to monitor appropriate motor responses and account for other afferents recruited by the intervention. Stimulating an entire mixed muscle nerve, for example, can excite different sensory fibres and motor axons; the final response cannot automatically be attributed exclusively to Ib input.

A comparison with Ia feedback now becomes possible. A spindle primarily reports mechanical conditions related to local length and rate of length change, with sensitivity modified by the fusimotor system. A tendon organ primarily reports transmitted force associated with its mechanical attachments. The direct homonymous Ia excitation is a well-defined central connection, whereas the classical inhibitory Ib influence is mediated through interneurons and its net effect can vary across behaviours. Both sources contribute to the regulation of movement and neither is reducible to a simple emergency mechanism. The following sections examine how their signals combine in muscle tone and mechanical stability.

11. Muscle tone: separating neural and passive resistance

Muscle tone is commonly described in physiology as the resistance encountered when a relaxed limb or muscle is moved passively. In other contexts the term is used more broadly for low-level ongoing activation that helps maintain posture. These usages are related but not identical. A resting muscle can resist elongation even when no active motor units are detectable, and a postural muscle can show low-level electrical activity while the person is standing still. To avoid ambiguity, it is helpful to specify whether the measured variable is passive resistance, background activation or reflex-induced activation.

Passive mechanical resistance arises from the properties of muscle fibres, connective tissue, tendons and other structures spanning the joint. Elastic components resist deformation, while viscous and other time-dependent properties influence how resistance changes with the rate and history of movement. Titin and connective tissue contribute to passive tension within skeletal muscle; the actual resistance measured at a joint also includes the mechanical contributions of surrounding tissues. These components do not require a new action potential in a motor neuron at the instant the limb is displaced.

The active neural component can include background motor-unit discharge that maintains force and reflex recruitment triggered by a stretch. During quiet standing, some muscles are active as part of the ongoing task of balancing the body. In a supported relaxed limb, background EMG may be low or undetectable. The absence of substantial motor-unit activity does not mean that the muscle or joint offers no resistance to passive movement. Conversely, increased EMG during a stretch supports an active component but does not establish that all resistance arises from reflex activity.

Consider a laboratory comparison in which a relaxed elbow is displaced by a motorised apparatus while torque and EMG are measured. A gradual movement can reveal passive resistance over the joint's range, provided voluntary and reflex activity are minimised. A faster movement may evoke additional stretch-related neural activity. If torque rises with speed even without detectable EMG, mechanical viscosity or other passive effects may contribute. If an additional time-locked EMG response accompanies an increase in torque, a neural component becomes more plausible. Neither observation alone provides a perfect decomposition because EMG has limitations and active and passive properties interact.

Stiffness refers to the change in force or torque per unit change in length or angle. For a linear approximation at a specified operating point, muscle stiffness can be expressed as k = ΔF/ΔL, with force in newtons and length in metres, giving N/m. Joint rotational stiffness is often expressed as Δτ/Δθ, with torque in N·m and angle in radians, giving N·m/rad. Stiffness is not identical to force: a muscle can generate a substantial force while showing a particular incremental response to an additional displacement. It is the slope of a mechanical relationship, not simply the force at one point.

In an illustrative test, a displacement of 4 mm increases passive muscle force by 8 N. The estimated stiffness over that interval is 8/0.004 = 2,000 N/m. If an otherwise comparable intact condition produces a 14 N force increase, the effective stiffness is 3,500 N/m. The difference of 1,500 N/m is the difference between the measured conditions. It may contain reflex-mediated force as well as other changes in background activation or mechanical state; it should not be labelled a pure neural stiffness unless the experimental design controls those factors.

A muscle's immediate mechanical response to a small displacement can precede the arrival of reflex-induced force. Existing cross-bridges and passive structures resist displacement without waiting for afferent conduction and motor transmission. Later, the stretch reflex can contribute an additional force. The time course of the response therefore helps identify mechanisms. The earliest mechanical change cannot be caused by a motor command triggered by the same perturbation if that change occurs before the required sensory and motor conduction delays. This reasoning is analogous to separating the direct physical effect of a stimulus from a delayed biological regulatory response in other physiological systems.

Background activation changes the immediate mechanical properties of muscle. When more force-producing fibres are active, their cross-bridges and associated structures can contribute to resistance to a small stretch. Thus an increase in stiffness during co-contraction does not require a larger reflex response, although reflex adjustments may also occur. To understand a joint's behaviour, one must consider intrinsic properties of the currently active muscles as well as sensory feedback and descending commands.

Co-contraction of antagonists provides a particularly clear illustration. If flexor and extensor torques are balanced at a particular joint angle, the net torque may be nearly zero even though both muscles produce considerable force. A small displacement away from that angle can change their opposing forces and generate restoring torque. Consequently a joint can become stiffer without a large change in its average position. The mechanical costs include energy expenditure and potentially greater joint loading. The nervous system can therefore regulate stiffness according to task demands rather than maximising it at all times.

Normal tone is not a single numerical property shared by every muscle at every moment. The posture of the animal, external support, level of arousal, temperature, recent movement, ongoing task and muscle properties influence measured resistance and activation. Even when neural activity is held constant, changing the joint angle can alter passive resistance. A scientific comparison of tone therefore requires a definition of the measurement and attention to the conditions under which it was obtained.

12. Descending modulation and physiological changes in tone

Spinal reflex circuits are connected to the rest of the central nervous system. Descending pathways can change the excitability of alpha motor neurons, gamma motor neurons and interneurons, as well as the transmission of incoming sensory signals. These influences help match local reflex activity to the animal's posture and intended movement. The detailed organisation of descending motor pathways belongs to the following chapter; here the emphasis is on the ways in which descending activity alters the operation of the local feedback system.

One mechanism is a change in alpha motor neuron excitability. A motor neuron receives excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs from multiple sources. If descending excitation brings it closer to firing threshold, a given Ia excitatory postsynaptic potential may be more likely to evoke a spike. If inhibition or reduced excitation moves the neuron farther from threshold, the same afferent input may produce no output spike. The size of the resulting reflex can change even if the number and timing of arriving sensory impulses remain unchanged. An experiment that records only muscle activation cannot distinguish this postsynaptic mechanism from a change in the sensory signal itself.

A second mechanism involves the fusimotor system. Descending influences on gamma motor neurons can change the mechanical responsiveness of the spindle. For a fixed disturbance of muscle length, the number of sensory impulses reaching the cord may increase or decrease. This is modulation before the signal reaches the central synapse. The effect can be distinguished conceptually from a change in alpha excitability by recording the afferent response as well as the motor response to an equivalent mechanical perturbation.

A third mechanism involves modulation of afferent transmission within the spinal cord. Presynaptic inhibitory processes can reduce the effectiveness of incoming sensory activity at particular terminals, so that fewer or smaller postsynaptic effects occur despite the arrival of a similar afferent volley. The terminology and mechanisms of presynaptic regulation are more complex than a simple on/off block, but the examination-level principle is that the nervous system can regulate the influence of an input at the site of its transmission. Such modulation provides a way to change reflex strength selectively during a task.

A fourth mechanism is a change in the balance of interneuronal pathways. Reciprocal inhibitory interneurons and circuits receiving Ib signals integrate inputs from several sources. Descending activity can alter which of these routes contributes to the final motor response. The resulting changes may concern not only the strength of the response but also its sign or the pattern of muscles recruited. This is particularly relevant when force feedback contributes to weight support rather than to autogenic inhibition.

Brainstem reticulospinal and vestibulospinal influences participate in the regulation of posture and muscle tone. Their functional effects depend on the relevant pathway, neural state and motor pool. It is unsafe to treat all descending influences as exclusively excitatory or exclusively inhibitory. Some can facilitate extensor activity in appropriate contexts, while others can reduce reflex excitability or redistribute activity among muscle groups. The following chapter examines these systems as part of central motor control, whereas the present chapter uses them to explain why the spinal feedback system cannot be analysed as an isolated fixed circuit in an intact animal.

When peripheral sensory input is lost, the ability to detect disturbances through that input is impaired. If the afferent limb of a stretch reflex is interrupted, a tendon tap may no longer evoke the normal response despite preservation of the muscle and its motor innervation. A voluntary motor command can still activate the muscle if the efferent pathway remains intact. Such a dissociation explains why weakness, sensory loss and reflex loss need not always occur together. It also provides a mechanistic distinction between interruption of sensory input and interruption of the final motor pathway.

When the alpha motor pathway is interrupted, both reflex-induced and voluntary activation of its target fibres are compromised. Passive mechanical resistance may still persist, and the affected muscle may undergo further changes over time. Thus reduced active tone after interruption of the motor pathway does not imply an immediate disappearance of every mechanical force acting at the joint. The time after a disruption matters because prolonged changes in muscle activation can alter muscle and connective tissue properties.

Some disturbances of descending motor control are associated with increased stretch-related reflex activity and velocity-dependent resistance, a feature commonly described as spasticity. It is a mistake to explain every such change solely by increased gamma discharge. Changes in spinal excitability, descending regulation and peripheral muscle mechanics can all contribute. Further, the total resistance experienced during passive movement may include nonreflex components. Rigidity describes a different pattern of increased resistance that is classically less dependent on stretch velocity; it should not be used as a synonym for spasticity. These distinctions are useful for physiological reasoning without requiring clinical diagnostic procedures.

A sudden spinal disruption may initially reduce reflex activity before some reflex responses recover or become exaggerated at later stages. The initial reduction is commonly discussed in the context of spinal shock. The sequence illustrates that the effect of loss of descending influence cannot be inferred from a single static circuit diagram: spinal neurons and their inputs operate in a changing physiological state. It also cautions against using a single observation of increased or decreased tone as proof of a particular mechanism without considering the timing of the observation.

From an examination perspective, the most reliable approach is to trace the altered component of the system and predict which functions remain possible. If sensory transduction is reduced, the mechanical stimulus may fail to generate normal afferent activity. If afferent transmission is reduced, sensory firing may be present without effective central excitation. If the motor pathway is reduced, motor commands may fail to generate normal muscle activation. If passive stiffness is increased, resistance can increase without an increase in afferent-driven EMG. These alternatives can be separated using carefully chosen measurements rather than by assuming that all manifestations of altered tone have the same cause.

13. Experimental methods: separating receptor, synapse and muscle

A basic experimental strategy in sensorimotor physiology is to control one part of the mechanical system while measuring more than one part of the neural response. A muscle–nerve preparation can be used conceptually to study the relation between length, force and sensory firing. A length controller imposes specified displacements, a force transducer measures tensile force, and an electrode records activity from a sensory fibre. If the sensory axon is identified, the investigator can test whether its discharge follows the mechanical changes expected of a spindle or a tendon organ. This type of preparation is useful because the timing of mechanical and neural events is known, but the absence of many inputs present in an intact animal limits the conclusions about natural motor behaviour.

For a ramp-and-hold experiment the independent variables can include initial muscle length, displacement, ramp speed and fusimotor stimulation. The dependent variables can include baseline firing, the firing near the end of the ramp, the mean firing during a specified interval of the hold and the recorded force. A comparison of two ramp speeds must hold the displacement constant if the aim is to separate velocity-related and length-related responses. Similarly, a comparison of two hold lengths should standardise prior stimulation and contraction history. Failure to control these variables can make a change in one mechanical property appear to be a change in another.

An identified sensory axon can be recorded extracellularly, with action potentials appearing as brief voltage deflections. The number of deflections per second provides an estimate of average firing rate, whereas the reciprocal of the interval between successive impulses provides an estimate of instantaneous frequency. If one interval is 25 ms, the corresponding instantaneous frequency is 1/0.025 = 40 Hz. If the next interval is 10 ms, the frequency is 100 Hz. These values concern one axon; a compound recording from many fibres cannot be interpreted in the same way without separating contributions from individual units.

The use of genetically altered organisms provides another route to understanding transduction. Woo and colleagues in 2015 studied Piezo2 in mouse proprioceptive neurons. Their work included recordings from sensory neurons and muscle–nerve preparations in models lacking normal Piezo2 function. Together with behavioural findings, the results supported a major role for Piezo2 in mechanically evoked proprioceptive signalling. The logic is that disrupting a candidate mechanically activated channel should impair the conversion of deformation into sensory activity if the channel is an essential component of that conversion.

An altered animal's movement alone would not establish this molecular mechanism. Poor movement might result from weakness, abnormal development, impaired sensory transduction or altered central processing. The combination of receptor-related electrophysiology and behavioural assessment helps narrow the inference. In an ideal interpretation, comparison with appropriate controls establishes whether the recorded mechanical response is reduced while other properties of the neuron remain sufficiently intact. The experiment does not imply that every type of proprioceptive sensation is mediated by one channel in every animal species. This is an example of the value of moving between levels of evidence without extending a result beyond its experimental support.

A different strategy tests the synaptic pathway rather than the receptor. Electrical stimulation of an appropriate peripheral nerve can excite large sensory axons directly, bypassing the mechanical conversion at the spindle. If the resulting afferent volley reaches the spinal cord and activates a motor pool, a muscle electrical response can be recorded. This approach underlies the Hoffmann reflex, or H-reflex. It is particularly useful as a conceptual contrast with a mechanically elicited tendon jerk because the electrical stimulus does not require the spindle to be deformed.

In a simplified H-reflex experiment the electrode is placed so that mixed peripheral nerve stimulation recruits sensory axons at relatively low stimulus intensities. The afferent volley travels toward the spinal cord; synaptic activation of alpha motor neurons can then produce a later EMG response. At higher intensities, direct activation of motor axons produces an earlier muscle response called the M wave. The M wave bypasses central synaptic processing because impulses generated in the motor axon can propagate directly toward the neuromuscular junction. In this context the letters H and M label different evoked electrical responses rather than separate muscles.

The two responses behave differently as stimulus intensity increases. The H response can initially grow because more afferent input and motor units contribute. The M wave grows as more motor axons are directly activated. Directly stimulated motor axons also conduct impulses toward the spinal cord, in the direction opposite to normal peripheral motor conduction. These antidromic impulses can collide with reflex-generated impulses travelling toward the muscle in the same axons. Consequently the H response can decline at high stimulation intensities even while the M wave becomes larger. A falling H response under those conditions does not by itself indicate reduced synaptic excitability.

An illustrative H-reflex comparison may use the ratio of the peak H response to the maximal M wave to express the response relative to a measure of the available muscle electrical signal. If the maximal M wave is 8 mV and the peak H response is 4 mV, the ratio is 0.50. In another condition with the same maximal M wave but a peak H response of 2 mV, the ratio is 0.25. The smaller ratio is consistent with a smaller evoked reflex response under the tested conditions, but it does not uniquely identify the cause. Afferent recruitment, presynaptic modulation, motor neuron excitability and concurrent inputs can all affect the H response.

The tendon jerk and H-reflex are therefore complementary but not interchangeable measurements. The tendon jerk depends on mechanical transmission to the spindle and the sensitivity of the receptor as well as subsequent neural events. The H-reflex bypasses receptor transduction, but its amplitude still reflects several central and peripheral influences. If a manipulation greatly reduces the tendon-jerk response without similarly reducing a carefully matched H response, altered mechanical coupling or receptor responsiveness becomes a plausible explanation. If both responses fall, a shared central or motor mechanism may be involved. These are hypotheses supported to different degrees, not conclusive diagnoses.

A surface EMG records a mixture of electrical activity arising from nearby active muscle fibres. Its amplitude depends on motor-unit recruitment and firing, but also on electrode placement, tissue conductivity and the timing of individual muscle fibre signals. It is not a direct measure of spindle discharge, alpha motor neuron membrane potential or force in newtons. To estimate the mechanical consequence of reflex activity, it should be interpreted alongside force, torque or movement measurements. Even if EMG onset precedes a force increase, temporal order alone does not identify the sensory receptor responsible.

The same principle applies to measuring proprioceptive sensation. In a position-matching task, an individual may compare the position of one limb with that of another without seeing them. Errors can result from altered sensory input in either limb, differences in motor command, memory of the target angle or mechanical history. A comparison between active and passive matching can help distinguish the role of self-generated motor signals from externally imposed movement, but the tasks are not perfectly identical. Good interpretation requires stating what information was available to the participant in each condition.

Vibration of a muscle or tendon can alter spindle activity and sometimes produce an illusion of movement. The apparent direction is related to the interpretation of input that would normally accompany lengthening of the affected muscle. Such observations support an important contribution of muscle afferents to movement sensation. They do not show that every vibratory stimulus exclusively activates spindle primary endings; skin receptors and other afferents may respond depending on the stimulus and the muscle's condition. The method is a useful example of manipulating sensory signals independently of large actual changes in joint position.

The general methodological lesson is that an experiment must discriminate between alternative explanations rather than merely reproduce an expected result. Recording length and force separates two candidate mechanical variables. Recording afferent activity and motor activity separates sensory encoding from the central response. Comparing mechanical and electrical stimulation helps distinguish receptor-dependent events from later stages. Recording torque and EMG helps separate passive and active contributions to resistance. These combinations form a practical reasoning toolkit for analysing unfamiliar examination data.

14. Quantitative reasoning about reflex timing and stability

A stretch reflex includes several successive processes, and their temporal contributions can be estimated independently. Let the length of the sensory path from the receptor to the relevant spinal region be La, its mean conduction speed va, the length of the motor path Lm and its speed vm. In a simple model, the conduction portion of the delay equals La/va + Lm/vm. Additional terms are needed for sensory transduction, spinal synaptic and integration processes, neuromuscular transmission and the electrical propagation within muscle. If the measured endpoint is force rather than electrical activity, excitation–contraction coupling and the development of measurable tension add further delay.

For a hypothetical short-latency reflex, assume a sensory path of 0.75 m at 75 m/s and a motor path of 0.75 m at 50 m/s. The sensory conduction time is 0.75/75 = 0.010 s, or 10 ms. The motor conduction time is 0.75/50 = 0.015 s, or 15 ms. Together they account for 25 ms. If transduction and central processing contribute 2 ms in the stipulated model and the neuromuscular and muscle electrical processes contribute 3 ms, the predicted EMG onset is 30 ms after the disturbance. If development of measurable force adds a further 20 ms, the mechanical onset is predicted at 50 ms. These are illustrative values chosen for arithmetic, not measured population norms.

Now imagine that the sensory conduction speed is reduced to 37.5 m/s while every other model parameter remains constant. The sensory time becomes 0.75/37.5 = 0.020 s, or 20 ms. The predicted EMG onset becomes 40 ms, ten milliseconds later than in the first case. This calculation illustrates that conduction time changes inversely with velocity. Halving conduction speed doubles the time spent on that part of the path, but it does not double the entire reflex latency unless every other term is zero or changes in the same way.

The model also helps explain why reflex latency cannot be used without qualification to count synapses. A longer limb can increase conduction distances, and a change in temperature or axon properties can alter conduction speed. A small additional delay might reflect an extra central synapse, a change in receptor transduction or a longer peripheral path. If two responses share the same measured peripheral conduction times and the same receptor and motor readouts, a difference in their central organisation becomes a stronger explanation. The evidence required depends on the precision of the claim.

Feedback control can be examined using a deliberately simplified mechanical model. Suppose an externally applied force increment Fext elongates a muscle by an amount x around a stable working length. Let the intrinsic mechanical stiffness be k0 and let the eventual reflex-generated restoring force be krx, where kr is a local effective feedback stiffness. At a new equilibrium the imposed force increment is balanced by the sum of intrinsic and reflex-generated restoring force. The relation is Fext = k0x + krx, or x = Fext/(k0 + kr). Both stiffness terms have units of N/m, and displacement is measured in metres.

This equation does not claim that actual muscle reflexes behave like ideal springs. It neglects neural delays, muscle shortening velocity, tendon compliance, changes in recruitment and saturation. It applies only to small perturbations in which the response can reasonably be approximated by a linear restoring relation and the system has reached a stable state. Its purpose is to show why increasing effective restoring stiffness can reduce the final displacement caused by a given disturbance. The equation describes a mechanical outcome rather than the firing rate of any particular neuron.

For example, if intrinsic stiffness is 1,000 N/m and an externally applied force rises by 10 N, a model with no reflex contribution predicts x = 10/1,000 = 0.010 m, or 10 mm. If reflex action contributes an effective additional 500 N/m, the predicted final displacement becomes 10/1,500 = 0.00667 m, about 6.7 mm. The reflex reduces the displacement in this idealised comparison but does not eliminate it. If a student instead adds the two predicted displacements, the resulting answer would incorrectly treat forces acting together as independent elongations.

The early response to the external force in this model need not be equal to its later response. Before reflex-generated muscle force develops, the intrinsic mechanical properties dominate the immediate response. As feedback becomes effective, an additional restoring force can alter the displacement. An experiment that examines only the final position cannot determine how rapidly the correction developed. A time-resolved record of force and displacement is necessary to investigate the contribution of delayed feedback.

A complementary force feedback model can illustrate the difference between inhibition and facilitation. Let the baseline motor drive in force-equivalent units be D and let a local inhibitory feedback action be proportional to the actual muscle force F. In a toy steady-state model, F = D − bF, where b is a nonnegative dimensionless coefficient. Rearrangement gives F = D/(1 + b). If D is equivalent to 30 N and b = 0.5, the model predicts F = 20 N. The force is smaller than the corresponding open-loop value of 30 N. This is a mathematical illustration of graded negative force feedback, not a calibrated model of a tendon organ or a particular Ib synapse.

If the feedback instead facilitates activation, an analogous idealisation gives F = D + bF, so that F = D/(1 − b) for b below one. With D = 30 N and b = 0.25, the predicted force is 40 N. This example illustrates how an appropriate facilitatory contribution can amplify load-bearing force. As b approaches one, the linear model predicts an unrealistically large output, revealing the need for mechanical limits, saturation, other regulatory pathways and a more realistic dynamic description. It is not legitimate to infer that a real muscle can generate unlimited force from this equation.

Latency introduces another limitation. A controller may generate a correction appropriate to a deviation that existed some time earlier. If the mechanical state changes substantially during that interval, the correction can arrive late. In a high-gain feedback system, successive delayed corrections can sometimes produce overshoot or oscillatory behaviour. This does not mean that every delayed reflex is unstable, because damping, intrinsic muscle mechanics and appropriate adjustment of feedback can reduce such effects. The physiological lesson is that an effective controller must balance speed, gain and stability rather than simply maximise the magnitude of every correction.

Joint torque offers another way to connect neural activity with behaviour. In a simplified single-joint model, torque from one muscle is τ = Fr, where F is tensile force in newtons and r is its perpendicular moment arm in metres. This follows from the definition of rotational moment. In a particular geometry, a muscle producing 100 N with a moment arm of 0.04 m produces 4 N·m. A second muscle with the same force but a moment arm of 0.02 m produces only 2 N·m. Equal muscle force does not guarantee equal rotational effect.

If a flexor produces 4 N·m in one direction and an extensor produces 3 N·m in the opposite direction, the net torque from these two muscles is 1 N·m in the flexion direction. An external opposing torque of 1 N·m could balance it at a stationary joint. Both muscles may be active in this situation even though the total torque is zero. A tendon organ in either muscle could be active because the individual muscle's force is not zero. Similarly, spindles may report changes in local length even when the net angular movement is small.

For small changes in joint angle, assuming a constant moment arm r and a simple linear muscle stiffness k, a length change of magnitude rΔθ yields a force change k rΔθ. Its restoring torque magnitude is r times that force change, or kr²Δθ. The effective joint stiffness contribution is thus kr², expressed in N·m/rad when the angle is in radians. For k = 2,000 N/m and r = 0.04 m, the contribution is 2,000 × 0.0016 = 3.2 N·m/rad. Real muscles have changing moment arms, and multiple muscles and passive structures contribute, so this estimate is local rather than universal.

These simplified calculations have a common purpose. They teach the student to specify the quantity being measured, to retain units and sign conventions, and to identify which assumptions make a conclusion possible. A force-sensitive receptor does not directly provide joint torque unless the geometry is known; a reflex latency does not directly reveal one synaptic delay unless peripheral contributions are accounted for; and an increased mechanical stiffness cannot automatically be attributed to the gamma system. Quantitative reasoning is valuable because it exposes these hidden assumptions.

15. Integrating feedback during posture, movement and sensory loss

To connect the mechanisms in this chapter, consider a person maintaining the forearm in a horizontal position while holding a container. The weight creates an external torque that tends to extend the elbow, and the flexors produce an opposing torque. The motor command establishes an initial level of activity according to the expected weight. Spindles report mechanical conditions within the muscles and tendon organs report the force transmitted by associated fibres. The central nervous system receives these signals while maintaining its own information about the ongoing motor task. At equilibrium, the fact that the arm is not moving does not imply that either receptor class is silent.

Suppose that an additional object is unexpectedly placed in the container. The initial mechanical effect may be a small downward displacement of the forearm, which elongates the flexors and changes their tensile force. Intrinsic muscle and tendon properties resist the disturbance immediately. Spindle afferent activity may increase, and the relevant spinal circuits can promote flexor activation. Tendon organ activity may also change, with motor consequences depending on the current task. Longer neural pathways can contribute further adjustments, and the final posture depends on the combined mechanical and neural response.

Now compare an identical addition of weight that the person expects. Before the load arrives, motor commands may increase flexor activity or change co-contraction. The initial displacement can be smaller because the mechanical system was prepared in advance. This does not mean that the spindle reflex was faster than neural conduction permitted. The preparation occurred before the disturbance, whereas feedback is a consequence of the disturbance. The distinction between feedforward preparation and feedback correction is a common theme in homeostatic physiology and central motor control.

The two mechanisms also operate together in walking. The supporting limb must produce adequate force when it contacts the ground. The mechanical conditions at contact depend on surface height and compliance, and the effects of a load differ across phases of the step. Length-related afferent signals can contribute to compensation for perturbations; force-related signals can contribute to appropriate extension and support. A response useful while the limb is supporting body weight may be inappropriate during the swing phase. The phase-dependent weighting of sensory signals makes the overall control system more flexible than a fixed reflex acting with an unchanging gain.

During quiet standing, a small movement of the body may change the lengths of muscles around several joints. The information from ankle muscles is particularly useful, but the nervous system also uses sensory inputs from the feet, other muscles, the vestibular apparatus and vision. When support conditions change, the reliability of one source of information may change relative to others. For example, movement of the support surface can make a particular ankle-related signal difficult to interpret as movement of the body relative to the external environment. Integration of several sources allows more robust estimation than exclusive reliance on a single receptor.

The anatomical route of proprioceptive information helps explain its multiple functions. Some ascending pathways convey information used in conscious perception of body position and movement. For the body, the dorsal column–medial lemniscal system is a major route for such information. Spinocerebellar pathways convey important signals about peripheral and spinal activity for coordination that need not enter conscious awareness. The afferent branches that influence local motor pools can operate independently of the complete ascending path to the cerebral cortex. Detailed tract anatomy and cerebellar integration are developed in their respective nodes.

Suppose that a hypothetical manipulation selectively interrupts a pathway required for conscious perception while leaving peripheral afferents and segmental spinal connections intact. A person might show impaired conscious position estimation but retain a local tendon reflex. Conversely, damage to the sensory afferent supplying a particular spindle could reduce both local feedback from that receptor and its contribution to ascending information. Neither conclusion follows from the broad term sensory loss alone. The anatomical location and selectivity of the manipulation determine the expected functional dissociation.

Another useful comparison concerns weakness and incoordination. A subject with intact strength but greatly reduced proprioceptive information may have difficulty accurately reproducing an unseen limb position or compensating for unexpected changes in load. Vision may partly compensate because it supplies another estimate of position, but visual feedback does not replace all the properties of local muscle feedback. A subject with intact proprioception but impaired motor output may detect a displacement without being able to generate an adequate corrective force. These two cases show that perception and performance depend on different, interacting components.

Proprioception also has a comparative biological context. Animals with different body plans have different sensory structures for detecting position, strain and movement. An arthropod with an external skeleton does not need to possess the precise mammalian muscle spindle arrangement in order to obtain mechanical feedback about its appendages. Chordotonal organs and strain-sensitive receptors are examples of different solutions to related mechanical problems. The examination-level principle is that sensory feedback is a general requirement for coordinated movement, whereas the alpha–gamma spindle arrangement described in detail in this chapter is a particular vertebrate solution. It should not be assumed to occur in identical form across all animals.

In biological control, the variable of interest may change with the task. Maintaining an angle, controlling grip force, supporting body weight and allowing a limb to yield to a disturbance impose different requirements on the sensory and motor systems. A large stretch response might be advantageous for one task and unnecessarily resist a desired movement in another. The nervous system can adjust spindle sensitivity, afferent transmission, motor pool excitability and the mechanical state of muscles. These coordinated adjustments explain why the concepts of tone and reflex gain should be regarded as regulated properties rather than fixed measures of strength.

The conceptual model established here has clear boundaries. Local length and force feedback explain important mechanisms of rapid correction, muscle resistance and sensory estimation. They do not fully explain the selection of a voluntary action, the timing of a complex learned sequence or the integration of vestibular and visual signals into an overall postural strategy. Those functions require a wider account of central motor networks. Understanding the peripheral receptors and spinal circuits first makes it possible to interpret that wider account in terms of the actual signals available to the nervous system and the effectors that ultimately execute movement.

16. Original examination practice

These questions are original CSIR-NET/SET-style practice, not previous-year examination questions. Work through the mechanism and calculations before opening the answer.

Part B — foundational concepts

1. Which receptor lies mechanically in parallel with local extrafusal muscle fibres?

  1. Golgi tendon organ
  2. Pacemaker cell
  3. Muscle spindle
  4. Renshaw cell
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Muscle spindle — The spindle is embedded alongside local force-producing fibres and responds to their mechanical length changes. The tendon organ is in series with associated fibres; the other two choices are not muscle proprioceptors.

2. The principal afferent of a Golgi tendon organ is classified as:

  1. Ib
  2. Ia
  3. II
  4. Gamma
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Ib — Ib is the tendon-organ sensory group. Ia and II mainly supply spindle sensory endings; gamma refers to a motor pathway rather than an afferent.

3. Which intrafusal regions are the major targets of gamma motor innervation?

  1. Central sensory equator
  2. Capsular connective tissue
  3. Extrafusal sarcomeres
  4. Contractile polar regions
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Contractile polar regions — Gamma motor neurons activate contractile intrafusal poles, thereby adjusting tension at the sensory region. The equator is specialised for sensory endings, and alpha axons supply extrafusal fibres.

4. Which fibre class is predominantly associated with dynamic gamma input?

  1. Nuclear bag2
  2. Nuclear bag1
  3. Nuclear chain
  4. Extrafusal type I
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Nuclear bag1 — Bag1 is classically associated with dynamic fusimotor effects. Bag2 and chain fibres contribute strongly to static responses; extrafusal type I fibres are force-producing fibres.

5. Group II spindle endings are particularly associated with which signal?

  1. Direct measurement of blood pressure
  2. Maintained muscle length-related deformation
  3. Direct recording of motor neuron membrane voltage
  4. Total force in every muscle of a limb
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Maintained muscle length-related deformation — Secondary spindle endings have a prominent static component. Their firing is not a direct recording of motor voltage or total limb force; arterial pressure is detected by other receptors.

6. Where are the cell bodies of the sensory afferents supplying limb muscle spindles?

  1. Ventral horn
  2. Sympathetic ganglion
  3. Neuromuscular junction
  4. Dorsal root ganglion
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Dorsal root ganglion — The primary sensory neurons have cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia. Motor cell bodies are in the ventral horn, and the neuromuscular junction is a peripheral synaptic site.

7. What is the classical number of central synapses in the direct Ia-to-homonymous-alpha pathway?

  1. One
  2. Zero
  3. Two
  4. Three
Answer and scientific explanation

A. One — The direct sensory-to-alpha connection is one central synapse. Receptor transduction and the peripheral neuromuscular junction are additional processes but are not counted as CNS synapses.

8. Which central pathway is classically disynaptic?

  1. Direct homonymous Ia excitation
  2. Direct motor axon stimulation to evoke an M wave
  3. Ia-mediated reciprocal inhibition of an antagonist
  4. Sensory conduction through a dorsal root ganglion
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Ia-mediated reciprocal inhibition of an antagonist — An Ia afferent excites an inhibitory interneuron that inhibits the antagonist motor pool. The direct Ia route has one CNS synapse; an M wave bypasses the CNS; the ganglion is not a synaptic relay.

9. What is a major effect of alpha–gamma coactivation?

  1. Elimination of all spinal inhibition
  2. Direct production of all extrafusal force by gamma axons
  3. Converting Ib axons into Ia axons
  4. Maintaining spindle sensitivity during active muscle shortening
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Maintaining spindle sensitivity during active muscle shortening — Gamma activity helps maintain tension in the spindle sensory region when alpha-driven extrafusal shortening would otherwise unload it. It does not eliminate inhibition or change afferent identity.

10. Which stimulus ordinarily initiates the canonical excitatory patellar tendon jerk?

  1. Tendon organ inhibition alone
  2. Muscle pain receptors alone
  3. Stretch of quadriceps muscle spindles
  4. A voluntary cortical command
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Stretch of quadriceps muscle spindles — A tendon tap mechanically stretches the quadriceps spindle apparatus. The tendon organ may also receive mechanical input but is not the primary initiator of the canonical excitatory stretch reflex.

11. Which is true of primary Ia afferents?

  1. They carry force exclusively
  2. They can encode both static and dynamic aspects of spindle deformation
  3. They are motor axons
  4. They have no static response
Answer and scientific explanation

B. They can encode both static and dynamic aspects of spindle deformation — Ia endings respond to maintained length and to changes in length. They are sensory rather than motor, and force-related signalling is prominently supplied by Ib endings.

12. A receptor potential at the spindle is best described as:

  1. A graded electrical response to mechanical deformation
  2. A propagating all-or-none skeletal muscle action potential
  3. A neurotransmitter secreted by an alpha motor neuron
  4. A fixed number of motor units
Answer and scientific explanation

A. A graded electrical response to mechanical deformation — The mechanosensitive sensory ending generates a graded receptor potential; sufficiently strong depolarisation can lead to propagating action potentials. Receptor potential and muscle action potential are distinct.

13. Which protein is a major mechanotransduction channel in mammalian proprioceptive neurons?

  1. Piezo2
  2. Myosin II
  3. Acetylcholinesterase
  4. Troponin C
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Piezo2 — Piezo2 contributes to conversion of mechanical stimuli into ionic currents. Myosin and troponin participate in contraction, and acetylcholinesterase terminates cholinergic transmission.

14. Which description of resting muscle resistance is most accurate?

  1. It is always entirely reflex-generated
  2. It is always absent if surface EMG is quiet
  3. It can include passive mechanical as well as neural components
  4. It is identical to maximum voluntary muscle strength
Answer and scientific explanation

C. It can include passive mechanical as well as neural components — Connective tissue, titin and other mechanical structures contribute to resistance, alongside background and reflex-related activity where present. A quiet EMG does not imply zero mechanical resistance.

15. The classical autogenic inhibitory pathway from a tendon organ acts through:

  1. Direct inhibitory action of a gamma axon on extrafusal fibres
  2. A spinal inhibitory interneuronal route
  3. An obligatory direct Ib synapse on the same alpha neuron
  4. A synapse in the dorsal root ganglion
Answer and scientific explanation

B. A spinal inhibitory interneuronal route — The canonical Ib inhibitory effect is mediated indirectly through spinal interneurons. Its net motor effect can be different in other behavioural contexts.

16. Which route directly activates muscle fibres during the M wave?

  1. Mechanical activation of Ia endings alone
  2. Ascending conscious proprioceptive transmission
  3. Gamma-mediated activation of Ib receptors
  4. Electrical recruitment of motor axons
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Electrical recruitment of motor axons — Directly stimulated motor axons conduct to their neuromuscular junctions. The H response, in contrast, depends on an afferent volley and central circuitry.

17. Which readout directly provides a measure of tensile force?

  1. Surface EMG amplitude
  2. Single Ia spike amplitude
  3. Force transducer
  4. Dorsal root ganglion location
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Force transducer — A calibrated force transducer measures mechanical load. EMG and spike recordings are electrical readouts and do not directly specify force in newtons.

18. A stretch-induced increase in agonist activation may oppose the original stretch. This is an example of:

  1. An invariably positive mechanical loop
  2. Negative mechanical feedback despite an excitatory synapse
  3. A direct chemical inhibition of all sensory neurons
  4. A reflex that cannot involve sensory input
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Negative mechanical feedback despite an excitatory synapse — The sign of the complete loop is determined by the effect on the original disturbance. Excitatory Ia-to-alpha transmission can contribute to a restoring contraction.

19. The dorsal column–medial lemniscal system contributes importantly to:

  1. Direct gamma innervation
  2. The peripheral M wave
  3. Skeletal muscle cross-bridge cycling
  4. Conscious proprioception from the body
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Conscious proprioception from the body — It is a major ascending route for conscious position and movement information. Local reflexes can occur without traversing this full ascending route.

20. Which statement about tendon organ activity is correct?

  1. It can report ordinary forces during movement, not only dangerous loads
  2. It always inhibits every muscle at all times
  3. It responds only to joint angle
  4. It has the same intrafusal motor apparatus as a spindle
Answer and scientific explanation

A. It can report ordinary forces during movement, not only dangerous loads — Tendon organs contribute to routine force signalling. Their spinal influence varies with context, and their series mechanical arrangement differs from the fusimotor-controlled spindle.

Part C — experimental and analytical reasoning

21. At 0 mm a receptor fires at 12 Hz; at a maintained 4 mm elongation it fires at 36 Hz. What is its local static sensitivity?

  1. 3 Hz/mm
  2. 6 Hz/mm
  3. 9 Hz/mm
  4. 24 Hz/mm
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 6 Hz/mm — Use the change in maintained rate, 36 − 12 = 24 Hz, divided by 4 mm, yielding 6 Hz/mm. Dividing the final rate by displacement ignores the baseline; 24 Hz is the change, not the slope.

22. An afferent fires at 60 Hz at the end of a 5 mm/s ramp and 35 Hz on the later hold. Under a simple additive model, the apparent velocity coefficient is:

  1. 25 Hz/(mm/s)
  2. 7 Hz/(mm/s)
  3. 5 Hz/(mm/s)
  4. 12 Hz/(mm/s)
Answer and scientific explanation

C. 5 Hz/(mm/s) — Subtract the steady component: 60 − 35 = 25 Hz. Divide by 5 mm/s to obtain 5 Hz/(mm/s). The estimate assumes equivalent end-ramp and hold length and ignores history-dependent transients.

23. A model has f0 = 20 Hz, ks = 3 Hz/mm and kv = 4 Hz/(mm/s). A 2 mm ramp at 5 mm/s ends at what predicted rate?

  1. 46 Hz
  2. 26 Hz
  3. 34 Hz
  4. 40 Hz
Answer and scientific explanation

A. 46 Hz — The additive prediction is 20 + 3×2 + 4×5 = 46 Hz. The 26 Hz alternative describes the subsequent hold when velocity becomes zero; the other values omit or miscalculate terms.

24. Two identical displacements have different ramp speeds. A receptor has identical hold rates but larger transient firing at the faster speed. What follows?

  1. The receptor must be a tendon organ
  2. Final lengths must differ
  3. The receptor has no static response
  4. A velocity-related response is supported
Answer and scientific explanation

D. A velocity-related response is supported — Matching final length while varying speed isolates a dynamic contribution under controlled conditions. The pattern alone does not prove a receptor identity, and identical hold rates do not imply absence of a static response.

25. During a controlled ramp, gamma stimulation raises hold firing in Ia and II endings with only a modest increase in transient response. Which action is most consistent?

  1. Alpha motor interruption
  2. Exclusive dynamic bag1 activation
  3. Direct blockade of receptor channels
  4. Predominantly static fusimotor drive
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Predominantly static fusimotor drive — Enhanced sustained discharge in primary and secondary endings is consistent with static gamma influence. Dynamic gamma predominantly enhances the Ia dynamic response. The pattern is evidence, not proof of a pure motor subtype.

26. During matched active shortening, condition A uses alpha stimulation alone and B adds gamma stimulation. Ia firing drops in A but is preserved in B. What mechanism is supported?

  1. Intrafusal polar contraction offsets receptor unloading
  2. Gamma produces the same external torque as alpha
  3. Ia conduction is reversed in B
  4. The tendon organ switches to a spindle
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Intrafusal polar contraction offsets receptor unloading — Alpha shortening can reduce spindle tension. Gamma activation contracts intrafusal poles, restoring tension in the central region. Matching shortening controls the simplest alternative mechanical explanation.

27. Afferent firing remains normal during a stretch, but the reflex motor response is markedly reduced. Which explanation remains plausible?

  1. A complete loss of stretch transduction in the recorded ending
  2. A necessary increase in spindle length
  3. Reduced central transmission or alpha excitability
  4. An obligatory increase in extrafusal force
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Reduced central transmission or alpha excitability — The observed sensory firing shows that transduction in that recorded ending is still functioning. A downstream change in afferent transmission or motor neuron excitability can weaken the motor response.

28. In an isolated classical reflex circuit, Ia inhibitory interneurons are selectively silenced. Which response is most likely preserved?

  1. All antagonist inhibition must increase
  2. Direct excitation of the homonymous alpha pool
  3. No sensory impulse can enter the spinal cord
  4. The M wave must disappear
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Direct excitation of the homonymous alpha pool — The direct homonymous excitatory pathway does not require the reciprocal inhibitory interneuron. Antagonist suppression through that route is diminished; the afferent and peripheral motor axons remain available.

29. A dorsal sensory root carrying the relevant Ia input is interrupted but the corresponding motor root is intact. What is the best prediction?

  1. The associated stretch reflex is impaired while direct motor activation can remain possible
  2. All passive stiffness vanishes
  3. Motor axon conduction necessarily ceases
  4. The tendon organ becomes an intrafusal fibre
Answer and scientific explanation

A. The associated stretch reflex is impaired while direct motor activation can remain possible — The interruption affects the afferent limb, not the intact efferent route. Direct motor stimulation can still activate the muscle, and passive resistance need not vanish.

30. In two states the same Ib input produces extensor inhibition at rest but facilitation during weight-bearing. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Ib afferents always release inhibitory transmitter and reverse their axonal conduction
  2. The sensory receptor changes mechanical arrangement
  3. The result proves that the force transducer is broken
  4. Central pathways weight the same class of input differently
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Central pathways weight the same class of input differently — Ib input enters context-sensitive spinal networks. The net output can change sign through different interneuronal contributions. The result does not imply reversal of the receptor's identity or conduction.

31. A tendon-organ afferent fires at 8 Hz at 3 N and 38 Hz at 13 N. Its local force sensitivity is:

  1. 2 Hz/N
  2. 3 Hz/N
  3. 38 Hz/N
  4. 10 Hz/N
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 3 Hz/N — The difference is 30 Hz over 10 N, giving 3 Hz/N. The answer describes the specified local interval and recruitment pattern, not a universal calibration of the receptor.

32. At a fixed whole muscle–tendon length, active contraction raises tendon force while the fascicles shorten. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. All spindle firing must increase because external length is fixed
  2. All tendon-organ firing must remain constant
  3. Ib firing may increase while spindle firing depends on local length and fusimotor state
  4. Both receptor classes exclusively encode joint angle
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Ib firing may increase while spindle firing depends on local length and fusimotor state — Force and fascicle length can change independently because tendons are compliant. Tendon organs respond to transmitted force; spindle response depends on local mechanics and intrafusal activation.

33. A 3 mm passive displacement increases force by 6 N. What is the estimated linear stiffness?

  1. 18 N/m
  2. 0.002 N/m
  3. 2,000 N/m
  4. 500 N/m
Answer and scientific explanation

C. 2,000 N/m — Convert 3 mm to 0.003 m. Stiffness is 6/0.003 = 2,000 N/m. Multiplying displacement by force or using millimetres without conversion gives incompatible units.

34. For a stable linear model k0 = 1,000 N/m and kr = 1,000 N/m. A 12 N force increment produces what equilibrium displacement?

  1. 6 mm
  2. 12 mm
  3. 24 mm
  4. 0.6 mm
Answer and scientific explanation

A. 6 mm — The restoring stiffness is 2,000 N/m, so x = 12/2,000 = 0.006 m = 6 mm. Without the additional restoring term it would be 12 mm. This model assumes a stable small-perturbation equilibrium.

35. A local inhibitory force model gives F = D/(1+b), D = 36 N and b = 0.2. What is F?

  1. 43.2 N
  2. 45 N
  3. 7.2 N
  4. 30 N
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 30 N — The calculation is 36/1.2 = 30 N. The denominator contains a plus sign for negative feedback. Multiplying D by b yields only a coefficient-weighted drive, not the equilibrium.

36. In a facilitatory model F = D/(1−b), with D = 24 N and b = 0.4, what is predicted?

  1. 17.1 N
  2. 40 N
  3. 9.6 N
  4. 60 N
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 40 N — The denominator is 0.6, so F = 24/0.6 = 40 N. This is an illustrative bounded linear-model calculation, not a prediction of unlimited physiological force.

37. Afferent distance 0.6 m at 60 m/s and efferent distance 0.8 m at 40 m/s. Other EMG delays total 4 ms. What is predicted onset?

  1. 34 ms
  2. 24 ms
  3. 44 ms
  4. 30 ms
Answer and scientific explanation

A. 34 ms — Sensory conduction is 10 ms; motor conduction is 20 ms. Add 4 ms to obtain 34 ms. The 30 ms choice includes conduction only.

38. In the preceding model, motor conduction velocity doubles with all else fixed. What is the new predicted EMG onset?

  1. 17 ms
  2. 24 ms
  3. 14 ms
  4. 44 ms
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 24 ms — The motor conduction term falls from 20 to 10 ms; sensory time is unchanged at 10 ms and other terms remain 4 ms. The total is 24 ms, not half the original total.

39. A mechanically imposed stretch produces a force increase 5 ms after onset; the earliest possible perturbation-evoked muscle EMG occurs at 30 ms. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The 5 ms response proves faster-than-normal gamma conduction
  2. All force after 30 ms is passive
  3. The initial force increase cannot be caused by a new reflex contraction triggered by that stretch
  4. There can be no spinal feedback later
Answer and scientific explanation

C. The initial force increase cannot be caused by a new reflex contraction triggered by that stretch — Intrinsic mechanics and existing activation can act immediately. The reflex-generated response to this perturbation must respect the required neural and muscular delays; later responses can still contain neural contributions.

40. Maximal M wave is 10 mV in both conditions, while peak H is 5 mV in A and 3 mV in B. What is the correct comparison?

  1. H/M changes from 2 to 3.33, proving spindle failure
  2. M wave proves identical central excitability
  3. H/M remains constant
  4. H/M changes from 0.50 to 0.30, without uniquely identifying the mechanism
Answer and scientific explanation

D. H/M changes from 0.50 to 0.30, without uniquely identifying the mechanism — Normalise H by M: 5/10 = 0.50 and 3/10 = 0.30. The smaller reflex may involve several factors; electrical stimulation bypasses spindle transduction.

41. The tendon jerk becomes smaller while a carefully matched H response and direct M response remain similar. Which hypothesis merits testing?

  1. Complete motor-axon interruption
  2. Obligatory total loss of central synapses
  3. Loss of all proprioceptive receptors
  4. Changed mechanical transmission or spindle responsiveness
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Changed mechanical transmission or spindle responsiveness — The mechanically initiated response has extra dependencies on stretch and receptor sensitivity. A preserved electrically evoked reflex supports investigation of these earlier stages, without excluding every other difference.

42. A muscle generates 150 N at a perpendicular moment arm of 0.03 m. What torque does it contribute?

  1. 0.2 N·m
  2. 50 N·m
  3. 4.5 N·m
  4. 5,000 N·m
Answer and scientific explanation

C. 4.5 N·m — Torque magnitude equals force multiplied by perpendicular moment arm: 150 × 0.03 = 4.5 N·m. Direction depends on the muscle's mechanical action.

43. Flexor torque rises from 3 to 6 N·m while opposing extensor torque rises from 3 to 6 N·m. Which statement is correct?

  1. Net torque from the pair remains zero, but joint stiffness may increase
  2. Net flexion torque must rise to 6 N·m
  3. Neither muscle can contain active motor units
  4. Tendon force must be zero
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Net torque from the pair remains zero, but joint stiffness may increase — Opposing torques cancel in both conditions. Increased co-contraction can increase restoring resistance to displacement even without a change in mean net torque.

44. A Piezo2-deficient animal has impaired movement. Which additional observation most directly strengthens the mechanotransduction hypothesis?

  1. An increase in food intake only
  2. Reduced mechanically evoked sensory responses with suitable controls of neuronal function
  3. Unrelated changes in body mass only
  4. A different coat colour
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Reduced mechanically evoked sensory responses with suitable controls of neuronal function — Recording the sensory response tests the proposed conversion of deformation into electrical activity. Behaviour alone cannot distinguish sensory, muscular and central mechanisms; appropriate controls narrow the inference.

  2. Tendon organ → gamma motor axon → conscious decision → contraction

Answer and scientific explanation

D. Spindle deformation → Ia volley → alpha excitation → extrafusal contraction — The primary spindle ending transduces stretch; the Ia axon enters the cord and directly excites the homonymous alpha pool, which activates extrafusal fibres.

49. Which two statements are correct? I. Gamma drive can alter spindle sensitivity. II. A tendon organ has a gamma-innervated intrafusal equator. III. Passive stiffness can persist without reflex EMG. IV. All Ib effects are inhibitory.

  1. I and II
  2. I and III
  3. II and IV
  4. III and IV
Answer and scientific explanation

B. I and III — I and III are correct. Tendon organs lack the spindle's intrafusal fusimotor apparatus; Ib motor effects depend on circuit state.

50. A spindle-afferent interspike interval is 20 ms. The instantaneous discharge frequency is:

  1. 20 Hz
  2. 200 Hz
  3. 0.05 Hz
  4. 50 Hz
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 50 Hz — Convert 20 ms to 0.020 s. Its reciprocal is 50 s⁻¹ or Hz. The interval itself is not the frequency.

51. Which comparison would best isolate the dynamic component of a spindle response?

  1. Two unrelated muscles with unknown length histories
  2. A tendon tap compared with maximal voluntary contraction only
  3. Matched displacement at two different ramp velocities with initial conditions controlled
  4. Two recordings with different unidentified axons and no length measurement
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Matched displacement at two different ramp velocities with initial conditions controlled — Holding displacement and starting conditions constant while varying velocity makes a dynamic contribution interpretable. The alternatives confound receptor, load or history.

52. A selective disruption of conscious ascending proprioceptive transmission leaves segmental spinal connections intact. Which dissociation is plausible?

  1. Conscious position perception worsens while some local reflexes persist
  2. Local reflexes necessarily disappear while position perception improves
  3. All extrafusal fibres become intrafusal
  4. Passive resistance must become zero
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Conscious position perception worsens while some local reflexes persist — Local afferent branches can influence motor circuits without completing the ascending route to the cortex. The predicted dissociation depends on the stipulated selectivity.

17. Common misconceptions and examination traps

1. Tapping a tendon activates the tendon organ as the principal receptor of the tendon jerk.

Correct concept: The canonical excitatory jerk begins mainly with muscle spindle stretch.

Why the error occurs: The name describes the site of stimulation, not receptor identity.

Examination reasoning: If asked to name the receptor in the knee jerk, trace mechanical displacement to quadriceps spindle and Ia input.

2. Ia afferents encode only stretch velocity.

Correct concept: Ia discharge includes both dynamic and maintained length-related components.

Why the error occurs: A prominent dynamic peak makes the static plateau easy to overlook.

Examination reasoning: Compare the end-ramp rate with the later hold rate and the original baseline.

3. Group II spindle afferents never respond to changing length.

Correct concept: Group II endings typically emphasise maintained length; their dynamic response is generally less prominent, not necessarily absent.

Why the error occurs: A contrast in relative sensitivity is mistaken for absolute exclusivity.

Examination reasoning: Look for comparative words such as predominantly, not universal claims.

4. Gamma motor neurons directly generate the main external skeletal muscle force.

Correct concept: Gamma axons supply intrafusal poles; alpha axons activate extrafusal force-producing fibres.

Why the error occurs: Both pathways are somatic motor outputs and both cause some contraction.

Examination reasoning: Identify the target fibre type before predicting force or sensory firing.

5. Alpha–gamma coactivation means alpha and gamma fire at the same frequency.

Correct concept: Coactivation is coordinated recruitment, with task-dependent relative drive.

Why the error occurs: The prefix co- is mistaken for equality.

Examination reasoning: Predict maintained sensitivity without assuming equal spike rates.

6. The direct stretch reflex has no chemical synapse because it is monosynaptic.

Correct concept: It has one central synapse plus a peripheral neuromuscular synapse.

Why the error occurs: Students count the wrong anatomical compartment.

Examination reasoning: When asked about CNS synapses, count afferent-to-alpha only.

7. The dorsal root ganglion is a synaptic relay in the stretch reflex.

Correct concept: The sensory cell body belongs to a pseudounipolar neuron; no relay is required there.

Why the error occurs: Autonomic ganglia contain synapses and share the word ganglion.

Examination reasoning: Trace the sensory neuron through its peripheral and central branches.

8. An excitatory Ia synapse makes the mechanical feedback positive.

Correct concept: The muscle contraction can reduce the original stretch, making the complete loop negative.

Why the error occurs: The sign of a synapse is confused with the sign of the whole loop.

Examination reasoning: Start with a positive length disturbance and follow its final mechanical effect.

9. Reciprocal inhibition means antagonists can never contract together.

Correct concept: Antagonists can co-contract when the task requires greater joint stiffness.

Why the error occurs: The simplified resting reflex diagram is mistaken for a compulsory rule.

Examination reasoning: Distinguish the potential inhibitory circuit from the net activity of motor pools.

10. A tendon organ responds only when force is dangerous.

Correct concept: Tendon organs signal ordinary tensile forces during contraction.

Why the error occurs: The traditional protective-reflex description omits normal regulation.

Examination reasoning: Predict Ib firing during submaximal load-bearing contraction.

11. Ib feedback always suppresses the homonymous muscle.

Correct concept: The classical inhibitory route is context-dependent; facilitation can occur in appropriate locomotor tasks.

Why the error occurs: One circuit diagram is treated as a complete description of behaviour.

Examination reasoning: Read the task and behavioural phase before predicting the final motor sign.

12. At a fixed joint angle, muscle receptor firing must be unchanged.

Correct concept: Fascicle length, tendon strain, force, history and fusimotor drive can change without joint rotation.

Why the error occurs: Joint angle is confused with all internal mechanical variables.

Examination reasoning: Ask which length or force the receptor actually samples.

13. An increased stretch response proves increased gamma activity.

Correct concept: Spindle sensitivity, central transmission, motor pool excitability and mechanics can all influence the response.

Why the error occurs: A single output is mistaken for a uniquely identified cause.

Examination reasoning: Seek paired afferent and motor measurements at controlled mechanics.

14. Quiet EMG implies zero resistance to passive movement.

Correct concept: Passive tissue stiffness and viscosity can remain without detectable muscle activation.

Why the error occurs: Electrical activity is confused with total mechanical resistance.

Examination reasoning: Separate torque or force data from EMG and consider their time courses.

15. An H-reflex is a direct measure of spindle sensitivity.

Correct concept: Electrical afferent stimulation bypasses mechanical transduction, and the reflex has several determinants.

Why the error occurs: Both H and tendon-jerk responses engage spinal motor pathways.

Examination reasoning: Compare mechanical and electrical stimulation under matched conditions.

16. All elevated resistance to passive stretch is spasticity.

Correct concept: Mechanical stiffness, background activity and velocity-dependent stretch hyperreflexia can contribute differently.

Why the error occurs: A broad observable phenomenon is confused with a specific neural component.

Examination reasoning: Check the dependence on velocity, EMG, and passive mechanical contributions.

17. If conscious proprioception is lost, all spinal stretch reflexes must disappear.

Correct concept: Ascending perception and local spinal feedback use partially distinct routes.

Why the error occurs: Students assume every sensory signal must reach cortex before eliciting a reflex.

Examination reasoning: Identify whether an interruption is peripheral, segmental or ascending.

18. Doubling one conduction velocity halves the entire reflex latency.

Correct concept: Only the delay of that conduction segment is halved; other terms remain.

Why the error occurs: The total time is treated as a single distance divided by one velocity.

Examination reasoning: Compute each segment separately, then add the other stated delays.

18. Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

Mechanical disturbances act on muscles and tendons before delayed neural corrections can develop. Muscle spindles sample local length-related deformation, whereas Golgi tendon organs sample transmitted force. Intrafusal polar contraction can adjust spindle sensory tension and compensate for extrafusal shortening. The canonical Ia pathway directly excites a homonymous alpha motor pool; an additional inhibitory interneuronal branch can suppress an antagonist. Force-related Ib signals enter task-dependent interneuronal networks. Descending modulation, receptor sensitivity, spinal excitability and muscle mechanics together determine the resulting tone and corrective action. A local reflex can remain functional without conscious perception of the disturbance.

Equation recap

  • Local spindle approximation: f = f0 + ksΔL + kvv; coefficients apply over a specified operating range.
  • Local stiffness: k = ΔF/ΔL (N/m); convert millimetres to metres.
  • Stable linear restoring model: x = Fext/(k0 + kr).
  • Conduction delay: La/va + Lm/vm; add transduction, synaptic and peripheral delays for EMG and force onset.
  • Torque magnitude: τ = Fr; local rotational stiffness contribution kr² for constant moment arm r.
  • Illustrative force feedback: F = D/(1+b) for inhibitory and F = D/(1−b) for facilitatory steady-state feedback (b < 1 for the latter idealisation).
