1. From amino acid sequence to a folded chain

Imagine two proteins made from exactly the same number of alanine, glycine, lysine and valine residues, but in different orders. Their masses can be identical and yet their shapes and functions can differ greatly. The order of the residues determines which interactions are possible when the chain begins to bend. This is why we distinguish amino acid composition from primary structure: composition tells us what is present; sequence tells us how it is connected.

In the preceding topic, amino acid chemistry explains the formation of peptide linkages. The resulting polypeptide is directional. By convention, Ala–Gly–Ser is written from its N terminus to its C terminus. Reversing it changes the molecule. A mature protein can differ from the original translation product because of terminal processing, cleavage, chemical modification and disulfide formation. Primary structure ordinarily means the amino acid sequence, while a complete account of covalent structure also includes modifications and crosslinks.

Secondary structure describes local backbone conformations and recurring hydrogen-bond patterns. Tertiary structure describes the overall organisation of one chain; quaternary structure describes the arrangement of several chains. These categories are related but not identical. Two distant segments can participate in one beta sheet, so secondary structure is not necessarily an interaction between neighbours in sequence. A single chain may also have numerous secondary elements without forming a compact globular fold.

The historical progression is instructive. Pauling, Corey and colleagues proposed regular hydrogen-bonded protein structures in 1951 using chemical geometry. Ramachandran and colleagues in 1963 demonstrated how steric constraints limit backbone conformations. The development of experimental structures made it possible to compare these predictions against actual proteins. Today we can connect sequence, local geometry and measured structural evidence instead of simply memorising pictures of helices.

In this node we will learn to identify peptide planes, calculate backbone torsions, interpret Ramachandran plots, explain secondary structure and its energetic basis, predict the effects of sequence changes, and analyse experimental data. Detailed assembly into motifs and domains belongs to 1.C.03; full folding landscapes and denaturation belong to 1.C.04.

2. Primary structure and the repeated backbone

Write the backbone as N–Cα–C′–N–Cα–C′, where C′ is the carbonyl carbon and Cα is the carbon bearing the side chain. The backbone repeats the same main atom order even when the side chains change. A carbonyl oxygen is attached to C′. An ordinary internal amide nitrogen carries a hydrogen that can participate in hydrogen bonding. Proline is exceptional: its side chain joins the backbone nitrogen, leaving no internal amide N–H donor.

A chain of n residues with free termini has n−1 peptide bonds. The formal mass balance for an unmodified linear peptide is M = ΣM(free amino acids) − (n−1) × 18.015 Da. This accounts for net loss of the elements of water; it does not imply that each ribosomal transfer step releases a water molecule, since the ribosome uses activated aminoacyl-tRNA substrates. A cyclic peptide has an additional bond and a different mass balance. An approximate average residue mass of 110 Da can be useful for estimating a protein's size, but not for calculating an exact mass.

Typical peptide C′–N bond lengths are around 1.33 Å, N–Cα around 1.46 Å and Cα–C′ around 1.52 Å. These are useful ideal values rather than invariant constants. Cα generally has approximately tetrahedral bonding, while the carbonyl carbon and amide nitrogen are approximately trigonal planar. The geometry sets the framework on which all later torsional changes occur.

Protein sequence can be inferred from a gene's coding sequence, measured using tandem mass spectrometry, or determined chemically using methods such as Edman degradation of an accessible N terminus. DNA sequence alone does not reveal every post-translational change. Ordinary mass differences cannot distinguish leucine and isoleucine, which are isomers of equal mass. Structural knowledge therefore requires more than one type of evidence.

For example, a 12-residue chain whose free constituent amino acids have total molar mass 1,500 g/mol contains 11 peptide bonds and has approximate molar mass 1,500−11×18.015 = 1,301.8 g/mol. Changing the sequence order would preserve this mass but could change conformational preferences. This provides a simple demonstration of why mass and folding are different properties.

3. Amide resonance, peptide planarity and cis–trans geometry

The amide group has a nitrogen lone pair that interacts with the carbonyl π system. Resonance contributors can be drawn as O=C–NH and ⁻O–C=NH⁺, but the actual molecule is a delocalised electronic structure rather than a molecule alternating between two separate structures. The C′–N bond has partial double-bond character. Twisting it reduces orbital overlap and costs energy, which explains its restricted rotation.

The atoms Cα(i), C′(i), O(i), N(i+1), H(i+1) and Cα(i+1) are approximately coplanar for an ordinary peptide unit. In proline the relevant substituent on N replaces H. Think of a chain of relatively stiff planar plates joined by movable hinges at the alpha carbons. Rotating the hinges can change the shape of the entire chain without rotating freely inside each plate.

The torsion ω describes the arrangement about C′–N. In trans, the adjacent Cα atoms lie on opposite sides of the amide bond and ω is near ±180°. In cis, they lie on the same side and ω is near 0°. Trans is favoured for most peptide bonds because the adjacent alpha-carbon substituents are less crowded. Real peptide groups can deviate modestly from ideal planarity; unusually large deviations should be evaluated against experimental evidence.

A peptide bond preceding proline, written X–Pro, has a higher cis population than most ordinary peptide bonds. The proline ring makes the steric differences between the two states smaller. Several percent of X–Pro bonds can occur in cis, depending on sequence and environment. Cis–trans isomerisation is slow in many contexts because of the rotational barrier, and peptidyl-prolyl isomerases can accelerate the transition. They do not change the equilibrium merely by acting as catalysts.

Notice the difference between an activation barrier and an equilibrium free-energy difference. A rare conformation can be difficult to reach, but these are separate quantities. This distinction is important in folding kinetics and in interpreting an unexpected cis peptide bond in a structural model.

4. Phi, psi, omega and side-chain torsions

A dihedral angle is defined by four consecutive atoms. It measures the relative orientation of the planes containing atoms 1–2–3 and 2–3–4, using the central 2–3 bond as the rotation axis. The sign is determined by the standard atom-order convention; it cannot always be inferred reliably from a flat sketch.

For residue i, φ (phi) is defined by C′(i−1)–N(i)–Cα(i)–C′(i), with N–Cα as the axis. ψ (psi) is defined by N(i)–Cα(i)–C′(i)–N(i+1), with Cα–C′ as its axis. ω(i) is defined by Cα(i)–C′(i)–N(i+1)–Cα(i+1), with the peptide C′–N bond as its axis. Do not swap the definitions of phi and psi.

Because ω is usually close to the trans planar state, phi and psi account for most backbone conformational variability. Given bond lengths, bond angles, torsions and starting coordinates, one can reconstruct the idealised chain geometry. However, phi and psi cannot vary independently without considering steric interactions. A turn of one bond alters the distances that are possible around the next.

At an unmodified free N terminus the standard φ is undefined because the preceding carbonyl carbon is absent; at a free C terminus the standard ψ is undefined because the following nitrogen is absent. Missing atoms in an experimental model can also make a torsion undefined. Such residues should not be assigned arbitrary values to make a plot look complete.

Side-chain torsions are called χ₁, χ₂ and so forth. Their preferred combinations are rotamers. They are different from backbone angles but interact with them through steric and energetic constraints. A residue with a permitted backbone conformation may still have an unfavourable side-chain orientation.

5. Ramachandran plot: from steric constraints to a map

If phi and psi could each adopt any angle with equal probability, the chain could explore enormous numbers of conformations. In reality, atoms have excluded volumes. When a bond rotates, carbonyl oxygen, neighbouring amide groups and side chains can approach each other too closely. Repulsive interactions make many angle combinations energetically costly.

Ramachandran and colleagues mapped geometrically permissible pairs of φ and ψ. The horizontal axis is φ and the vertical axis is ψ; both conventionally extend from −180° to +180°. The boundaries wrap because these angles are periodic. Modern empirical plots use distributions observed in well-refined protein structures. Favoured regions contain commonly observed conformations, allowed regions are less common but plausible, and outliers deserve further investigation.

Right-handed α helices cluster near (−60°, −45°). Extended β-strand conformations commonly lie near (−120° to −140°, +120° to +140°). A smaller left-handed α region lies near (+60°, +40°). Extended polyproline II-like conformations lie near (−75°, +145°). The precise centres and shapes depend on residue class and reference dataset.

A point in the α region indicates compatible local geometry, not proof that the residue is part of a regular helix. A repeated sequence of appropriate angles and hydrogen bonds is needed. Similarly, an isolated residue in the β region can belong to an extended chain without joining a sheet. Many α-region points do not tell us whether the protein has one long helix or numerous short ones. The scatter plot alone does not preserve sequence order.

An outlier is not automatically a modelling error. It can occur at a strained active site, unusual turn or other genuine structure. It can also arise from a misplaced atom, incorrect sequence register, poor map fit or inadequate refinement. A careful validator uses the correct residue-specific plot together with bond geometry, nonbonded contacts, density or map fit, and biological context. A high percentage of favoured residues is useful but not sufficient to certify a structure.

6. Why glycine, proline and other residues differ

Glycine has two hydrogens attached to Cα and no Cβ. It is achiral and experiences fewer steric restrictions than most residues. Its Ramachandran distribution is broader and more nearly symmetric, making positive φ conformations much more accessible. This explains its frequent occurrence in tightly reversing turns. However, glycine's flexibility can increase the entropy cost of immobilising it in a regular helix; it is not automatically a stabilising residue in every position.

Proline has a pyrrolidine ring connecting its side chain to the backbone nitrogen. The ring constrains φ, often to approximately −60° to −75°. An internal proline lacks the usual backbone N–H donor but retains the carbonyl acceptor. It can interrupt a helix's repeating hydrogen-bond network and introduce a kink. It can nevertheless occur near helix ends or at functional bends in membrane proteins. Proline is not an absolute helix prohibition.

The residue immediately preceding proline experiences distinctive steric constraints from the following ring. Empirical validation commonly distinguishes general residues, glycine, proline and pre-proline; some schemes further separate isoleucine and valine. The latter have β-branched side chains that influence accessible torsions and often favour extended structures relative to some other residues.

For a corresponding D-amino acid in a mirrored environment, the ideal steric preferences are approximately reflected in both φ and ψ. This is important in engineered D-peptides and unusual proteins. Glycine has no D/L stereocentre. In real mixed-chirality environments, interactions can modify the simple mirror relationship.

In exam problems, identify the residue class before interpreting a positive φ angle or outlier. An alanine at (+80°,0°) may be unusual, while glycine at the same point may be expected in a tight turn. The observation alone does not tell you the free energy of the complete protein.

7. Alpha helix: repeating intrachain hydrogen bonding

The backbone contains numerous polar carbonyl and amide groups. One elegant way to bring them together is to coil the chain so that the carbonyl oxygen of residue i accepts a hydrogen bond from the amide N–H of residue i+4. Repeating the arrangement yields a compact α helix. Most α helices in natural L-amino-acid proteins are right-handed.

The ideal α helix has about 3.6 residues per turn and an axial rise of approximately 1.5 Å per residue. The pitch is therefore 5.4 Å per turn, and the angular rotation per residue is 360°/3.6 = 100°. Its torsions commonly cluster around φ = −57°, ψ = −47°. The backbone forms the central scaffold and side chains point outward, allowing a wide variety of sequence-dependent interactions.

The i→i+4 hydrogen bond involves backbone groups, not necessarily side chains. Four residue steps do not equal exactly one complete turn; the angular displacement is 400°, or 40° after removing a full rotation. An ideal uninterrupted helix of n residues contains max(n−4,0) internal hydrogen bonds of this register. The first four amide donors and last four carbonyl acceptors cannot all find partners inside the isolated segment. Solvent, side chains and neighbouring structures may compensate.

Hydrogen bonds are directional and their strength depends on geometry and environment. Forming a hydrogen bond in water can replace favourable hydrogen bonds to solvent. In a nonpolar membrane interior, satisfying backbone polar groups is especially important. Hence a helix is not stabilised by a fixed energy per hydrogen bond independent of the rest of the system.

For a 20-residue ideal helix, the approximate turn count is 20/3.6 ≈ 5.56 and the conventional approximate length is 20×1.5 = 30 Å. The actual axial separation between first and last Cα positions in ideal geometry is (20−1)×1.5 = 28.5 Å. There are 16 internal i→i+4 bonds under the ideal assumptions. Exam questions may use either length convention; check the wording.

8. Helical faces, dipoles, caps and sequence effects

A helix presents different side chains in different directions. With about 100° rotation per residue, i and i+3 are separated by 300° (equivalent to −60°); i and i+4 by 400° (equivalent to +40°); and i and i+7 by 700° (equivalent to −20°). Thus a sequence with hydrophobic residues recurring at intervals of three or four can create a hydrophobic face. A helical wheel represents the positions as viewed down the helix axis.

An amphipathic helix has a hydrophobic face and a more polar face. It may bind an interface or pack against a nonpolar region of a protein. A typical transmembrane helix instead needs favourable exposure to the nonpolar bilayer across much of its circumference. A 20–25-residue straight helix extends approximately 30–38 Å using the residue-count convention, though membrane thickness, tilt and boundary residues change the required length.

Peptide groups carry dipoles. Their approximate alignment in an α helix produces a macrodipole, with a partially positive N-terminal end and partially negative C-terminal end. A negative charge near the N end or positive charge near the C end can be favourable. But solvent, other charges and specific interactions also contribute; the polarity is not a universal rule for the effect of every charged substitution.

Capping interactions help satisfy backbone groups near the ends that lack a normal intrahelical partner. Asparagine, aspartate, serine and threonine can act as N-cap residues in appropriate geometries. A capping residue can have distinct torsion angles from the helical core. The exact boundaries are therefore not necessarily the most obvious sequence-prediction positions.

Alanine often shows high intrinsic helix propensity. Leucine and glutamate frequently occur in helices, depending on environment. An internal proline loses a donor and constrains backbone geometry, while glycine can have an unfavourable conformational entropy cost. Both can also be functionally useful at particular positions. Like charges may repel, whereas appropriately spaced unlike charges can interact favourably. These effects depend on pH, salt concentration and local contacts. A propensity is a tendency, not a mandatory instruction encoded by one residue.

9. 3₁₀, π and polyproline helices

A 3₁₀ helix forms C=O(i)···H–N(i+3) hydrogen bonds. It has approximately three residues per turn and a ten-atom hydrogen-bonded ring under the conventional counting scheme. Its rise is near 2 Å per residue and pitch near 6 Å. It often occurs as a short segment at the end of an α helix or near a local transition, rather than as a long independent helix.

A π helix uses i→i+5 hydrogen bonds, has around 4.4 residues per turn and a rise near 1.15 Å per residue. Its hydrogen-bonded ring contains sixteen atoms. Long ideal π helices are uncommon, but local π-like bulges can alter side-chain orientation and contribute to biological function. The α helix can similarly be described as a 3.6₁₃ helix: the subscript counts atoms in the hydrogen-bonded ring, not residues or hydrogen bonds.

Polyproline II is a left-handed extended helical conformation with approximately three residues per turn and 3.1 Å axial rise per residue. Typical angles are near φ=−75°, ψ=+145°. It lacks the regular internal hydrogen-bond network of an α helix. It is often solvent exposed and can be adopted by residues other than proline. Its torsions are extended, but it is not equivalent to a β sheet.

Polyproline I is a different compact right-handed conformation, involving cis peptide bonds in polyproline models. Polyproline II generally uses trans bonds. Collagen contains left-handed PPII-like individual chains assembled into a right-handed triple helix. It is not made from three ordinary α helices. The detailed structural basis of collagen function is reserved for a later node.

Mixed helical registers may occur within one segment. An isolated i→i+3 bond can be part of a β turn instead of a 3₁₀ helix. Repetition, torsions and the neighbouring geometry are needed to identify a regular element.

10. Beta strand and beta sheet architecture

Instead of coiling, the backbone can extend in an approximately zigzag arrangement. A β strand often has φ and ψ near −120° and +130°, though actual values vary. It extends roughly 3.2–3.5 Å per residue along the strand, considerably more than an α helix. When adjacent strands form repeating backbone hydrogen bonds, they constitute a β sheet.

Successive side chains point approximately to opposite faces of a β strand. A sequence with alternating nonpolar and polar residues can therefore present a nonpolar face and a polar face. The repeat is approximately two residues, unlike the helical periodicity of 3.6 residues per turn. This difference is useful for predicting which sequence patterns might be compatible with each geometry.

A sheet may consist of adjacent stretches of one chain, widely separated stretches of one chain, or different polypeptide molecules. Thus interstrand hydrogen bonds are not necessarily intermolecular. The sheet is usually twisted rather than perfectly flat, because of backbone stereochemistry and interactions in its environment.

Sheet edges can expose hydrogen-bonding groups. Edge-strand geometry, charged residues, loops and β bulges may reduce inappropriate sheet extension. Intermolecular extension can contribute to amyloid assemblies, which feature a cross-β organisation. The hydrogen-bonding principle is shared with ordinary β sheets, but the larger assembly and biological consequences differ.

A β strand is not itself proof of a sheet. To assign a sheet, identify neighbouring strands and their repeated hydrogen-bonding contacts. An extended loop or PPII-like chain may occupy some similar regions on a torsion plot without participating in a sheet.

11. Parallel, antiparallel and mixed sheets

Every strand has an N→C direction. In a parallel sheet, adjacent strands run in the same direction; in an antiparallel sheet, they run in opposite directions. Mixed sheets contain both kinds of neighbouring relationships. Ribbon diagrams conventionally show a β strand as an arrow pointing toward its C terminus.

Antiparallel arrangements tend to allow more nearly linear backbone hydrogen bonds and direct pairing between opposing residues. In parallel arrangements, the hydrogen-bond partners are commonly offset. This difference is about the alignment of donors and acceptors; the strands themselves can twist and deviate from ideal geometry.

It is an oversimplification to declare all antiparallel sheets more stable than all parallel sheets. Free energy also depends on solvent exposure, packing, loop connections, sequence and other interactions. The hydrogen-bond geometry is only one factor. Parallel arrangements can be highly stable in suitable protein folds.

A β hairpin contains two antiparallel strands connected by a short reversal. Connecting neighbouring sequential strands in parallel generally requires a crossover to return to the appropriate end of the sheet. This can be achieved with a longer loop or an intervening helix, as in a β–α–β motif. Detailed motif organisation belongs to 1.C.03.

Register specifies which residue positions on one strand align with those on the other. Changing the register can change hydrogen bonds and side-chain packing. A single-residue insertion may disturb a previously regular pairing and create a local β bulge. Thus even a small sequence change can have a substantial geometric consequence.

12. Beta turns, gamma turns, bulges, loops and disorder

A protein must repeatedly redirect its chain. Turns provide short reversals while loops connect structural elements over variable lengths. These regions are not necessarily unstructured: many loops have highly specific conformations, contribute to recognition surfaces or position active-site residues.

A classical β turn spans four consecutive residues i through i+3, with the terminal Cα atoms usually within about 7 Å and the central residues not belonging to a regular α helix. A hydrogen bond from the carbonyl of i to the amide of i+3 is common but not required by all geometric definitions. The φ/ψ values of the two central residues define traditional β-turn types.

For type I, the ideal central pairs are (−60°,−30°) and (−90°,0°). Type II has (−60°,+120°) and (+80°,0°). The positive φ at i+2 is difficult for many ordinary L-amino acids, explaining why glycine often occupies that position. Proline frequently occurs at i+1 because its restricted φ matches the first central position, although neither preference is obligatory.

Type I′ and II′ are approximate mirror counterparts of I and II, obtained by reversing the signs of both central pairs. These geometries are often relevant to β hairpins. Type VI includes a cis central peptide bond, commonly involving proline; its subtypes have different torsions. Type VIII has extended-like geometry at its second central residue. Empirical turn classifications use tolerances and include additional subclasses.

A γ turn spans three residues and may contain an i→i+2 hydrogen bond; classical and inverse forms have opposite central torsions. Longer α and π turns span five and six residues, respectively, and can contain i→i+4 or i→i+5 hydrogen bonds. A single such contact is not sufficient to define a repeating helix.

A β bulge disrupts regular strand pairing, often because an extra residue on one strand alters the register. An omega loop is a longer nonregular connecting segment whose ends are relatively close. Loops can be rigidly maintained by surrounding interactions or can move as part of a protein's function.

Intrinsic disorder describes an ensemble without a single stable conformation under the specified conditions. Disordered regions may have transient local helical or extended preferences and can fold upon binding. A loop visible in an atomic model, a missing region of electron density and an intrinsically disordered region are not synonymous. A three-state structural annotation may label all as coil in some contexts, but that simplified label does not establish identical physical behaviour.

13. Thermodynamics and cooperativity of local structure

The existence of allowed torsions does not mean all allowed conformations are equally stable. The free-energy change of a transition is ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, with T in kelvin. For coil→helix, define ΔG as G(helix)−G(coil). A negative value favours the helix. For the reverse transition the sign changes. Always establish the direction before interpreting an exam question.

A regular structure constrains the chain and reduces its conformational entropy. Hydrogen bonding, solvent rearrangement, side-chain contacts, electrostatics and packing may compensate for that cost. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds can replace bonds to water, so one cannot count each bond as an identical free-energy contribution in all environments.

In a simplified two-state model with coil C and helix H, equilibrium constant K=[H]/[C], ΔG°=−RT ln K and helical fraction fH=K/(1+K). Here R=8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. A 75% helical population gives K=3 and at 298 K ΔG°≈−2.72 kJ/mol. The interpretation assumes the stated two-state model; many real peptides populate partly helical states.

Helix–coil models such as Zimm–Bragg distinguish nucleation from propagation. A small nucleation factor σ represents the cost of establishing a helical segment, and a propagation weight s reflects extension under a common convention. Once several residues are organised, adding another can be easier than initiating a new segment. This provides a mechanistic basis for cooperative transitions, while exact parameter definitions vary between models.

Changes in pH affect charges, salt screens electrostatic interactions, and temperature changes the balance of enthalpy and entropy. Urea and guanidinium can favour unfolded ensembles through solvent interactions. Trifluoroethanol can promote helicity in isolated peptides but may also stabilise structures not present in the native aqueous protein. A peptide adopting a helix in one cosolvent is not proof of its conformation in a cell.

To interpret a mutation, distinguish the local effect from the whole protein's stability. Glycine replacing alanine may increase unfolded-state entropy or disrupt packing; proline may remove an expected donor or alter torsions. A charge change may affect solvent or a salt bridge. The measured result depends on the difference between states, not only on the new side chain.

14. Experimental evidence: coordinates, spectroscopy and exchange

X-ray crystallography measures diffraction from a protein crystal. A structural model is refined against the diffraction data. Cryo-electron microscopy can provide maps supporting atomic models when the local resolution is sufficient. The torsions of a protein are calculated from its model coordinates. A Ramachandran plot is therefore derived from the model; it is not a direct measurement of the angles by the instrument.

Solution NMR provides multiple types of constraints. Nuclear Overhauser effects can inform short interatomic distances. Scalar couplings depend on torsions through relations such as J(θ)=A cos²θ+B cosθ+C, where θ is the relevant dihedral and coefficients are calibrated for the coupling. A measured coupling may fit several angles, so independent constraints are important. Chemical shifts also depend on local conformation: Cα secondary shifts are often positive for α helices and negative for β strands, while Cβ shifts often show the opposite trend. These are statistical trends and require appropriate reference values.

Far-UV circular dichroism measures differential absorption of left- and right-circularly polarised light. An α-helical sample typically shows negative bands near 208 and 222 nm with a positive feature near 190 nm. β-rich samples often show a negative band near 215–218 nm and a positive feature around 195 nm. Disordered samples frequently show a strong negative band around 195–200 nm. Spectral shapes depend on composition, solution conditions and measurement quality.

CD reports an ensemble-averaged global structural signal. It cannot locate the exact residues forming helices. A decline in negative ellipticity at 222 nm may be consistent with reduced helicity, but concentration errors, baseline, light scattering and aggregation can affect the measurement. Mean-residue ellipticity requires appropriate concentration and path-length normalisation. Near-UV CD often reports aromatic side-chain and disulfide environments, providing complementary information on tertiary organisation.

Infrared spectroscopy examines vibrational absorption. The amide I band, dominated by carbonyl stretching, is sensitive to secondary structure. Helical contributions frequently appear around 1650–1658 cm⁻¹; β-sheet contributions often around 1620–1640 cm⁻¹, with higher-frequency components possible in antiparallel sheets. Turn and disorder bands overlap. Exact band positions depend on solvent, isotope substitution and other factors, and interpretation generally requires a spectrum rather than one isolated number.

Hydrogen–deuterium exchange probes the protection and exchange of backbone amide hydrogens. A protected amide can indicate hydrogen bonding or limited solvent accessibility, but exchange also depends on local unfolding, pH, temperature and exchange chemistry. It is not unique evidence of one structural class. Exchange combined with mass spectrometry or NMR can provide sequence-localised information.

A strong experiment combines complementary observations. If far-UV CD suggests helicity and a well-supported model shows consecutive α-compatible torsions and i→i+4 contacts, the interpretations reinforce one another. If a predicted helix lacks supporting density, sequence propensity alone cannot establish it. A good Part C answer states what is measured, what is inferred, and what alternative explanations remain.

15. Prediction versus assignment: Chou–Fasman, GOR and DSSP

Predicting a secondary structure from a sequence is not the same as assigning it from known atomic coordinates. Prediction estimates which structures are likely; assignment examines geometry and hydrogen-bond patterns. This distinction is especially important when evaluating the outputs of computational models.

The Chou–Fasman approach uses empirical propensities for different residue types to occur in helices, sheets or turns. One can define a propensity as the fraction of residues of a given type within a structural class divided by their fraction in the background set. A value greater than one indicates enrichment, not a probability exceeding one. The method uses local sequence rules to identify and extend candidate segments. GOR approaches use information from neighbouring residues to improve estimates of local conformation.

Modern prediction methods may incorporate evolutionary profiles, sequence alignments and machine learning. Three-dimensional structure predictors generate models that can subsequently be analysed for secondary elements. Predictions can be useful but must be interpreted with confidence estimates and experimental context, particularly for alternate conformations, disordered regions and binding-dependent structure.

DSSP assigns secondary structure from coordinates using backbone hydrogen-bond patterns and geometry. Familiar labels include H for α helix, G for 3₁₀ helix, I for π helix, E for a strand in a sheet, B for isolated β bridge, T for hydrogen-bonded turn and S for bend; unassigned residues may use a blank or a program-specific coil symbol. Newer implementations can provide additional labels. STRIDE is another approach using hydrogen-bond information and backbone-angle preferences.

A three-state reduction commonly groups H/G/I as helix, E/B as strand and other labels as coil. The exact mapping should be stated. Assignment algorithms can disagree near boundaries or for irregular segments without either being universally wrong. A Ramachandran point classifies local torsions; a hydrogen-bond-based assignment additionally considers neighbouring structural patterns.

Prediction performance can be evaluated using a confusion matrix. If 40 residues are actually helical and 45 are predicted helical, with 35 correct, helix precision is 35/45=77.8% and recall is 35/40=87.5%. Overall three-state accuracy requires the numbers of correctly assigned strand and coil residues as well. Good helix prediction on one protein does not prove that every class or protein is predicted equally well.

16. Integrating geometry, mutation and experimental reasoning

Suppose the first 12 residues of an 18-residue chain form an ideal α helix. There would be eight internal i→i+4 hydrogen bonds. Replace residue 7 with proline: the expected N–H donor at 7 disappears, so C=O(3) cannot make that specific bond. The carbonyl at 7 may still accept a hydrogen bond from residue 11 if the geometry allows. A real mutation could also kink the helix and disrupt additional interactions. Counting the missing bond is a first mechanistic inference, not a complete folding prediction.

Suppose a model contains an alanine at φ=+80°, ψ=0° in a type II turn. This is unusual for an ordinary L residue, but not logically impossible. Glycine might fit the position more readily. Before declaring the original model wrong or the mutant more stable, check map fit, clashes, residue-class distributions and surrounding contacts. The free energy of a mutation is a difference between states, not merely a measure of steric comfort at one position.

A 24-residue ideal helix has approximate axial length 36 Å. Tilt it by 30° relative to a membrane normal: its projected length is 36 cos30°≈31.2 Å. This helps explain how tilting can match hydrophobic thickness. But hydrophobic sequence, orientation, membrane composition and boundary effects are still required to infer membrane insertion.

In a β strand with sequence V–D–I–E–L–K, positions 1,3,5 are hydrophobic while positions 2,4,6 are charged. The ideal alternating geometry produces distinct faces. The same alternation in an α helix would distribute side chains differently because adjacent residues rotate about 100°, not 180°. Therefore sequence periodicity provides evidence for compatible geometry but cannot independently prove it.

If a mutation preserves the far-UV CD spectrum while sharply reducing activity, it is consistent with preservation of global secondary content and a change in local chemistry, packing or dynamics. It is not proof that all tertiary structure is unchanged. This reasoning will carry forward into the next node: the spatial arrangement of helices, strands and loops is distinct from the identity of the individual elements.

When solving problems, first identify the covalent backbone, then the relevant torsions, then the hydrogen-bond register. Next ask which measurements support the proposed geometry and what assumptions enter any numerical estimate. This procedure makes it possible to solve unfamiliar problems without relying only on memorised facts.

17. Part B — original CSIR-NET and SET conceptual questions

Choose the best answer before revealing the reasoning. These are original practice questions, not represented as verbatim previous-year questions.

1. The central axis of φ is which bond?

A C′–N; B N–Cα; C Cα–C′; D Cα–Cβ

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B

φ uses the N–Cα axis. ψ uses Cα–C′ and ω uses C′–N.

2. Peptide planarity is principally explained by:

A ionic attraction; B resonance; C disulfides; D hydrophobicity

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B

Amide resonance gives C′–N partial double-bond character.

3. A residue at (−60°,−45°) is most compatible with:

A right-handed α; B left-handed α; C β; D necessarily cis

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A

These are characteristic αR angles, not a direct measurement of ω.

4. Which residue frequently tolerates positive φ in a type II turn?

A Val; B Ile; C Gly; D Leu

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

Gly lacks Cβ, reducing steric crowding.

5. The α helix register is:

A i→i+2; B i→i+3; C i→i+4; D i→i+5

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

The carbonyl at i accepts from the backbone donor at i+4.

6. Internal proline lacks which group?

A carbonyl O; B amide N–H; C nitrogen; D Cα

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B

The side chain bonds to the amide nitrogen; the carbonyl remains.

7. Antiparallel sheet strands have:

A opposite N→C directions; B equal mass; C separate chains necessarily; D no H bonds

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A

Direction is the defining relationship.

8. The 3₁₀ helix register is:

A i→i+5; B i→i+3; C i→i+4; D none

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B

The conventional ten-atom ring follows from this register.

9. Which can identify residue-specific helical geometry?

A CD 222 nm alone; B total mass; C reliable atom-resolved model; D pI

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

Coordinates establish local torsions and bonding; CD is global.

10. A Ramachandran outlier establishes:

A unfolding; B definite error; C need for inspection; D cis peptide

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

Unusual geometry may be genuine or a model error.

11. Ideal α-helix pitch is:

A 1.5 Å; B 3.6 Å; C 5.4 Å; D 10 Å

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

3.6 × 1.5 = 5.4 Å per turn.

12. Negative CD bands near 208 and 222 nm suggest:

A α helix; B B-DNA only; C turns only; D PPII only

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A

This is the characteristic α-helical far-UV pattern.

13. PPII is typically:

A right-handed α; B i→i+5 bonded; C extended left-handed; D exclusively proline

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

It lacks a regular internal H-bond network and can include other residues.

14. Cis versus trans peptide configuration is defined by:

A χ₁; B φ; C ψ; D ω

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: D

ω ≈0° cis and ±180° trans.

15. DSSP E conventionally means:

A α helix; B 3₁₀; C extended strand in sheet; D turn

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

H is α; G is 3₁₀; T is turn.

16. An unlabelled Ramachandran scatter plot omits:

A φ; B ψ; C sequence order; D local torsion distribution

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

The dots do not preserve sequence connectivity.

17. The α helix macrodipole has approximate polarity:

A N positive, C negative; B reversed; C both positive; D none

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A

Aligned peptide dipoles produce this polarity.

18. A classical β turn typically spans:

A two residues; B six residues; C four residues; D a full sheet

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C

The outer residues approach and central torsions define type.

19. An amphipathic β strand may have:

A alternating nonpolar/polar residues; B every fourth only; C all charged necessarily; D no carbonyls

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: A

Successive side chains alternate faces.

20. Ordinary protein denaturation may:

A require every peptide bond to break; B disrupt secondary structure without hydrolysis; C always form new disulfides; D always cleave termini

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: B

Disrupting noncovalent organisation need not sever the backbone.

18. Part C — original analytical and numerical questions

Solve each problem before opening the explanation. Distinguish idealised geometric calculations from experimentally supported conclusions.

1. An ideal α helix contains 36 residues. Find turns, approximate length and H-bond count.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: 10 turns, ~54 Å, 32 bonds.

36/3.6=10; 36×1.5=54 Å; 36−4=32. First-to-last Cα span is 52.5 Å.

2. A 16-residue α segment contains Pro at position 8. Which expected donor interaction is absent?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: C=O(4)···H–N(8).

Internal Pro cannot donate that N–H. Its carbonyl may still accept from position 12.

3. Four consecutive residues have angles near (−60°,−45°). Is an α helix proven?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: No.

Local geometry supports the hypothesis; consecutive register and context are needed.

4. A turn has central angles (−60,+120) and (+80,0). Identify it and likely i+2 residue.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: Type II; glycine often at i+2.

The positive φ is more easily accessible to Gly.

5. A 22-residue straight α helix tilts 40° from membrane normal. Estimate projected length.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: ~25.3 Å.

22×1.5×cos40°≈25.3 using the approximate residue-count convention.

6. Helicity changes from 25% to 75% at 298 K in a two-state model. Find ΔΔG° for coil→helix.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: −5.44 kJ/mol.

K1=1/3, K2=3; −RT ln(9)≈−5.44 kJ/mol.

7. 48 residues truly helical; 60 predicted helical; 36 correctly predicted. Precision and recall?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: 60% and 75%.

Precision=36/60; recall=36/48. Overall accuracy needs other classes.

8. Compare ideal 10-residue α and 3₁₀ segments by length and bond count.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: α ~15 Å, 6; 3₁₀ ~20 Å, 7.

Use n×rise and n−register. More bonds do not prove higher stability.

9. A mutant loses negative CD bands at 208/222 nm and gains a negative band at 198 nm. Interpret.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: Reduced helicity and more disorder are consistent.

Check baseline, concentration, aggregation and solvent; no residue-specific location follows.

10. A bond X–Pro has ω=3°; a neighbour has ω=177°. Is the first necessarily wrong?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: No.

Cis X–Pro is plausible. Both angles are near planar states; inspect experimental evidence.

11. Two neighbouring β strands run in opposite N→C directions and are connected by a short reversal. Identify.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: Antiparallel β hairpin.

Opposite directions and a short connector are characteristic; check the sheet contacts.

12. Two models have 98% favoured Ramachandran points; one fits its map poorly. Are they equally reliable?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: Not necessarily.

Torsion quality is only one validation dimension; map fit and register also matter.

13. For L–K–V–E–I–D–F–Q in a β strand, which residues form a likely hydrophobic face?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: L,V,I,F.

Odd positions lie on one face of the ideal strand.

14. Find α helix rotation from residue 2 to residue 9.

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: 700°, equivalent to −20°.

Seven steps ×100°; side chains point approximately toward the same face.

15. CD suggests 50% α structure in a 20-residue peptide. Does this establish one 10-residue helix?

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer: No.

It can be multiple segments or a population average. CD lacks sequence resolution.

19. Consolidation, glossary and references

The central story begins with sequence and covalent geometry, continues through peptide planarity and accessible torsions, and ends with regular secondary structures and connecting elements. Remember that φ and ψ describe local geometry, while hydrogen-bond networks establish repeating structures. Glycine and proline illustrate how residue chemistry influences conformational possibilities.

For rapid revision, draw the peptide plane and mark φ, ψ and ω. Sketch the main Ramachandran regions and explain the exceptions. Derive 100° rotation per residue, 5.4 Å pitch and n−4 hydrogen bonds for an ideal α helix. Compare α, 3₁₀, π and PPII, then contrast parallel and antiparallel β sheets and classify a type II turn.

Finally, explain what a CD spectrum can tell you and what it cannot. Practice calculating simple equilibrium and helical dimensions, and remember that propensity and validation do not constitute proof of a complete structure. The next node, 1.C.03, extends these ideas to motifs, domains, tertiary structure and multimeric assemblies.
