Three molecular families, three analytical problems

A germinating seed contains enzymes that mobilize stored nutrients, carbohydrates that provide energy and structural material, and lipids that act as reserves or membrane components. Grinding the seed does not release these substances as separate, pure compounds. It produces a complicated mixture of soluble molecules, membranes, cell wall fragments, suspended particles and complexes. A scientist must choose which molecular population to recover before interpreting any measurement. This decision is the starting point of biochemical isolation. If the aim is to measure activity of an enzyme, preservation of its native structure matters. If the aim is to measure amino acid composition, preservation of that native structure may be unnecessary. If the aim is to identify the fatty acids in a storage oil, the treatment needed for the measurement may deliberately destroy information about the original lipid molecules.

An extraction transfers a molecular population out of its original matrix into a form suitable for further examination. Fractionation divides the mixture according to a physical or chemical property. Purification enriches a target relative to contaminants, and analysis produces evidence about the identity, amount, properties or activity of a component. These words describe different operations even when one operation serves more than one purpose. Removing insoluble material may clarify an extract without purifying its soluble target very much. Concentrating the extract by removing solvent may increase its protein concentration without changing the fraction of protein represented by the target enzyme. Measuring the resulting concentration does not establish its biological activity.

Proteins are polymers of amino acids connected by peptide bonds. Their different side chains permit hydrophobic, electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions, making the folded structure chemically heterogeneous. Carbohydrates include small sugars and long chains connected by glycosidic bonds. The number and accessibility of hydroxyl groups influence hydration, but polymer architecture and intermolecular interactions also determine solubility. Lipids are defined largely by their hydrophobic or amphipathic properties rather than by one common polymeric backbone. A neutral storage lipid and a charged membrane phospholipid behave differently during extraction. Thus it is inappropriate to treat the three families as though one generic solubility rule were sufficient.

The surrounding sample material is the matrix. A seed extract may contain pigments that absorb visible light, phenolic compounds that bind proteins, soluble sugars that participate in redox reactions, and particles that scatter light. When an analytical reagent produces a response, the response may include contributions from the analyte, the matrix and the reagent itself. A blank estimates selected background contributions; a calibration standard supplies a known relationship between response and amount. A biological replicate represents independently sampled biological material, whereas repeatedly measuring the same extract estimates technical variation. Neither replication nor calibration alone corrects systematic extraction bias.

A sample can be divided into matched portions for separate extraction schemes. This approach often gives more interpretable results than seeking a single treatment that preserves all three macromolecular classes. A denaturing treatment that facilitates carbohydrate measurement may destroy enzyme activity. An aqueous extraction that preserves a soluble enzyme may fail to recover a neutral oil. A total lipid extraction may remove the native surroundings of membrane proteins. The best method is consequently defined by the biological question and by the chemical form in which the target needs to be measured.

The difference between concentration and total recovered amount is central to all three branches of analysis. A fraction containing 4 mg of a compound per millilitre in 5 mL holds 20 mg. Another fraction containing 1 mg per millilitre in 40 mL holds 40 mg. The first is more concentrated but the second contains more material. Whenever an experimental table gives both concentration and volume, multiply them before comparing yields. The mass of a target in a fraction and the mass of all recovered material are not necessarily identical. A mass balance requires a defined analyte, consistent units and all relevant fractions.

Protein solubility and preservation

A soluble protein exists in dynamic exchange with its aqueous surroundings. Water molecules interact with exposed polar groups, and the protein's charged side chains attract counterions. A folded globular protein commonly shields many nonpolar side chains from water while presenting more hydrophilic groups to the solvent. This organization is not absolute: a soluble protein can have hydrophobic patches for binding partners, and integral membrane proteins commonly have exposed hydrophobic surfaces compatible with the membrane interior. Solubility therefore depends on the folded structure, solvent composition and interactions between neighboring protein molecules, not merely on the amino acid sequence considered as a list of residues.

The pH of an extract changes the protonation of ionizable groups. Below a protein's isoelectric point, its average net charge is positive; above that point, the average net charge is negative. At the isoelectric point the average net charge is zero, but positive and negative groups have not disappeared. A reduction in net charge can reduce electrostatic repulsion between molecules and favor association or precipitation. Many proteins consequently show relatively low solubility near their isoelectric point. This is a useful generalization rather than a universal solubility minimum. A highly hydrated protein, an unusual surface-charge distribution or binding to a partner can alter the relationship between charge and precipitation.

Consider two soluble proteins with isoelectric points of 5 and 8. At pH 6 the first has an average negative charge and the second an average positive charge. They may still both remain soluble. This charge contrast can be exploited in ion exchange chromatography, which is studied later, but it does not automatically predict which protein precipitates. Solubility requires accounting for both molecule–water and molecule–molecule interactions. It is possible to separate charged molecules without precipitating either one.

Ionic strength describes the concentration of ionic species with weighting for the square of their charges. Electrolytes screen electrostatic interactions. At low to moderate salt concentrations, screening can weaken attractive interactions between protein molecules and sometimes increase solubility, a phenomenon called salting in. At sufficiently high concentrations of suitable salts, hydration and preferential interactions change so that the dissolved state of some proteins becomes less favorable. Proteins may then associate and separate as a precipitate. This is called salting out. The effect is protein dependent. Describing it as salt simply removing every molecule of water from a protein surface would miss the thermodynamic nature of the competition.

Ammonium sulfate is classically used for salting out because it is highly soluble and can often precipitate proteins without permanently destroying their folded state. Different proteins precipitate over different ranges of salt saturation, allowing sequential fractionation. A salt saturation percentage refers to the concentration relative to the solubility limit under defined conditions, not directly to grams of salt per 100 mL. The practical relationship depends on temperature and how salt is introduced. The conceptual prediction is that a protein remaining in solution at one salt level may precipitate at a higher one; it does not follow that every target is recovered at the highest level.

Preserving native activity requires more than maintaining solubility. The folded structure may be destabilized by inappropriate pH, heat, oxidation or interactions with proteases released during extraction. A soluble but unfolded enzyme may contribute to a protein concentration assay while producing little catalytic activity. Buffering reduces unwanted pH changes, and cold handling often slows degradative processes. Protective additives are selected for the target's chemistry. A chelating agent can limit metal-dependent proteolysis but also remove a required catalytic metal from a desired metalloenzyme. A reducing agent can protect accessible thiols yet disrupt an essential disulfide linkage or interfere with a subsequent assay.

Integral membrane proteins require particular attention. Their hydrophobic surfaces are normally surrounded by membrane lipids. Disrupting the membrane and removing the protein from that environment can expose those surfaces to water, promoting aggregation. Amphipathic detergents can surround the hydrophobic portions of the protein and present hydrophilic groups to water. A detergent that preserves the association or activity of one membrane protein may disrupt another. Strong ionic detergents such as SDS commonly denature proteins and are useful for specific analytical purposes rather than for recovering native enzyme activity. A soluble detergent–protein complex is not equivalent to a completely lipid-free native protein.

Protein precipitation is also possible through changes in pH, organic solvent composition or strongly denaturing conditions. A decrease in solvent polarity and changes in hydration can reduce solubility. A precipitated protein is not necessarily denatured: reversible precipitation and irreversible unfolding are distinct processes. Conversely, denaturation is not necessarily accompanied by visible precipitation. These distinctions explain why a clear supernatant and an insoluble pellet cannot be interpreted without examining both the molecular recovery and biological function.

Fractionation, desalting and the purification balance

In a fractionation scheme the target is followed through successive operations, rather than assuming that the most obvious pellet contains it. A clarified seed extract contains soluble proteins and potentially suspended or dissolved contaminants. An initial separation may remove insoluble cell wall material and large debris. The fractionation of the remaining soluble proteins by salt can create multiple precipitates and supernatants. A target enzyme may appear in an intermediate salt fraction while abundant storage proteins precipitate earlier or later. The separation depends on differential solubility and is rarely absolute.

A fraction that has been collected as a precipitate may require redissolution. If the goal is a native enzyme, the relevant evidence is recovery of catalytic activity after redissolution rather than merely the disappearance of visible solid. Removal of small solutes can then make the fraction compatible with further separation or assays. Dialysis separates species by diffusion across a semipermeable membrane. Molecules that pass through the membrane redistribute between the retained sample and the surrounding solution. Proteins larger than the effective cutoff are preferentially retained. A membrane's molecular-weight cutoff is an empirical retention characteristic, not a perfectly sharp boundary or a guarantee of recovery for every molecular shape.

In an ideal single dialysis exchange, a freely permeable small solute reaches equal concentrations inside and outside. If the initial inside volume is V and the outside volume is W, and the outside initially contains none of the solute, the fraction of the original small solute left inside is V divided by V plus W. This follows from conservation of solute and equality of final concentration. For a 10 mL sample equilibrated against 90 mL outside solution, one exchange leaves one tenth of the original small solute inside. Two ideal exchanges with fresh outside solution leave one hundredth. This model assumes unchanged volumes, complete equilibration, no membrane binding, no solute production and perfect permeability of the small solute. A protein cannot be assigned the same calculation if it is substantially retained by the membrane.

Ultrafiltration instead uses pressure-driven solvent movement across a membrane that preferentially retains macromolecules. The retained fraction may become more concentrated while small solutes pass into the filtrate. Concentration is distinct from desalting: simply reducing the retained volume need not produce the same reduction in small-solute concentration as repeated replacement of permeating solution. Binding to the membrane, aggregation near its surface and target passage are possible causes of loss. Choosing the operation therefore depends on whether the goal is concentration, buffer exchange or size-selective recovery.

An enzyme purification table should follow both total protein and total enzyme activity. Total protein is obtained by multiplying protein concentration in mg/mL by fraction volume in mL. Total activity is calculated in units of activity (U) from U/mL multiplied by volume. Specific activity is total activity divided by total protein, in U/mg. Under matched conditions this quantity often rises when the target enzyme becomes enriched relative to other proteins. Purification fold is specific activity of a fraction divided by specific activity of the initial extract; percentage activity yield is total activity of that fraction divided by initial total activity, multiplied by 100.

Suppose an illustrative initial extract contains 100 mL at 5 mg/mL protein and 20 U/mL activity. Total protein is 500 mg and total activity is 2000 U, giving a specific activity of 4 U/mg. After a fractionation step, 20 mL remains at 2 mg/mL protein and 60 U/mL activity. Total protein is now 40 mg and total activity is 1200 U. Specific activity is 30 U/mg, enrichment is 7.5-fold and activity yield is 60 percent. The second fraction has less total activity but higher activity per milligram of protein. Neither quantity should be interpreted as a direct percentage of chemical purity without independent knowledge of the specific activity of fully active pure enzyme.

A subsequent step may yield 8 mL at 1 mg/mL protein and 100 U/mL. It contains 8 mg protein and 800 U activity, making specific activity 100 U/mg, a 25-fold enrichment from the original extract and an overall activity yield of 40 percent. The choice of final fraction depends on the experiment. If sufficient active enzyme is required, the intermediate fraction could be preferable despite lower purity. If interfering proteins prevent a structural measurement, additional purification may be warranted. A rational purification does not maximize a single number without reference to the downstream objective.

Sometimes apparent activity yield exceeds 100 percent. This does not demonstrate spontaneous creation of enzyme mass. Removal of a reversible inhibitor, removal of interfering color or restoration of a required cofactor can increase the measured activity relative to the initial extract. Conversely a highly selective fraction may have poor activity yield because the target was inactivated during handling. Activity yield describes a measured functional response, not a conserved molecular count. Where actual mass recovery is the question, use an appropriate target-specific measurement alongside the activity assay.

Protein amount and catalytic activity

The absorption of ultraviolet light by a protein depends strongly on its aromatic residues, especially tryptophan and tyrosine, with a contribution from disulfide bonds. At 280 nm the absorbance of a pure protein can be related to concentration using its extinction coefficient. The Beer–Lambert relation is A = εlc, where A is dimensionless absorbance, ε is the molar absorption coefficient in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l is optical path length in cm and c is molar concentration. The equation assumes the relevant absorption coefficient is known, measurements are within the usable range and scattering and other absorbing species are negligible. The coefficient differs between proteins because their amino acid compositions differ.

For a protein with ε = 40,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and molecular mass 50,000 g/mol, an absorbance of 0.4 in a 1 cm path corresponds to 0.4 divided by 40,000, or 10 micromolar. Multiplying by molecular mass gives 0.50 g/L, equal to 0.50 mg/mL. If that measured solution was a fivefold dilution, the original sample concentration was 2.50 mg/mL. If nucleic acids or pigments also absorb at the measurement wavelength, the calculation overestimates protein concentration. A known extinction coefficient is most useful when the measured protein identity and sample purity are established.

Colorimetric protein assays generate a product or spectral change that can be measured with visible light. The biuret reaction depends on coordination of copper ions by peptide bonds under alkaline conditions. Because short peptides and proteins present these binding sites, the response is related to peptide content rather than requiring a particular enzyme function. The classical Lowry assay combines an alkaline copper-dependent reaction with reduction of the Folin reagent. Its sensitivity is useful for dilute protein samples, but the reaction is affected by protein composition and certain reducing compounds. Lowry and colleagues published the method in 1951.

The Bradford assay, introduced in 1976, uses Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250. Binding to proteins stabilizes a blue form of the dye measured near 595 nm. Basic residues, especially arginine, and hydrophobic interactions contribute to differences in response among proteins. A calibration constructed using bovine serum albumin provides an albumin-equivalent concentration unless the target protein has been shown to respond similarly. Detergents can cause background or interfere with dye binding. Bradford measurements are therefore not automatically accurate for samples containing detergent-solubilized membrane proteins.

The bicinchoninic acid or BCA assay relies on protein-mediated reduction of Cu(II) to Cu(I) in alkaline conditions, followed by formation of a colored Cu(I) complex with bicinchoninic acid, typically measured near 562 nm. Reducing agents can produce a response in the absence of protein. Chelating compounds may alter the availability of copper. An assay that is compatible with one additive is not necessarily compatible with all additives or all concentrations of them. Selection of a protein assay must therefore follow examination of the extraction buffer and the sample matrix.

An enzyme activity measurement asks how rapidly an enzyme catalyzes a specified reaction under specified conditions. One conventional unit is the amount catalyzing formation of one micromole of product per minute under the defined assay conditions. This unit is not interchangeable with milligrams of protein. A rate measured after substrate becomes depleted may underestimate initial activity. The rate may also vary with substrate concentration, pH, temperature, cofactors and inhibitors. Comparing fractions requires equivalent measurement conditions, ideally the initial linear phase and a validated proportional relationship between added enzyme and measured rate.

Suppose product accumulation is monitored at a wavelength where the product has an extinction coefficient of 10,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. In a 1 cm optical path the blank-corrected absorbance rises by 0.05 per minute. The concentration change per minute is 0.05 divided by 10,000 = 5 micromolar per minute. In a reaction volume of 2 mL, that is 0.010 micromole per minute, or 0.010 U in the reaction. If 0.020 mL of undiluted enzyme fraction supplied that activity, the fraction contains 0.50 U/mL. The calculation illustrates that the reaction volume and the enzyme volume are different quantities. Confusing the two can introduce a large systematic error.

A no-enzyme control checks nonenzymatic product formation, while a no-substrate control helps identify background changes associated with the enzyme preparation. An inactive or heat-treated control may be informative where heating does not itself introduce major interference. A positive control with known enzyme activity establishes that the assay system can function. If an extract has measurable protein but no observed activity, these controls help distinguish true absence or inactivation of enzyme from failure of the measurement system.

Standards, standard curves and uncertainty

A standard curve links a measurable signal to known amounts of an analyte. Its usefulness depends on using standards that represent the chemistry of the unknown samples. A reagent blank contains the analytical reagents without the analyte and estimates their background signal. A matrix blank may additionally represent the non-analyte constituents of the sample. When these constituents change the slope of the response rather than merely the baseline, subtracting a blank cannot fully correct the error.

Within an experimentally established linear range the response may be represented by y = mx + b, where y is the measured signal, x is concentration or amount, m is the sensitivity and b is the intercept. The unknown x is obtained by subtracting the intercept and dividing by the slope. The units of m depend on the units of x. A calibration may use mass per assay tube rather than concentration in the original extract; the sample volume and dilution must then be used to convert the result.

Consider an illustrative set of standards containing 0, 20, 40, 60 and 80 micrograms of protein per assay, with absorbances of 0.04, 0.16, 0.28, 0.40 and 0.52. The slope is 0.006 absorbance units per microgram and the intercept is 0.04. An unknown giving absorbance 0.34 contains (0.34 − 0.04)/0.006 = 50 micrograms in the aliquot. If the aliquot was 0.10 mL of a fourfold dilution, the diluted concentration is 500 micrograms per millilitre, and the original concentration is 2 mg/mL. If the reagent blank was already subtracted before fitting the standards, it must not be subtracted a second time from the unknown.

A response outside the calibrated interval is not made reliable merely by inserting it into the equation. At high analyte levels, a colorimetric reagent may become limiting or its response may depart from linearity. At very low levels, uncertainty in blank subtraction may dominate. Diluting a high-concentration sample and correcting by the known dilution factor can bring it into range. A nonlinear calibration may also be valid if appropriately established, but a curve must not be extended beyond the evidence supplied by the standards.

A useful diagnostic is dilution agreement. If independent dilutions of the same extract produce substantially different back-calculated concentrations, the response may be nonlinear, the matrix may interfere, or the dilution process may have introduced a technical error. Agreement does not prove that the method is specific: an interfering compound that dilutes and responds in the same way as the analyte can remain undetected. This is why comparison of independent analytical principles is often stronger than repetition of one principle.

Spike recovery examines the response to a known addition. The difference between measured amounts in the spiked and unspiked samples is divided by the added amount. Suppose an extract gives 30 micrograms before a 20 microgram spike and 46 micrograms after it. Apparent spike recovery is (46 − 30)/20 × 100 = 80 percent. Suppression of signal or loss of the added material is possible. A spike introduced after extraction evaluates the measurement matrix, but cannot assess target loss during the extraction itself. A spike introduced before extraction can assess more of the process, provided it actually behaves like the endogenous target. A free added standard may not mimic a molecule bound within the original tissue structure.

Carbohydrate extraction and separation

Carbohydrates span freely soluble monosaccharides, soluble oligosaccharides, storage polymers and relatively insoluble structural polysaccharides. Their hydroxyl groups interact with water, but the way these groups are presented in a polymer determines whether they are accessible. Starch contains amylose and branched amylopectin organized in granules. Cellulose chains form associated structures stabilized by hydrogen bonding and other intermolecular interactions. Neither can be treated simply as a solution of glucose monomers. Extraction conditions that recover small sugars do not necessarily recover all carbohydrate in a plant sample.

For a seed, a useful analytical distinction is between a soluble sugar fraction and a polymer-rich residue. Polar solvents can recover many soluble sugars while leaving much of the insoluble storage and structural material behind. Alcohol-containing extraction systems are often used to separate soluble sugars from certain higher molecular weight carbohydrates. However, the selectivity depends on solvent composition, temperature and the structure of the carbohydrate. A polysaccharide may be soluble in water yet precipitate when the solvent environment changes. Ethanol precipitation is often used to enrich polymers from aqueous extracts, but it can also collect other macromolecules or salts. It is not a specific test for a particular polysaccharide.

Hydrolysis cleaves glycosidic bonds and converts a carbohydrate into smaller units. Enzymatic hydrolysis can be selective for certain linkages, whereas acid-mediated hydrolysis may act more broadly and can also degrade released sugars. A measurement of glucose after starch hydrolysis is indirect evidence of starch only if alternative sources of glucose and incomplete hydrolysis are controlled. A mixture of polysaccharides containing glucose cannot be distinguished by a final glucose measurement alone. The molecular form must be inferred from the selectivity of the treatment and supporting separation data.

A glycosidic linkage forms when the anomeric carbon of one sugar is linked to another group. In many carbohydrates a free anomeric center can equilibrate with an open-chain form capable of participating in reducing-sugar reactions. Glucose and maltose are reducing sugars, while intact sucrose is nonreducing because its two anomeric centers participate in the glycosidic linkage. Hydrolysis of sucrose produces glucose and fructose, which can give reducing-sugar responses. Fructose is a ketose but can behave as a reducing sugar in alkaline assays through rearrangements. The term reducing sugar describes a chemical reaction under the assay conditions, not simply the presence of an aldehyde in a static structural formula.

A long linear polysaccharide may have only one reducing end per chain, although it contains many sugar residues. Branching produces additional nonreducing ends without necessarily producing additional reducing ends in a connected molecule. An assay responding to reducing ends therefore measures a different property from one responding to total carbohydrate. Hydrolysis increases the number of separate reducing molecules and can greatly increase the reducing-sugar response even when the carbohydrate mass has not changed substantially. This distinction is especially useful when monitoring digestion of a carbohydrate polymer.

Separation of small sugars may exploit differential partition between phases or differential interaction with a stationary phase. A chromatographic spot or peak is compared with authentic standards under matched conditions, but identical migration alone does not prove molecular identity because different sugars can co-migrate. A method that separates intact polysaccharides by size provides information about molecular size distribution, not automatically about monomer composition or linkage position. Conversely, a chromatogram of hydrolyzed monomers can reveal composition while losing the original sequence of residues. An analytical strategy should preserve the molecular information required for the intended conclusion.

Carbohydrate assays: total sugar versus reducing sugar

The phenol–sulfuric acid method developed by DuBois and colleagues in 1956 is a widely recognized broad carbohydrate assay. Under acidic conditions sugars yield dehydration products; pentoses can yield furfural, and hexoses can yield hydroxymethylfurfural. Reaction with phenol produces colored products. The resulting response can be calibrated against a selected carbohydrate standard. The method can respond to many carbohydrate forms, including sugars released from certain polymers during treatment. It does not identify the original carbohydrate, and different monosaccharides can produce different responses per unit mass. Reporting glucose-equivalent carbohydrate is often more defensible than claiming an exact mass of an unknown mixed carbohydrate population.

The anthrone assay similarly relies on acid-mediated carbohydrate dehydration followed by formation of colored products with anthrone. Color development depends on the analyte, reaction conditions and matrix. Both broad colorimetric methods are destructive measurements and are not tests of the intact biological function of a carbohydrate. They can be useful for comparing extracts of similar composition if validated against appropriate standards, but their numerical results need not agree exactly for samples that differ strongly in sugar composition.

Reducing-sugar assays distinguish compounds able to participate in suitable reduction reactions. In a classical copper-based test reducing sugars can reduce Cu(II) to Cu(I) under alkaline conditions. The dinitrosalicylic acid method gives a color response to reducing sugars under its reaction conditions. Neither response is uniquely specific to glucose. Other reducing compounds can interfere, and the response per mole of sugar varies. For example, if a polymer is partially hydrolyzed to a mixture of oligosaccharides and monosaccharides, the reducing-sugar signal may increase without a corresponding increase in the total carbohydrate signal.

The iodine test provides a useful contrast. Iodine-containing species associate with ordered helical regions of amylose, producing a characteristic strong color. Amylopectin and shorter chain fragments can show different colors or responses because their architecture differs. The assay thus depends on molecular organization, not just the amount of glucose residues. During starch digestion the iodine response can decline as long helical chains are disrupted, while the reducing-sugar response rises. The opposing trends are compatible: one method reports a structural feature and the other reports newly generated reducing molecules.

A glucose-specific enzymatic method can use glucose oxidase to oxidize glucose, with hydrogen peroxide formation linked to a detection reaction, frequently involving peroxidase and a chromogenic substrate. Under validated conditions the response provides more specific evidence of glucose than a broad reducing-sugar assay. However, an upstream hydrolysis or enzymatic digestion may produce glucose from several different sources. Specificity of the detector does not automatically confer specificity on the extraction or sample conversion stage. The biological interpretation still depends on what was present before treatment.

When a polymer is hydrolyzed to monomers, water is incorporated across the cleaved linkages. For a large glucose polymer approximated by anhydroglucose repeat units of molar mass 162 g/mol, complete hydrolysis can produce glucose of molar mass 180 g/mol per repeat unit. If a selective, complete starch hydrolysis yields 100 mg glucose after subtraction of pre-existing glucose, the corresponding dry starch mass is approximately 100 × 162/180 = 90 mg. The correction neglects small end-group effects and assumes the source is exclusively starch. Reporting 100 mg starch would confuse hydrolysis product mass with polymer mass.

One useful biological experiment compares starch and soluble sugar across stages of seed germination. If the total starch estimate declines while soluble sugar concentration rises, mobilization is a plausible explanation. Yet concentration may also rise because of changes in water content or extraction recovery. Measurements normalized to dry mass, with independently sampled seeds and consistent extraction and assay conditions, give a more interpretable comparison. Even these measurements do not by themselves establish which enzyme caused the change; enzyme activity or other mechanistic evidence is needed.

Lipid extraction and phase selectivity

Lipids differ from proteins and carbohydrates because many of their defining interactions arise from long hydrophobic regions. Triacylglycerols consist of glycerol esterified to three fatty acids and are neutral storage lipids. Phospholipids possess a polar or charged head group as well as hydrophobic chains and are amphipathic. Sterols contain a rigid ring system with a small polar region, and free fatty acids can change their ionization with pH. These differences are important because solvent systems do not recover every lipid class with equal efficiency.

A neutral lipid is poorly compatible with bulk water. An organic solvent with appropriate properties can provide a more favorable environment for its nonpolar surface. Amphipathic lipids present a more complex problem: their head groups interact with water, ions and other membrane components while their hydrophobic tails associate with other lipid chains. Mixed solvent systems can disrupt lipid–protein associations and permit recovery of both neutral and many polar lipids. The exact composition of the mixture and the water content of the sample influence which molecules partition into each phase.

The classical Folch method, published by Folch, Lees and Sloane Stanley in 1957, established a solvent-based approach to extraction of total lipids from animal tissues. The related Bligh and Dyer approach was developed for lipid extraction and purification using a mixed solvent system. These historical methods illustrate the use of solvent composition and phase behavior to separate lipids from hydrophilic material. In the classical chloroform-rich biphasic systems the lipid-rich organic phase is generally the denser lower phase. This is not a universal rule for organic solvents; many other organic solvent phases are less dense than water. The phase must be identified by its composition, not by memorizing upper or lower.

The equilibrium distribution of an analyte between two immiscible phases can be represented by a distribution ratio D = Corganic/Caqueous for the total analytical concentration under specified conditions. If the organic phase has volume Vo and the aqueous phase has volume Va, conservation of analyte gives the fraction in the organic phase as DVo/(DVo + Va). This relationship assumes equilibrium, constant phase volumes, no degradation, no adsorption and a constant distribution ratio. If a substance can ionize, D may depend strongly on pH because the charged and uncharged forms often partition differently.

For example, with D = 4 and equal organic and aqueous volumes, the ideal fraction in the organic phase is four fifths, or 80 percent. If the same total organic volume is divided into two equal fresh portions, and the aqueous volume stays constant, each extraction leaves one third of the starting aqueous amount for that step. Two extractions leave one ninth, so the combined ideal recovery is eight ninths, or about 88.9 percent. The comparison demonstrates why serial partition can improve recovery under the model assumptions. Real tissues can show emulsions, interfacial binding, solvent retention and incomplete equilibration, reducing the usefulness of the ideal calculation.

Free fatty acids illustrate pH-dependent partition. Their carboxyl group can dissociate into a negatively charged carboxylate. The charged form tends to be more compatible with polar surroundings than the uncharged form, although the hydrocarbon chain remains hydrophobic and association with other molecules complicates the distribution. Changing pH may therefore change the proportion of free fatty acid recovered in an organic fraction. Neutral triacylglycerols do not exhibit the same direct carboxyl ionization behavior because their fatty acid carboxyl groups are esterified. An extraction optimized for neutral lipids can underestimate more polar or ionizable lipid species.

Lipid oxidation and hydrolysis can change the sample before analysis. Unsaturated chains are susceptible to oxidation, generating products that may partition or react differently. Lipases released during disruption can hydrolyze ester bonds and alter the relative amounts of free fatty acids and intact lipid molecules. Total solvent-extractable mass can remain similar even while the molecular profile changes markedly. When the goal is to compare lipid species, preservation of composition is at least as important as recovery of total mass.

Lipid analysis from total mass to molecular composition

A gravimetric lipid determination estimates the mass of recovered extract after solvent removal. The measured residue can include nonlipid compounds that partition into the same solvent, residual solvent or other contaminants. Some lipid classes may have been incompletely recovered. The result therefore represents an operationally defined extractable fraction under specified conditions. It is not automatically the exact total lipid mass in the original sample. A procedural blank and stable mass measurements help evaluate non-analyte contributions.

Consider a dry seed sample of 2.00 g that yields 0.32 g of dried extract. If an equivalent process blank contributes 0.02 g, the blank-corrected extract is 0.30 g and the apparent extractable lipid percentage is 0.30/2.00 × 100 = 15 percent of dry mass. If the original seed sample contained 50 percent water, the wet-mass percentage for the same material would be 7.5 percent. The normalization basis must be stated. Comparisons between tissues on different mass bases can suggest biological differences that arise entirely from water content.

Lipid-soluble dyes provide visible or fluorescent evidence of hydrophobic compartments or extracts. Sudan dyes and Oil Red O preferentially partition into lipid-rich material; Nile red fluorescence depends on the polarity of its environment. Staining intensity can depend on dye accessibility and lipid class as well as the amount present. A strong stain demonstrates enrichment of a suitable hydrophobic environment, not an exact mass of triacylglycerol. Appropriate calibration and consistent sample preparation are needed for quantitative use.

Class-selective lipid assays exploit distinctive molecular features. Phospholipid phosphorus analysis can estimate the phosphorus associated with an appropriately isolated phospholipid fraction, but free phosphate or other phosphorus-containing compounds can interfere. Not all phospholipid classes have identical phosphorus-to-mass relationships, and phosphorus assays cannot quantify neutral triacylglycerols. Enzymatic cholesterol assays use reactions specific to cholesterol under defined conditions; an additional ester hydrolysis step can make esterified cholesterol available to a subsequent detector. A total cholesterol result does not establish the concentration of every sterol or every lipid class.

Thin-layer chromatography separates lipid components according to their differential interactions with the stationary and mobile phases. On a normal-phase silica system polar lipid head groups often interact more strongly with the polar stationary phase than neutral lipids. Migration depends on the solvent and adsorbent, so the relative order cannot be generalized to every chromatographic arrangement. Comparison with authentic lipid class standards on the same plate supports tentative class assignment. A spot can still contain multiple molecular species. The retention factor Rf is the distance traveled by the spot center divided by the distance traveled by the solvent front, measured from the same origin.

Gas chromatography can be used to compare fatty acids after conversion into suitable volatile derivatives, commonly fatty acid methyl esters. The resulting chromatogram gives evidence of fatty acid composition, not the original intact lipid molecular species. For example, detecting palmitate and oleate does not show whether they occurred together in the same triacylglycerol molecule or separately in membrane phospholipids. Derivatization efficiency, sample losses, detector response and peak overlap affect the accuracy of the reported composition. Authentic reference compounds and appropriately chosen internal standards strengthen interpretation.

Liquid chromatography can separate intact lipid species or lipid classes without the same requirement for volatility. Mass spectrometric analysis supplies mass-related evidence and, where fragmentation is used, further structural clues. Isomers may share an exact mass, and some structural distinctions require additional separation or informative fragment evidence. Signal intensity is not always proportional to lipid concentration across different classes because ionization and matrix effects vary. The comprehensive instrumental principles belong to the later chromatography and biophysical nodes; at this stage the key point is that a lipid assay is defined by which molecular information its signal retains.

Acid value, saponification value and iodine value are classical chemical indices used to characterize lipid preparations. Acid value describes the amount of alkali required to neutralize free acids in a gram of sample, conventionally expressed as mg KOH/g. Saponification value includes alkali consumption associated with ester hydrolysis and free acid neutralization; for comparable neutral triacylglycerols, shorter average chains correspond to more ester groups per gram and hence a higher value. Iodine value measures the amount of iodine-equivalent reagent consumed by unsaturation, conventionally grams of iodine per 100 g lipid. These values are aggregate chemical properties, not complete molecular identities. A mixture of different lipid species can have the same iodine value as another mixture with a different composition.

Choosing evidence and testing alternative explanations

A biochemical measurement is most informative when the question, extraction operation and analytical signal are matched. Suppose a researcher compares three seed samples for a storage protein, starch and neutral oil. A broad protein assay cannot establish the amount of the named storage protein; it measures a protein-equivalent response. A starch estimate based on released glucose must distinguish starch from free glucose and other glucose polymers. A solvent-extractable mass may include pigments or other nonlipid compounds. The three results may each be useful, but they must be stated according to what the method measures.

A target-specific analytical method and a broad class assay can complement one another. An enzyme activity assay can track an active protein through fractionation, while a total protein assay gives the denominator for specific activity. A glucose-specific detection reaction after selective starch digestion can estimate starch, while a broad carbohydrate assay measures a wider carbohydrate fraction. A lipid class separation can show which categories are present, while a gravimetric measurement estimates recovered extractable mass. Agreement between two methods that rely on different physical principles increases confidence if their expected relationship is correctly defined.

Consider an illustrative comparison of two extraction procedures for a carbohydrate-rich sample. The first recovers 80 mg total measured carbohydrate from a reference input of 100 mg and the second recovers 60 mg. If the first fraction contains 40 mg of the desired carbohydrate and the second contains 48 mg, their target mass fractions are 50 percent and 80 percent, respectively. The second gives less overall carbohydrate but more target material and better selectivity. The best method cannot be selected merely from the largest recovered bulk amount. One must specify whether the desired outcome is total recovery, target recovery, target enrichment, or preservation of a particular structural property.

Extraction efficiency is difficult to infer when the amount present in the original biological material is unknown. An independently characterized reference material can test method performance. Comparison of successive fractions may reveal residual target. A known spike added before extraction can be helpful but may not reproduce the association of endogenous molecules with tissue components. A spike added after extraction diagnoses downstream analytical effects rather than release from tissue. In an examination, identify exactly which stage each control interrogates instead of treating recovery and accuracy as synonyms.

The role of normalization is equally important. A rate per milligram of extracted protein answers a different question from a rate per gram of original tissue. An extraction that removes abundant inactive protein can raise the first while decreasing the second. For a biological comparison of enzyme abundance or physiological capacity, the chosen denominator must match the claim. For purification tracking, specific activity may be useful because contaminating proteins contribute to the denominator. Both measurements can be reported when they address complementary questions.

Finally, uncertainty cannot be replaced with precision in numerical formatting. An absorbance read to three decimal places does not imply that sample extraction, calibration and biological sampling are equally precise. A table with measured replicates should distinguish a reproducible signal from a validated estimate of the analyte. Independent checks of matrix interference, selectivity and extraction recovery are necessary before assigning a biological meaning to small differences. The next node develops centrifugation in greater detail, providing a physical explanation for the separation of suspended biological material and the interpretation of sedimented fractions.

Original examination practice

Part B 1. At a pH well above its isoelectric point, a typical protein has which average net charge?

  1. Negative
  2. Positive
  3. Zero at all pH values
  4. Unrelated to protonation
Answer and explanation

A. Deprotonation generally increases negative net charge above pI. The isoelectric point is where the mean net charge is zero, not where all ionizable groups vanish.

Part B 2. Which property is most directly exploited by ammonium sulfate fractionation?

  1. Sequence-specific recognition
  2. Differential protein solubility with salt concentration
  3. Different peptide bond lengths
  4. Different absorption wavelengths
Answer and explanation

B. Salt changes the solvent environment and protein association. Fractionation exploits the different precipitation ranges of proteins; it is not a sequence-specific assay.

Part B 3. What is retained preferentially during conventional dialysis of a large soluble protein against buffer?

  1. All inorganic ions
  2. Water exclusively
  3. The sufficiently large protein
  4. Every small molecule bound or unbound
Answer and explanation

C. The membrane preferentially retains the large protein while permeable small solutes redistribute. Bound small molecules may behave differently from freely diffusible species.

Part B 4. Which is the most appropriate measure for following a purified enzyme relative to contaminating proteins?

  1. Volume
  2. Total protein concentration alone
  3. Absorbance at an arbitrary wavelength
  4. Specific activity
Answer and explanation

D. Specific activity expresses enzyme activity per unit total protein mass. Volume or bulk concentration cannot establish enzyme enrichment.

Part B 5. Which structural feature most strongly contributes to protein absorption at 280 nm?

  1. Aromatic amino acid side chains
  2. All glycosidic bonds
  3. Phosphate groups alone
  4. Aliphatic side chains equally
Answer and explanation

A. Tryptophan and tyrosine contribute strongly, with a smaller contribution from disulfides. The response therefore differs among proteins.

Part B 6. The biuret test depends principally on interaction of copper with which feature?

  1. Sterol ring
  2. Peptide bonds
  3. Free glucose anomeric center
  4. Fatty acid double bonds
Answer and explanation

B. Peptide bonds coordinate copper under alkaline conditions. The test is not a specific measurement of lipid, glucose or catalytic activity.

Part B 7. Which dye is used in the Bradford protein assay?

  1. Sudan III
  2. Iodine
  3. Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250
  4. Nile red
Answer and explanation

C. The Bradford method uses Coomassie dye binding and measures the blue form. The other reagents are typically used for lipid or starch-related detection.

Part B 8. BCA color formation depends on a complex with which metal species?

  1. Ferric iron
  2. Calcium
  3. Sodium
  4. Cu(I)
Answer and explanation

D. Protein-associated copper reduction generates Cu(I), which complexes with BCA. Other reductants may also generate Cu(I) and interfere.

Part B 9. Which preparation most directly tests reagent-derived absorbance?

  1. Reagent blank without analyte
  2. An unrelated tissue replicate
  3. A pure target with a high concentration
  4. A pooled positive control only
Answer and explanation

A. The blank represents reagent background. A positive standard measures response to known analyte, not the signal in its absence.

Part B 10. Which is a nonreducing disaccharide before hydrolysis?

  1. Maltose
  2. Sucrose
  3. Lactose
  4. Cellobiose
Answer and explanation

B. Both anomeric centers of sucrose are involved in its linkage. The other listed disaccharides have a free reducing end.

Part B 11. What does the phenol–sulfuric acid method primarily estimate?

  1. Enzyme catalytic rate
  2. Protein sequence
  3. Broad carbohydrate-equivalent content
  4. Intact lipid acyl positions
Answer and explanation

C. The acid-dependent formation of sugar-derived chromogens gives a broad carbohydrate response. It does not identify every molecular species.

Part B 12. Iodine staining of starch chiefly reports which property?

  1. Total peptide bonds
  2. All glucose molecules regardless of structure
  3. Fatty acid unsaturation
  4. Association with ordered glucan helices
Answer and explanation

D. Iodine-containing species associate with helical glucan structures, particularly amylose. Free glucose does not reproduce the characteristic amylose response.

Part B 13. Which method most specifically detects glucose under appropriately validated conditions?

  1. Glucose oxidase-based assay
  2. Bradford assay
  3. Gravimetric lipid extraction
  4. Biuret assay
Answer and explanation

A. Glucose oxidase-based detection is more chemically selective for glucose than broad carbohydrate colorimetry. The other assays measure unrelated properties.

Part B 14. Which lipid is normally neutral and mainly serves energy storage?

  1. Phosphatidylserine
  2. Triacylglycerol
  3. Ionized free fatty acid
  4. Phosphatidylinositol
Answer and explanation

B. Triacylglycerols are neutral glycerol triesters. The other options are ionizable or polar lipid forms.

Part B 15. What distinguishes an amphipathic lipid?

  1. Absence of all covalent bonds
  2. Exclusive solubility in water
  3. Hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions
  4. A peptide backbone
Answer and explanation

C. Amphipathic molecules have chemically distinct regions, explaining their association with membranes and their solvent-dependent extraction.

Part B 16. Which is the usual lipid-enriched phase in the classical chloroform-rich biphasic extraction?

  1. Always the upper layer in every solvent system
  2. A solid salt pellet
  3. Only the aqueous phase
  4. The denser lower organic-rich layer
Answer and explanation

D. Chloroform-rich organic mixtures are usually denser than the aqueous phase. Other solvents can reverse the layer order.

Part B 17. What is the principal output of gravimetric lipid analysis?

  1. Recovered extract mass
  2. Exact fatty acid arrangement
  3. Complete lipid identity
  4. Catalytic turnover
Answer and explanation

A. Gravimetry gives mass of the recovered residue. Co-extracted material or missing lipid classes limit interpretation as total biological lipid.

Part B 18. A fatty acid methyl ester chromatogram chiefly informs which property?

  1. Original association of all intact lipids
  2. Fatty acid composition
  3. Protein secondary structure
  4. Polysaccharide branch length
Answer and explanation

B. Derivatized fatty acids retain chain-related information but generally lose the original parent-lipid association.

Part B 19. Iodine value is a classical aggregate index of which lipid property?

  1. Protein contamination only
  2. Free glucose
  3. Unsaturation
  4. Nucleic acid integrity
Answer and explanation

C. Iodine value reflects reaction with unsaturated bonds, not complete structural identification or the amount of protein.

Part B 20. Which is a biological replicate in a seed experiment?

  1. A second reading from the same tube
  2. A duplicate aliquot of one extract
  3. A repeat calculation
  4. An independently sampled seed
Answer and explanation

D. Independent biological samples capture biological variation; duplicate readings or aliquots primarily capture technical variation.

Part C 1. An extract is 40 mL at 3 mg/mL. A later fraction is 10 mL at 6 mg/mL. What is total protein recovery?

  1. 200%
  2. 50%
  3. 25%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

B. Initial protein = 120 mg and final protein = 60 mg; recovery is 60/120 × 100 = 50%. Concentration alone doubled but total mass halved.

Part C 2. A crude fraction contains 600 U and 120 mg protein. A purified fraction contains 240 U and 12 mg protein. What are fold purification and activity yield?

  1. 2-fold, 20%
  2. 5-fold, 40%
  3. 4-fold, 40%
  4. 4-fold, 80%
Answer and explanation

C. Specific activities are 5 and 20 U/mg, so fold = 4. Activity yield is 240/600 = 40%; protein mass yield is a separate quantity.

Part C 3. A pure protein with ε = 50,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ gives A280 = 0.25 at 1 cm. Molecular mass is 40,000 g/mol. What is concentration?

  1. 0.02 mg/mL
  2. 2 mg/mL
  3. 5 mg/mL
  4. 0.20 mg/mL
Answer and explanation

D. c = 0.25/50,000 = 5 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L. Multiply by 40,000 g/mol to obtain 0.20 g/L = 0.20 mg/mL.

Part C 4. A standard curve is A = 0.01m + 0.03 with m in µg. A diluted sample gives A = 0.23 for 0.2 mL. Dilution factor is 5. Original concentration?

  1. 0.50 mg/mL
  2. 0.10 mg/mL
  3. 1.00 mg/mL
  4. 5.00 mg/mL
Answer and explanation

A. m = (0.23−0.03)/0.01 = 20 µg. Diluted concentration = 100 µg/mL, then multiply by 5 = 500 µg/mL.

Part C 5. Dialysis of 5 mL against 95 mL of fresh solution is repeated twice, assuming ideal equilibrium. What fraction of freely permeable solute remains?

  1. 5%
  2. 0.25%
  3. 10%
  4. 95%
Answer and explanation

B. Each exchange retains 5/100 = 0.05 of remaining solute. Two exchanges retain 0.0025, or 0.25%.

Part C 6. A membrane enzyme gives normal protein mass after detergent extraction but loses activity. Which inference is strongest?

  1. The enzyme is absent
  2. All protein was hydrolyzed
  3. Protein recovery and functional preservation are distinct
  4. Detergent always improves function
Answer and explanation

C. The bulk assay can detect inactive protein. Loss of activity could reflect denaturation, disruption of a cofactor or other assay effects, and warrants controls.

Part C 7. After removal of small solutes, measured activity rises from 100 U to 140 U without increase in target mass. What best explains this?

  1. The law of mass conservation fails
  2. More enzyme must have been synthesized
  3. Protein concentration must double
  4. Removal of a reversible inhibitor is plausible
Answer and explanation

D. An inhibitor can suppress the initial rate. An apparent activity yield above 100% need not represent production of enzyme molecules.

Part C 8. A protein assay gives 25 µg for an unspiked sample and 39 µg after addition of 20 µg standard. Spike recovery?

  1. 70%
  2. 56%
  3. 95%
  4. 195%
Answer and explanation

A. The spike contributes a measured increment of 14 µg. Divide by the known 20 µg addition to get 70%, indicating possible matrix suppression or loss.

Part C 9. A reagent blank is already subtracted in both the standards and unknowns. The analyst subtracts it again from unknowns only. Likely effect?

  1. No effect
  2. Negative bias
  3. Positive bias necessarily
  4. Higher true protein amount
Answer and explanation

B. The unknown signal is artificially decreased relative to the curve. Correcting for the same blank twice creates a systematic underestimate.

Part C 10. Two fivefold and tenfold dilutions give calculated original concentrations of 1.2 and 1.9 mg/mL. What should be done?

  1. Choose the larger value automatically
  2. Average without investigation
  3. Check linearity, dilution and matrix effects
  4. Report both as pure target concentration
Answer and explanation

C. Back-calculated concentration should agree within method uncertainty. The disagreement suggests a validation problem, not a reason to select a preferred number.

Part C 11. A starch digest yields 180 mg glucose after free-glucose correction. Assuming exclusive and complete starch hydrolysis, approximate original dry polymer mass?

  1. 180 mg
  2. 200 mg
  3. 90 mg
  4. 162 mg
Answer and explanation

D. Each anhydroglucose residue has molar mass about 162 compared with 180 for glucose. Multiply 180 by 162/180 = 162 mg.

Part C 12. During controlled starch hydrolysis the iodine response decreases while reducing sugar response increases. Best explanation?

  1. Helical chains break into smaller reducing molecules
  2. All carbohydrate has disappeared
  3. The iodine method directly measures protein
  4. Glucose polymerization has increased
Answer and explanation

A. Fragmentation reduces ordered helical regions while creating more reducing ends. The two assays measure different properties.

Part C 13. Two equally concentrated polysaccharides give distinct phenol–sulfuric acid signals. Which explanation is defensible?

  1. The less colored polymer is necessarily absent
  2. Their constituent sugars can have different color response factors
  3. All polysaccharides have identical molar response
  4. The more colored sample contains only glucose
Answer and explanation

B. The response depends on monosaccharide composition and treatment chemistry. Equal mass need not give equal chromogenic response.

Part C 14. A post-extraction carbohydrate spike is fully recovered, yet a reference tissue gives low carbohydrate yield. Which step may still fail?

  1. Only detector calibration, never extraction
  2. The post-spike mass itself
  3. Release of endogenous carbohydrate during extraction
  4. All subsequent analysis is proven correct
Answer and explanation

C. Post-extraction spiking bypasses the extraction process. It cannot demonstrate that matrix-bound endogenous material was quantitatively released.

Part C 15. D = 3 for a lipid between equal organic and aqueous volumes. Ideal one-pass recovery?

  1. 25%
  2. 33%
  3. 50%
  4. 75%
Answer and explanation

D. Recovery = DVo/(DVo+Va) = 3/(3+1) = 0.75 under the stated equilibrium assumptions.

Part C 16. With D = 2 and aqueous volume 100 mL, two successive 50 mL fresh organic extractions leave what fraction in water?

  1. 25%
  2. 50%
  3. 75%
  4. 12.5%
Answer and explanation

A. Each step leaves 100/(100+2×50) = 1/2. Two independent equilibrium steps leave one quarter.

Part C 17. A 4 g wet tissue contains 75% water and yields 0.15 g blank-corrected lipid. Apparent lipid percentage of dry mass?

  1. 3.75%
  2. 15%
  3. 20%
  4. 60%
Answer and explanation

B. Dry mass = 1 g; 0.15/1 × 100 = 15%. The wet mass percentage is 3.75%, a different denominator.

Part C 18. A lipid class TLC spot travels 3.2 cm and solvent front travels 8 cm from origin. What is Rf?

  1. 2.5
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.40
  4. 4.0
Answer and explanation

C. Rf = 3.2/8 = 0.40, dimensionless. It is not a molecular weight or proof of unique identity.

Part C 19. Two extracts show equal total fatty acid composition by GC, but different intact lipid profiles. Is this contradictory?

  1. Yes, GC uniquely defines every parent molecule
  2. Yes, equal fatty acids force equal lipid classes
  3. Yes, lipid extraction cannot vary
  4. No, parent molecular associations can differ
Answer and explanation

D. Fatty acid derivatization removes information about which acyl groups occurred on which parent lipid. The same aggregate composition can arise from distinct intact species.

Part C 20. Method X recovers 50 mg target in 100 mg total material; method Y recovers 45 mg target in 50 mg total. Which maximizes target fraction?

  1. Y, at 90% target mass fraction
  2. X, at 90%
  3. X and Y equally
  4. Cannot compare mass fractions
Answer and explanation

A. X has 50% target and Y has 90%. X recovers more absolute target mass but Y is more selective.

Part C 21. An oil sample consumes more KOH per gram during saponification than another sample of comparable neutral triacylglycerols. Which is a plausible interpretation?

  1. Longer average chains
  2. Shorter average chains
  3. Lower number of ester groups per gram
  4. Identical average mass necessarily
Answer and explanation

B. Shorter average chains reduce the molar mass per triester, increasing ester equivalents per gram. Free acids and other constituents must be controlled.

Part C 22. Two protein assays disagree only when a reducing additive is present. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

  1. Both necessarily measure exact target protein
  2. The additive must increase protein synthesis
  3. Copper-based assay interference is plausible
  4. The original protein has no peptide bonds
Answer and explanation

C. Reducing additives can alter copper-based assay response. Matched buffer controls and a compatible independent method can diagnose this.

Answer and explanation

A. Reversible salting out can preserve the native fold. Precipitation and denaturation describe distinct phenomena.

  2. Both true; reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

B. Glucose and fructose produced by sucrose hydrolysis can participate in reducing-sugar reactions under the assay conditions.

  1. Wet mass and color only
  2. Reagent blank and sample volume only
  3. Total target mass and target enzyme activity
  4. Total lipid and total carbohydrate only
Answer and explanation

C. Target mass measures recovery; enzyme activity tests functional preservation. Bulk protein alone is less informative than a target-specific measurement.

  1. Biological variation between seeds
  2. The true lipid content of a tissue
  3. All incomplete extraction
  4. Residue contributed by processing in absence of sample
Answer and explanation

D. The procedural blank estimates contamination or residual material from the process. It does not reproduce lipid loss from a real tissue.

SET and integrated reasoning 5. Which statement best describes an internal standard for lipid analysis?

  1. A known distinguishable compound used to track response or recovery
  2. A guaranteed identical response for every lipid class
  3. An unknown endogenous peak used without validation
  4. A substitute for independent biological samples
Answer and explanation

A. An appropriate standard tracks specified sources of analytical variation. Its ability to correct recovery depends on timing and similarity to the analyte.

SET and integrated reasoning 6. A broad protein assay estimates a BSA-equivalent concentration. What should be reported when the unknown protein response factor is not established?

  1. Exact sequence-specific mass
  2. BSA-equivalent concentration with its limitation
  3. The concentration of only active enzyme
  4. An absolute purity percentage
Answer and explanation

B. The calibration defines the operational concentration. Absolute mass is not established if the unknown responds differently from BSA.

SET and integrated reasoning 7. Which observation most supports selective starch digestion rather than general hydrolysis?

  1. A rise in arbitrary absorbance
  2. A decrease in volume only
  3. Glucose release with appropriate starch-positive and non-starch controls
  4. Complete loss of all sample mass
Answer and explanation

C. Selectivity requires evidence that the treatment releases glucose from starch and does not produce a comparable signal from relevant non-target material.

SET and integrated reasoning 8. For extraction validation, which design best evaluates both biological variation and technical reproducibility?

  1. One seed measured once
  2. One seed measured ten times only
  3. Ten calculations of one reading
  4. Independent seeds with replicate measurements of each extract
Answer and explanation

D. Independent samples estimate biological variation, while replicate assays estimate technical variation. The two sources of uncertainty are not interchangeable.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. A high protein concentration means high purity

Concentration is amount per volume, not the target fraction. Exams often reduce volume without changing composition; calculate target mass fraction or specific activity.

2. The pI means absence of charged groups

Opposing charges balance on average. Confusion follows from equating net charge with individual charge. Predict the sign of net charge relative to pI, not absence of ionic sites.

3. All proteins precipitate at one salt saturation

Solubility ranges differ. A question may give activity in a supernatant and pellet; follow measured target activity rather than automatically selecting the pellet.

4. Precipitation always destroys native activity

Reversible association may occur without irreversible unfolding. Distinguish a collected precipitate from a functionally tested redissolved protein.

5. Dialysis concentrates every retained molecule

Diffusion primarily redistributes permeable solutes. Volume changes and binding can occur; do not equate buffer exchange with concentration.

6. Bradford gives equal absorbance per gram for all proteins

Dye response depends on composition and matrix. A standard curve measures equivalents unless the response factor is validated.

7. A280 detects all proteins equally

Aromatic content and other absorbing material matter. Use a known extinction coefficient for a pure protein, not a universal conversion.

8. Protein amount establishes enzyme activity

Inactive proteins can retain peptide bonds and colorimetric response. Use a functional assay with controls.

9. A purification yield above 100% creates protein

Removal of an inhibitor can increase apparent activity. Distinguish measured rate from mass conservation.

10. A straight standard curve licenses unlimited extrapolation

Linearity must be established over the working range. Dilute samples above the highest validated response.

11. Sucrose is reducing because it contains glucose

Both anomeric centers are linked before hydrolysis. Compare the intact molecule with its released monosaccharides.

12. Every glucose residue produces a reducing end

A connected polymer can have only one reducing end despite many residues. Hydrolysis changes the number of reducing molecules.

13. Total carbohydrate colorimetry identifies each sugar

It supplies a broad, composition-dependent response. Use selective methods or separation for identities.

14. The lipid layer is always below water

Layer order depends on solvent density. Identify the system before choosing the phase.

15. All lipid classes have equal extraction efficiency

Head-group charge, associations and solvent polarity affect partition. A standard for neutral lipids need not track polar lipids.

16. A lipid stain is a direct universal mass assay

Staining depends on access and molecular environment. Quantitation requires appropriate standards and consistent conditions.

17. A fatty acid chromatogram uniquely identifies intact lipids

Derivatization loses parent association. Fatty acid composition does not specify the original lipid class or arrangement.

18. A successful post-extraction spike proves complete recovery from tissue

The spike bypasses release from tissue. Pre-extraction controls and reference materials interrogate more of the process.

Revision and active recall

Choose the analytical method from the property that must survive extraction and the quantity that must be measured. Track recovery and selectivity separately, compare chemical mass with functional activity, and interpret each calibrated signal within its validated range. Distinguish whole molecule information from information obtained after chemical or enzymatic conversion.

Essential equations

  • Total amount = concentration × volume.
  • Specific activity = total activity / total protein.
  • Purification fold = final specific activity / initial specific activity.
  • Activity yield (%) = 100 × final activity / initial activity.
  • Beer–Lambert relationship: A = εlc.
  • Linear calibration: x = (y − b)/m.
  • Partition recovery fraction = DVo/(DVo + Va).
  • Ideal dialysis residual fraction per exchange = V/(V+W).
