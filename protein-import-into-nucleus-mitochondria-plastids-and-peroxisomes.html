1. The problem of intracellular protein distribution

Prerequisite reading: nucleus; mitochondria; plastids and chloroplasts; peroxisomes; cytosolic translation initiation.

A cell can produce a protein with exactly the right amino acid sequence and nevertheless fail to obtain its biological activity. Consider a DNA polymerase synthesized in the cytoplasm of a eukaryotic cell. The enzyme may bind nucleotides and catalyse polymerization when tested in a tube, yet it cannot participate effectively in replication if it never reaches the nuclear DNA. Similarly, a mitochondrial matrix enzyme that remains in the cytosol may retain its catalytic potential but be separated from its substrates, cofactors and partner enzymes. A chloroplast enzyme trapped outside the plastid cannot become a normal component of the photosynthetic apparatus. These examples separate two dimensions of protein function: molecular competence and spatial availability. Translation determines the identity of a polypeptide, folding establishes much of its molecular competence, and intracellular targeting ensures that the protein is available in the appropriate biological environment. A defect in any of these stages can produce a loss of function.

The underlying problem arises from the organization of eukaryotic cells. The nuclear envelope separates nucleoplasm from cytoplasm, mitochondria contain two boundary membranes and several internal environments, and chloroplasts possess an envelope and an additional thylakoid system. Peroxisomes establish a distinct oxidative environment inside a single membrane. Lipid bilayers are useful barriers because their hydrophobic interiors oppose the passage of most polar macromolecules. A membrane that prevented all traffic, however, would isolate an organelle from the synthetic activities of the cell. Eukaryotic organization is therefore possible only because barriers are combined with selective transport. An organelle membrane maintains a characteristic chemical environment while dedicated machinery admits the materials required for that environment. Protein import does not simply overcome a disadvantage of membranes; it allows a cell to exploit compartmentalization without losing coordination between compartments.

The distribution challenge becomes particularly striking when the origin of proteins is compared with their destinations. Nuclear genes encode essentially all ordinary nuclear proteins and most of the proteins of mitochondria and plastids. Their messenger RNAs reach the cytosol, where cytosolic ribosomes translate them. Peroxisomes have no independent DNA and ribosomal system and therefore obtain their proteins from the surrounding cell. Mitochondria and plastids retain genomes and ribosomes, and some of their proteins are synthesized internally, but the overwhelming majority of their protein species are supplied from the cytosol. A nuclear gene encoding a mitochondrial protein is not normally translated inside the mitochondrion just because that is where its protein functions. The location of the gene, the location of translation and the location of the mature protein must be considered separately.

An address encoded in the protein itself is one important solution. A short stretch of amino acids or a combination of structural features can be recognized by a transport receptor. The receptor binds the cargo and introduces it into a pathway that gives access to a particular compartment. The address need not be a sequence of unique letters read in the same way as a DNA promoter. Some targeting signals are defined by a recognizable arrangement of side chains in a three-dimensional structure; others use a characteristic position in the polypeptide. Even the same amino acid sequence may function differently when buried within a protein rather than exposed on its surface. Transport specificity depends on signal structure, accessibility and the availability of matching receptors.

A useful starting model is to picture a newly synthesized soluble protein at a junction. One route leaves it in the cytosol, another grants passage through nuclear pores, others guide it across mitochondrial or plastid envelopes, and another permits entry into peroxisomes. A different route connects the cytosolic translation machinery to the endoplasmic reticulum, which is the gateway to the ordinary secretory pathway. These are not five universal, mutually exclusive fates assigned mechanically to every chain. Some proteins have competing or regulated signals, some associate with independently targeted partners, and some change compartments during their functional lifetime. Nevertheless, the junction model is useful because each branch places distinct demands on the substrate and on the energy supply.

The physical nature of the boundary predicts part of the transport strategy. Between nucleoplasm and cytosol, proteins can move through large aqueous assemblies called nuclear pore complexes; transport need not expose the cargo to membrane lipid. In contrast, an imported mitochondrial matrix protein must traverse two membranes using specialized protein-conducting complexes. A chloroplast stromal precursor encounters two envelope translocons, while a protein that continues into the thylakoid lumen faces a further membrane. Peroxisomal matrix proteins cross a membrane through machinery that is unusually capable of admitting already folded cargo. These distinctions explain why it is misleading to state that all organelle import requires unfolding or that all proteins travel through the Golgi.

The word import indicates delivery across a defined boundary. Targeting refers more broadly to the processes that bring a molecule to the correct site, including recognition before crossing. A precursor docked on the surface of a mitochondrion has reached the organelle but has not necessarily been imported into its matrix. The term sorting includes decisions between possible destinations and decisions within an organelle. A protein may be sorted first into a chloroplast and then from stroma to thylakoid lumen. In an experiment these stages cannot be treated as interchangeable: observing a protein associated with an isolated organelle is weaker evidence than demonstrating that it has reached the protected interior.

Spatial availability also requires a consideration of time. A newly produced protein might spend a short interval in the cytosol, accumulate in an organelle after import, and later be degraded. A transcription factor may alternate between nuclear and cytoplasmic locations. A mitochondrial precursor whose import has slowed may be removed by quality-control systems before it accumulates to a detectable concentration. Thus a localization measurement represents the balance of movement, retention, synthesis and removal. It is not always a direct measurement of transport speed. This distinction will become central when interpreting steady-state fluorescence and when comparing experimental treatments.

2. Reading molecular addresses

Before comparing the individual pathways, it is helpful to examine the meaning of a targeting signal in protein chemistry. The primary structure of a polypeptide has direction: its amino terminus is synthesized before its carboxyl terminus. Side chains can be hydrophobic, positively or negatively charged, polar but uncharged, or capable of special interactions. Receptors discriminate among these features rather than recognizing all terminal extensions equally. An amino-terminal extension enriched in certain residues can adopt an amphipathic helix, in which hydrophobic side chains occupy one face and polar or basic residues occupy another. A short nuclear signal, in contrast, can present a patch of positive charge. A terminal peroxisomal motif uses the exposed carboxyl end as part of its recognition surface. Consequently the length, composition, position and structural presentation of the signal matter.

The distinction between a signal sequence and a signal patch is useful. A signal sequence consists of residues that occur next to one another in the primary chain. A signal patch may emerge when residues that were separated along the chain come together after folding. Many standard examples in organelle import are short or extended sequences, but receptors encounter physical protein surfaces, not a written sequence. An otherwise valid sequence can become inaccessible when enclosed within a protein domain or covered by a binding partner. Conversely, unfolding can temporarily expose features normally unavailable to receptors. Predicting the itinerary of a protein from a sequence is therefore easier when a strong, accessible and well-characterized signal dominates its behavior.

Many mitochondrial and chloroplast precursors contain amino-terminal extensions that are removed by processing enzymes after delivery. The precursor is usually larger than the mature polypeptide, and this difference provides experimental evidence about transport and maturation. Signal removal makes biological sense when the targeting extension is useful during delivery but unnecessary for the mature enzyme. It also prevents the extension from interfering with the final fold or catalytic activity. A nuclear localization signal often remains as part of the protein because nuclear proteins may have to enter the nucleus repeatedly or be imported after nuclear envelope reformation. The common peroxisomal type 1 signal likewise remains at the carboxyl terminus. These contrasts are typical, not a universal rule relating every signal's position to its processing.

When a sequence is claimed to be a targeting signal, two complementary experimental questions should be asked. Is the sequence necessary for normal targeting of the native cargo under the conditions tested? Is the sequence sufficient to redirect another suitable cargo? Necessity can be investigated by deletion or selective mutation of the candidate region, while sufficiency can be examined by adding it to a reporter with a different normal localization. If deletion causes mislocalization but the remaining protein also becomes unstable, the result does not independently establish a recognition defect. If a signal redirects a reporter, the result demonstrates sufficiency in that reporter context; it does not prove that every natural cargo with an identical motif is imported by exactly the same route.

The simplest reporter comparison includes an unmodified reporter, a reporter carrying the wild-type candidate signal and a reporter carrying a disrupted signal. The same promoter, cell type and general expression conditions should be used where possible. Detecting the reporter at the correct location requires a compartment marker, and comparing its total abundance helps distinguish altered targeting from degradation. A reporter that forms a small molecule may enter the nucleus passively, so a large soluble reporter is particularly informative for testing receptor-mediated nuclear localization. For a terminal PTS1, a tag added after the signal can disrupt recognition. For an amino-terminal presequence, attaching a large fluorescent tag before the extension can similarly mask the signal. The experimental construct is part of the hypothesis and cannot be treated as neutral by default.

A receptor-cargo interaction is selective because its binding surface is complementary to the signal. The association is usually reversible, which permits cargo capture at the origin and subsequent delivery at the destination. A receptor that bound a substrate permanently would be of little use as a reusable carrier. Cells solve the problem of release in several ways. Nuclear receptors change their affinity in response to Ran bound to different nucleotides. Organelle translocons cooperate with chaperones and processing events. Peroxisomal receptors undergo regulated recycling, involving a reversible ubiquitin modification. Transport is therefore a cycle rather than a one-time binding reaction.

The same chemical feature can have different meanings in different signals. A positive patch can be part of a nuclear localization signal; positive residues within an amphipathic mitochondrial presequence help recognition and can contribute to movement driven by the inner membrane potential. Basic residues alone do not specify one organelle. Similarly, an amino-terminal signal directing a protein into the ER commonly contains a conspicuous hydrophobic core, whereas a typical mitochondrial matrix presequence lacks the long uninterrupted hydrophobic core characteristic of many ER signals. The distinction is based on the overall arrangement of features and on the receptor that interprets them. When solving a sequence-based question, the correct approach is to combine the stated signal position and composition with evidence about the transport machinery.

Import competence is another independent property. A precursor can possess the correct targeting information and still be unable to traverse a channel because it has formed a tightly folded domain or aggregated with other proteins. Cytosolic chaperones often preserve or restore a translocation-compatible conformation. Conversely, nuclear pores normally accommodate folded proteins in receptor-mediated transport, and peroxisomes can admit folded proteins as well. A signal does not guarantee import under every physiological condition. The molecular address specifies a route, while the cargo's physical state and the machinery determine whether the journey succeeds.

A final distinction concerns signals acting in cis and factors acting in trans. A targeting sequence is part of the cargo molecule and acts on that molecule, even though it was encoded in the gene. A receptor, translocon component or chaperone is supplied by another gene and acts on many appropriate substrates. Mutating one cargo signal typically produces a cargo-specific defect. Mutating an essential common receptor can alter the distribution of an entire class of proteins. This logic allows a student to interpret genetic experiments before memorizing the names of particular transport factors. It also explains why defects in the general import machinery can produce broader physiological disturbances than deletion of the targeting sequence of a single enzyme.

3. Nuclear pores and selective access to the genome

The nuclear envelope consists of inner and outer membranes separated by a lumen that is continuous with the ER lumen. At nuclear pore complexes these membranes join around an aqueous passage. It is through this route, rather than by passage through the hydrophobic core of either membrane, that ordinary soluble nuclear proteins reach nucleoplasm. Cytosol and nucleoplasm are separated in composition but are topologically connected by the pore. This makes nuclear transport fundamentally different from translocating a protein chain across the mitochondrial inner membrane. A protein in the nucleus may remain folded throughout passage because it does not have to thread through the narrow kind of channel used to move many mitochondrial precursors.

Nuclear pore complexes are large multiprotein assemblies built from nucleoporins. At a conceptual level, a relatively stable scaffold supports the structure, and flexible nucleoporins containing repeated phenylalanine-glycine motifs contribute to the central selective barrier. These FG-rich regions interact transiently with nuclear transport receptors. A macromolecule lacking suitable receptor interactions faces a much larger barrier than a receptor-bound cargo. The pore is neither an open hole freely permeable to everything nor a rigid gate that opens only when ATP is hydrolysed at the pore itself. Its selectivity emerges from the physical organization of the barrier and the biochemical properties of molecules attempting passage.

Small soluble molecules and many small proteins can exchange passively through nuclear pores. Passive movement results from thermal motion and does not require the molecule to carry a receptor-recognized signal. Larger molecules generally cross more slowly without receptors. In teaching examples, a molecular size around several tens of kilodaltons is often used as a convenient guide to passive permeability, but there is no single sharp cutoff that is valid for all molecules, times and cell types. Shape, flexibility, interactions and the time allowed for diffusion all influence observed exchange. A useful examination answer therefore states a qualitative difference: unrestricted passive exchange is much more limited for large macromolecules than for small solutes.

Receptor-mediated passage has a different objective from simple diffusion. A large regulatory protein may need to concentrate in the nucleus despite being synthesized outside it. If transport merely accelerated equilibration, the protein would tend toward similar free concentrations on both sides in the absence of retention. To achieve regulated accumulation, the machinery must couple cargo binding and release to a biochemical asymmetry between the two spaces. The nuclear Ran system supplies this asymmetry. Before examining Ran, it is necessary to understand how a nuclear protein is recognized.

Classical nuclear localization signals contain clusters of basic residues, particularly lysine and arginine. The classical signal of SV40 large T antigen is often represented by the core sequence PKKKRKV. It illustrates a monopartite signal, meaning that a compact basic cluster is sufficient in the familiar example. Bipartite classical signals contain two basic regions separated by a spacer. The difference is not a requirement for two separate passages through a nuclear pore: it concerns the way a single cargo is recognized by the receptor surface. Nuclear signals may occur near either end or in an internal region and are usually not excised during import.

The classical import receptor system contains importin alpha and importin beta. Importin alpha binds the basic signal on the cargo; importin beta associates with importin alpha and interacts with the FG nucleoporins. This division of labor is particularly useful in an examination because it distinguishes recognition of the cargo from interaction with the gate. If importin alpha can still bind the signal but its association with importin beta is prevented, recognition may occur without efficient pore passage. If a receptor traverses the pore normally but cannot bind a particular cargo, pore function can be retained while that class of cargo is mislocalized. Many nuclear cargos use other members of the importin beta family, including receptors that bind their cargo directly, so alpha is not obligatory for every nuclear import event.

Nuclear import can also serve a structural assembly process. Ribosomal proteins are made on cytosolic ribosomes and subsequently transported into the nucleus, where many join developing ribosomal subunits in association with ribosomal RNA. The assembled subunits are eventually exported for participation in cytosolic translation. The sequence of movements is consistent with the locations of ribosomal RNA production and ribosomal protein synthesis. It illustrates why traffic is bidirectional at the system level: macromolecules enter and leave according to their biological task. The mature cytosolic ribosome is not itself evidence that every one of its constituents was synthesized or assembled entirely in the cytosol.

The foundational evidence for a protein-encoded nuclear address came from the study of SV40 large T antigen. Kalderon and colleagues reported in 1984 that a short region of this nuclear protein could redirect normally cytoplasmic proteins when transferred to them. Their experimental reasoning was more powerful than localization alone. Altering a critical region of a normally nuclear protein disturbed its localization, while adding the region to an unrelated protein provided positive evidence that it could specify a destination. Transfer to bacterial beta-galactosidase and pyruvate kinase supported the concept of an autonomous signal. These findings established the principle that nuclear location is encoded by recognizable protein features, rather than being an inevitable consequence of protein size or of the identity of its gene.

There are limits to what those early reporter studies could establish. A sequence that directs a particular reporter to the nucleus does not by itself reveal the identity of the receptor or its nucleotide-dependent cycle. It does not exclude additional signals in the natural protein. Subsequent biochemical and structural work was required to explain classical signal recognition by importins and the role of Ran. Keeping these levels of evidence separate is essential: localization experiments identify a functional signal, binding measurements establish an interaction, and transport reconstitution or perturbation tests the role of an interaction in movement.

4. Ran-dependent import and export

Ran is a small GTP-binding protein that can exist in a GTP-bound or GDP-bound state. GTP binding and hydrolysis change the conformation and interaction partners of the protein. Two spatially segregated activities create a characteristic nuclear transport cycle. The Ran guanine nucleotide exchange factor RCC1 is associated with nuclear chromatin and promotes release of GDP, allowing Ran to bind GTP. Ran GTPase-activating protein, RanGAP, promotes hydrolysis of Ran-bound GTP on the cytoplasmic side. As a result, Ran-GTP is enriched in the nucleus and Ran-GDP in the cytoplasm. These are relative distributions, not claims that each nucleotide state is absolutely absent from the other side.

The important quantity is the spatial difference in the nucleotide state of Ran. A cargo bearing a classical NLS binds importin alpha in the cytosol, and importin beta associates to form an import complex. The cytoplasmic environment favors formation of this complex because Ran-GTP is comparatively scarce there. Importin beta then makes a succession of reversible contacts with FG regions as the complex passes through the nuclear pore. Once the complex encounters the nuclear environment, Ran-GTP binds importin beta, causing rearrangement or dissociation of the import complex. Additional factors help release the classical NLS cargo from importin alpha. The cargo is now available in the nucleus to bind its physiological partners or continue diffusing within nucleoplasm.

Receptors must be returned to the cytoplasm if more cargo is to be carried. Importin beta returns with Ran-GTP. Importin alpha is recycled by an export receptor commonly called CAS, together with Ran-GTP. In the cytoplasm, RanGAP-stimulated hydrolysis promotes disassembly of the returning complexes and makes the receptors available for another round. Ran-GDP is itself returned to the nucleus with the help of nuclear transport factor 2, where RCC1 regenerates Ran-GTP. The overall cycle consumes chemical free energy from GTP hydrolysis and sustains an asymmetric system that would otherwise tend toward equilibrium. Different receptor routes have different detailed stoichiometries; assigning exactly one GTP to every cargo molecule is not a generally valid accounting rule.

A frequent conceptual error is to describe Ran as a motor that mechanically pulls each cargo through the pore by hydrolysing GTP in the middle of the channel. The central translocation step is mediated by receptor interactions and stochastic movement. The nucleotide cycle mainly ensures that loading is favored on one side and unloading on the other. Direction is imposed on the completed cycle even though individual microscopic steps can be reversible. This is an example of how living systems use chemical energy to bias a series of otherwise reversible events. It is not necessary to imagine that every encounter with a nucleoporin consumes a separate GTP.

Export reverses the pattern of cargo binding. A protein containing an appropriate nuclear export signal can associate with an exportin such as CRM1, also called exportin 1 or XPO1. In the familiar CRM1 route, Ran-GTP helps stabilize the export complex inside the nucleus. The ternary complex passes through the nuclear pore, and hydrolysis of Ran-GTP in the cytoplasm promotes cargo release. Hence nuclear Ran-GTP encourages release of import cargo but helps assemble export complexes. Students often expect the same nucleotide state to have the same effect on both kinds of receptor. The opposite effects are precisely what allow the same spatial asymmetry to support transport in both directions.

Many familiar CRM1-dependent export signals include appropriately arranged hydrophobic residues, often including leucines. They are not equivalent to the basic sequence of a classical NLS. Nor is every nuclear export pathway dependent on CRM1: for instance, the principal pathway for bulk messenger RNA export involves different receptor machinery and is not simply the protein NES pathway applied to RNA. The purpose of introducing export here is to explain protein localization and cycling, rather than to repeat all of RNA transport.

Consider a mutation that diminishes nuclear RCC1 activity. Ran may still be present, pores may still exist and the cargo NLS may be intact. Nevertheless, the normal nuclear Ran-GTP enrichment can weaken. Import complexes then release cargo inefficiently and export complexes form poorly. A defect in cytoplasmic RanGAP can also disturb the gradient, although the immediate biochemical change differs: cytoplasmic GTP-bound Ran may persist longer, interfering with efficient receptor recycling and cargo loading. In either case, simple measurement of total Ran abundance is inadequate. The relevant variable is the nucleotide distribution and the ability to complete the cycle.

Localization can also be controlled without altering the pore or Ran. A transcription factor may possess both import and export signals, with accessibility depending on a modification or binding partner. Activation may expose an NLS and increase import, reduce export or strengthen chromatin retention. When the stimulus disappears, the factor can redistribute to the cytoplasm. This is a rapid method of regulation because the cell can use a pre-existing protein instead of waiting for transcription and translation of a new molecule. The distinction between signal accessibility, receptor affinity and retention will be useful in interpreting cellular signaling experiments in later units.

A nuclear accumulation experiment should therefore distinguish transport from retention. Suppose a fluorescent factor becomes strongly nuclear after stimulation. This observation is compatible with faster import, slower export, more nuclear binding, or altered degradation outside the nucleus. If an export inhibitor alone causes similar accumulation, export is implicated but the original stimulus need not necessarily act on that same step. Time-resolved tracking, transport signal mutants and measurements of total reporter abundance provide additional tests. A complete causal explanation requires more than observing a change in the final nuclear-to-cytoplasmic fluorescence ratio.

Physiological signaling provides an example of this regulated access. A transcription factor that has an exposed NLS in its activated state can reach DNA more efficiently than the same factor with an inaccessible signal. In the NFAT system, for example, calcium-dependent activation of calcineurin promotes dephosphorylation of NFAT, facilitating its nuclear accumulation. Rephosphorylation contributes to redistribution. The detailed signaling network belongs to the study of cell communication, but the transport principle is clear: a change in the modification state of a protein can alter its access to the genome without changing the amino acid sequence encoded by the gene. Such regulated localization is not equivalent to activating RNA polymerase directly.

5. Mitochondrial protein import and its physical constraints

Mitochondria require a different solution to the protein distribution problem. An imported soluble matrix enzyme must cross an outer membrane, pass through or across the intermembrane space and cross an inner membrane. The inner membrane has an especially important role because it maintains the electrochemical gradient used in oxidative phosphorylation. If the machinery that transported proteins left a permanently open, nonselective hole in this membrane, ions could dissipate the gradient and undermine energy production. A translocation mechanism must therefore combine selective movement of protein with preservation of the membrane's general barrier function. The arrangement of two translocases acting in concert is a solution to this constraint.

The mitochondrion contains its own genome, but this fact should not be mistaken for genetic autonomy. In human mitochondria, mitochondrial DNA encodes a small set of polypeptides that participate in oxidative phosphorylation, alongside mitochondrial RNAs. Many other respiratory-chain subunits, enzymes of intermediary metabolism, components of mitochondrial ribosomes, enzymes involved in mitochondrial DNA maintenance and the import machinery itself are nuclear encoded. These proteins are usually synthesized in the cytosol before delivery. An imported mitochondrial DNA polymerase thus provides an especially clear example: the nuclear genome supplies a protein required for maintenance of the mitochondrial genome. The two genetic systems depend on one another.

A mitochondrion is not a uniform bag of enzymes. Matrix proteins occupy the innermost aqueous region, inner membrane proteins participate in transport and energy conversion, intermembrane space proteins include soluble factors and proteins attached to membranes, and the outer membrane contains channels, receptors and other components. Crossing the outer membrane is not enough to determine the final destination. Many cargos share an initial encounter with the outer membrane import system but are subsequently directed to distinct subcompartments. A single label such as mitochondrial protein therefore does not describe the complete itinerary.

For a typical nuclear-encoded soluble matrix enzyme, translation produces a preprotein with an amino-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence, also termed a presequence. Such sequences are commonly enriched in arginine and other positively charged or helix-compatible residues and tend to contain few acidic residues. Their hydrophobic and polar residues can be arranged to form an amphipathic alpha helix when the sequence engages its partners. One face presents hydrophobic side chains; the other presents polar or positively charged side chains. Recognition depends on this arrangement, so two presequences may share little exact residue-by-residue identity and yet be recognized by the same general pathway. A universal short consensus is not required.

The positive character of many matrix-targeting presequences has a functional consequence beyond receptor binding. During normal mitochondrial respiration, the matrix is electrically negative relative to the other side of the inner membrane. Movement of a positively charged group into this negative environment is energetically favored. The electric field can contribute to translocation of a presequence into the inner membrane import channel. This is not an explanation for all steps of mitochondrial import: the precursor must first be recognized, reach the channel, negotiate its physical conformation and complete passage. Nor does this potential act across the outer membrane in the same way. The outer membrane's permeability properties differ fundamentally from those of the inner membrane.

A precursor with a correct signal can fail if its mature domain becomes tightly folded before it reaches the translocase. A folded enzyme has a larger cross-sectional size than an extended chain, and a stable three-dimensional domain may present a significant unfolding barrier. Mitochondrial import generally requires substrate proteins to attain a sufficiently unfolded conformation during passage through the canonical channels. The term import competent does not mean that the precursor must float as a completely structureless chain throughout its entire cytosolic lifetime. It means that it can be delivered and converted into a form compatible with translocation. Some precursors are kept loosely folded by chaperones, while others must unfold when the import system engages them.

Cytosolic Hsp70 family chaperones assist many precursors by binding exposed hydrophobic regions, thereby reducing aggregation and maintaining a pool of soluble material that can reach receptors. The chaperone cycle requires nucleotides and appropriate cochaperones, but it is not the same cycle as the matrix import motor. One operates outside the mitochondrion and influences substrate delivery and conformation; the other acts on the emerging polypeptide on the matrix side. Experimental ATP depletion may interfere with both, which is why measurements of ATP effects require care when assigning a specific molecular target.

These considerations help resolve a common apparent contradiction. The preceding chapter on folding explains that newly synthesized proteins tend to acquire native conformations with assistance from chaperones. The import chapter explains that certain precursors must unfold to cross membranes. Both are correct because folding is regulated in relation to the protein's itinerary. A soluble enzyme cannot perform its normal function before it reaches the compartment in which it is needed, and premature stabilization can prevent that delivery. Cells manage the timing of folding, rather than treating the fastest possible folding as a universal goal.

6. Following a matrix precursor through TOM and TIM23

The translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane is abbreviated TOM. It contains receptor components and a channel formed by Tom40. Tom20 and Tom22 are important examples of receptors associated with recognition of presequence-containing proteins, whereas Tom70 is often discussed in relation to chaperone-associated substrates with internal targeting information, although the division of substrates is not absolute. At examination level, the distinction that matters most is between a receptor that recognizes a targeting feature and a channel through which the chain crosses the membrane. A receptor can bind cargo without completing passage, whereas a channel defect may prevent movement even when the signal was correctly recognized.

Consider a soluble matrix enzyme produced as a precursor on a cytosolic ribosome. Its targeting extension is available for interaction with cytosolic factors and mitochondrial receptors. The precursor approaches the mitochondrial surface and binds components of TOM. The presequence is directed into the Tom40 channel. As the chain begins to cross the outer membrane, the amino-terminal end becomes accessible from the intermembrane-space side. TOM is a major entry route for many mitochondrial precursors, but passing through TOM is not the same as completing matrix import. The precursor must be transferred to machinery of the inner membrane.

The inner membrane presequence pathway involves TIM23, named for the translocase of the inner mitochondrial membrane and one of its key components. Functional coordination between TOM and TIM23 permits the same precursor chain to traverse the two membranes. It is not generally necessary for a complete matrix precursor to accumulate as a freely diffusing folded protein in the intermembrane space before beginning its second crossing. Instead, the import complexes can cooperate at sites where the two membranes lie near each other. The amino terminus can enter the matrix while a later region of the same chain has not yet crossed the outer membrane.

The electric component of the inner membrane potential promotes movement of the positively charged targeting extension and supports function of the TIM23 system. The mature domain presents a further problem because much of its length may have no strongly positive charge. The presequence translocase-associated motor, often abbreviated PAM, contains mitochondrial Hsp70 and associated factors that support import of the chain into the matrix. Matrix Hsp70 binds segments that have emerged from the channel, and its ATP-dependent binding and release cycle helps prevent backward movement and promotes completion. It can also contribute to the unfolding of domains that resist translocation. Detailed mechanical models of chaperone action differ in emphasis, but for NET reasoning the central fact is that successful matrix import depends on an inner membrane potential and on the ATP-supported matrix motor.

An attractive but incomplete explanation would be that the signal acts like a magnet and that the negative matrix pulls the entire protein inside. A presequence is short compared with many mature mitochondrial proteins. Even if the electrical contribution is substantial for the signal, the remaining chain must still move through the channels. It may contain negatively charged segments, hydrophobic surfaces or stable domains. Binding of matrix Hsp70 to newly accessible segments changes the likelihood of backward movement and helps couple ATP hydrolysis to the completion of import. The combined contributions of recognition, electrical potential, motor activity and substrate unfolding account for the process more convincingly than any one factor considered in isolation.

Once the targeting extension reaches the matrix, mitochondrial processing peptidase commonly cleaves it. The released mature domain may then finish folding and assemble with other subunits, sometimes with further assistance from mitochondrial chaperones. The precursor-to-mature size change is a valuable molecular marker of processing. However, because the amino-terminal signal reaches the matrix before the entire chain has completed passage, cleavage can occur while part of the precursor is still associated with the translocation machinery. It would therefore be unsafe to equate every processed band on a gel with a completely imported and functional protein.

The mature matrix enzyme may need a cofactor, an oligomeric partner or a particular ionic environment before it becomes active. In experimental work the most convincing case for successful import combines multiple observations: the precursor is converted to an appropriate mature product, the product is protected within an intact organelle, and the delivered protein is present in the expected subcompartment. If biological function is relevant, activity provides an additional criterion. Failure of activity alone is not adequate evidence of failed import because folding or cofactor assembly might be defective after import.

The pathway has several distinguishable points of failure. A signal deletion can reduce interaction with surface receptors. A Tom40 defect can interfere with outer membrane translocation. Loss of inner membrane potential can interfere with presequence movement through TIM23. Failure of mitochondrial Hsp70 activity can particularly compromise completion of matrix delivery. Loss of processing peptidase activity can produce an uncleaved precursor that may nevertheless have reached the matrix. These predictions are not all equivalent and need appropriate controls. A mutation in one import component can secondarily affect membrane potential or organelle health, so an observed defect may not be confined to the earliest direct molecular consequence.

7. Mitochondrial subcompartment sorting

The matrix pathway provides a useful reference case but should not be extended to all mitochondrial proteins. Some inner membrane proteins contain a hydrophobic sorting segment after a cleavable presequence. When such a segment reaches TIM23, it may stop complete translocation and be released laterally into the lipid bilayer. In this stop-transfer pathway, an initial targeting signal establishes entry and a later hydrophobic segment helps determine membrane residence. A protein using such a route need not have its entire mature domain imported into the matrix before insertion. The position and orientation of its hydrophobic region contribute to the final topology.

Another class of inner membrane proteins includes many metabolite carriers, which contain multiple transmembrane helices and internal targeting information instead of a typical cleavable amino-terminal extension. Their import commonly involves the TOM system, chaperone-like small TIM proteins in the intermembrane space and the TIM22 pathway for insertion into the inner membrane. Small TIM proteins assist movement of hydrophobic precursors across the aqueous intermembrane space. The physiological problem is evident: exposed hydrophobic segments of a carrier could aggregate or interact inappropriately with aqueous surroundings. The sorting machinery keeps those segments manageable until they reach the bilayer in which they will function.

This contrast provides a particularly useful example of the limitations of memorizing a universal signal. A matrix-targeting presequence may be absent from a correctly localized inner membrane carrier. Its targeting information is distributed differently, its final destination is different and the downstream translocase differs. A student should not predict that every mitochondrial precursor becomes smaller by cleavage or that every mitochondrial membrane protein uses TIM23. Likewise, observing resistance to protease in intact mitochondria does not establish matrix localization because the outer membrane protects proteins in other subcompartments as well.

Some proteins destined for the intermembrane space are retained there after undergoing interactions that differ from ordinary matrix targeting. One well-established route involves the mitochondrial intermembrane space import and assembly system, commonly called MIA, which supports oxidative folding of particular cysteine-containing proteins. Formation of disulfide bonds can stabilize a protein after it enters the intermembrane space and help retain it there. The final state of the protein consequently influences the directionality of sorting. Other intermembrane space proteins use different routes, including pathways involving presequences and subsequent proteolytic processing. The appropriate examination-level lesson is the existence of alternative sorting and retention strategies, not the detailed catalogue of all intermembrane space clients.

Outer membrane proteins also have diverse targeting requirements. Some beta-barrel proteins pass through TOM and are assembled into the outer membrane by a sorting and assembly machinery often abbreviated SAM. Other outer membrane proteins containing alpha-helical membrane anchors can follow different insertion routes. The fact that a beta-barrel outer membrane protein may enter the organelle through an import channel before being assembled into the outer membrane is not contradictory. Its final position is the outcome of a sequence of interactions and assembly steps, not necessarily the first membrane it encounters.

Some inner membrane proteins that are synthesized in the matrix, or delivered to the matrix before insertion, can use an insertion machinery related to the bacterial YidC system, Oxa1 in mitochondria. This illustrates a principle also encountered in plastids: an organelle derived from a bacterial ancestor can retain machinery for inserting proteins from its interior into an internal membrane. Newly acquired systems for importing cytosolically synthesized proteins coexist with older mechanisms for distributing proteins made on the organelle side. For this chapter it is sufficient to recognize the existence of matrix-side insertion and to avoid the inference that every inner membrane protein arrives through the same route.

The differences among these pathways allow logical predictions from cargo properties. A soluble precursor with a strong cleavable presequence is a reasonable candidate for the matrix route. A hydrophobic protein bearing multiple internal targeting features and no cleavable extension may be a carrier-pathway substrate. A mutation in a later stop-transfer segment can alter the final topology without necessarily eliminating initial mitochondrial targeting. A defect in TOM may affect multiple downstream routes because many precursors share this entry system, whereas a defect in TIM22 can disproportionately affect carrier insertion. These predictions should be stated for the defined substrates and conditions, not used as absolute descriptions of every mitochondrial protein.

8. Experimental evidence for mitochondrial import

The canonical mitochondrial import model was tested using isolated organelles and newly synthesized precursor proteins. In a simplified cell-free import experiment, a labelled precursor is mixed with an organelle preparation under conditions that support import. Samples collected over time can be analysed to see whether the precursor decreases, a mature product appears, or labelled material becomes resistant to a protease added outside the intact organelles. The experiment isolates much of the transport process from the complexities of ongoing cellular transcription and translation. It also permits comparison between energized and de-energized organelles, or between otherwise matched substrates with and without a targeting sequence.

The logic of protease protection rests on membrane impermeability. A protease added to the outside should digest susceptible proteins that remain exposed in the surrounding medium or at the cytosolic surface of an intact organelle. A protein enclosed by an intact membrane is protected because the enzyme cannot readily reach it. Addition of an appropriate detergent disrupts the barrier and should make formerly protected susceptible proteins accessible. A no-protease sample indicates total recoverable labelled protein; the protease sample tests accessibility; and the detergent-plus-protease sample establishes whether protection depended on a membrane. This is an assay of physical location and accessibility, not direct measurement of receptor binding affinity.

In mitochondria, a more refined version distinguishes subcompartments by selectively disrupting the outer membrane while preserving inner membrane integrity. A soluble intermembrane space protein exposed after outer membrane disruption can become protease sensitive, whereas a matrix protein remains protected by the inner membrane. An outer membrane surface protein may be sensitive even before disruption. Appropriate compartment markers establish that the manipulation had the intended selectivity. Without marker controls, the result is ambiguous because damaged inner membranes can make matrix proteins accessible and falsely suggest that they were never imported.

The size difference between a precursor and its processed product provides a second kind of evidence. Imagine a radiolabelled matrix precursor of 40 kilodaltons that loses an approximately 3 kilodalton targeting extension. A band near 37 kilodaltons appears during incubation with mitochondria. If that band is protected from external protease but becomes sensitive after membrane solubilization, the observations together support translocation and processing. If a 37 kilodalton band appears without protection, proteolytic processing alone has not established complete import. If the 40 kilodalton precursor becomes protected without being processed, defective processing is one possible explanation. In each case one should inspect the combined pattern rather than selecting one preferred observation in isolation.

A landmark study by Eilers and Schatz in 1986 used a fusion involving mouse dihydrofolate reductase and a mitochondrial targeting extension. Dihydrofolate reductase can be stabilized in a folded conformation by binding methotrexate. Addition of the ligand inhibited import of the fusion precursor into isolated mitochondria. This provided direct experimental support for the proposition that translocation requires a cargo conformation compatible with passage through the import machinery. A stable folded domain can present a physical obstacle despite the presence of a correct targeting signal. The experiment linked a manipulable biochemical property of a protein domain to its ability to traverse an organelle boundary.

The underlying reasoning is particularly instructive because the ligand is not simply a conventional poison that destroys the target organelle. Its useful role in the experimental design is to stabilize the reporter domain. A comparison between the same fusion precursor with and without the ligand holds the targeting sequence constant. If the mitochondria remain competent to import an appropriate unrelated substrate, a generalized failure of the organelle is less plausible. The conclusion is not that methotrexate is a universal mitochondrial import inhibitor; it is that stabilization of a specifically ligand-binding cargo can interfere with translocation. Nor does the experiment prove that every imported precursor begins completely unfolded in the cytosol. It shows that the stable fold can become a barrier at a step requiring conformational flexibility.

To investigate energy dependence, one may compare import in mitochondria with a normal inner membrane potential and mitochondria in which that potential has been dissipated. A decrease in import of a typical presequence-containing matrix precursor is expected. But a treatment that collapses the potential may also alter ATP production, redox conditions and organelle integrity. To attribute the effect to the electrical contribution with greater confidence, the experiment should verify the potential and, where feasible, distinguish it from ATP availability. Comparing multiple substrates with different route requirements can further help. A single treatment is rarely a perfectly selective test of one energy-consuming reaction.

Time also provides information about mechanism. If the precursor appears first in a receptor-bound state, then in a partly protected state, and finally as a fully protected mature product, the order is compatible with sequential translocation and processing. Arresting a precursor at an intermediate stage can provide evidence of physical contact with components of the translocase. Yet a stalled intermediate may be an abnormal state created by the perturbation. Its association with a component is useful evidence but should be interpreted together with other data showing that the component normally contributes to import. A careful experimental argument distinguishes the existence of an interaction from proof that it is a necessary, productive step.

9. Quantifying mitochondrial import and its energetic contribution

A simple calculation illustrates why a matrix-negative potential favors the entry of positively charged groups. Define the potential difference as the electrical potential of the matrix minus that of the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane. If this difference is minus 0.150 volts, the electrical free-energy contribution for one mole of charges moving across the entire difference is z multiplied by the Faraday constant and by the potential difference. Here z is the signed charge number and the Faraday constant is approximately 96,485 coulombs per mole. Since one coulomb-volt equals one joule, the resulting quantity is in joules per mole.

For a hypothetical group with net charge plus three moving from the outer side of the inner membrane into the matrix, the electrical contribution is three times 96,485 times minus 0.150, or approximately minus 43.4 kilojoules per mole. A negative value means the electrical contribution favors movement in that direction. Reversing the movement changes the sign of the electrical contribution. Reversing the sign of the transported charge would also reverse the sign. This simple calculation helps a student understand why positively charged residues are useful in a presequence under the normal orientation of the mitochondrial potential.

Several limitations prevent the number from being interpreted as the actual total energy cost of importing a protein. A real presequence does not necessarily move its entire net charge through the full field as a point charge. Local protonation, the distribution of the electric field, interaction with the translocase, hydration and the motion of different regions all complicate the process. The expression also omits chemical interactions, conformational free energy and ATP-driven motor activity. It describes a specified idealized electrical contribution, not the complete thermodynamics of a mitochondrial precursor. For examination problems, the correct sign and units matter as much as the magnitude.

A second useful quantitative approach is to describe import as a flux. Suppose a pool of labelled, import-competent precursor is supplied to isolated mitochondria and the preparation converts a constant fraction of the remaining pool per unit time. In the simplest approximation, the rate of disappearance of unimported material equals a first-order rate constant multiplied by the remaining precursor amount. If the rate constant is k, the amount remaining at time t is the starting amount multiplied by exp(-kt). The complementary fraction imported is one minus exp(-kt), provided degradation, loss to nonspecific binding and other fates are negligible. The half-time of this idealized process is ln(2) divided by k.

For example, if k is 0.20 per minute, the model predicts that after five minutes the fraction imported is one minus exp(-1), or about 0.632. If the same assay under a defined treatment yields k equal to 0.05 per minute, after five minutes the predicted fraction is one minus exp(-0.25), about 0.221. These are hypothetical values used to illustrate the model. Their biological interpretation is a fourfold decrease in the fitted rate constant, not a fourfold decrease in the fraction imported at every time. At long times both idealized systems would approach complete conversion. Consequently, an endpoint measurement taken after a long incubation may conceal a substantial kinetic defect.

Another complication is that not all precursors may be competent to import. If only sixty percent of the starting population is capable of reaching the matrix, the upper limit of the import curve under an otherwise ideal first-order model is 0.60 rather than 1. The apparent plateau and the approach to that plateau convey distinct information. A treatment that slows passage could change the rate constant without changing the competent fraction. A treatment that induces irreversible aggregation could lower the plateau even if the remaining soluble precursor imports normally. A time series with several points helps distinguish these possibilities, although the fitted parameters are still descriptive unless additional experiments establish the molecular cause.

The concept of limiting capacity also follows from the existence of receptors and channels. At low precursor concentration, the rate can increase roughly proportionally to available substrate because most import sites are unoccupied. At high concentration, occupancy and turnover of the finite machinery can limit the rate. A saturation curve is therefore plausible. Nevertheless, an apparent plateau need not prove that one particular receptor is saturated; ATP depletion, substrate aggregation or a downstream channel can also limit import. A mathematical curve provides an organized description of the observation. Identification of the underlying molecular bottleneck requires perturbation and independent evidence.

Energy accounting should similarly distinguish stoichiometry from energetic dependence. Matrix import depends on the electrochemical state of the inner membrane and on ATP-driven reactions, but there is no universal number of ATP molecules consumed per imported polypeptide that can be calculated merely from the number of amino acids. A longer or more stable chain can impose different demands, and the chaperone cycle is not one ATP for each peptide bond. Translation energy bookkeeping, studied earlier, counts events of synthesis under explicitly defined assumptions. Import energy bookkeeping concerns different molecular events and cannot be obtained by transferring that formula to a new process.

10. Plastid protein import in the context of plant cell biology

A chloroplast illustrates how a cell coordinates proteins produced by separate genetic systems to construct one functional organelle. The plastid genome encodes some components of the photosynthetic apparatus and its own gene-expression machinery. Many other components, including numerous stromal enzymes and proteins of the photosynthetic membrane, are encoded in the nucleus. During evolution from a cyanobacterial ancestor, extensive loss and transfer of genetic information contributed to this division of labor. A nuclear-encoded protein that serves the chloroplast must be synthesized by cytosolic ribosomes and subsequently delivered to the plastid. Without a specific protein-import pathway, the combination of nuclear and plastid gene expression could not produce a coherent photosynthetic system.

The consequences of import can be understood through the composition of a photosynthetic complex. Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, commonly known as Rubisco, illustrates coordination of the two genomes in many land plants. Its large subunit is plastid encoded and synthesized within the organelle, whereas its small subunit is usually nuclear encoded and arrives as a precursor produced in the cytosol. Formation of the ordinary functional complex requires both kinds of subunit in the stroma and appropriate assembly assistance. The small subunit therefore needs targeting information that is distinct from the information specifying its contribution to the enzyme's mature structure. Deleting the targeting extension can prevent assembly in the chloroplast even though the mature domain retains the residues necessary for interacting with other subunits.

The word plastid includes more than chloroplasts. Plant tissues contain plastids that specialize in photosynthesis, storage, pigment accumulation or other metabolic functions. For example, amyloplasts are associated with starch storage and chromoplasts with the accumulation of particular pigments. These organelles share ancestry and many aspects of protein delivery. A plastid-specific signal need not always specify one developmental form of the organelle independently of the cell type and its import machinery. Plastid differentiation changes the set of proteins required, so regulation of protein production and import specificity helps the organelle adapt to its developmental and physiological role. The chloroplast is the most familiar system for examining the general principles of plastid targeting.

Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have an outer and an inner envelope membrane. Unlike the canonical mitochondrial matrix system, the chloroplast also contains an extensive thylakoid membrane that separates the stroma from a lumen. These compartments must not be confused. The stroma is the aqueous interior enclosed by the inner envelope. The lumen of a thylakoid is separated from the stroma by the thylakoid membrane, where light-driven reactions establish an electrochemical proton gradient. A protein that reaches the stroma has crossed the envelope, but it has not necessarily crossed the thylakoid membrane. A stromal enzyme and a soluble thylakoid lumen enzyme therefore have different terminal sorting requirements even if their early targeting steps are similar.

Typical soluble nucleus-encoded chloroplast proteins are produced with an amino-terminal extension called a transit peptide. Many transit peptides are relatively rich in serine, threonine and other residues compatible with flexible interactions, often contain basic residues and tend to lack strongly acidic stretches. They do not share one strict sequence that identifies every chloroplast precursor. Their physicochemical properties overlap in part with those of other targeting sequences, and recognition depends on more than bulk amino acid composition. In particular, one should not equate a chloroplast transit peptide with the typical amphipathic, strongly positively charged mitochondrial matrix presequence. Chloroplast import across the envelope does not use the matrix-negative electrical potential characteristic of the mitochondrial inner membrane.

A transit peptide is the initial address for many proteins destined for the plastid interior. It can be combined with additional targeting information that becomes relevant after the envelope has been crossed. A precursor destined for the thylakoid lumen may contain a bipartite amino-terminal targeting sequence: a first region directs entry into the chloroplast and a following region directs transport across the thylakoid. The first address is interpreted in the cytosol and at the envelope, while the second is interpreted within the chloroplast. This hierarchical organization reduces ambiguity because distinct parts of a precursor can interact with machinery that is available only at a particular stage of the itinerary.

As in other targeting systems, a signal's activity can be studied by fusing it to an unrelated reporter. A stromal transit peptide attached to the amino terminus of a suitably chosen reporter can cause chloroplast localization. The unmodified reporter should remain outside the chloroplast under the same conditions, unless it has its own targeting information. A mutated transit peptide can be compared with the wild-type fusion. In plant cells, chlorophyll autofluorescence can help identify photosynthetic plastids, but fluorescence overlap alone should not be taken as proof of stromal localization. A reporter on the chloroplast surface may appear close to chlorophyll at the resolution of ordinary light microscopy. Additional markers or biochemical evidence are required to determine the final subcompartment.

The existence of many plastid forms also complicates an overly literal destination prediction. A leaf chloroplast contains extensive thylakoids, whereas a nonphotosynthetic plastid may not have the same internal membrane arrangement. A signal that is efficient in a green leaf may encounter a different receptor complement or physiological context in a root cell. This does not mean that the general import principles are unreliable. It means that the relevant biological question includes the type of plastid and the substrate's requirements. The safest examination reasoning applies the mechanism to the organelle and experimental conditions explicitly provided.

11. TOC and TIC: passage across the plastid envelope

The translocon at the outer chloroplast envelope is abbreviated TOC, and the translocon at the inner chloroplast envelope is abbreviated TIC. These assemblies cooperate in the delivery of many transit-peptide-bearing precursors from the cytosol into the stroma. The names are deliberately parallel to those of the mitochondrial outer and inner membrane systems, but the molecular components are not interchangeable. TOM and TIM identify mitochondrial transport machines; TOC and TIC identify plastid envelope machines. This naming distinction is especially helpful when interpreting a genetic mutation or organelle-specific import assay.

Cytosolic chaperones can interact with newly synthesized chloroplast precursors and help prevent inappropriate folding or aggregation before engagement with the import machinery. The exact cytosolic delivery partners vary among substrates and organisms. The general requirement is that the precursor must encounter the envelope in a soluble, import-compatible form with its transit peptide accessible. A precursor whose signal has been removed by accidental cleavage, or whose mature domain has aggregated, may fail to reach or traverse the translocon even when the chloroplast itself is functioning normally.

At the outer envelope, receptor GTPases including Toc34 and members of the Toc159 family participate in the recognition and regulated engagement of precursor proteins. Toc75 is a major channel component of the canonical TOC machinery. Naming the GTPases as receptors and Toc75 as a channel helps avoid the mistake of assigning one undifferentiated role to all TOC proteins. Binding of a transit peptide to a receptor is an early recognition event. Coordinated nucleotide-dependent interactions influence productive transfer of the precursor to the translocation machinery. A mutation in a receptor's cargo-recognition surface may affect the selection of substrates, while a defect in a general channel may compromise passage of a broader group.

The initial segment of the precursor enters the outer envelope channel and subsequently engages the TIC machinery associated with the inner envelope. Cooperation between the envelope systems permits translocation into the stroma. A general schematic can portray a precursor extending through the two membranes, with its amino terminus reaching the stromal side before the rest of the chain. It is not necessary to assume that every chloroplast precursor crosses the outer envelope completely, folds in the intermembrane space and then unfolds again for passage through the inner envelope. Such a sequence would introduce avoidable conformational barriers.

ATP-dependent chaperone or motor activities on the stromal side provide support for completion of translocation. As a precursor emerges in the stroma, binding of successive regions by import-associated factors can favor its progress and reduce backward movement. The precise organization and composition of the inner envelope apparatus and its associated motor have been subjects of continued investigation. At the present level, it is scientifically appropriate to use TIC as the general functional machinery for inner envelope translocation and ATP-driven stromal assistance as a major energetic contribution, without asserting that one fixed subunit composition or one detailed motor model applies identically to all plastids and all substrates.

After entry, the transit peptide of many stromal precursors is cleaved by stromal processing peptidase, abbreviated SPP. The smaller mature product can acquire its final conformation, bind a cofactor or assemble with other protein subunits. As with mitochondria, cleavage is an indicator of processing but should not be used alone as proof that the complete functional cargo has reached the stroma. It is possible for an amino-terminal part of a chain to become accessible to a stromal enzyme while another part has not completed movement. Thus the same general principles of biochemical interpretation apply to both double-membrane import systems.

The energy requirements of envelope import provide a major comparison with mitochondria. The TOC receptor system involves GTP-binding and hydrolysis events, and efficient translocation across the envelope requires ATP-dependent activities. The canonical chloroplast envelope route does not depend on a mitochondrial-style, strongly negative inner membrane electrical potential to pull a positively charged presequence inward. Chloroplasts certainly possess important electrochemical gradients, but the photosynthetically generated proton gradient is principally associated with the thylakoid membrane. Confusing the envelope with the thylakoid produces incorrect predictions about which step of import will respond to the collapse of a particular gradient.

An isolated chloroplast import assay can distinguish receptor association, partial translocation and delivery into the protected interior. Labelled precursor may be incubated with intact chloroplasts under specified energy conditions and analysed for association, processing and protease resistance. A targeting peptide deletion should reduce efficient entry for a substrate that depends on the deleted region. Restricting nucleotide availability may permit some initial binding but impair subsequent productive passage. Results must be interpreted with attention to the membrane integrity of isolated organelles, since a damaged envelope can alter accessibility and create misleading apparent import or protection.

Imagine a hypothetical experiment in which an unmodified precursor produces both a surface-associated band and a mature stromal band after incubation. A receptor mutant shows little associated precursor and little mature product. An inner envelope translocation mutant, by contrast, retains considerable precursor association but yields little mature product. The second phenotype suggests a defect after initial recognition. These results would not establish the exact molecular action of the mutated protein without further evidence, but they would distinguish stages of the pathway more effectively than measuring total chloroplast-associated material alone.

Different plastid types can adjust the spectrum of imported proteins partly through variation in receptor complements and the production of different precursor populations. This is biologically sensible because an immature plastid or a plastid specialized for storage does not require exactly the same mixture of proteins as a mature photosynthetic chloroplast. A defective receptor may therefore affect some classes of precursors more strongly than others. At the same time, many cargos use common downstream machinery. The combination of selective recognition and shared translocation capacity allows an organelle to coordinate a large proteome without building a wholly independent channel for every protein.

12. Secondary targeting within the chloroplast

Crossing the envelope is sufficient for the delivery of many soluble stromal proteins, but it cannot deliver a soluble protein into the thylakoid lumen by itself. The lumen lies across another lipid bilayer. A typical nucleus-encoded lumen protein therefore requires targeting information in addition to its stromal transit peptide. Following the first processing event, a thylakoid targeting sequence becomes available to machinery associated with the thylakoid membrane. A second translocation and, in many cases, another processing event complete delivery. This provides an instructive example of sequential sorting with a change of biological context between stages.

Two familiar systems for transport across the thylakoid membrane are related to bacterial protein-export pathways: the Sec pathway and the twin-arginine translocation pathway, often abbreviated Tat. Their occurrence is consistent with the bacterial evolutionary origin of plastids. The Sec pathway transports suitable substrates in an unfolded or translocation-compatible conformation, with ATP-dependent contributions from the chloroplast SecA system for typical soluble lumenal substrates. The Tat system can translocate certain folded proteins and depends on the proton motive force across the thylakoid membrane. The two systems solve different physical problems and should not be collapsed into a generic chloroplast channel.

In the Tat pathway, the signal commonly includes a characteristic pair of arginine residues in an appropriate sequence context. These residues are part of a recognizable targeting motif, not a sufficient description of the entire substrate. The system is particularly useful for proteins that need to acquire a stable structure or, in certain cases, assemble a cofactor before crossing the membrane. Moving such a protein through a pathway that required complete unfolding might be inefficient or destroy the state needed for function. Tat demonstrates that even within a single organelle there can be transport routes with different substrate folding requirements.

This example also prevents a common overgeneralization from the mitochondrial pathway. Most canonical chloroplast envelope precursors need to be compatible with translocation through TOC and TIC. A Tat-dependent protein may subsequently fold in the stroma before crossing the thylakoid. Therefore the statement that chloroplast import requires unfolded precursors at the envelope can be compatible with the statement that a thylakoid pathway transports folded substrates. The apparent contradiction disappears when the two different boundaries are identified. An exam question that fails to specify whether it refers to the envelope or the thylakoid may be testing exactly this distinction.

A further thylakoid sorting system involves chloroplast signal recognition particle, abbreviated cpSRP. It participates in targeting important classes of thylakoid membrane proteins, including many light-harvesting chlorophyll-binding proteins delivered through the stroma. The proteins subsequently interact with insertion machinery such as Alb3, which is related to bacterial membrane protein insertases. The cpSRP pathway uses its own set of factors and must be distinguished from the cytosolic SRP system directing nascent secretory proteins to the ER. Both involve recognition and delivery of substrates to membranes, but their location, clients and detailed mechanism differ.

Not every thylakoid membrane protein uses cpSRP, Sec or Tat in the same way. Some proteins synthesized within the chloroplast are inserted in association with the organelle's own translation machinery. Some relatively simple membrane proteins can insert by other routes. Likewise, plastid envelope proteins may have targeting information that differs from a conventional cleavable stromal transit peptide. The central conceptual distinction is between import from the cytosol into the organelle and sorting from the organelle interior to a specific internal membrane or lumen. Detailed cataloguing of all exceptional pathways is unnecessary to understand this logic.

Consider a precursor with two signal regions, A and B. Region A directs transport across the envelope; region B directs a soluble lumen protein to Tat. Deleting region A ordinarily prevents the precursor from reaching the stroma, so the Tat signal may never encounter its receptor. Deleting region B while retaining a functioning region A may allow accumulation in the stroma but prevent normal lumenal delivery. Mutating the Tat recognition motif could generate a similar second-stage defect. If the molecule depends on both processing events, gels may show precursor, intermediate and mature products. The intermediate is not simply a partially translated protein; it can be a correctly translated chain from which the first targeting sequence has been removed.

A protease-protection assay designed to distinguish these stages needs more than one membrane condition. An intact chloroplast can shield both a stromal precursor and a lumenal mature product from external protease. Rupturing the envelope while leaving thylakoids intact exposes the stromal protein but may preserve protection of the lumenal protein. Solubilizing the thylakoid membrane removes that protection for susceptible proteins. Markers for each compartment and controls for membrane damage are essential. These nested barriers give the experiment its discriminatory power. Simply reporting that a protein is protected in an intact chloroplast would not distinguish its location in stroma from its location in lumen.

The energy distinction can also be used experimentally. If envelope import is supported but the thylakoid proton motive force is selectively diminished, a Tat-dependent precursor may accumulate in the stroma rather than reach the lumen. A Sec-dependent substrate has a different principal energy requirement, although physiological perturbations may affect multiple aspects of chloroplast function. If a treatment changes both ATP availability and thylakoid energization, the resulting phenotype cannot automatically be assigned to Tat alone. Comparing appropriate pathway-specific substrates and monitoring the relevant energy variables improves causal inference.

The coordinated assembly of photosynthetic machinery brings these routes together. A nuclear gene can encode a protein that first receives an envelope targeting extension, then crosses or inserts into the thylakoid, and finally associates with products synthesized in the plastid. The final arrangement places catalytic and structural components in the orientation needed for energy conversion. A failure in import or internal sorting can disturb photosynthesis even when transcription, translation and the catalytic domain of a particular protein are unchanged. This is one reason why correct localization is a biologically essential aspect of protein maturation.

13. Comparative evidence and the endosymbiotic perspective

The coexistence of organelle and nuclear genetic systems provides a mechanistic link between molecular evolution and modern protein targeting. Mitochondria and plastids retain features associated with their bacterial ancestry, including internal gene expression and, in certain contexts, membrane insertion or export systems related to bacterial machinery. Nuclear-encoded organelle proteins require additional transport across the boundary separating the host cytosol from the organelle. Evolution of such delivery systems made it possible for proteins encoded outside an organelle to function inside it. Gene transfer without corresponding targeting would not have preserved the original protein function.

Comparing plastids and mitochondria reveals convergent constraints rather than identity of every molecular solution. Both organelles contain double boundary membranes. Both commonly use amino-terminal extensions that are removed following import, and both must maintain substrate conformations compatible with passage through channels. Both employ energy-dependent processes that cooperate with recognition and translocation. Nevertheless, their receptors, translocons and energetic contributions differ. The canonical mitochondrial matrix pathway uses the inner membrane electrical potential, while chloroplast envelope transport involves GTPase receptors and ATP-dependent import without the same electrical driving mechanism. The similarities arise partly because both solve the problem of crossing two membranes, while the differences reflect their distinct evolutionary origins and membrane physiology.

These systems also illustrate why one cannot classify every targeting sequence by counting only positively charged residues. A transit peptide with a particular arrangement of polar, basic and hydrophobic residues may be preferentially recognized by a plastid receptor. A mitochondrial presequence may form a distinctive amphipathic helix. Some natural proteins can be directed to both organelles in plant cells, through ambiguous targeting signals or other means of generating distinct protein forms. The existence of dual targeting is evidence that specificity is not an infallible binary decision based on a universal consensus motif. It does not invalidate the useful typical patterns learned from canonical substrates.

A reporter bearing a chloroplast signal and a reporter bearing a mitochondrial signal can be expressed in the same plant cell. If the reporters show different compartment distributions, the result supports discrimination by the cell's targeting systems. If a hybrid signal produces both distributions, one must distinguish genuine dual targeting from experimental artifacts. Overexpression could saturate one route, the tag could affect recognition, and closely apposed organelles can be difficult to distinguish by low-resolution microscopy. The strongest interpretation uses independent organelle markers, suitable expression levels, and where appropriate biochemical measurements of the protected reporter in isolated fractions.

Finally, the import apparatus depends on imported proteins in a recurring way. Many components of TOM, TIM, TOC and TIC are themselves nuclear encoded. Their delivery and assembly require previously existing organelles or suitable membrane-biogenesis pathways. Cells inherit organelles and their machinery through growth and division and renew them by supplying newly synthesized components. This continuity is an important implication of compartmentalization: the amino acid sequence of a targeting signal is useful only in a cell that possesses the machinery capable of interpreting it. Protein targeting is thus a property of the interaction between a cargo and its living cellular context, not a property of the sequence in isolation.

The address-transfer principle also underlies practical biotechnology. A researcher seeking to place a reporter or a useful enzyme in a plant plastid can choose a characterized transit peptide and test whether the fusion reaches the appropriate compartment. Correct localization matters because plastids provide a metabolic environment different from that of the surrounding cytosol. A successful delivery construct does not guarantee useful catalytic activity: the imported enzyme may still need an appropriate cofactor, assembly partner or compatible substrate. Conversely, loss of reporter fluorescence can reflect poor folding after delivery rather than absence of targeting. An effective engineering decision therefore tests transport and function separately, while respecting the biological characteristics of the selected plastid and host tissue.

14. Peroxisomes: importing folded proteins into a specialized compartment

Peroxisomes are membrane-enclosed organelles whose protein import system poses a different physical and biochemical problem. Unlike mitochondria and plastids, peroxisomes do not possess an independent genetic and translational apparatus. Their matrix enzymes and membrane proteins are supplied by genes expressed elsewhere in the cell. The matrix is important for metabolic reactions including aspects of fatty acid oxidation and hydrogen peroxide metabolism. Catalase provides a familiar example of the value of correct localization: it decomposes hydrogen peroxide, a reactive product that can arise during oxidative metabolic reactions. The advantage of concentrating relevant enzymes within a defined environment depends on the cell's ability to deliver them reliably.

The characteristic feature of peroxisomal matrix import is its capacity to admit proteins that have already achieved substantial native structure. Some substrates can enter as folded proteins, and in suitable circumstances protein complexes can be transported. This is a striking contrast with the narrow-chain translocation model of typical mitochondrial matrix import. A protein that is no longer able to unfold readily need not be excluded from peroxisomes if its targeting information remains accessible and the peroxisomal import machinery can accommodate it. The contrast should not be exaggerated into a claim that every peroxisomal protein must be folded or that every oligomer is imported intact. It is the demonstrated capacity for transport of folded cargo that distinguishes this system.

The best-known peroxisomal matrix targeting sequence is PTS1, or peroxisomal targeting signal type 1. Its classical example consists of the amino acids serine, lysine and leucine at the extreme carboxyl terminus of the protein, written SKL. Related terminal sequences can also function as PTS1 signals, depending on their context and recognition by the receptor. The position of the sequence is particularly important. The signal is not merely three letters anywhere in a long polypeptide. The receptor recognizes the terminal region, including features made available by the free carboxyl end. Moving SKL away from the end or placing a bulky fusion after it can prevent targeting even though the three residues themselves have not changed.

Unlike the usual mitochondrial presequence or chloroplast transit peptide, PTS1 is generally not removed from the mature cargo during import. Its retention does not prevent the imported matrix enzyme from functioning if the enzyme has evolved a compatible terminal region. The common signal is thus short, terminal and retained. A student should be able to distinguish these three features from the longer, often cleavable amino-terminal addresses of mitochondrial and plastid soluble precursors. However, peroxisomal targeting as a whole is not defined exclusively by PTS1.

A second major signal, PTS2, occurs near the amino terminus of certain matrix proteins. It is commonly described as a nine-residue motif with conserved basic and hydrophobic positions, rather than as a terminal SKL sequence. A typical consensus is represented as (R/K)(L/V/I)X5(H/Q)(L/A), where X can vary; actual recognized signals and sequence context can vary between organisms. PTS2 is recognized by a different receptor, Pex7. In some organisms and for some substrates, an amino-terminal region containing PTS2 is removed after import. In other cases it remains. Therefore, the statement that all peroxisomal signals are carboxyl-terminal and noncleavable is incorrect. The location, processing and receptor dependence of PTS2 distinguish it from the canonical PTS1 route.

Early experimental studies of firefly luciferase made the PTS1 concept especially clear. Gould and colleagues reported in 1989 that a conserved carboxyl-terminal tripeptide could direct proteins to peroxisomes. Deletion or alteration of the terminal sequence and the transfer of targeting information to other proteins helped establish that the terminal motif was a functional address. This work is analogous in experimental logic to the transfer of a nuclear localization signal to a cytoplasmic reporter. In both cases, the scientific issue was whether an identifiable protein feature could cause delivery to a compartment. The two signals nevertheless differ in chemical character, positional constraints and transport machinery.

An especially useful experimental comparison involves a fluorescent reporter ending in SKL and another otherwise equivalent reporter in which SKL is followed by several additional amino acids. If only the terminal-SKL fusion displays a punctate pattern coinciding with a validated peroxisomal matrix marker, the observations support the importance of the carboxyl-terminal position. Total reporter abundance and solubility should also be checked. A punctate distribution by itself is not sufficient, because protein aggregates, other organelles and fluorescent inclusions can also produce spots. Colocalization, dependence on the known receptor and biochemical accessibility can help establish that the fluorescent structures are genuine peroxisomes containing imported cargo.

Peroxisomal membrane proteins present a separate targeting problem. A soluble PTS1-bearing enzyme must cross the peroxisomal membrane to enter the matrix; an integral membrane protein must instead become correctly inserted into the membrane. The routes for these two classes of cargo differ. Pex19, Pex3 and, in relevant organisms, Pex16 are familiar examples of factors involved in peroxisomal membrane protein targeting and biogenesis. Some membrane proteins reach peroxisomes through routes involving other cellular membranes, including the ER. These facts do not mean that a typical PTS1 matrix enzyme enters the ER first. The cell uses distinct pathways to build the boundary and to fill the interior.

A further exception to simple one-cargo-one-signal reasoning is piggyback import. In suitable circumstances, a matrix protein lacking its own recognizable targeting motif can associate with another protein that carries a functional signal, and the complex can be delivered to the peroxisome. This is particularly plausible in a system that can accommodate folded assemblies. It does not imply that every untargeted protein will be admitted merely because it binds some peroxisomal component in a test tube. The interaction must persist at the relevant time and must not obstruct the recognition feature. Comparing localization of an untagged partner in the presence and absence of a targeted binding partner can test this possibility.

15. The Pex receptor cycle and its energetic logic

Peroxins are proteins involved in peroxisome assembly, maintenance or import and are commonly named Pex followed by a number. The matrix import machinery includes soluble receptors, docking components, factors that support passage across the membrane, and enzymes that enable receptor recycling. For PTS1-containing cargo, Pex5 is the central receptor. Pex5 binds the targeting region in the cytosol, and the cargo-receptor complex then associates with the import machinery at the peroxisomal membrane. The receptor can accommodate a folded cargo, provided the signal is available for recognition.

Pex7 recognizes PTS2-containing substrates. Productive delivery in this pathway commonly requires additional partners, which vary between organisms; for instance, a long form of Pex5 serves as a Pex7-associated coreceptor in mammals. In some fungi, other Pex proteins perform a related coreceptor function. For examination purposes, Pex5 and Pex7 should be associated respectively with the characteristic PTS1 and PTS2 recognition systems, while the contribution of a coreceptor explains why the two routes are not necessarily independent at every later stage. A Pex5 defect can affect more than one class of cargo in organisms where Pex5 is also required for productive PTS2 delivery.

The cargo-receptor complex interacts with docking components such as Pex13 and Pex14. The translocation process is unusual because it permits cargo passage without obliging every substrate to unfold into a narrow extended chain. The import machinery can generate a selective transport environment adapted to these substrates. Different models have been proposed for the architecture and dynamics of this conduit, including evidence for a selective environment associated with Pex13. At the required examination level it is sufficient to understand receptor-dependent docking and translocation, the capacity for folded cargo import, and the subsequent need to recycle the receptor. It would be misleading to draw the apparatus as a permanently open channel allowing all cytosolic proteins to diffuse freely into the matrix.

After cargo delivery, Pex5 must be made available for further rounds of import. This involves a reversible ubiquitin modification of the receptor and ATP-dependent extraction from the membrane-associated import machinery. In canonical systems, monoubiquitination at a conserved cysteine of Pex5 contributes to receptor recycling. The AAA ATPases Pex1 and Pex6 use ATP to support extraction of the receptor back to the cytosol. The ubiquitin tag can then be removed and the receptor reused. These steps connect receptor recycling to chemical energy consumption. A defect in the extraction machinery may reduce the cytosolic supply of free receptor and progressively compromise import of subsequent cargo molecules.

This mechanism gives a different example of ATP dependence from the ATP-driven mitochondrial import motor. Matrix Hsp70 acts in close association with the translocating mitochondrial precursor. In the peroxisomal system, a major identified ATP-dependent step is receptor extraction and recycling. Energy expenditure need not coincide spatially or temporally with the passage of every cargo molecule. A treatment that inhibits ATP hydrolysis can interfere with the complete import cycle even if initial cargo binding and some early delivery steps remain possible. The distinction between single-round transfer and sustained flux is therefore important.

The role of ubiquitin is equally instructive. Ubiquitin can act as a reversible regulatory modification rather than an automatic instruction to destroy a protein. In the normal Pex5 recycling cycle, a defined ubiquitin modification facilitates reuse of the receptor. Other ubiquitination outcomes can contribute to receptor quality control or organelle removal when the cycle fails, but these should not be conflated with the ordinary recycling signal. A student who learns ubiquitin exclusively as a proteasomal degradation tag will have difficulty interpreting the Pex cycle. This observation also prepares the transition to the next chapter on protein degradation, where the meaning of ubiquitin depends on its attachment pattern and biological context.

A comparison between defects in cargo recognition and receptor recycling illustrates distinct mechanistic phenotypes. A mutation in a Pex5 cargo-binding region could prevent efficient capture of a PTS1 protein even when Pex5 itself remains soluble and abundant. A mutation impairing docking could allow cargo-receptor binding in the cytosol but reduce association with the peroxisomal membrane. A defect in Pex1 or Pex6 could permit early binding and docking but impair return of the receptor, changing its distribution and eventually reducing import. The broad endpoint, cytosolic accumulation of a matrix enzyme, can therefore result from different molecular failures. Identifying the affected step requires additional information.

A receptor-cargo binding assay and a fractionation assay can be combined in a hypothetical problem. In a first mutant, Pex5 fails to associate with a reporter carrying an exposed SKL motif, despite normal amounts of both proteins. In a second mutant, association is retained, but receptor accumulates in a membrane fraction and repeated import cycles are inefficient. The first observation points toward defective recognition, while the second is more consistent with a defect after binding, possibly in recycling. Neither result independently proves the identity of the mutated gene. Restoring a candidate wild-type protein and examining rescue of both the molecular defect and cargo localization would strengthen the interpretation.

Some peroxisome biogenesis disorders illustrate the biological significance of this machinery. Defects in particular PEX genes can impair import of many matrix enzymes, affecting multiple peroxisome-dependent metabolic processes rather than one enzymatic reaction alone. In contrast, loss of an individual peroxisomal enzyme may primarily disrupt the metabolic reactions in which that enzyme participates. The two types of mutation can have different biochemical consequences even when both reduce peroxisomal metabolic function. The relevant mechanistic distinction is between an organelle-wide protein-delivery defect and a defect in a particular cargo. Detailed clinical classification is beyond the scope of this chapter.

16. Comparing the four pathways with the secretory pathway

The preceding systems can be understood through a common set of questions. Where was the protein synthesized? What property identifies its destination? What receptor recognizes that property? What physical boundary must it cross? In what conformational state can it cross? Which energy-dependent events sustain the process? Is the targeting region retained or cleaved? Does the cargo require further sorting within the destination? These questions are more powerful than memorizing a disconnected catalogue of receptor names because they allow the same reasoning to be applied to a novel protein or an unfamiliar experiment.

The ordinary secretory route often begins when an amino-terminal hydrophobic signal emerges from a cytosolic ribosome. The cytosolic signal recognition particle interacts with the signal and guides the ribosome-nascent-chain complex toward the ER. Following docking, the nascent chain can enter the Sec61 translocon during translation. Secreted soluble proteins enter the ER lumen and subsequently move through the secretory system, while appropriate membrane proteins insert into membranes with specific orientations. The ER pathway also has posttranslational variants, so cotranslational delivery is characteristic rather than universal. Its key distinction here is that normal ER-to-Golgi traffic occurs after initial entry into the secretory system.

The four systems examined in this chapter generally do not require soluble nucleus-encoded matrix or nucleoplasmic proteins to enter the ER lumen and pass through the Golgi before reaching their destination. Instead, the relevant targeting information is interpreted from the cytosolic side of the destination's machinery. A nuclear localization signal permits gated transport; a mitochondrial presequence engages TOM and the appropriate downstream route; a chloroplast transit peptide engages TOC and TIC; and a peroxisomal terminal PTS1 is recognized by Pex5. Some membrane-biogenesis routes involve additional organelles, and some mitochondrial or plastid precursors may begin targeting before translation is complete, but these exceptions do not overturn the basic comparison.

The orientation of a targeting signal in a reporter is often diagnostic. A classical NLS can function when accessible at various positions. A typical mitochondrial presequence or stromal transit peptide is located at the amino terminus. The usual PTS1 requires the extreme carboxyl terminus. A generic fusion construct is therefore not equally suitable for all tests. Putting a reporter after a mitochondrial presequence preserves its amino-terminal position, whereas putting SKL after the reporter preserves its carboxyl-terminal position. The reverse arrangement may be informative as a negative control but cannot be assumed to preserve biological targeting.

The pathways use energy to solve different problems. Nuclear transport maintains a difference in receptor-loading conditions through the Ran GTPase cycle. Mitochondrial matrix delivery uses the electrical contribution of the inner membrane potential together with ATP-supported precursor handling and the matrix import motor. Plastid envelope import uses nucleotide-dependent receptor and motor activities without relying on the same inner membrane electrical mechanism; the thylakoid Tat system separately uses the proton motive force. Peroxisomal matrix import relies on an ATP-supported receptor recycling cycle. No single rule such as ATP pulls proteins through all membranes describes these systems correctly.

Protein targeting is not synonymous with membrane insertion. A soluble matrix enzyme must remain in an aqueous compartment after import. An inner membrane carrier must acquire a defined orientation in a bilayer. A nuclear transcription factor crosses a gate between aqueous compartments. A lumenal thylakoid protein must cross an additional membrane after envelope import. Sorting signals and later interactions together establish these different outcomes. A mutation can disrupt the second step even if the first targeting event remains fully functional.

The role of folding provides another unifying comparison. Nuclear and peroxisomal transport systems can accommodate folded protein cargos. Typical mitochondrial matrix and chloroplast envelope translocation requires a suitable unfolded or flexible state during channel passage. A chloroplast Tat substrate may subsequently fold before crossing the thylakoid membrane. The physical requirements of transport therefore depend on the boundary being crossed and the machinery used, rather than on whether the protein belongs to an organelle with one or two membranes. This is why a folding-stabilizing ligand can be a useful probe of one pathway but cannot be assumed to inhibit all cellular protein transport.

17. Quantitative localization and causal interpretation

The concentration of a protein in a compartment cannot always be inferred from its fraction of the total cellular amount. Suppose a cell contains 600 molecules of a protein in the nucleus and 400 in the cytoplasm. If the nuclear volume is 200 cubic micrometres and the cytoplasmic volume is 800 cubic micrometres, the average concentrations are respectively three and one-half molecules per cubic micrometre. The concentration ratio is six even though the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic molecule-number ratio is only 1.5. Volume normalization is therefore important when comparing microscopy measurements or compartment fractions. Fluorescence intensity per unit volume can serve as a relative concentration measure only if brightness, optical sampling and measurement conditions are comparable.

A simple kinetic model can clarify nuclear shuttling. Let C and N denote the numbers of freely exchanging cargo molecules in the cytoplasm and nucleus. Suppose no synthesis or degradation occurs during the observation interval and that the effective import and export fluxes are k_in C and k_out N, with both rate constants expressed in inverse time. The nuclear amount changes according to dN/dt = k_in C - k_out N. At steady state the opposing fluxes are equal, so N/C = k_in/k_out. Since the total number is fixed, the fraction in the nucleus equals k_in divided by the sum of the two rate constants. This model treats volumes, binding and transport capacities as already incorporated into the effective rates.

With an import rate constant of 0.30 per minute and an export rate constant of 0.10 per minute, the predicted steady-state nuclear fraction is 0.75. Halving the export constant to 0.05 per minute while leaving import unchanged raises the fraction to approximately 0.857. Increasing import could also raise the nuclear fraction, so an increase in nuclear fluorescence alone does not identify the changed rate. The relaxation time in this linear model depends on the sum of the rates; the same endpoint can be reached through different time courses. These calculations are hypothetical examples, not universal measurements of nuclear transport.

A second simple model distinguishes cargo production from receptor capacity. If an organelle receives 100 newly synthesized precursor molecules per minute but can productively deliver at most 70 per minute, the system cannot sustain complete delivery of that supply. In the absence of other losses, at least 30 molecules per minute accumulate outside the destination after the transport machinery reaches capacity. If a quality-control mechanism removes the excess precursor, its concentration may not rise indefinitely. The steady-state cytosolic abundance could even remain modest while a large fraction of newly synthesized protein is lost. An assay that measures only the concentration of accumulated precursor might therefore underestimate the severity of an import bottleneck.

These models clarify why import failure and degradation are closely related. An incorrectly localized precursor can expose surfaces that would normally be buried after assembly or fail to acquire stabilizing partners present in the destination. Cells may recognize such material and remove it. Consequently, a signal mutant might yield less mature protein without producing a readily detectable cytosolic band. To investigate the cause, one can compare newly synthesized material, total abundance and compartment distribution at multiple time points. An increase in precursor accumulation after inhibiting an appropriate degradation pathway can support the idea that failed delivery is followed by removal, although secondary effects of the treatment must also be considered.

Finally, experiments that alter one targeting feature should be interpreted at the level supported by their controls. A reporter fusion tests whether a sequence can specify location in a particular construct. Colocalization identifies a candidate compartment at the resolution of the imaging method. Fractionation tests association with a biochemical fraction but can be affected by contamination. Protease protection tests access across intact barriers but requires integrity controls. Processing demonstrates exposure to an appropriate proteolytic activity but may not prove complete translocation. Functional rescue tests whether the delivered product can restore a biological activity but may depend on events after delivery. The most persuasive scientific account combines several complementary observations into a coherent causal explanation.

The protein life cycle is now nearly complete. A newly synthesized molecule can become folded, undergo processing, reach a specified compartment and participate in an appropriate biochemical reaction. Yet neither successful delivery nor initial activity guarantees permanent residence. Proteins can become damaged, fail to assemble, change localization or cease to be needed. The cell therefore requires systems that selectively remove unwanted proteins and renew its functional proteome. Understanding the delivery pathways studied here makes it possible to distinguish a protein that was never correctly localized from one that reached its destination but was subsequently selected for turnover.

Part C: Analytical, experimental and quantitative practice

All numerical datasets and experimental scenarios below are hypothetical educational cases unless a historical study is explicitly named.

31. A 75-kDa reporter is cytosolic alone, nuclear when fused to a candidate sequence, and cytosolic when the candidate's key lysines are replaced. All constructs have equal abundance. What conclusion is strongest?

experimental interpretation

  1. The signal is sufficient in this reporter and its basic residues contribute
  2. Every native protein with a lysine cluster uses importin α
  3. RCC1 must directly bind the signal
  4. The reporter was translated in the nucleus
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. The signal is sufficient in this reporter and its basic residues contribute

The gain-of-function fusion and the mutation support signal-dependent localization under the tested conditions. They do not identify the receptor without binding or perturbation evidence, or show nuclear translation.

32. An NLS cargo binds importin α normally, but a mutation prevents association of α with β. Which early phenotype is most plausible?

experimental interpretation

  1. Normal classical pore passage and failed peptide cleavage
  2. Selective impairment of efficient receptor-mediated passage
  3. Increased mitochondrial membrane potential
  4. Unchanged transport because α must always act alone
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Selective impairment of efficient receptor-mediated passage

Cargo recognition survives but recruitment of the pore-interacting β subunit is defective. Classical import is therefore impaired despite intact NLS binding.

33. Nuclear Ran-GTP enrichment falls following a defect in nuclear RCC1. Which paired change is expected?

experimental interpretation

  1. Both cargo import and export necessarily accelerate
  2. Only Golgi transport is affected
  3. Import-complex release and CRM1 export-complex formation are impaired
  4. Pex5 monoubiquitination necessarily stops
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Import-complex release and CRM1 export-complex formation are impaired

Nuclear Ran-GTP normally favors import-complex disassembly and CRM1 export-complex formation. Reducing it disrupts both steps; effects on unrelated machinery are not established.

34. A factor accumulates in nuclei after signaling, but total protein is unchanged. Which observation best distinguishes faster import from slower export?

analytical

  1. A single endpoint image
  2. The genomic sequence alone
  3. A measurement of total DNA
  4. Time-resolved import and export measurements under controlled conditions
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Time-resolved import and export measurements under controlled conditions

Both kinetic changes can produce the same steady-state distribution. Separately tracking influx and efflux helps resolve the mechanism; endpoint localization cannot.

35. Hypothetical shuttling cargo has k_in = 0.24 min⁻¹ and k_out = 0.06 min⁻¹. Under the two-pool model, what is its steady-state nuclear fraction?

numerical

  1. 0.80
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.60
  4. 0.20
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. 0.80

At steady state the fraction is k_in/(k_in+k_out) = 0.24/0.30 = 0.80. The ratio N/C is 4, not the fraction. This assumes no synthesis or degradation and first-order exchange.

36. A nucleus contains 300 labelled molecules in 100 µm³, while cytosol contains 900 in 600 µm³. What is the nuclear-to-cytosolic concentration ratio?

numerical

  1. 1/3
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 6
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 2

The nuclear concentration is 300/100 = 3 molecules/µm³; cytosol is 900/600 = 1.5 molecules/µm³. The ratio is 2. The number ratio 1/3 is not the concentration ratio.

37. A mitochondrial precursor is processed to a shorter form, but the mature-sized band is destroyed by protease added to intact mitochondria. Markers confirm membrane integrity. What is justified?

experimental interpretation

  1. Complete matrix import is established
  2. The precursor is necessarily translated in mitochondria
  3. Processing occurred, but complete protected delivery is not established
  4. All mitochondrial membrane proteins are absent
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Processing occurred, but complete protected delivery is not established

The band shift supports processing; external protease sensitivity demonstrates accessibility and prevents concluding complete protected import. An intermediate could have an exposed region.

38. Protein X resists protease in intact mitochondria, becomes sensitive after outer-membrane disruption, and matrix markers remain protected. X is soluble. Which compartment is most consistent?

experimental interpretation

  1. Cytosol
  2. Matrix
  3. Nucleus
  4. Intermembrane space
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Intermembrane space

The outer membrane protected X but the intact inner membrane still protects matrix markers. A soluble intermembrane-space location is the most consistent; membrane association would need additional checks for nonsoluble substrates.

39. The same mitochondrial presequence-DHFR fusion is imported in one sample but fails when a ligand stabilizes DHFR. An unrelated matrix cargo imports normally. What is best supported?

experimental interpretation

  1. A stable fold can obstruct import of the tested substrate
  2. The ligand destroys all mitochondria
  3. The presequence is absent from the inhibited construct
  4. All protein import requires unfolding
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A stable fold can obstruct import of the tested substrate

The signal and organelle are controlled; the cargo's ligand-stabilized fold is the relevant difference. The result supports a conformational barrier in this route, not a general block of all organelle import.

40. For an idealized +2 charge crossing Δψ = −0.120 V into the mitochondrial matrix, estimate zFΔψ with F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹.

numerical

  1. −11.6 kJ/mol
  2. −23.2 kJ/mol
  3. +23.2 kJ/mol
  4. −231.6 kJ/mol
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. −23.2 kJ/mol

2 × 96,485 × (−0.120) = −23,156.4 J/mol, or approximately −23.2 kJ/mol. The sign represents a favorable electrical contribution, not total import free energy.

41. In an ideal first-order import assay with k = 0.10 min⁻¹, what fraction is imported after 10 min?

numerical

  1. 10.0%
  2. 36.8%
  3. 63.2%
  4. 90.0%
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. 63.2%

f = 1 − e^(−kt) = 1 − e^(−1) ≈ 0.632. The remaining fraction is 0.368. This assumes a wholly competent pool and negligible competing loss.

42. A TIM22-specific defect strongly impairs a multi-pass mitochondrial carrier but a standard matrix preprotein remains imported. Which interpretation is most coherent?

experimental interpretation

  1. All mitochondrial proteins use TIM22
  2. TOM is completely absent
  3. Nuclear export has stopped
  4. Different cargo classes use different inner-membrane sorting pathways
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Different cargo classes use different inner-membrane sorting pathways

The carrier commonly uses TIM22 whereas the classical matrix protein uses TIM23/PAM. Preserved matrix import argues against generalized failure of all mitochondrial import.

43. A mitochondrion imports a matrix protein in the normal condition. A mutant produces a full-length band that is protease protected even after selective outer-membrane disruption. A matrix marker remains protected. What should be examined next?

experimental interpretation

  1. The processing peptidase or its cleavage site
  2. Whether SKL is at the C terminus
  3. Nuclear Ran-GTP hydrolysis
  4. Thylakoid proton gradient
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. The processing peptidase or its cleavage site

The full-length protein appears protected by the inner membrane despite absent processing. A processing defect is a plausible explanation, although internal localization and cleavage should be confirmed by independent evidence.

44. A chloroplast precursor binds the outer envelope normally in a mutant, yet no protected stromal product forms. Which inference is most defensible?

experimental interpretation

  1. The transit peptide is definitely missing
  2. A post-recognition import or processing step may be defective
  3. Every TOC receptor has disappeared
  4. The precursor must be a mitochondrial protein
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A post-recognition import or processing step may be defective

Normal association argues that at least some recognition remains. Failure after that point could involve transfer, translocation, energy or processing. The exact mutated component cannot be identified from these observations alone.

45. An otherwise functional chloroplast lumen precursor has an intact envelope transit peptide but its second Tat-targeting signal is disrupted. What is the expected primary defect?

experimental interpretation

  1. It is never synthesized
  2. It passes into the ER lumen
  3. It reaches stroma but is impaired in delivery to thylakoid lumen
  4. It must be delivered to the mitochondrial matrix
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. It reaches stroma but is impaired in delivery to thylakoid lumen

The first signal can direct envelope entry; the disrupted second signal impairs internal targeting. Accumulation or degradation of the stromal intermediate depends on the system.

46. Two chloroplast preparations import the same stromal reporter normally. Only the preparation with intact thylakoid proton motive force delivers a Tat-dependent reporter into the lumen. What is supported?

experimental interpretation

  1. Envelope transport requires the mitochondrial presequence potential
  2. The stromal reporter must be Tat dependent
  3. The nuclear pore is defective
  4. The thylakoid gradient is important for the specified second transport step
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. The thylakoid gradient is important for the specified second transport step

The stromal control establishes that envelope transport remains competent. The selective effect is consistent with Tat's dependence on the thylakoid proton motive force.

47. A chloroplast precursor becomes a smaller intermediate protected in intact chloroplasts but sensitive after envelope rupture, while lumenal markers remain protected. What is the best explanation?

experimental interpretation

  1. The intermediate is consistent with stromal localization rather than lumenal delivery
  2. The intermediate must be cytosolic
  3. The intermediate must be inside the thylakoid lumen
  4. The protein never encountered a processing enzyme
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. The intermediate is consistent with stromal localization rather than lumenal delivery

Envelope integrity explains protection; once it is removed the protein is accessible while lumen markers remain protected. Processing plus protection supports an internal intermediate, not completion of the second step.

48. A folded reporter ending in SKL localizes to peroxisomal matrix. Adding a bulky tag after SKL disrupts localization despite similar protein abundance. Which mechanism is most consistent?

experimental interpretation

  1. The reporter no longer contains serine
  2. The terminal position or accessibility needed for PEX5 recognition is disrupted
  3. The tag abolishes all organelle ATP synthesis
  4. The signal has become an NLS
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The terminal position or accessibility needed for PEX5 recognition is disrupted

PTS1 recognition depends on the extreme carboxyl-terminal presentation. The sequence is still present but its functional context changed. Equal abundance helps exclude a simple synthesis difference.

49. In a peroxisomal mutant, Pex5 binds PTS1 cargo normally and reaches the membrane, but accumulates there; sustained import falls. Which class of defect is most consistent?

experimental interpretation

  1. Cytosolic ribosome initiation only
  2. Nuclear export via CRM1
  3. ATP-dependent Pex5 extraction/recycling
  4. Deletion of the cargo's SKL motif
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. ATP-dependent Pex5 extraction/recycling

Recognition and docking are observed; recycling is the likely downstream problem. Pex1/Pex6 or associated steps should be investigated rather than claiming a specific gene from localization alone.

50. A mammalian PEX7-deficient cell imports a standard PTS1 reporter efficiently but fails to import a validated PTS2 reporter. Which interpretation fits?

analytical

  1. Pex7 is the common PTS1 receptor
  2. All peroxisomal matrix import has ceased
  3. PEX7 is required for TOM
  4. The PTS2 recognition route is selectively disrupted
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. The PTS2 recognition route is selectively disrupted

PEX7 recognizes PTS2. PTS1 relies on PEX5 and can remain functional. This comparison is more informative than observing only one cargo.

51. A hypothetical cell supplies 160 precursors/min and has a saturated productive import capacity of 120/min. If no alternative loss occurs, how many unimported molecules accumulate over 5 min?

numerical

  1. 200
  2. 600
  3. 800
  4. 40
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. 200

The excess rate is 160 − 120 = 40 molecules/min; multiplying by 5 min yields 200. The result assumes constant rates and no degradation.

52. An isolated organelle assay reaches a plateau of 60% of the starting pool. If the competent fraction follows first-order k = 0.2 min⁻¹, what percentage of the original pool is imported at t = 5 min?

numerical

  1. 63.2%
  2. 37.9%
  3. 60.0%
  4. 22.1%
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 37.9%

The imported fraction is 0.60 × (1 − e^(−1)) = 0.3792, or about 37.9%. The remaining 40% is assumed incompetent. A plateau alone does not identify why that fraction is incompetent.

53. At steady state a shuttling reporter is 75% nuclear. If k_in = 0.15 min⁻¹, what is k_out under the simple two-pool model?

numerical

  1. 0.45 min⁻¹
  2. 0.20 min⁻¹
  3. 0.05 min⁻¹
  4. 0.1125 min⁻¹
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. 0.05 min⁻¹

N/C = 0.75/0.25 = 3 = k_in/k_out; k_out = 0.15/3 = 0.05 min⁻¹. Multiplying 0.75 by k_in is not the appropriate relationship.

54. A cell expressing a signal-deletion mutant has low total cargo and almost no mature organelle product. Briefly reducing precursor degradation reveals a cytosolic mutant band. Which explanation fits best?

analytical

  1. The mutation necessarily prevents transcription
  2. The protein is synthesized exclusively inside the organelle
  3. Processing and protein degradation are identical events
  4. Failed targeting may be followed by removal of mislocalized precursor
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Failed targeting may be followed by removal of mislocalized precursor

The appearance of cytosolic precursor when degradation is reduced supports synthesis followed by loss. It does not by itself specify the degradation machinery or prove that the signal deletion has no additional folding effects.

55. A ligand locks four reporters into stable folded domains without masking their signals: NLS, mitochondrial presequence, plastid stromal transit peptide, and PTS1. Which comparison is most reasonable for their canonical routes?

integrative

  1. Mitochondrial and plastid envelope entry may be preferentially impaired; nuclear and peroxisomal routes can accommodate folded cargo
  2. All four must stop equally because all membranes are lipid
  3. Only the PTS1 reporter necessarily fails because Pex5 must unfold it
  4. Only nuclear entry necessarily fails because pores accept solely unfolded chains
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Mitochondrial and plastid envelope entry may be preferentially impaired; nuclear and peroxisomal routes can accommodate folded cargo

The canonical mitochondrial and plastid envelope systems require translocation-compatible cargo. Nuclear and peroxisomal import can admit folded proteins, although particular reporters and complexes may have size, accessibility or receptor limitations.

Revision and active recall

Worked models and assumptions

Electrical contribution: ΔGelec = zFΔψ, with Δψ defined as destination minus origin in volts, F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹ and z a signed charge number. For +3 and −0.150 V, ΔG ≈ −43.4 kJ mol⁻¹. This is not total import energy.

First-order delivery: f(t) = q[1 − exp(−kt)], q is competent fraction, k is in inverse time. If q = 1, k = 0.20 min⁻¹ and t = 5 min, f = 0.632. The half-time of the competent pool is ln 2/k.

Shuttling: dN/dt = kinC − koutN. With conserved total and first-order exchange, N/(N+C) = kin/(kin+kout) at steady state. Concentration ratio = (N/VN)/(C/VC).

Capacity: unimported accumulation = (supply − productive delivery) × time, if the difference is constant and no competing loss occurs.

High-yield distinctions and examination traps

Gene location is not translation location
Most mitochondrial and plastid proteins are nuclear encoded and translated in the cytosol; the organelle genomes encode a subset.
Targeting is not complete import
Surface binding is compatible with a translocation defect. Require appropriate internal accessibility evidence.
NLSs are not universally terminal or cleaved
Classical NLSs can be internal and are usually retained.
No absolute pore size cutoff
Passive nuclear exchange depends on size, shape and observation time.
Ran is not a mechanical pore motor
The nucleotide gradient biases receptor loading and release; hydrolysis is mainly associated with recycling on the cytoplasmic side.
Ran-GTP acts differently on import and export
Nuclear Ran-GTP favors import-complex disassembly but export-complex assembly for CRM1.
TOM is not TIM
TOM crosses the outer mitochondrial membrane; the inner pathway is cargo dependent.
Processing alone is insufficient
A presequence may be cleaved before the complete chain is protected.
The mitochondrial potential is not the chloroplast envelope driving force
Chloroplast thylakoid proton motive force is a distinct gradient used by Tat.
All mitochondrial proteins do not use TIM23
Carriers, outer-membrane proteins and IMS substrates have alternate pathways.
Plastid import is not identical to thylakoid sorting
A stromal protein has crossed the envelope but not necessarily the thylakoid.
A folded cargo is not universally import-incompetent
Nuclear pores and peroxisomal matrix import can accommodate folded proteins; thylakoid Tat is another example.
SKL must be presented at the extreme C terminus in the canonical route
Adding a tag after SKL can disrupt Pex5 recognition even though SKL remains present.
PTS2 differs from PTS1
PTS2 is near the amino terminus and is recognized by Pex7, with relevant coreceptors; processing varies.
Ubiquitin does not always mean degradation
Pex5 monoubiquitination participates in normal recycling.
A fluorescent endpoint is not a transport rate
Steady-state distribution depends on import, export, binding, volumes, synthesis and removal.

Active recall

1. What are the three distinct locations associated with a nuclear-encoded mitochondrial protein?

Its gene is nuclear, its translation is ordinarily cytosolic and its function is mitochondrial.

2. How would you test whether a targeting signal is necessary and sufficient?

Compare a native signal mutant and transfer the signal to a suitable reporter, with expression and compartment controls.

3. Why can folded nuclear proteins enter the nucleus?

Receptor-mediated transport through an aqueous selective nuclear pore does not require ordinary membrane-channel threading.

4. How does Ran establish directionality?

Nuclear RCC1 promotes GTP loading; cytoplasmic RanGAP promotes hydrolysis. Different receptor affinities create polarized cargo loading and release.

5. Name the canonical mitochondrial matrix import sequence.

TOM → TIM23/PAM → processing and folding in the matrix.

6. What distinguishes a protected IMS protein from a protected matrix protein?

Selective outer-membrane disruption exposes the IMS protein while an intact inner membrane still protects matrix cargo.

7. Why can a folded DHFR reporter reveal a translocation constraint?

Ligand stabilization of the folded domain can block passage of a correctly targeted mitochondrial precursor.

8. What distinguishes TOC from TIC?

TOC operates at the outer plastid envelope and TIC at the inner envelope.

9. What is the difference between thylakoid Sec and Tat?

Typical Sec lumenal transport uses ATP-assisted passage of unfolded cargo; Tat can transfer folded cargo using the thylakoid proton motive force.

10. Why is a reporter-SKL fusion different from SKL-reporter?

The canonical PTS1 requires an exposed extreme C terminus.

11. What is the principal receptor recycling step in peroxisomal matrix import?

Reversible Pex5 ubiquitination followed by ATP-dependent extraction involving Pex1/Pex6.

12. What can a low mature-protein level mean besides reduced synthesis?

Failed import, defective processing or assembly, or enhanced removal; additional measurements distinguish these.

Student mastery checklist

  • Explain why a functional catalytic domain can be biologically ineffective in the wrong compartment.
  • Distinguish signal sequence, receptor recognition, translocation, processing and final sorting.
  • Trace a classical NLS cargo through importin α/β and the Ran cycle.
  • Predict consequences of perturbing RCC1, RanGAP or cargo NLS accessibility.
  • Trace TOM/TIM23, distinguish TIM22, and predict effects of depolarization and ATP-motor failure.
  • Interpret precursor bands, protease protection and membrane integrity controls.
  • Explain TOC/TIC, SPP and secondary thylakoid Sec/Tat/cpSRP routes.
  • Distinguish PTS1 and PTS2, Pex5/Pex7 recognition and receptor recycling.
  • Calculate and interpret idealized import kinetics, electrostatic energy and two-pool localization.
  • Compare direct organelle import with ordinary secretory targeting and identify meaningful exceptions.
