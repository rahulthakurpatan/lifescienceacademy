1. The molecular design problem

Imagine pulling a tendon, bending a hair and breathing deeply. All three depend on proteins, yet the first must resist tensile force, the second must withstand bending and the third must reversibly bind and release a gas. Different biological tasks demand different architectures. The preceding nodes established amino-acid chemistry, backbone geometry, tertiary and quaternary organisation and folding energetics. Here we connect these ideas to biological work. Sequence constrains a conformational ensemble; the ensemble positions chemical groups and creates interfaces; local chemistry and dynamics determine the functions possible under particular environmental conditions. A native protein is not a frozen sculpture.

Four distinct functional properties

Stability is resistance to unfolding or dissociation. Affinity is the equilibrium preference for binding a partner. Specificity is preference for one partner over alternatives. Activity is the magnitude or rate of functional output. A mutation can leave a fold intact while damaging its catalytic site; a high-affinity carrier can be ineffective if it cannot unload. Biological selection rewards appropriate stability, affinity and dynamics rather than the greatest possible value of each.

Proteins achieve function through geometric positioning of residues, surface chemistry, repeated structural assemblies and communication between distant sites. Post-translational modification, ligand binding, membrane association and oligomerisation can modify these properties without altering the original sequence. These ideas explain why the same general globin fold can provide oxygen storage in one context and cooperative transport in another.

2. Fibrous and globular architecture

Fibrous proteins often have extended repetitive structures and assemble into higher-order filaments or fibrils. Their sequence patterns provide repeated interactions which distribute mechanical stress across many molecules. Mature fibres are often poorly soluble, but their precursors need not be. Globular proteins typically fold into compact domains with a less polar interior and a water-compatible surface; specialised cavities, loops and interfaces provide binding and catalysis. Soluble examples include myoglobin, haemoglobin and many enzymes. Membrane proteins may have hydrophobic external surfaces and polar internal passages; intrinsically disordered proteins can regulate interactions without a single stable tertiary structure.

Actin and tubulin illustrate that globular units can assemble into long polymers. The fibrous/globular distinction describes architecture, not an absolute classification. Mechanical properties also need precision: tensile strength is resistance to failure under tension, stiffness is resistance to deformation, elasticity is recovery after force removal, and toughness is energy absorbed before failure. Covalent cross-links prevent slippage; reversible contacts can redistribute stress. Fibre alignment gives tendon strong directional resistance, while crossed arrangements in skin resist stress from different directions. A mutation that blocks assembly can impair tissue function despite a normally folded isolated subunit.

3. Collagen triple helix: why Gly–X–Y matters

Collagen forms an important extracellular framework in tendon, bone, skin, cartilage and basement membranes. In the continuous collagenous region, the sequence repeats Gly–X–Y; X is often proline and Y often 4-hydroxyproline. Each of three chains adopts an extended left-handed polyproline-II-like helix, and the three chains wind into a right-handed triple helix. They are not three ordinary α helices. Every third side chain points towards the crowded central axis, so glycine, whose side chain is only hydrogen, is essential at many such positions. Proline limits backbone flexibility; hydroxyproline supports preorganisation and hydration through stereoelectronic effects. Interchain backbone hydrogen bonds, tight packing and conformational constraints stabilise the assembly. Saying that hydroxyproline stabilises collagen simply by forming extra direct hydrogen bonds is an oversimplification.

Type I collagen usually consists of two α1(I) and one α2(I) chains; α is a chain designation, not a statement of α-helical secondary structure. A typical fibrillar collagen molecule is roughly 300 nm long and 1.5 nm across. Type II and III commonly contain three identical chains of the respective types. A glycine substitution can disrupt packing, delay propagation, produce excess modification and alter fibril strength. The severity depends on position, substitution and assembly context.

Consider a heterozygous COL1A1 variant producing equal amounts of mutant and wild-type α1 chains. If assembly is random and independent, the fraction of type I trimers containing at least one mutant α1 is 1−(1/2)²=3/4; a variant in the single α2 chain gives 1/2 under analogous assumptions. Selective secretion, allele expression and quality control may change real fractions. This illustrates dominant-negative effects through multimer assembly.

An ordinary right-handed α helix has predominantly intrachain i to i+4 hydrogen bonds. Collagen has three extended chains with critical interchain interactions; it is a different kind of helix. Bone is a composite: the collagen matrix provides toughness and mineral supplies compressive stiffness. Neither phase alone reproduces the mechanical properties of intact bone.

4. Collagen biosynthesis and hierarchical assembly

Fibrillar collagen is synthesised as a soluble precursor and assembled outside the cell. This avoids uncontrolled intracellular fibril formation. Translation of prepro-α chains begins on ribosomes; the signal peptide directs entry to the rough ER and is cleaved to form pro-α chains. Terminal propeptides assist chain selection and solubility.

Modification inside the ER

Prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases act on accessible residues before triple helix completion. They require Fe²⁺, O₂ and 2-oxoglutarate; ascorbate helps maintain catalytically competent iron. The overall hydroxylation reaction is residue + 2-oxoglutarate + O₂ → hydroxyresidue + succinate + CO₂. Selected hydroxylysines are glycosylated with galactose or glucose–galactose. The modification pattern influences assembly and interactions. C-terminal propeptides align compatible chains, disulfides help association, and folding propagates generally from C to N terminus. HSP47 and other ER factors aid maturation; delayed folding may permit excessive modification.

Extracellular processing

Procollagen passes through the Golgi and is secreted. Extracellular N- and C-proteinases cleave terminal propeptides from fibrillar precursors, permitting staggered assembly. Short telopeptides remain. Copper-dependent lysyl oxidase oxidatively deaminates selected lysine or hydroxylysine side chains to aldehydes, releasing ammonia and hydrogen peroxide. Condensation reactions create intermolecular covalent links. Lysyl oxidase is not lysyl hydroxylase: the former acts primarily outside the cell and needs copper; the latter acts in the ER and needs iron and ascorbate support. Staggered packing produces the approximately 67 nm D-period of mature fibrils; this is not the length of one collagen molecule.

Type I supports tendon, skin and bone; type II is characteristic of cartilage; type III forms reticular fibrils in soft tissues and vessels; type IV forms flexible basement membrane networks rather than classic fibrils; type V regulates fibril diameter with type I; type VII forms anchoring fibrils. Type IV has interrupted collagenous regions and specialised terminal domains, enabling network junctions. Network formation also involves laminin and other matrix components.

Disease and experimental evidence

Vitamin C deficiency compromises hydroxylation and causes scurvy with weakened connective tissue. Many forms of osteogenesis imperfecta involve type I collagen quantity or quality. Ehlers–Danlos syndromes are heterogeneous defects of matrix proteins or processing, with vascular EDS often involving type III. Alport syndrome involves specialised type IV collagen networks. Copper handling defects can impair lysyl oxidase. Collagenase can open fibrillar helices to further proteolysis. Gelatin is denatured collagen and differs from mature cross-linked fibres.

Circular dichroism and thermal unfolding report helix integrity; electron microscopy shows fibril banding; mass spectrometry identifies modified residues; mechanical tests quantify tensile behaviour. Normal hydroxylation and triple-helix stability with low extracellular cross-link density suggest defective cross-link maturation, not necessarily defective folding. Pulse-chase labelling can distinguish delayed secretion from rapid extracellular loss.

5. α-keratin and other fibrous proteins

Mammalian α-keratins are intermediate filament proteins with a central α-helical rod and non-helical head and tail domains. In the rod, a heptad repeat a–b–c–d–e–f–g often has hydrophobic residues at a and d. This periodicity matches a hydrophobic face of an α helix; two right-handed α helices form a generally left-handed coiled coil. Electrostatic contacts can contribute to partner selection. Type I keratins are generally acidic and type II basic to neutral. Typical epithelial dimers contain one type I and one type II chain in parallel. Antiparallel staggered dimer association produces tetramers without overall polarity; lateral and longitudinal association produces approximately 10 nm intermediate filaments. Unlike actin or microtubules, these filaments do not require nucleotide hydrolysis to drive assembly.

Epithelial keratins connect to junctions and distribute mechanical stress. Hard hair and nail materials are reinforced by cysteine-derived disulfides, including cross-links with keratin-associated proteins. Reduction can break disulfides and oxidation reform them; temporary water-dependent shape changes also involve hydrogen bonds. Keratin KRT5 or KRT14 variants can weaken basal keratinocytes in epidermolysis bullosa simplex. More cross-linking can increase stiffness but does not automatically improve toughness.

Bird and reptile corneous beta-proteins (historically β-keratins) are distinct from mammalian α-keratin intermediate filaments. Silk fibroin is also β-sheet-rich but is not keratin. Its small Gly/Ala-rich repetitive sequences pack in antiparallel β-sheet crystallites, with less ordered regions contributing extensibility. Elastin has flexible hydrophobic domains and lysine-rich regions that form desmosine-related covalent cross-links. Stretching limits conformational freedom; relaxation restores a favourable ensemble. Entropic elasticity and hydrophobic effects help produce recoil. Collagen limits overextension and elastin provides resilience.

The sequence cues are useful but not absolute: Gly every third position suggests collagen, periodic hydrophobic heptads suggest a coiled coil, and small-residue repeats may favour fibroin β-sheet packing. Test the inference with structural and assembly evidence. Reducing disulfides, hydrolysing peptide bonds and disrupting noncovalent contacts act at different levels of organisation.

6. Ligand binding: from mass action to free energy

A ligand is an ion or molecule that binds a protein, such as O₂ in globins, a hormone in a receptor or a substrate in an enzyme. Binding depends on complementary geometry and chemistry, solvent displacement, entropy loss and possible conformational change. Specificity reflects relative binding free energies and ligand availability.

For P + L ⇌ PL, Kd=[P][L]/[PL] with free concentrations at equilibrium. Total protein PT=[P]+[PL]. Fractional occupancy θ=[PL]/PT. Substitute [P]=Kd[PL]/[L] to obtain θ=[L]/(Kd+[L]). At free ligand equal to Kd, occupancy is 1/2; at 9Kd it is 0.9, and at Kd/9 it is 0.1. Lower Kd means higher equilibrium affinity under matched conditions. Independent identical sites each follow this hyperbola regardless of the number of sites.

Kinetics and thermodynamics

For a one-step mechanism d[PL]/dt=kon[P][L]−koff[PL]. At equilibrium Kd=koff/kon. Units: kon M⁻¹s⁻¹, koff s⁻¹. Mean residence time is 1/koff for a single exponential dissociation. Two proteins with equal Kd can have very different residence times. Standard binding free energy ΔG°=RT ln(Kd/c°), c°=1 M. A tenfold increase in Kd at 298 K gives ΔΔG°=RT ln 10≈+5.7 kJ/mol, i.e. less favourable binding.

Ligand depletion

When ligand is not in great excess, total ligand LT cannot substitute for free [L]. Let x=[PL]; Kd x=(PT−x)(LT−x). The physical solution is x=(PT+LT+Kd−√[(PT+LT+Kd)²−4PTLT])/2. For PT=LT=Kd=1 µM, x=(3−√5)/2=0.382 µM, occupancy 38.2%, not 50%. The other quadratic root is physically impossible because it exceeds available protein and ligand.

Competing ligands

If A and B are mutually exclusive at one site, the occupancy of A is ([A]/KA)/(1+[A]/KA+[B]/KB). A lower-affinity ligand can dominate when its concentration is sufficiently high. Stoichiometry is the site count, affinity is equilibrium binding strength, and activity is functional output. In enzyme kinetics Km is not generally Kd; equality requires particular kinetic limits. Avidity is the combined strength of multiple interactions and should be distinguished from single-site affinity.

7. Heme pocket and reversible oxygen chemistry

Myoglobin and haemoglobin share an α-helical globin fold surrounding heme b, iron protoporphyrin IX. Four porphyrin nitrogens coordinate Fe²⁺ in the plane. Proximal histidine (F8) supplies the fifth coordination bond, and O₂ can occupy the sixth site. Distal histidine (E7) lies near the bound oxygen, helps stabilise its geometry and affects ligand discrimination; it is not normally the fifth iron ligand.

In deoxyglobin, five-coordinate high-spin iron is displaced slightly from the porphyrin plane. Binding oxygen changes its electronic structure and draws it toward the plane. The Fe–O₂ bond has mixed electronic character, including Fe(III)–superoxide resonance contributions. Functional reversible oxygenation is distinct from oxidation to ferric Fe³⁺ metglobin, which cannot bind oxygen normally. Free heme is prone to unwanted reactions; the surrounding globin stabilises its environment and regulates access and selectivity. Heme is a prosthetic group; apoglobins lack heme and hologlobins contain it.

CO prefers a near-linear geometry while O₂ binds bent. Pocket sterics and electrostatics reduce CO preference relative to free heme, but CO still binds Hb much more strongly than oxygen. The distal histidine helps discriminate ligands, whereas proximal His communicates iron movement to the protein. A mutation can alter selectivity or autoxidation even if gross secondary structure is preserved.

8. Myoglobin: intracellular oxygen reservoir

Myoglobin is a compact monomeric globin, especially abundant in skeletal and cardiac muscle. Classic sperm whale myoglobin has 153 residues and one heme; each functional molecule binds one O₂. It buffers intracellular oxygen and can facilitate its movement, releasing it as intracellular oxygen pressure falls. Diving mammals often possess high muscle myoglobin concentrations and sequence adaptations limiting aggregation.

For dissolved oxygen at fixed temperature and solvent, free concentration is proportional to partial pressure. Thus Y=pO₂/(P50+pO₂), with P50 the pressure giving half saturation, not a concentration Kd. Myoglobin P50 is often a few mmHg; use 2.5 mmHg as an illustrative value. At p=10, Y=0.8; at 2.5, Y=0.5; at 0.5, Y=0.167. The hyperbola permits substantial occupancy at pressures where Hb is already unloading. It does not imply irreversible oxygen trapping.

One conventional O₂ site cannot display homotropic cooperativity between multiple sites on the same monomer. Its ideal equilibrium Hill slope is 1. Monomers can still show heterotropic allostery or multiple conformations. John Kendrew's myoglobin and Max Perutz's haemoglobin crystallography helped establish structural biology, recognised by the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The important advance was connecting atomic arrangements to physical function.

9. Haemoglobin architecture and T–R transition

Adult human HbA contains two α chains (141 residues each) and two β chains (146 each), with one heme per chain. It consists of two αβ dimers; contacts within dimers are relatively stable during oxygenation while contacts between dimers rearrange. Maximum capacity is four O₂ molecules per functional tetramer, not four oxygen atoms.

Haemoglobin must load oxygen in the lungs and release a substantial fraction in tissue. Very high affinity everywhere would impede unloading; low affinity everywhere would impair loading. A cooperative sigmoid curve gives a plateau at high pressure and a steep response in the intermediate range. Typical adult whole-blood P50 is about 26–27 mmHg at 37 °C, pH 7.4 and normal red-cell effector levels. This is not a universal purified protein constant.

Deoxy Hb preferentially occupies the lower-affinity T (tense) ensemble, with a relatively open central cavity and stabilising ion pairs. Oxy Hb favours higher-affinity R (relaxed) ensembles. Oxygen binding pulls Fe toward the porphyrin plane, moving the proximal His and the F helix. Changes in the heme pocket and helix corners propagate to the αβ dimer interfaces. In classical T-to-R structures the dimers rotate relative to each other by roughly 15°; contacts reorganise and the central cavity narrows. The binding free energy shifts the ensemble to higher-affinity states, facilitating further ligation.

T and R are conformational labels, not synonyms for zero and four oxygen ligands. Partial ligation can occur in either state. In unloading, oxygen loss favours lower-affinity conformations and promotes further release. No ATP is required. O₂ is a homotropic positive effector; H⁺, CO₂ and BPG are heterotropic negative modulators under typical physiological conditions. An allosteric ligand acts by coupling distinct sites, not necessarily by direct competition. A mutant at an interface can impair cooperativity while preserving local heme binding. Tetramer dissociation often reduces cooperative behaviour.

10. Oxygen binding curves and physiological delivery

A hyperbola on linear pressure axes becomes sigmoid-like on a logarithmic pressure axis, so inspect axes before inferring cooperativity. P50 is pressure at 50% fractional saturation. A right shift increases P50 and decreases affinity; a left shift does the reverse. At fixed pressure the high-affinity protein binds more oxygen, but oxygen delivery depends on the difference between loading and unloading saturation, blood flow and functional Hb concentration.

Suppose Hb loads at 97% saturation and unloads at 75%. The mean O₂ released is 4(0.97−0.75)=0.88 molecules per tetramer; a population average can be fractional though individual molecules bind integer ligand counts. At 15 g/dL Hb and oxygen capacity of 1.34 mL O₂/g Hb, bound oxygen difference is 1.34×15×0.22≈4.42 mL/dL.

Arterial oxygen content CaO₂≈1.34[Hb]SaO₂+0.003PaO₂ in mL/dL, with Hb g/dL, saturation as a fraction, pressure mmHg. At Hb=15, saturation 0.98 and pressure 100, content≈20 mL/dL. At Hb=7.5 with same saturation and pressure, content≈10.15 mL/dL. Normal saturation alone does not establish normal content. Oxygen delivery rate is arterial content times blood flow; tissue uptake is blood flow times arterial–venous content difference (Fick principle). A right shift can improve unloading at a fixed tissue pressure but impair loading when lung pressure is low. The balance matters.

11. Hill equation and microscopic versus macroscopic binding

The empirical Hill equation is Y=pⁿ/(P50ⁿ+pⁿ). Rearrangement gives Y/(1−Y)=(p/P50)ⁿ and log[Y/(1−Y)]=n log p−n log P50. The local Hill coefficient is d log[Y/(1−Y)]/d log p. Real Hb Hill plots are curved and approach slope 1 at extreme low and high pressures. A central slope around 2.8–3 is typical under some conditions, not a constant of four. nH=1 is consistent with independent equivalent sites; nH>1 indicates positive cooperativity in standard equilibrium analysis; nH<1 can indicate negative cooperativity or heterogeneous independent affinities. The slope is not the number of sites; under ordinary equilibrium assumptions it cannot exceed the number of sites.

Example: Y changes from 0.2 to 0.8 as p increases from 15 to 45 mmHg. Odds change from 0.25 to 4, a factor 16; slope=ln16/ln3≈2.52. With illustrative n=2.8 and P50=26, at p=13, Y≈0.126 and at 52, Y≈0.874.

Adair binding polynomial

For four sites Q=1+β₁p+β₂p²+β₃p³+β₄p⁴. βi is the cumulative macroscopic coefficient including statistical degeneracy (units pressure⁻ⁱ). Probability of i ligands is βi pⁱ/Q. Average ligand number is p d ln Q/dp, and Y=(β₁p+2β₂p²+3β₃p³+4β₄p⁴)/(4Q). This represents occupancy without imposing a particular structural mechanism.

For four independent equivalent sites with microscopic association coefficient k, Q=(1+kp)⁴=1+4kp+6k²p²+4k³p³+k⁴p⁴, so Y=kp/(1+kp). The macroscopic stepwise association constants are 4k, 3k/2, 2k/3 and k/4; differences arise from statistics alone. At kp=1, the probability distribution for 0,1,2,3,4 ligands is 1:4:6:4:1 divided by 16. Only 6/16 have exactly two even though mean occupancy is two. A single Hill slope cannot determine the distribution of partially ligated molecules.

12. Allostery: MWC, KNF and thermodynamic linkage

The Monod–Wyman–Changeux concerted model assumes a symmetric oligomer interconverting between T and R even without ligand. All subunits of an oligomer share one conformation, and within each state sites are independent and equivalent. The ligand stabilises the higher-affinity state and changes its population. Define L0=[T0]/[R0] in absence of ligand, KR and KT as state-specific dissociation pressures, c=KR/KT<1 and a=p/KR. For N sites the binding polynomial is Q=(1+a)ᴺ+L0(1+ca)ᴺ. R fraction=(1+a)ᴺ/Q. Fractional occupancy Y=[a(1+a)ᴺ⁻¹+L0ca(1+ca)ᴺ⁻¹]/Q. These expressions result from summing the state weights and differentiating the binding polynomial.

For N=4, L0=100, c=0.01, a=10, the R weight=11⁴=14641, T weight=100×1.1⁴=146.41; R fraction≈99.0%. At zero ligand R fraction=1/101≈0.99%. Yet fractional occupancy at a=10 is only about 90.1%: within R individual occupancy is 10/11 and within T 0.1/1.1. R population and saturation are distinct.

The Koshland–Némethy–Filmer sequential model emphasises ligand-associated local changes propagated through neighbours. Mixed subunit conformations are allowed; positive or negative cooperativity can result depending on interactions. Ideal MWC with equivalent sites and differing state affinities favours positive cooperativity. The sequential model is associated with induced fit, concerted with conformational selection; real proteins may combine both processes. Real Hb exhibits tertiary changes within quaternary ensembles and more complexity than either idealisation. An equilibrium curve alone cannot establish which kinetic pathway is used.

Thermodynamic linkage

For ligand L and effector E binding distinct sites, the free energy around a closed equilibrium cycle sums to zero. Coupling free energy ΔGcoupling=RT ln(Kd,L|E/Kd,L). If E weakens L affinity tenfold, the positive coupling is about 5.7 kJ/mol at 298 K; L must weaken E affinity by the corresponding factor under matched equilibrium conditions. This is reciprocal thermodynamic coupling, not direct competition. Allostery can occur in monomers as well as oligomers. A ligand can modify dynamics and affinity even without a large average structural change.

13. Bohr effect, Haldane effect and BPG

Active tissues generate CO₂ and acids. Carbonic anhydrase in red cells accelerates CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Protonation of particular groups preferentially stabilises deoxy Hb; classical examples include the βHis146 and βAsp94 interaction, though multiple residues contribute. Lower pH shifts the O₂ curve right and increases P50 (Bohr effect). At the lungs CO₂ is eliminated and oxygenation favoured. The Bohr coefficient Δlog10 P50/ΔpH is usually negative: a change from P50=32 at pH 7.2 to 24 at 7.4 gives log10(24/32)/0.2≈−0.625. It is not a pKa or Hill slope.

Most CO₂ travels as bicarbonate; smaller fractions travel dissolved or as carbamino compounds. CO₂ reacts with unprotonated globin amino termini to form carbamates and release H⁺; added negative charges can stabilise T. It does not mainly occupy heme oxygen sites. Bicarbonate exits red cells in exchange for chloride in tissues; the exchange reverses in lungs. Deoxygenated Hb carries more H⁺ and carbamino CO₂, and oxygenation promotes CO₂ release (Haldane effect). Bohr describes the effect of H⁺/CO₂ on oxygen binding; Haldane describes the effect of oxygenation on CO₂/proton carriage.

2,3-bisphosphoglycerate

Red-cell glycolysis produces 2,3-BPG through the Rapoport–Luebering shunt: 1,3-BPG → 2,3-BPG → 3-phosphoglycerate. Bypassing phosphoglycerate kinase forfeits a potential ATP-generating step. BPG binds preferentially in the positively charged central cavity between the β chains of deoxy Hb, involving βHis2, βLys82 and βHis143 among other contacts. One central site per tetramer is the conventional model. The cavity narrows with oxygenation, reducing BPG binding. BPG stabilises T, decreases O₂ affinity and increases P50 without occupying heme sites.

Stripped Hb often has higher affinity than Hb in red cells. Chronic hypoxia or anaemia can increase BPG, favouring unloading. Acute altitude exposure may initially produce respiratory alkalosis and left shift; later adaptations can oppose it. Net effects depend on conditions and timing. Higher temperature generally lowers oxygen affinity because oxygenation is overall exothermic under physiological conditions. Active muscle combines heat, CO₂ and acidity, promoting unloading. Ionic strength and chloride can modulate electrostatic interactions. Match pH, temperature and BPG before comparing P50 values.

14. Fetal Hb, HbS, metHb and other functional variants

HbF is α₂γ₂, whereas HbA is α₂β₂ and minor adult HbA₂ is α₂δ₂. The fetal γ chain has Ser143 rather than adult βHis143, among other differences, weakening BPG binding. HbF has greater affinity in the red-cell environment, aiding placental oxygen transfer. It still has four hemes and exhibits cooperativity. Oxygen moves down gradients; there is no Hb-driven oxygen pump.

HbS contains β6 Glu→Val in conventional mature-chain numbering. Replacement of a negatively charged surface residue by hydrophobic valine creates a patch that can associate with another deoxy tetramer. Deoxy HbS polymerises and distorts red cells; oxygenation disfavors polymerisation, but repeated cycles cause damage. Polymer formation is sensitive to intracellular concentration and ligation. This is primarily altered intermolecular assembly rather than destruction of the globin fold or heme. HbF interferes with HbS polymerisation. Thalassaemias instead primarily reflect insufficient production of α or β chains and stoichiometric imbalance, not necessarily a defective binding pocket.

CO binds ferrous heme strongly, removes sites from oxygen carriage and favours high-affinity states, impairing unloading from remaining sites. MetHb contains ferric Fe³⁺, unable to bind O₂ normally; normal erythrocytes limit its accumulation using reducing systems, especially NADH-dependent cytochrome b5 reductase. Arterial pO₂ measures dissolved O₂ and may remain normal even when Hb function is impaired. Co-oximetry distinguishes Hb species more directly than conventional pulse oximetry. These are mechanistic distinctions, not clinical diagnostic instructions.

A mutation stabilising R relative to T may increase affinity while impairing tissue delivery; a mutation stabilising T can decrease affinity. Other mutations alter heme incorporation, oligomer stability, or amount of functional protein. Thus maximum binding capacity, P50 and Hill slope must be interpreted separately.

15. Experimental structure–function analysis

X-ray crystallography reveals atomic arrangements under crystallisation conditions; cryo-EM can resolve suitable assemblies and conformational classes; solution NMR probes dynamics and local structure; circular dichroism reports ensemble-averaged secondary structure. A structure is not direct proof of physiological function. UV–visible spectra distinguish globin ligation and oxidation states; equilibrium oxygen tonometry measures saturation at controlled oxygen pressures; stopped-flow spectroscopy measures binding or release kinetics. Isothermal titration calorimetry can estimate enthalpy, affinity and stoichiometry for suitable ligands; equilibrium dialysis measures free versus bound ligand. SEC-MALS and analytical ultracentrifugation probe oligomeric mass. Electron microscopy and mechanical tests investigate fibres.

For single-site binding, a Scatchard plot B/F versus B follows B/F=(Bmax−B)/Kd, with bound ligand B and free ligand F. Slope −1/Kd and intercept Bmax/Kd. Curvature can reflect heterogeneous sites, cooperativity or artefact. Nonlinear fitting of original data is usually preferable because linear transformations distort errors.

Compare wild type and mutant at equal active concentration, temperature, buffer and effector concentrations. Verify heme incorporation and oligomer state before inferring allostery. A sample with decreased maximum oxygen uptake but an unchanged curve after normalisation to active sites may have lost functional hemes, not necessarily changed affinity. A mutant with unchanged P50 but lower Hill slope may have reduced coupling without a different half-saturation pressure. A binding curve measured before equilibrium can be misleading; check reversibility and equilibration time. Temperature-dependent apparent enthalpies include proton linkage and state redistribution, not simply a single bond energy.

16. The same design rules across protein biology

Antibody variable-domain loops recognise ligands while constant regions recruit partners; multivalent avidity differs from affinity. Enzymes position catalytic groups and stabilise transition states, but activity also depends on reaction chemistry and product release. Ion channels couple a selective pore to a gate; motor proteins couple nucleotide-linked conformation to mechanical work. Phosphorylation can add charge or docking sites, proteolysis can activate an enzyme, and glycosylation can change interactions. Intrinsically disordered regions can acquire structure upon binding. Flexibility is often a functional property.

Allosteric enzymes such as aspartate transcarbamoylase connect regulatory ligands to catalytic output. Do not equate an equilibrium oxygen occupancy curve with an enzyme velocity curve. For cooperative enzymes a half-maximal substrate concentration S0.5 or K0.5 is often used instead of assuming Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Detailed catalytic mechanisms belong to the subsequent enzyme curriculum.

The conceptual sequence of this sub-unit is now connected: amino-acid chemistry provides local interactions; backbone geometry limits conformations; higher-order organisation builds surfaces and assemblies; folding energetics explain accessible states; structure–function relationships explain how those states do biological work. The key prediction strategy is to identify the structural feature, determine the interaction it controls, predict a measurable property, and test the prediction with a matched experimental control.

C. Lysyl oxidase

Copper-dependent oxidative deamination enables cross-links.

B4. Basement membrane network collagen?

  1. Type I
  2. Type II
  3. Type III
  4. Type IV
Show answer and reasoning

D. Type IV

Type IV forms flexible networks rather than classic D-banded fibrils.

B5. Heptad a/d hydrophobic pattern suggests?

  1. Coiled coil
  2. Collagen triple helix
  3. DNA helix
  4. Heme porphyrin
Show answer and reasoning

A. Coiled coil

A periodic hydrophobic interface supports helix association.

B6. Polarity of keratin intermediate filaments?

  1. GTP-dependent polarity
  2. Apolar
  3. Plus-end only
  4. Minus-end only

B. Apolar

Antiparallel dimers eliminate overall polarity.

B7. Fifth coordination ligand in deoxyglobin?

  1. O₂
  2. BPG
  3. Proximal histidine
  4. Distal histidine
Show answer and reasoning

C. Proximal histidine

Four porphyrin nitrogens plus proximal His.

B8. MetHb contains?

  1. Copper
  2. Ferrous iron only
  3. No heme
  4. Ferric Fe³⁺
Show answer and reasoning

D. Ferric Fe³⁺

Ferric heme cannot bind oxygen normally.

B9. Increased BPG usually causes?

  1. Increased P50
  2. Decreased P50
  3. More hemes
  4. Irreversible binding
Show answer and reasoning

A. Increased P50

Preferential T-state stabilisation lowers affinity.

B10. Haldane effect describes?

  1. Fetal Hb has no iron
  2. Oxygenation influences CO₂/proton carriage
  3. CO₂ competes for all hemes
  4. Acid raises oxygen affinity
Show answer and reasoning

B. Oxygenation influences CO₂/proton carriage

Bohr describes the reverse dependence.

B11. HbF chain composition?

  1. α₂δ₂
  2. β₄
  3. α₂γ₂
  4. α₂β₂
Show answer and reasoning

C. α₂γ₂

Gamma chains bind BPG less strongly in the physiological setting.

B12. Hill slope 2.8 measures?

  1. 2.8 hemes
  2. P50
  3. Iron valence
  4. Steepness of log-odds response

D. Steepness of log-odds response

Not a literal number of sites.

B13. Ideal KNF permits what ideal MWC excludes?

  2. Equilibrium
  3. Binding
  4. Oligomers

A. Mixed subunit conformations

Sequential transitions need not affect all chains at once.

B14. HbS mechanism?

  1. No β chains
  2. Deoxy tetramer polymerisation through an altered hydrophobic patch
  3. Loss of all α helices
  4. Zn replaces Fe

B. Deoxy tetramer polymerisation through an altered hydrophobic patch

β6 Glu→Val modifies intermolecular association.

B15. Four independent equivalent sites yield?

  1. Exactly two ligands on every molecule at half saturation
  2. Sigmoid on linear axes
  3. Hyperbolic fractional occupancy
  4. Hill coefficient 4
Show answer and reasoning

C. Hyperbolic fractional occupancy

Stoichiometry increases capacity, not Hill slope.

B16. Which statement is correct?

  1. Kd always equals Km
  2. Affinity equals catalytic rate
  3. Low Kd always means low kon
  4. Kd and residence time are distinct
Show answer and reasoning

D. Kd and residence time are distinct

Kd=koff/kon; residence time=1/koff for a single step.

  4. Ligand strengthens effector affinity tenfold
