1. A protein must arrive at the place where it can work

Prerequisite reading: ER structure and function, Golgi organisation and intracellular transport.

The primary sequence of a protein is specified by the nucleotide sequence of its messenger RNA. But making the correct sequence is only one part of producing a functional cellular molecule. Consider an antibody secreted by a plasma cell, a receptor that binds a hormone at the cell surface, and an enzyme that catalyses a reaction in the cytosol. Their genes may be expressed in the same cell, using the same genetic code and the same general machinery for translation. Nevertheless, the three products must reach different physical environments. A soluble cytosolic enzyme is useful only if it has access to its substrates. An antibody must enter the extracellular fluid to bind antigens outside its cell of origin. A receptor requires a particular orientation in the plasma membrane so that its extracellular domain can bind a ligand while its intracellular domain communicates with cytosolic signalling molecules.

This requirement poses a problem that does not arise in the same way for a small molecule capable of readily crossing a membrane. A polypeptide contains a long backbone with polar peptide bonds and numerous side chains. Even a chain with many hydrophobic residues generally cannot simply diffuse through a lipid bilayer in its entirety. Moving such a polymer from an aqueous environment to the hydrophobic interior of a membrane is energetically unfavourable unless a suitable molecular mechanism is available. Integral membrane proteins present a second problem: some portions must occupy the lipid interior, while other portions must remain in water. Thus, the cell has to distinguish between moving a chain across a membrane and inserting selected segments into the membrane.

For the majority of conventional secreted and endomembrane proteins, the first destination is the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). The ER membrane separates the cytosol from an aqueous internal compartment called the lumen. A ribosome can remain entirely on the cytosolic side while its growing polypeptide enters the lumen. The ER is not a site of independent ribosomal translation comparable to the bacterial ancestry of mitochondria and chloroplasts. Its attached ribosomes are cytosolic ribosomes associated temporarily with protein-conducting machinery in its membrane.

A crucial principle is that ER targeting is not the same as secretion. Entering the ER gives a protein access to a series of subsequent destinations. A soluble protein may remain in the ER, be exported to a Golgi compartment, become a lysosomal hydrolase, or ultimately be released from the cell. An integral membrane protein may remain in an organelle membrane or be transported to the cell surface. The early targeting decision establishes which side of the membrane the chain occupies; subsequent sorting decisions determine which member of the endomembrane system receives it. Correct entry is necessary for many secretory proteins, but it is not sufficient for every later event.

The distinction is especially clear when analysing a mutant. Imagine that a cell synthesises normal amounts of an extracellular enzyme but releases none. It is tempting to conclude that translation has failed. Yet the enzyme may have been made correctly and retained in the cytosol because its targeting signal was missing. Alternatively, it may have entered the ER but failed to fold, reached the Golgi but been incorrectly sorted, or reached a secretory vesicle whose fusion was prevented. A single observation of reduced extracellular protein does not discriminate among these possibilities. Throughout this chapter, we will associate each proposed defect with a distinct intermediate that can be measured.

2. The physical meaning of a compartment and its topology

A membrane defines two surfaces even when it bends into a complicated shape. Imagine a sheet of lipid bilayer surrounding a small vesicle. One face contacts the surrounding cytosol, and the other faces the enclosed aqueous space. A vesicle generated from the ER can bud into the cytosol without reversing these faces. Its outer surface was previously exposed to cytosol; its inside was previously connected to ER lumen. Fusion with another organelle membrane joins the enclosed spaces, while the exterior surface remains exposed to cytosol.

This geometric property explains why the ER lumen is described as topologically equivalent to the extracellular space. Equivalence does not mean identical chemical composition, pH, protein content or ion concentration. It means that lumenal cargo can reach the outside by transport within enclosed compartments followed by fusion, without ever crossing another lipid bilayer. When a secretory vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, its lumen is opened to extracellular fluid. Its membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane, and the portions of proteins that faced the vesicle lumen now face the cell exterior. Cytosolic domains remain cytosolic throughout these events.

To use this principle, a student must distinguish the topological origin of a domain from the final position of the protein. A membrane protein can pass through the ER, Golgi and plasma membrane while keeping exactly the same arrangement of cytosolic and noncytosolic domains. Its whole molecule changes location, but its chain does not rotate through the bilayer during every transfer. Flipping a large charged protein domain across a membrane would require overcoming a large energetic barrier. The usual vesicular mechanism avoids this problem rather than repeatedly solving it.

An amino-terminal domain bearing a carbohydrate attached by lumenal glycosylation machinery illustrates the consequence. If the protein is delivered to the plasma membrane in its usual orientation, that carbohydrate will appear extracellularly. The carbohydrate itself does not dictate which side is extracellular; its location records an earlier event in the secretory pathway. A glycan originally added in the ER provides an experimental marker of access to the ER lumen, whereas later glycan processing can provide evidence of passage through subsequent compartments.

Not all intracellular protein movement follows this topology. Soluble cytosolic proteins can move through nuclear pores without crossing a phospholipid bilayer. Many mitochondrial precursor proteins cross mitochondrial membranes through dedicated translocators. A protein targeted to the ER generally enters a lumenal or membrane environment that is part of a distinct delivery system. These differences matter when predicting whether a targeting sequence will be cleaved, whether the chain must remain unfolded, and whether a domain can be modified before reaching its destination.

3. Free ribosomes and membrane-associated ribosomes

A common misconception arises from diagrams that place rough ER next to free ribosomes. The two groups can look like distinct machines. In reality, the ribosomes attached to rough ER are members of the same cytosolic ribosomal population used to produce soluble cytosolic proteins. The term rough refers to the appearance of the cytosolic face of the membrane when many ribosomes are attached. An individual ribosome is not permanently assigned to making secretory proteins.

For the conventional pathway, translation starts when the cytosolic translation machinery assembles on a suitable mRNA. As the chain grows from its amino end toward its carboxyl end, the sequence inside the ribosomal exit tunnel becomes progressively exposed. If an appropriate hydrophobic targeting segment emerges, it can be recognised by a targeting factor. That factor brings the ribosome together with its nascent chain and associated mRNA to ER membrane. Translation continues there while the chain is moved through a channel or inserted into the lipid bilayer.

This description also explains why the location of an mRNA alone cannot always predict the final destination of its product. Some mRNAs can remain enriched at the ER or use additional localisation mechanisms, and membrane-associated ribosomes may initiate further rounds of translation. The simple model in which all relevant messages first encounter freely diffusing ribosomes and then become targeted is a useful introduction to the canonical nascent-chain pathway, not an absolute account of every cellular translation event. The essential point is that ribosomal catalytic identity does not determine protein destination.

After termination, the newly made product is released from its tRNA. Ribosomes can be recycled, and their subunits remain available to participate in further initiation events. Protein targeting therefore changes the use and location of a ribosome without requiring the cell to construct a separate population of genetically different ribosomes for secretory translation.

This principle suggests an experiment. If the ribosomes themselves were the determinants of secretion, isolated free ribosomes should be unable to synthesise a correctly targeted protein even when supplied with its mRNA and appropriate membranes. If the protein sequence carries targeting information, however, functional targeting should be possible with a common translation system provided that the relevant recognition and translocation factors are present. A related prediction is that changing the coding sequence of a cytosolic reporter can change its localisation even while the promoter and ribosome source are kept the same. The latter result separates the role of the encoded product from that of the general transcriptional and translational machinery.

4. The signal hypothesis and experimental logic

The early secretory pathway presented a puzzle for investigators studying protein synthesis. Secretory proteins became associated with the membrane-enclosed ER despite being made by ribosomes located outside the lumen. If proteins were simply released into cytosol and later accumulated in the ER, their precursor forms might be expected to persist in the cytosol and to cross the membrane readily after completion. If instead the membrane interacted with growing chains, transfer should depend on the timing of membrane access during synthesis.

A useful experimental system comes from the way ER membranes behave when cells are homogenised. Pieces of the membrane reseal into small vesicles called microsomes. They are not normal organelles present as discrete structures inside intact cells. In a preparation derived from rough ER, a proportion of the vesicles preserve lumenal components and the orientation of the membrane. Such microsomes can be added to a cell-free translation system so that the requirement for membrane-dependent processing can be tested.

In a classic series of studies published in 1975, Günter Blobel and Bernhard Dobberstein examined immunoglobulin light chain synthesis and functional reconstitution of rough microsomes. They obtained evidence that the translated precursor carries a transient amino-terminal extension, and that membrane-dependent processing is linked to the transfer of the nascent product. The significance of this approach was not just the finding of a larger precursor. By varying the source of translation components and membrane components, the researchers could test whether the information specifying transfer belonged to the translation apparatus or to the message that encoded the chain.

Consider a simplified version of this reasoning. Translation of the same mRNA in two conditions yields a larger product without microsomes and a smaller product when functional microsomes are present during synthesis. This pattern is consistent with removal of a segment from the precursor. However, it does not by itself prove that the smaller product occupies the lumen. A proteolytic enzyme might cleave an exposed chain at the outside of the membrane, or an unrelated activity might produce a similarly sized fragment. To address that alternative, a separate test of accessibility is required.

A protease-protection assay asks whether a protein can be attacked by an externally added protease. In an intact microsomal suspension, the protease generally cannot enter the sealed lumen. A lumenal product is protected, whereas a protein exposed on the outside is susceptible. Adding a detergent that disrupts the membrane should remove the protection. Comparing the same translation product in the absence and presence of detergent helps establish that protection depends on a physical barrier rather than merely on resistance of the protein to enzymatic cleavage.

There are several reasons to include independent controls. A known cytosolic substrate confirms that the protease is active. A known lumenal protein provides evidence that the membrane preparation is sealed. Equal amounts of translation mixture and comparable exposure conditions are important when interpreting band intensities. If a reporter itself resists proteolysis because it has a compact fold, an untreated band may remain even without a membrane. Detergent sensitivity together with appropriate markers is stronger evidence than apparent resistance in a single condition.

The signal hypothesis emerged from such observations and proposed that nascent polypeptides possess encoded information recognised by components of the membrane-targeting system. It transformed a question about the gross appearance of rough ER into a question about sequence recognition, receptor-mediated targeting and protein-conducting channels. It also provided a testable prediction: an appropriate targeting segment, when placed in a suitable new protein context, may redirect that protein. The cell can translate two otherwise similar messages using the same ribosome and nevertheless deliver their products differently.

The historical studies should not be interpreted as establishing that all proteins enter the ER exclusively while they are being translated. They established a major route and a powerful framework for studying membrane transfer. Subsequent work identified post-translational and alternative insertion routes. The distinction between a central biological mechanism and the limits of its universality is important in examination questions that use words such as all, always and exclusively.

5. Signal peptides: reading an address from amino acid chemistry

An ER signal peptide is usually a short segment near the amino terminus of a precursor. It often contains a region with one or more positively charged residues, a central hydrophobic stretch and a more polar region near a potential cleavage site. This is a useful organisation into amino-terminal, hydrophobic and carboxyl-terminal regions. It does not specify an invariant sequence of letters or a fixed number of residues shared by every signal peptide.

The hydrophobic portion commonly contains side chains such as leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine and alanine in a suitable arrangement. These residues interact favourably with hydrophobic environments or binding pockets. The central part can form a helix during recognition or insertion. The ability to form a sufficiently hydrophobic segment helps distinguish many ER targeting sequences from ordinary hydrophilic stretches of a cytosolic protein. Nevertheless, hydrophobicity alone is not a complete code. The position of the segment, its length, flanking charge, nearby sequence and emerging-chain context all influence what happens.

A cleavable signal peptide is an extension of the precursor that is removed during or shortly after transfer. It must be distinguished from an internal signal-anchor sequence, which performs a targeting or insertion function but generally remains in the protein as a membrane-spanning segment. A signal peptide and a signal anchor may both contain a hydrophobic core. The difference is not that one is hydrophobic and the other is hydrophilic. The difference is the manner in which the chain engages the translocation machinery, the presence and accessibility of an appropriate cleavage region, and whether the hydrophobic segment remains in the final product.

Signal peptidase recognises structural and sequence features near the lumenally accessible cleavage region. The commonly taught minus-three and minus-one rule describes the preference for small neutral residues at positions three and one residues before many cleavage sites. These preferences are not sufficient by themselves to predict a cleavage site unambiguously. A suitable pair of residues located within an inaccessible cytosolic region cannot serve as a normal lumenal cleavage site. The context and presentation of the sequence matter.

Imagine a precursor with an effective ER signal peptide followed by a soluble enzyme domain. Mutating several hydrophobic core residues to charged residues could weaken recognition and translocation. In contrast, a mutation that changes residues near the cleavage site while preserving an effective hydrophobic targeting segment may allow entry but prevent normal processing. Both mutants could produce an abnormal band on electrophoresis, but their localisation and protease protection would differ. One may remain exposed to the cytosol, while the other can be inserted or otherwise associated with the ER in an uncleaved state. Thus, signal recognition and signal cleavage are separable processes.

Another experiment uses fusion constructs. A reporter that normally stays in the cytosol is expressed with an amino-terminal signal peptide from a secretory protein. If the fusion acquires a lumenal glycan, displays membrane-dependent protection or enters the secretion medium, the new sequence has supplied targeting information in this particular context. To interpret the result correctly, the investigator needs a reporter lacking the signal peptide, evidence of similar expression, and a control for general cell damage. Extracellular reporter released from dying cells is not evidence of conventional secretion. A complementary deletion experiment tests whether removing a sequence from its native precursor interferes with delivery.

A targeting segment can be necessary without being universally sufficient. A transplanted signal may fail when the recipient folds too early, places the cleavage region in an unfavourable context or contains competing signals. Conversely, a protein can possess more than one route to membrane association. The strongest inference comes from combining sequence mutations with localisation, biochemical processing and accessibility assays rather than inferring destination from one hydropathy plot.

6. Recognition of the emerging signal by SRP

The ribosomal exit tunnel provides the growing chain with a narrow path to the cytosol. During early translation, a signal peptide may still be buried within the tunnel. As additional residues are polymerised, the hydrophobic segment becomes available for contact with cytosolic targeting factors. This creates a temporal window in which a potentially aggregation-prone segment can be captured before the chain has folded into its final structure.

Signal recognition particle, abbreviated SRP, is a ribonucleoprotein. In mammals it comprises a small RNA, usually called 7SL RNA, associated with six protein subunits. A key protein, SRP54, contains a region that interacts with hydrophobic targeting sequences and a GTPase domain involved in receptor-dependent targeting. The RNA is a functional component of the particle rather than a messenger that encodes the six proteins. Other subunits and RNA regions contribute to organisation, ribosome association and modulation of elongation.

SRP recognises the combined context of an emerging signal and a translating ribosome. This recognition is significant because a hydrophobic region in a completed soluble protein does not necessarily require transport to the ER. Such a region may normally be buried in the interior of the folded product. Recognising hydrophobic information as it emerges from a ribosome allows targeting to be coupled to the process of production. SRP therefore interacts with a ribosome-nascent-chain complex rather than waiting for the mature protein to diffuse to its final location.

In the classical mammalian model, binding of SRP slows or temporarily pauses translation elongation. This provides an opportunity for targeting before the remaining polypeptide is made in the wrong environment. If the ribosome continued rapidly for a long period without a destination, a secretory protein could form structures in the cytosol that complicate translocation or its hydrophobic sequences could engage inappropriate partners. The delay is not a permanent inhibition of the ribosome; delivery to the ER and release of SRP permit continuation.

The degree of elongation slowing is context dependent. Cell-free systems have been particularly useful for identifying the effect, but in intact cells the pathway is coordinated with many translation and targeting events. One should not infer that every ER-directed ribosome halts at exactly the same codon or that every organism has a mammalian-type elongation arrest module. The mechanistic principle is coordination of an emerging chain with the available membrane translocation apparatus.

Walter, Ibrahimi and Blobel showed in 1981 that SRP interacts preferentially with ribosomes synthesising secretory rather than typical cytosolic products in a reconstituted system. In related experiments, SRP-dependent membrane association could be abolished by extracting microsomal components and restored by adding purified factor. Elongation inhibition could be relieved when suitable membrane components were provided. The studies connect recognition, translation timing and membrane association, rather than merely showing that SRP and secretory protein are found in the same fraction.

If SRP is depleted, a secretory reporter with a conventional SRP-dependent signal may show reduced translocation, whereas the synthesis of a control cytosolic reporter may remain comparatively intact. This is a prediction, not a universal consequence of removing SRP from a living cell. A severe perturbation may cause secondary stress, changes in translation and loss of viability. An acute, specific disruption with suitable controls is more informative than a prolonged perturbation that alters many cellular processes at once.

7. SRP receptor and delivery to the membrane

Recognition of a signal peptide is only useful if it leads to a productive interaction with the appropriate organelle. A second recognition step occurs at the cytosolic face of the ER, where the signal recognition particle receptor is located. The mammalian receptor is composed of an SR alpha subunit associated with a membrane-anchored SR beta subunit. Its position is essential to its function. A receptor capable of binding SRP but freely dispersed through cytosol would not, by that interaction alone, bring the nascent chain next to a suitable protein-conducting channel.

SRP54 and SR alpha are GTP-binding proteins. GTP binding supports the formation of a targeting complex in which these proteins interact. Their interaction is coupled to rearrangements that allow the ribosome and its signal segment to be transferred to a translocation-competent site. GTP hydrolysis then helps disassemble the targeting complex and recycle the components. This description is intentionally a functional sequence: recognition, membrane engagement, handover and recycling. It does not imply that every molecular movement occurs simultaneously or that the same pair of GTP molecules mechanically pulls each amino acid through the membrane.

A GTPase is a molecular switch whose state is altered by the nucleotide it binds and by nucleotide hydrolysis. Its role may be to establish an interaction, stabilise an intermediate, change affinity for partners or terminate a process. In the SRP cycle, such changes help make targeting directional and coordinate delivery with release. If GTP could bind but not be hydrolysed, some targeting intermediates might still form. However, normal cycling and repeated turnover of the factors would be impaired. This is why a nonhydrolysable GTP analogue cannot simply be described as preventing all membrane binding. The outcome depends on the step under investigation.

One useful distinction is between membrane docking and productive handover. A ribosome-nascent-chain complex can be near the ER without having established a continuous path for its peptide. If a mutation preserved interaction between SRP and its receptor but prevented transfer to Sec61, membrane association might remain measurable even though lumenal processing and protease protection would be reduced. Conversely, a mutation disrupting SRP receptor attachment to the ER might permit soluble SRP-receptor association without productive recruitment to membranes. Measuring the amount of protein in a membrane pellet would not reveal these distinctions by itself.

A further distinction concerns the identities of the GTP-dependent steps. Translation elongation uses GTP in the delivery of aminoacyl-tRNA and in ribosomal translocation. SRP targeting uses GTP in a different molecular cycle. Membrane traffic also employs GTPases, including proteins involved in vesicle formation or destination recognition. A reagent that affects all GTP-binding proteins could influence all three stages, making it difficult to infer which one produced a secretion defect. To study the targeting step, an investigator should compare a specific receptor perturbation with general translation controls and a marker of later vesicular transport.

The receptor need not stay associated with an individual polypeptide during its entire passage across the ER membrane. Once the ribosome has been transferred to the protein-conducting channel, the targeting factors become available to assist another nascent chain. This separation of tasks allows a limited pool of targeting components to service many actively translating ribosomes. It also prevents the mistaken picture in which SRP becomes a permanent structural part of the mature secreted protein.

8. Sec61: a selective passage through the lipid bilayer

The difficulty of moving a long, chemically heterogeneous polypeptide across a phospholipid membrane can be understood from the arrangement of the bilayer. Water surrounds the polar head groups at the two surfaces, but the centre of the membrane consists predominantly of hydrocarbon chains. A peptide backbone contains carbonyl and amide groups that can form hydrogen bonds. Charged or strongly polar side chains interact favourably with water. Direct transfer of a typical unfolded chain across the hydrocarbon interior would interrupt many favourable interactions.

A membrane protein-conducting channel solves this problem by providing a controlled pathway that shields much of the moving chain from the surrounding lipids. In the ER, the conserved core of this channel is the Sec61 complex. Its three subunits are called Sec61 alpha, beta and gamma. The alpha subunit forms the principal protein-conducting pathway and contains a lateral gate that communicates with the lipid bilayer. The other subunits are part of the conserved complex and support its function and stability. The complex is not the same thing as the ribosome, the signal peptidase or the SRP receptor.

A structural challenge is that the channel cannot remain permanently open. The ER maintains a chemically distinct lumen, including a specialised ion environment. An unrestricted water-filled opening would compromise the barrier properties of its membrane. Structural and biochemical studies support a gated channel that changes conformation during engagement with substrates. A central constriction formed by hydrophobic residues and a movable plug are important features of the channel family. The engaged substrate, associated ribosome and channel geometry help limit inappropriate leakage during transport. This is not a claim that all ion passage is impossible; rather, the conducting route must be regulated to balance polypeptide passage with membrane integrity.

The structure of the archaeal SecY complex described by Van den Berg and colleagues in 2004 provides a useful comparative illustration of the conserved translocon architecture. SecY is homologous to eukaryotic Sec61 alpha. The structure gave a physical basis for thinking about a central passage and an opening toward the surrounding membrane. It did not itself display an actively translating mammalian ribosome undergoing every phase of secretory protein transfer. Such a structure is a snapshot, interpreted in conjunction with functional and biochemical studies. The distinction between directly observed structural features and inferred dynamic movements is a good example of how scientific evidence is combined.

Two different exits are relevant for a growing chain. A hydrophilic segment can move through the conducting pathway into the ER lumen. A hydrophobic membrane-spanning segment can interact with the lateral gate and partition into the surrounding lipid. These alternatives are related: they use a common translocation machinery but yield different locations for parts of the chain. A soluble secretory protein can leave behind a cleaved targeting segment while the rest of its chain enters the lumen. An integral membrane protein can retain one or more hydrophobic segments in the membrane, with loops and domains distributed on both sides.

The direction of ordinary cotranslational transfer is determined in part by the ordered synthesis of the chain. The amino terminus emerges before the carboxyl terminus. Once an appropriate targeting signal has engaged the translocon, successive residues become available as the ribosome continues translation. The chain is not inserted as a finished rigid object. This matters especially for a protein that ultimately forms a large folded lumenal domain. It can enter the channel as a relatively extended chain and acquire its stable tertiary structure after sufficient sequence has reached the lumen.

A defective translocon may produce more than one molecular phenotype. A mutation in a region involved in ribosome engagement can prevent efficient delivery of the chain. A change affecting gate opening may allow recruitment but reduce transfer. A defect in discrimination between hydrophobic and hydrophilic segments may alter membrane insertion or topology. Since Sec61 performs essential functions, a prolonged severe defect may also change lumenal homeostasis or activate stress responses. A precise interpretation requires identifying which readout changes first, under conditions that preserve the other stages as much as possible.

For an exam question, distinguish between the three roles by asking what each component directly recognises or provides. SRP captures an appropriate signal on the growing chain. The receptor provides a membrane-localised partner for targeting. Sec61 supplies the controlled conducting route and permits appropriate membrane integration. Signal peptidase performs cleavage when the appropriate cleavage site is presented. These proteins cooperate but cannot be substituted for one another.

9. Cotranslational translocation of a soluble secretory precursor

Consider a hypothetical soluble enzyme that is normally released into the extracellular space. Its precursor begins with an effective cleavable ER signal peptide and has no other membrane-spanning segment. The precursor is synthesised in the usual amino-to-carboxyl direction. As the targeting segment becomes accessible, SRP recognises it in the context of a translating ribosome. The complex interacts with receptor at the ER and transfers the growing chain to a Sec61 channel. These events establish a physical connection between translation and transfer.

The ribosome remains outside the lumen, where it can interact with the same kinds of cytosolic tRNAs, translation factors and energy substrates that support synthesis of cytosolic proteins. Neither the mRNA nor the complete ribosome needs to pass through the translocon. It is the polypeptide that enters the conducting route. A drawing that places the ribosome on the lumenal face of the ER membrane would therefore reverse the actual organisation of the canonical pathway.

During productive engagement, the signal peptide interacts with the channel and nearby lipid. For an ordinary cleavable amino-terminal signal, the chain is arranged so that the sequence after the hydrophobic segment is translocated into the lumen. The signal peptide cleavage region becomes available to the lumenally oriented catalytic machinery of signal peptidase. The peptide bond at the cleavage site is hydrolysed, separating the signal peptide from the rest of the precursor.

Cleavage does not require translation of the entire chain before it can occur. The cleavage region can become accessible while synthesis continues, although the timing varies among substrates. The mature amino terminus of the soluble enzyme is therefore distinct from the amino terminus initially made by the ribosome. A gene sequence may predict a longer precursor than the experimentally purified extracellular protein. If the difference corresponds to a cleaved signal peptide and possibly other maturation steps, this is not necessarily evidence of an error in the genetic code or premature termination.

The hydrophobic signal peptide can remain temporarily associated with the membrane after release and undergo further disposal. The rest of the chain enters the aqueous lumen. When a termination codon arrives at the ribosomal decoding centre, release factors promote hydrolysis of the linkage between the completed chain and its tRNA. The lumenal soluble product can then separate from the translocation machinery. The channel and ribosome are available for further cycles.

Energy is used at several different levels. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthesis requires ATP; ribosomal elongation consumes GTP; the targeting factors use a GTP-dependent cycle; and lumenal folding machinery may consume ATP. The movement of the growing chain during conventional cotranslational transfer is closely coupled to the energy-driven process of peptide synthesis. It is therefore misleading to imagine an independent Sec61 ATPase hydrolysing one ATP per amino acid. Sec61 is a regulated channel, not a standalone ATP-driven polymer motor. Lumenal interactions can support the efficiency and direction of transfer, particularly in pathways that differ from the canonical cotranslational route.

A translation inhibitor that blocks elongation after successful targeting may leave unfinished chains associated with the translocon. In contrast, inhibition before the signal emerges may prevent formation of productive targeting complexes. Both treatments could reduce production of mature secretory protein while producing different molecular intermediates. Measurements of chain length, ribosome association and protease protection can distinguish them. This causal approach extends the preceding chapter on translation inhibitors without requiring a second comprehensive catalogue of inhibitor binding sites.

10. Signal peptide cleavage and the identity of a mature chain

Signal cleavage is one of the earliest forms of protein processing associated with the secretory pathway. A signal peptide should not be confused with a propeptide whose removal activates a precursor at a later stage. A preproprotein contains a signal sequence and an additional precursor segment. The signal sequence is often removed during ER entry, whereas the propeptide may be removed by a different enzyme after folding or later transport. The timing and location of these two hydrolysis events are distinct even though both shorten the chain.

Preproinsulin offers a familiar example of why these distinctions matter. Its initial signal sequence directs the precursor into the ER and is removed to generate proinsulin. The proinsulin chain folds and forms disulfide bonds; later processing produces mature insulin and C-peptide. The initial targeting signal is not the same as C-peptide. Failure to remove the signal sequence and failure to process proinsulin would affect different stages and should not be assumed to produce identical distributions of intermediates.

Consider a simplified mass calculation. A precursor consists of 250 residues, including a 22-residue signal peptide. If the only processing event is removal of that segment, the mature chain contains 228 residues. With an approximate mean residue mass of 110 daltons, the estimated difference is 22 multiplied by 110, or 2,420 daltons (2.42 kDa). The actual mass difference depends on the residues removed and the chemical termini. If the chain also acquires carbohydrate, its observed molecular mass can increase despite removal of the peptide segment. On a denaturing gel, the final band might therefore run above rather than below the unmodified precursor.

The interpretation of molecular mass is particularly important when comparing wild-type and mutant signal peptides. An uncleaved precursor that nonetheless enters the ER may acquire the same glycans as the wild type but retain additional residues. A targeting-deficient mutant may fail to undergo both cleavage and glycosylation. If the carbohydrate contribution is larger than the mass of the retained peptide, the targeting-deficient product could appear smaller than the correctly processed glycoprotein. The general expectation that an uncleaved precursor is always the larger gel band is therefore unsafe unless the modification states are controlled.

Signal peptidase is not required for every protein entering the ER. Many integral membrane proteins use an uncleaved signal anchor. Their first targeting segment must remain hydrophobic because it is incorporated into the bilayer. The presence of an amino-terminal hydrophobic segment on a sequence diagram does not establish whether that segment is removed. To predict processing, combine signal features with its intended topological role and experimental evidence.

Cleavage can be investigated using N-terminal sequencing or mass spectrometry of the product, comparison of products made with and without microsomes, or analysis of defined cleavage-region mutants. A gel shift alone gives preliminary evidence; determining the actual amino-terminal residue establishes the cleavage boundary more directly. Protease protection and glycosylation measurements provide complementary evidence for the location of the chain at the time of processing.

11. A single membrane span after a cleavable signal: type I topology

The topology of a single-pass membrane protein can be derived by tracking the chain as it is made. Suppose the precursor begins with a cleavable ER signal peptide, followed by a hydrophilic domain, a second hydrophobic segment and a hydrophilic carboxyl-terminal tail. The first targeting sequence establishes translocation of the following chain into the lumen. The amino-terminal domain therefore occupies the lumen after the initial signal peptide is removed.

As translation proceeds, the second hydrophobic segment enters the translocation machinery. If this segment functions as a stop-transfer sequence, it becomes incorporated into the bilayer through the lateral gate. Movement of the subsequent hydrophilic part of the chain into the lumen ceases. Translation itself continues on the cytosolic side. The carboxyl-terminal tail is consequently synthesised into the cytosol. The final protein has one retained membrane-spanning helix, a lumenal amino-terminal domain and a cytosolic carboxyl-terminal region.

Such an arrangement is commonly called type I single-pass topology. At the plasma membrane, following delivery through the secretory pathway, the amino-terminal domain is extracellular and the carboxyl-terminal domain remains cytosolic. Many cell-surface receptors use this basic arrangement. The ligand-binding domain can undergo lumenal folding and glycosylation, while the cytosolic region can interact with intracellular signalling components. Not all receptors are type I; the example shows how topology contributes to function.

Stop-transfer describes cessation of transfer of the downstream chain through the channel, not cessation of ribosomal elongation. Confusing these meanings can lead a student to predict that the protein ends at the stop-transfer segment. The term is unrelated to a stop codon in mRNA. An mRNA encoding a membrane protein generally continues to specify its cytosolic tail after the stop-transfer segment has been synthesised.

A deletion experiment makes this mechanism testable. If the second hydrophobic segment is deleted and the remaining sequence does not supply another membrane anchor or retention signal, the amino-terminal targeting peptide may still direct the chain into the ER. The rest of the protein may then enter the lumen as a soluble product, losing its normal membrane tether. Depending on its folding and sorting information, it may be secreted. The important prediction is the change in membrane integration and topology, not a guarantee that every mutant will be efficiently released.

Conversely, if the initial cleavable signal peptide is removed but the second hydrophobic segment remains, that second segment might itself acquire signal-anchor activity. The result need not be a fully cytosolic protein. Its orientation and targeting efficiency could depend on the length and folding state of the preceding domain and on the characteristics of the retained segment. A simple prediction that every N-terminal signal deletion prevents all ER association is justified only when the rest of the sequence lacks a functional alternative targeting element.

The experimental signature of type I topology is asymmetric accessibility. In a sealed microsome, the lumenal domain should be relatively protected against an externally added protease, whereas the cytosolic tail is accessible. An antibody recognising the carboxyl terminus can detect loss of that epitope after protease treatment even when a protected amino-terminal fragment remains. Adding detergent allows the protease access to both domains. In a living cell, an antibody directed against an extracellular amino-terminal epitope may bind without permeabilisation if the protein has reached the cell surface. These experiments distinguish topology from final localisation.

12. Internal signal anchors: two opposite single-pass orientations

Some integral membrane proteins begin with a substantial amino-terminal region that does not contain a cleavable ER signal. Translation of this region occurs on the cytosolic side. When an internal hydrophobic segment emerges from the ribosome, it can be recognised by SRP and can establish interaction with the ER translocation apparatus. Because the same segment participates in targeting and remains in the membrane, it is known as a signal anchor.

An internal signal anchor can enter the translocon in different orientations. In one arrangement, the amino-terminal region remains cytosolic and the carboxyl-terminal region is transferred into the ER lumen. This arrangement is conventionally called type II topology. The hydrophobic segment functions as the start of lumenal transfer for the downstream part of the protein. An amino-terminal cytosolic tail may contain regulatory sequences, whereas the lumenal carboxyl-terminal domain can encounter enzymes involved in secretory processing.

In the opposite arrangement, the amino-terminal region ends up in the lumen and the carboxyl-terminal region stays cytosolic. This is frequently described as type III topology when a single uncleaved signal anchor generates this arrangement. Such a protein has the same sidedness as a type I protein but a different mechanism for establishing it. A type I protein generally has a separate cleavable targeting peptide and a retained stop-transfer segment. A type III protein uses an uncleaved signal anchor. If an examination question asks how the protein was synthesised, sidedness alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

One important determinant of orientation is the distribution of positively charged amino acids near the hydrophobic segment. Lysine and arginine tend to be enriched on the cytosolic side of many membrane-spanning helices, a tendency commonly known as the positive-inside rule. The term refers to the cytosol-facing side of the membrane, not the side facing the ER lumen. An excess of positive charges on the amino-terminal side of a signal anchor often favours retention of that side in the cytosol; an excess on the carboxyl-terminal side often favours the reverse orientation.

This rule is an empirical tendency rather than a law stating that every positively charged residue must remain cytosolic. Orientation also depends on hydrophobicity, segment length, the position of the signal anchor, the lengths and folding states of the flanking domains and subsequent interactions. It is particularly unsafe to apply a simple comparison of net charge to arbitrary long stretches of the protein. Charge near the transmembrane region and its presentation at the time of insertion are more relevant than the total charge of the completed protein.

The physical context explains why there is a tendency for charged residues to remain in water. Moving a charged side chain across the low-dielectric membrane interior is costly without compensating interactions. Protein-conducting channels reduce some of this energetic cost, but asymmetric interactions between the nascent chain, channel and membrane can favour one orientation. A simplistic explanation based solely on attraction of positive amino acids to an assumed negatively charged surface is incomplete. The two faces of a biological membrane differ in their lipids and proteins, and the orientation decision takes place in an organised insertion machinery, not in an isolated uniform slab.

Consider a hypothetical signal anchor flanked by three basic residues on its amino-terminal side and one on its carboxyl-terminal side. The positive-inside tendency favours an amino-terminal cytosolic orientation. If researchers replace the amino-terminal basic residues with uncharged residues and introduce several basic residues on the other side, the preferred orientation may change. A change of orientation can be detected by determining which of two engineered glycosylation sites becomes modified or which terminal epitope remains protected inside intact microsomes. However, incomplete inversion or a mixture of orientations is possible. The outcome need not be an all-or-none switch.

The insertion of an amino-terminal domain into the lumen presents a special temporal problem if that domain has already become long or stably folded before the hydrophobic segment is recognised. A short amino-terminal segment may be capable of crossing when the signal anchor engages the machinery; a large folded domain may be much harder to move. This is one reason why the position of the first targeting segment matters. It also helps explain why the positive-inside rule does not, by itself, establish the orientation of every protein that contains a hydrophobic segment.

For a type II protein, the amino-terminal epitope should be accessible to protease in intact microsomes and the lumenal carboxyl-terminal epitope should be protected, provided that the enzyme is active and membranes are sealed. For a type III protein, the pattern is reversed. If the two reporters differ in the presence of cleavage, molecular mass measurements alone cannot establish their topology. The experiment must identify which specific region is exposed rather than merely record whether a band remains.

13. Multipass proteins and the sequential construction of topology

A multipass membrane protein contains more than one segment that crosses the bilayer. Its final chain may form a channel, carrier, receptor or other structure whose function requires several helices. The translocation machinery must arrange these segments so that water-soluble loops appear on appropriate sides of the membrane. The usual elementary model starts with a signal sequence or signal anchor that brings the ribosome to the ER and then follows the effects of successive hydrophobic segments as translation proceeds.

Imagine a chain beginning with a cleavable signal peptide, followed by a lumenal domain and a first retained stop-transfer segment. After this first span, the next hydrophilic loop is cytosolic. Suppose a second retained segment then acts as a start-transfer sequence in the appropriate orientation. Transfer of the following hydrophilic loop into the lumen can resume. A third retained segment acting as a stop-transfer sequence returns the downstream chain to the cytosolic side. In this idealised example, three retained crossings connect a lumenal amino-terminal domain to a cytosolic carboxyl terminus. The initial cleavable signal is not counted among the retained membrane spans.

The number of complete crossings has a simple geometric consequence. If all spans cross the bilayer once and both termini occupy aqueous compartments, an even number of retained crossings places the two termini on the same side; an odd number places them on opposite sides. This follows because each crossing exchanges the side occupied by the next domain. The rule is a consequence of geometry, not a mechanism of recognition. It cannot specify the first orientation, the number of segments that actually insert or the final arrangement of helices that do not fully cross the membrane.

Consider a four-span protein whose amino terminus is cytosolic. After the first crossing, the chain is lumenal; after the second, it is cytosolic; after the third, lumenal; and after the fourth, cytosolic again. If a question places potential N-linked glycosylation motifs in the first and second interhelical loops, only the first loop is expected to be lumenal under this model. If both motifs are modified, then either the assumed topology, the interpretation of the modification or another feature of the experimental design requires revision.

Real multipass proteins are not always built from fully independent alternating start-transfer and stop-transfer elements. Some membrane segments are insufficiently hydrophobic to insert stably by themselves and depend on interactions with other helices or accessory machinery. A polar residue that would be unfavourable in contact with lipid can become important in the aqueous interior of a channel assembled from several helices. It is possible for a protein to contain a hydrophobic segment that does not span the bilayer completely or that changes its arrangement during maturation. Therefore, the alternating rule is appropriate for a question that specifies ordinary fully spanning helices, not an infallible predictor for every real sequence.

This distinction is biologically important for transport proteins. A channel may conduct ions through a central polar pathway even though its outer surface is compatible with lipid. Individual transmembrane helices contribute different surfaces to the assembly. If one helix is mutated, a protein might enter the ER but fail to assemble, or acquire the wrong topology and expose a domain on an unexpected side. A change in functional current or transport activity cannot by itself tell whether the mutant failed during insertion, folding, trafficking or operation at the plasma membrane.

An experiment that systematically places reporters into several loops can build a topological map. One approach is to introduce potential glycosylation sites into appropriately exposed lumenal candidate regions. Another is to use epitope-specific antibodies and selective membrane permeabilisation. The positions must be chosen carefully because adding a reporter can itself alter a protein's folding, insertion or signal recognition. Multiple consistent measurements support the proposed arrangement more strongly than a single reporter site.

For a simple example of examination reasoning, suppose a six-span transporter has its amino terminus on the lumenal side. Its carboxyl terminus is also expected to be lumenal. A mutation causes one normally retained span to fail insertion without compensating changes. If the remaining five complete spans retain their order and initial orientation, the termini become opposite in sidedness. The assumption about the other spans is vital. In a real transporter, failure of one span may instead disrupt insertion of neighbouring spans, promote degradation or yield more than one topology. The parity calculation is a test of the stated model, not a guarantee that a damaged protein will survive.

14. Hydrophobicity, membrane partitioning and quantitative prediction

A transmembrane alpha helix presents a predominantly nonpolar surface to the lipid bilayer while satisfying many of its backbone hydrogen bonds internally. The geometry of an alpha helix provides an approximate axial rise of 0.15 nanometre per residue. A segment of 20 residues in an ideal straight helix would extend about 3 nanometres along its axis. This is comparable to the hydrophobic thickness of many biological lipid bilayers. Helices may tilt, kink, extend beyond the hydrophobic core or adapt to local membrane thickness, so the calculation is a useful order-of-magnitude comparison rather than a universal length requirement.

A hydropathy plot assigns a numerical value to each amino acid and averages these values over a sliding sequence window. A sustained region of high hydrophobicity can suggest a signal peptide or membrane-spanning helix. The size of the window matters: a very narrow window produces many local fluctuations, whereas a broad window can dilute a short hydrophobic segment with surrounding polar residues. A plot may identify a candidate region without indicating whether that region is cleaved, which side the amino terminus occupies or whether it is recognised by the conventional ER machinery.

The energy of exposing a side chain to membrane depends on its chemical character and its position. A leucine side chain near the centre of a lipid bilayer interacts differently with its surroundings than an aspartate at the same position. A tryptophan or tyrosine near a membrane interface may interact favourably with both polar and nonpolar environments. A charged residue may be accommodated within a multipass protein if it forms a salt bridge or contributes to a hydrated pathway. Thus, membrane insertion is a collective property of the segment and its context, not simply a count of how many amino acids are labelled hydrophobic in a textbook table.

Hessa and colleagues used designed sequences to investigate how the ER translocon discriminates between candidate membrane-spanning segments. The results supported the importance of both amino acid identity and position in determining insertion propensity. Such experiments provide a way to connect a measured fraction of inserted products to an apparent energetic preference. Their interpretation requires distinguishing the observed distribution of experimentally defined products from a direct measurement of the intrinsic transfer energy of an isolated amino acid in a pure lipid bilayer.

A simplified two-outcome model is useful for examination calculations. Let f be the fraction of a designed segment incorporated into the membrane, while 1 minus f is the fraction observed in the alternative translocated state. The ratio K_app = f/(1 − f) describes the relative abundance of the two outcomes under the specified assay conditions. If these outcomes can be represented by an equilibrium-like partition model, the apparent free-energy difference for insertion relative to the alternative is ΔG_app = −RT ln K_app. Here R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature in kelvin and ln denotes the natural logarithm. The units of ΔG_app depend on the units of R. When R = 8.314 joules per mole per kelvin, the result is in joules per mole.

The negative sign is meaningful. If membrane insertion is the more common outcome, f exceeds one half, K_app exceeds one, and ln K_app is positive. The calculated insertion free-energy difference is negative. If insertion is the less common outcome, the calculated difference is positive. At equal fractions the apparent difference is zero. Solving the expression for f yields f = 1/[1 + exp(ΔG_app/RT)]. The response is nonlinear: the same change in apparent free energy does not produce the same absolute change in fraction at every starting point.

For a hypothetical dataset collected at 300 K, suppose 80 of 100 analysable molecules are classified as inserted and the remaining 20 as translocated. The ratio is four. Because RT is approximately 2.494 kJ per mole and ln 4 is approximately 1.386, ΔG_app is about −3.46 kJ per mole. A variant with 20 inserted and 80 translocated has a ratio of one quarter and an apparent difference of about +3.46 kJ per mole. The mutation changes the apparent preference by approximately +6.92 kJ per mole. These numbers are invented for teaching and are not reported measurements for a particular amino acid substitution.

The model assumes that the two categories exhaust the population used to calculate f and that their measurement is proportional to molecule number. If some chains are degraded or arrested, excluding them changes the meaning of the fraction. Likewise, if the two products are labelled or detected with different efficiencies, raw band intensities may not equal their molecular abundances. The translocation system operates in a living, energy-consuming context, so an apparent free-energy scale should not automatically be identified with a true equilibrium transfer energy. The mathematical relationship is valuable because it converts a ratio into a compact comparison, but the biological interpretation depends on assay design.

15. Alternative routes and the limits of a single canonical model

The classical SRP pathway explains how a nascent chain can be targeted before synthesis is complete. It does not follow that every ER protein must enter in precisely that way. A targeting segment positioned at the extreme carboxyl terminus cannot emerge early enough to initiate conventional cotranslational targeting of a large downstream domain because there is little or no downstream sequence. Tail-anchored proteins illustrate this timing constraint.

A tail-anchored protein generally contains a large cytosolic domain followed by a membrane-spanning helix close to the carboxyl terminus. When translation terminates, the hydrophobic tail becomes available for recognition by cytosolic factors. These factors can shield the segment from inappropriate interactions and deliver it to a suitable membrane insertion system. In many eukaryotes, the GET pathway in yeast or the related TRC pathway in mammals is an important route for a subset of such proteins. The hydrophobic tail remains in the membrane, leaving most of the protein in the cytosol and a short terminal portion on the noncytosolic side.

This is a useful counterexample to the statement that membrane proteins always begin their targeting while still being translated. It also illustrates the difference between a cleavable signal and a retained anchor. A tail-anchored protein does not normally lose its membrane tether as part of signal peptide cleavage. Its placement in the membrane enables its cytosolic domain to perform functions such as organising membrane traffic or interactions with other cytosolic proteins.

Some soluble proteins can also use a post-translational route into the ER, particularly well established in yeast. A completed chain must be kept in a state compatible with passage through the conducting channel. Cytosolic chaperones can help prevent premature aggregation or stable folding. Sec62 and Sec63 are accessory components associated with the ER translocation machinery in relevant pathways. The lumenal Hsp70-family chaperone BiP can bind emerging regions in an ATP-regulated manner and help favour continued movement toward the lumen. The idea that binding on the receiving side limits backward movement is often explained through a molecular ratchet model.

A Brownian ratchet model does not require each hydrolysis event to produce a fixed mechanical step of a particular length. Thermal fluctuations allow a segment to move in either direction. Binding on the receiving side can reduce the probability that a newly transferred segment returns through the channel. Repeated binding and release cycles can bias the net movement without implying that thermal motion itself has become directional in the absence of an energy-consuming process. ATP-dependent chaperone cycles help sustain this bias and maintain appropriate interactions.

Alternative insertion systems exist in addition to these examples. Some membrane proteins depend on accessory complexes that assist insertion of segments with particular properties. The exact route varies with substrate and organism. At NET and SET level, the important conclusion is that the canonical SRP-Sec61 mechanism is the principal model for many secretory and membrane proteins, not a universal description of all protein insertion events. A late-emerging signal, an unusually short secretory precursor or a different cellular context can change the mechanism available to a protein.

Comparative biology reinforces this conclusion. Bacteria do not possess an ER or Golgi apparatus, but they face the related physical problem of transferring proteins across or inserting them into their cytoplasmic membrane. The SecY channel family and bacterial SRP illustrate conserved principles of recognition and membrane transfer. Bacterial SecA-dependent export provides an example of an ATP-driven motor involved in transfer in a different cellular system. It would be incorrect to assign SecA as the ordinary motor of mammalian cotranslational ER targeting. Homology establishes a relationship between mechanisms; it does not make all their accessory factors or cellular destinations identical.

A final exception concerns the route to the exterior. Certain proteins lacking conventional ER signal peptides can be released through unconventional mechanisms. The existence of such pathways does not make the signal hypothesis obsolete. Instead, it restricts the claim to conventional secretory delivery. In a question, an extracellular protein with no hydrophobic signal peptide should prompt consideration of alternative release, cell damage or an overlooked targeting feature. It should not automatically be assumed that the protein followed the standard ER-Golgi route.

16. The ER lumen as a selective environment for maturation

Crossing the membrane does not automatically make a secretory protein functional. A growing chain entering the ER may expose hydrophobic surfaces that will be buried after folding, possess cysteines that have not yet formed their correct bonds, or lack its eventual assembly partners. The cell must allow the chain sufficient time to attain a useful conformation while preventing abnormal interactions among many simultaneously entering chains. This is the biological reason why targeting and maturation are intimately connected.

BiP is an ER-lumenal member of the Hsp70 family of molecular chaperones. It recognises exposed regions that are characteristic of many incompletely folded proteins. Its ATP-regulated interaction with a substrate can prevent inappropriate aggregation and allow the substrate additional opportunities to reach a more stable conformation. The chaperone does not provide a second amino acid sequence that specifies the final fold. It acts on the process by which the chain explores possible conformations. For a protein that normally assembles with another subunit, temporary chaperone association can also prevent exposed assembly interfaces from engaging unsuitable molecules.

The nucleotide state of an Hsp70 chaperone influences its substrate-binding behaviour. In broad terms, the ATP-bound state is associated with relatively rapid substrate exchange, whereas hydrolysis promotes a more stable binding state; nucleotide exchange facilitates release. The precise timing is regulated by interacting factors. This cycle allows a chaperone to alternate between capturing exposed sites and releasing them for folding or further interactions. A defect that prevents release can be harmful even if substrate binding itself is efficient. Folding requires repeated opportunity to form native interactions, not indefinite occupation by a chaperone.

Disulfide formation illustrates a chemical advantage of the ER environment. A disulfide bond is formed by oxidation of two cysteine thiol groups. It is covalent and can connect distant parts of a chain or separate chains within a protein assembly. The ER lumen has a redox environment and enzyme system that facilitate the formation and rearrangement of such bonds in many secretory proteins. In contrast, the cytosol is normally maintained in a relatively reducing condition that disfavors many stable structural disulfides.

Protein disulfide isomerase, often abbreviated PDI, catalyses thiol-disulfide exchange. The formation of a bond between two cysteines is not sufficient to establish correct folding because a protein with several cysteines may be able to form inappropriate pairings. Rearrangement of incorrect bonds is therefore part of the maturation process. The enzyme can participate in oxidation, reduction or isomerisation of substrate disulfides depending on its state and the cellular context. This explains why observing disulfide formation does not prove that the mature three-dimensional structure has been achieved.

For instance, an immunoglobulin light chain must achieve its proper fold and participate in a suitable assembly. A mutation disrupting a cysteine that normally contributes to stability can impair maturation without preventing the initial ER targeting event. The mutant protein might undergo signal peptide cleavage and glycosylation where applicable, but remain associated with quality-control machinery. An extracellular abundance measurement would show reduced secretion, even though the targeting signal and translation machinery were normal. The diagnostic signature is access to lumenal processing without efficient subsequent export.

Another experiment compares electrophoretic mobility under reducing and nonreducing conditions. A disulfide-linked pair of chains can appear as a larger covalent complex in a nonreducing sample but separate when the disulfide linkage is reduced. Intramolecular disulfide bonds may alter electrophoretic migration through conformational effects even when the actual chain mass is essentially unchanged. These observations should be interpreted together with appropriate size standards and knowledge of the protein's subunit composition.

17. N-linked glycosylation as processing and an experimental location marker

An important early modification of many proteins in the secretory pathway is N-linked glycosylation. It involves attachment of a carbohydrate structure to the side-chain amide nitrogen of a suitable asparagine. The conventional recognition motif is Asn-X-Ser or Asn-X-Thr, with proline excluded at the middle position. The presence of this motif makes the site a candidate for modification; it does not guarantee that a glycan will be added.

In the ER, a precursor oligosaccharide is assembled on a membrane-associated lipid carrier derived from dolichol. The completed precursor is transferred to an appropriate asparagine of a protein by oligosaccharyltransferase. Because the relevant catalytic access is on the lumenal side, a chain segment exposed only to cytosol cannot ordinarily receive this conventional N-linked glycan. The transfer of the assembled structure is distinct from the later stepwise removal or addition of particular sugars during processing.

The initial commonly described oligosaccharide contains fourteen sugar residues: three glucose, nine mannose and two N-acetylglucosamine residues. This numerical composition is useful for understanding that the first modification is not simply attachment of one glucose. It also explains why glycosylation can produce a readily detectable increase in the apparent molecular mass of a precursor. The mature glycan may differ substantially from the initially transferred oligosaccharide after subsequent enzymatic processing.

Whether a candidate sequon receives a glycan depends on physical access. It must be located on the correct side of the membrane and be presented appropriately to the enzyme. A motif too near a membrane-spanning segment or too near the end of certain chains can have reduced occupancy. Local folding and neighbouring residues can influence accessibility. Therefore, a modified reporter site provides positive evidence of lumenal access, but absence of modification at a single engineered sequon is not by itself proof of cytosolic location.

This asymmetry is valuable in topology experiments. Suppose two otherwise comparable sites are introduced into different hydrophilic loops of an integral membrane protein. If the first acquires a glycan and the second does not, the simplest explanation may be that the first loop is lumenal and the second cytosolic. To test that explanation, the researcher can combine the result with selective protease accessibility or antibody binding. A control construct known to present the unmodified sequon in the lumen can help determine whether it is intrinsically capable of receiving a glycan.

Glycan-dependent chaperones provide a second connection between modification and protein fate. Calnexin is an ER membrane protein with a lumenal lectin domain, whereas calreticulin is a soluble ER-lumenal protein with related recognition properties. They interact with suitable monoglucosylated N-linked glycans during the maturation of many glycoproteins. Removal of terminal glucose residues and, for some incompletely folded proteins, readdition of glucose allow repeated opportunities for chaperone interaction. The carbohydrate can thus serve as a reversible recognition feature in a quality-control cycle.

An essential distinction is that the glycan does not alone reveal the exact three-dimensional structure of the protein. Enzymes and chaperones recognise properties associated with folding intermediates and their glycans; these interactions change the probability of further maturation or retention. The system is not an abstract sensor that compares every atom with a stored model of the native protein. Some defective proteins can escape quality control, and some normally synthesised proteins require extended maturation.

A mutation that removes a glycosylation sequon may therefore have two separable effects. It removes the possibility of modification at that site, and it may alter the folding or chaperone interactions of the protein. If secretion falls, the result does not prove that the glycan itself functioned as a final extracellular address. It could instead indicate reduced folding efficiency, altered stability or loss of an interaction with an export factor. Distinguishing these possibilities requires examining intermediates before and after ER exit.

18. Quality control distinguishes entry from permission to leave

The ER must balance two competing requirements. It needs to export functional cargo efficiently, but it also needs to reduce the chance that incompletely folded or incorrectly assembled products reach later compartments. A protein that enters the ER and subsequently remains there is not necessarily an ER-resident protein. It may be a normal intermediate awaiting maturation, a defective protein undergoing retention, or a resident protein maintained by a specific localisation mechanism.

For many soluble and membrane proteins, completion of folding exposes appropriate surfaces for export or removes features associated with prolonged chaperone binding. A protein that has not assembled correctly may remain associated with chaperones or fail to engage a cargo receptor. This is a kinetic and molecular competition between maturation, retention, export and disposal. It is useful to ask whether a given intervention alters the rate of successful folding, the recognition of a folded cargo or the capacity of the transport apparatus.

ER-associated degradation, abbreviated ERAD, provides a route for disposal of many substrates that fail to mature appropriately. Such proteins can be recognised by quality-control machinery and transferred toward the cytosolic degradation system. Depending on substrate, removal from the ER and ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal destruction may be coupled to additional extraction and processing steps. The mechanism is covered more fully in the chapter on protein degradation. Here the relevant consequence is that disappearance of a lumenal precursor need not mean that it was successfully secreted.

A reduction in secreted protein can therefore be accompanied by a decrease in intracellular precursor rather than an accumulation. If a mutant is rapidly degraded, its steady-state concentration may be low even while its translation rate is similar to that of the wild type. A brief pulse labelling newly made chains can reveal the synthesis event before degradation has removed much of the product. Comparing the initial label with its subsequent persistence helps distinguish synthesis from stability.

If the rate of incoming clients exceeds the capacity for folding and processing, unfolded or misfolded proteins can accumulate in the ER. The unfolded protein response is a set of adaptive signalling mechanisms that helps adjust protein input, folding capacity and disposal. At the level relevant here, one important consequence is that an apparent reduction in translation can occur secondarily after a defect in protein maturation. A cell may reduce its workload when its secretory system is stressed. Thus, a late measurement of translation alone cannot establish that the original defect was in ribosomal initiation.

This logic explains the importance of the timescale of an experiment. A specific disturbance of SRP recognition may initially affect only the targeting of its clients. After many hours, altered protein synthesis, impaired organelle function or stress-dependent expression changes can complicate the phenotype. A short time-course with measurements of targeting, folding and later transport provides a stronger test of the primary event than a single late endpoint. General cell viability and unrelated protein synthesis should also be monitored.

19. Resident proteins, export signals and retrieval

An ER-resident soluble protein may require an amino-terminal signal peptide just as a secreted protein does. Both need access to the lumen. However, they need different final distributions. A useful example is BiP: it assists other proteins within the ER and therefore must remain available in that compartment over repeated cycles of synthesis and traffic. Its entry signal answers the question of how it reaches the lumen; a separate localisation mechanism helps answer why it does not accumulate predominantly outside the cell.

Many soluble ER residents in animal cells have a carboxyl-terminal KDEL sequence, corresponding to lysine, aspartate, glutamate and leucine. Related signals are used in different organisms; HDEL is important in budding yeast. The terminal position is a significant part of the recognition information. Appending a large tag after a KDEL sequence can interfere with its function, even though the four residues are still present somewhere within the protein.

Munro and Pelham showed that the carboxyl-terminal signal contributes to the localisation of soluble lumenal ER proteins. Later work supported a retrieval mechanism in which proteins escaping from the ER can be recognised in a post-ER compartment and returned. The term ER retention is often used broadly to describe the steady-state outcome. It should not be interpreted to mean that a protein carrying KDEL is physically incapable of leaving the ER even briefly.

The KDEL receptor is an integral membrane protein that recognises the soluble cargo from the lumenal side. Its binding depends on the local environment. The generally more acidic Golgi environment favours cargo binding, while the relatively less acidic ER environment favours release. A receptor that cycles between these compartments can therefore capture a molecule where it has escaped and release it where it is required. Retrograde transport returns the receptor-cargo complex toward the ER. This process depends on the correct organisation and traffic of the compartments; a signal on the soluble cargo is not sufficient without the corresponding recognition machinery.

Membrane-resident proteins present a related but distinct problem. Many ER membrane proteins contain cytosol-facing retrieval information, such as certain carboxyl-terminal dilysine motifs. These can interact with cytosolic sorting machinery. Such a signal is not equivalent to KDEL on a soluble lumenal protein. It must be exposed on the side where its recognition partner operates. Moving a cytosolic retrieval motif into the ER lumen can prevent the expected interaction even if the amino acid sequence has not changed.

Imagine two reporters. The first contains a cleavable ER signal peptide but no known retention sequence. The second is identical except for a functional carboxyl-terminal KDEL motif. Both may enter the ER and acquire equivalent early modifications. The second is expected to accumulate preferentially in early secretory compartments because escaped molecules can be retrieved, while the first may proceed toward secretion if it folds and lacks other sorting constraints. This result separates the targeting information used for entry from the information used after entry.

Deleting KDEL from a resident protein need not increase secretion to one hundred percent. Other interactions can retain the protein, and its folding state or assembly may affect export. Conversely, adding KDEL to a secretory protein may slow or reduce its release without completely eliminating secretion. The outcome depends on the capacity of the retrieval system, accessibility of the motif and competition with other sorting processes. The experimentally testable statement is a change in distribution or residence, not an absolute guarantee.

20. Following a correctly targeted protein from ER to Golgi and beyond

Once a protein is correctly assembled and eligible for export, it may leave the ER in transport intermediates that preserve the topology established during translocation. COPII-dependent export is a major route out of the ER toward early Golgi compartments. Soluble cargo may be captured by specific receptors or carried along with the contents of a forming carrier. Membrane cargo can possess cytosolic sorting information recognised by the export machinery. The distinction matters because soluble lumenal sequences cannot directly contact a cytosolic coat without an intervening membrane component.

In contrast to passage through Sec61, movement from ER to Golgi does not ordinarily require the folded lumenal protein to cross another membrane. The enclosed lumen of the carrier preserves the environment of the cargo. Many proteins can therefore travel after acquiring complex conformations and disulfide bonds. Requiring them to unfold for every subsequent transport step would defeat the earlier maturation process.

The Golgi provides a series of environments for further processing and sorting. A glycoprotein may undergo changes in its N-linked oligosaccharides as it passes through the pathway. Other modifications may occur in particular compartments. An integral membrane cargo retains its membrane orientation, so the domains accessible to lumenal enzymes remain distinct from those exposed to cytosolic enzymes. The detailed organisation of the stack and the mechanisms of traffic between cisternae belong to the earlier chapter on Golgi structure and sorting.

One outcome is constitutive delivery. Many cells continuously supply membrane proteins and lipids to the plasma membrane and release soluble cargo without requiring a specialised storage granule to await a signal. Constitutive does not mean that the process is completely unregulated; cargo synthesis, export capacity and membrane traffic can all be controlled. It means that the final delivery does not depend on the defining stimulus-triggered release of a stored secretory granule.

In specialised cells, regulated secretion allows a product to accumulate in secretory granules until an appropriate stimulus is received. A rise in cytosolic calcium is a common signal promoting exocytosis in several regulated systems. A cell can therefore release previously synthesised protein even when new translation has been stopped briefly, provided that a releasable pool remains and the release machinery is functional. This distinction is important in physiology: the time required to synthesise a new precursor differs from the time required to discharge pre-existing mature product.

Lysosomal hydrolases provide another important destination within the same early pathway. Many soluble lysosomal enzymes in animal cells receive a carbohydrate-based targeting mark involving mannose-6-phosphate. A receptor in the sorting pathway recognises this mark and directs cargo toward endosomal and lysosomal compartments. Failure of this later address can lead to secretion of an enzyme that was successfully targeted to the ER. Thus, an increase in extracellular enzyme is not always evidence of improved secretory function. It may reveal misdelivery away from the normal intracellular destination.

The important generalisation is that targeting information acts at different stages. An ER entry signal is recognised while the precursor is being delivered to the initial membrane. A lumenal retrieval sequence can act after entry. A glycan-based lysosomal address is generated through processing and read during later sorting. A cytosolic motif on a membrane protein can be recognised by a coat or adaptor without crossing the membrane. The chemical nature and accessibility of each signal match the machinery that reads it.

21. Tracking a cohort of proteins through the secretory pathway

Static micrographs show where cellular structures are located but do not by themselves demonstrate the order in which a newly made protein visits them. A labelled cohort provides a way to follow movement over time. In a pulse-chase experiment, cells are exposed briefly to labelled precursor molecules during the pulse, after which an excess of unlabelled precursor is supplied during the chase. The initial pulse labels proteins made during a restricted time interval. Later observations reveal the distribution of that cohort rather than the aggregate distribution of all proteins in the cell.

The classical studies of secretory traffic in pancreatic exocrine cells by Caro, Palade, Jamieson and colleagues established an ordered progression of newly synthesised proteins through rough ER, Golgi-associated compartments and secretory structures. Autoradiographic observations and cell fractionation provided complementary ways of following newly incorporated label. The timing of appearance in successive compartments supported the view that secretion involves an intracellular sequence of transport events rather than direct release from cytosolic ribosomes to extracellular fluid.

The important methodological feature is the temporal separation of synthesis and movement. If a labelled protein is initially found in ER and subsequently found in Golgi, the result is consistent with transfer of that cohort. If a second independent method identifies its molecular processing state, confidence increases. However, fractionation alone can be affected by contamination between organelles. A band in a Golgi-enriched fraction is not conclusive evidence unless the purity of that fraction is established with appropriate markers.

Consider an idealised first-order ER export model for a fully export-competent labelled cohort. Let E(t) denote the amount remaining in ER after t minutes, and k denote the export rate constant in reciprocal minutes. Assume there is no further labelling, no retrieval and no degradation. If the probability of exit per unit time is constant, then dE/dt = −kE. Separating the variables and integrating gives ln[E(t)/E(0)] = −kt. Exponentiating yields E(t) = E(0) exp(−kt). The half-time t_half follows by setting E(t_half)/E(0) = 1/2, giving t_half = ln 2/k.

Suppose a hypothetical cohort begins with 800 labelled units in the ER and 200 remain after 20 minutes. The remaining fraction is one quarter. Under the assumptions of the model, two half-times have elapsed, so the half-time is 10 minutes and k is approximately 0.0693 per minute. The amount that has exited the ER is 600 units, but it would be incorrect to conclude that all 600 units have already reached the medium. They may still occupy Golgi or other intermediates.

A second model accounts for competing degradation and export. Let k_e and k_d represent independent first-order exit and degradation rate constants for the same homogeneous population. The decline of the ER cohort follows dE/dt = −(k_e + k_d)E, so the observed half-time depends on their sum. Integrating the export flux k_e E over all future time gives an eventual exported fraction k_e/(k_e+k_d). Likewise the degraded fraction is k_d/(k_e+k_d). If k_e is 0.06 per minute and k_d is 0.02 per minute, the predicted export fraction is 0.75 and the degradation fraction is 0.25. The disappearance half-time is approximately 8.66 minutes.

This model exposes a frequent interpretation error. A rapidly disappearing ER band could indicate efficient export or rapid destruction. Measurements of downstream compartments and recovery of the original cohort are required to distinguish the two. If folding introduces a lag or the population contains several conformations with distinct rates, the single exponential may be inadequate. Retrieval introduces another route back into the ER. A good answer to an analytical question therefore states both the prediction of the model and the biological assumptions on which it depends.

22. Distinguishing targeting, insertion, folding and final sorting experimentally

When several sequential processes produce the same final readout, the most informative strategy is to measure an intermediate characteristic of each process. For ER targeting, useful observations include the dependence on the signal sequence, association of the translating complex with the membrane and evidence of entry into the lumen. For membrane integration, the essential observation is that a segment is stably incorporated with a measurable topology. For maturation, it is the presence of appropriate processing, folding and assembly states. For later traffic, it is the changing compartment distribution of a labelled product.

Membrane association can be assessed by separating membrane-containing material from soluble material. However, a membrane pellet may contain proteins attached peripherally to the surface, soluble proteins enclosed within vesicles, integral membrane proteins and aggregates that sediment independently. The mere recovery of a protein in the pellet does not prove it is an integral membrane protein. Carbonate extraction at alkaline pH is one approach used to distinguish many peripheral or lumenal proteins from integral membrane proteins, provided that controls establish membrane recovery and the behaviour of the protein in the extraction conditions. Very stable aggregates and unusual associations can complicate the result.

Protease protection determines whether a region is accessible to the aqueous phase outside intact microsomes. It is particularly useful when antibodies recognise different regions of an individual chain. An intact lumenal domain and a digested cytosolic tail can be consistent with type I membrane topology. If detergent removes protection, a membrane barrier is implicated. If the protein is resistant even after membrane disruption, intrinsic resistance or unsuitable protease conditions must be considered. Assays should therefore compare at least an untreated sample, a protease-only sample and a protease-plus-detergent sample.

Glycan-sensitive enzymes add temporal information. Endoglycosidase H can remove many high-mannose and certain hybrid N-linked glycans. Many complex glycans formed during passage through medial Golgi processing become resistant. A glycoprotein that changes from Endo H sensitive to resistant during a chase has acquired processing consistent with passage through suitable Golgi compartments. The converse is not universal: some correctly transported proteins retain Endo H-sensitive glycans, and some are not N-glycosylated at all. Resistance does not establish that a protein has reached the plasma membrane or been secreted. It identifies a particular glycan-processing state.

PNGase F removes most mammalian N-linked glycans, although there are structural exceptions, including certain plant or insect core-fucosylated glycans. Comparing its effects with those of Endo H can help identify whether changes in electrophoretic mobility are due to N-glycosylation rather than cleavage or another modification. The interpretation should include untreated controls and a known glycoprotein substrate. A negative result can reflect absence of the appropriate glycan, incomplete enzyme activity or poor access under the assay conditions.

A hypothetical case illustrates how these measurements can be combined. Four cells express the same secretory reporter. The control produces a cleaved, glycosylated and subsequently Endo H-resistant product, which appears in the medium. Mutant A produces a largely uncleaved chain without glycan or protection; this pattern suggests defective entry, although a signal cleavage defect alone must be excluded using accessibility. Mutant B produces a cleaved, protected and Endo H-sensitive form that remains intracellular; this pattern is consistent with entry followed by a maturation or export block. Mutant C produces an Endo H-resistant intracellular form but little extracellular protein; this pattern suggests a later defect, provided that the glycan marker has been validated for this substrate.

Even these combined observations do not establish the exact mutated gene. Mutant A could affect signal recognition, receptor-dependent delivery or translocon engagement. Mutant B could affect folding, cargo recognition or an early transport step. Mutant C could alter later sorting, fusion, degradation or the detection of the released product. Rescue by restoring a candidate factor, measurements of unrelated cargo and direct localisation of the reporter can discriminate among these possibilities. An advanced examination answer should make the strongest conclusion that follows from the data without treating every plausible mechanism as proven.

In recombinant protein production, the same reasoning helps diagnose low recovery. The amount of protein translated is not necessarily the amount correctly folded, delivered or secreted. A suitable host, compatible targeting sequence and appropriate maturation environment all affect the yield. A protein requiring structural disulfides and glycosylation may behave differently in a conventional bacterial expression system and a mammalian secretory system. The host choice is therefore a decision about biochemical requirements as well as expression level. In plants, analogous targeting principles help determine whether a recombinant protein reaches an extracellular, vacuolar or retained ER location. The next chapter extends this logic to proteins that use targeting systems outside the conventional secretory pathway.

1. Conceptual MCQ — Which property most directly explains why a typical unfolded secretory protein cannot diffuse freely through a lipid bilayer?

Answer and explanation

B. Its polar backbone and charged residues face an energetic barrier in the hydrocarbon core

The bilayer core is nonpolar; a long chain contains polar peptide bonds and often charged side chains. A protein-conducting channel provides a controlled route. mRNA capping and rRNA do not remove this barrier.

C. Its temporary association with the ER through targeting/translocation machinery

ER-associated ribosomes belong to the cytosolic population and remain on the cytosolic face. The emerging polypeptide, rather than an inherently different ribosome, usually establishes targeting.

Signals are recognised through sequence and physical features rather than one fixed length. KDEL participates in retrieval, and a cleavable signal is normally removed.

  2. Signal peptidase
  3. SRP

C. SRP

SRP combines RNA and proteins, including SRP54 in mammals. Sec61 conducts chains, signal peptidase cleaves appropriate signals, and PDI catalyses thiol-disulfide exchange.

  3. BiP

A. Sec61 alpha

Sec61 alpha forms the core channel and lateral gate. SR beta belongs to the SRP receptor, while BiP and calnexin serve other maturation or translocation-associated roles.

  4. Attach mannose-6-phosphate

B. Allow appropriate hydrophobic segments to interact with membrane lipid

Lateral access to lipid permits membrane integration. The mRNA remains cytosolic, GTP hydrolysis has other molecular catalysts, and the lysosomal address is generated later.

Stop-transfer concerns membrane topology, not termination at the ribosome. Its retention can provide a membrane anchor.

The cleavable signal initiates transfer and the retained stop-transfer segment leaves the tail cytosolic. A single complete span requires opposite sidedness of termini.

An internal signal anchor commonly directs the downstream portion into the lumen while retaining the preceding region in cytosol. The anchor remains in the membrane.

Local positive charge frequently favours cytosolic retention. It is a tendency, not an absolute rule or a prediction based on total protein charge.

31. Numerical or quantitative — A precursor has 310 residues, including a 25-residue cleaved signal. Estimate the mature chain length and peptide mass removed using 110 Da/residue.

  2. 335 residues; 2.75 kDa
  3. 285 residues; 27.5 kDa
  4. 310 residues; 0.275 kDa

A. 285 residues; 2.75 kDa

  1. 0.36 nm
  2. 1.6 nm
  3. 3.6 nm
  4. 36 nm
Answer and explanation

C. 3.6 nm

24 × 0.15 = 3.6 nm. Tilt and boundaries may change the effective projection across a real membrane.

33. Numerical or quantitative — At 300 K, 75% of a reporter is classified as inserted and 25% translocated. Using RT = 2.494 kJ/mol, estimate apparent insertion ΔG.

  1. +2.74 kJ/mol
  2. −2.74 kJ/mol
  3. −7.48 kJ/mol
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

B. −2.74 kJ/mol

K = 0.75/0.25 = 3; ΔG = −RT ln 3 ≈ −2.74 kJ/mol. Insertion predominance gives a negative sign.

34. Numerical or quantitative — At 300 K, a mutation changes inserted fraction from 0.5 to 0.2. Estimate ΔΔG for insertion (RT = 2.494 kJ/mol).

  3. +0.50 kJ/mol
  4. 0

B. +3.46 kJ/mol

Initial K = 1, ΔG = 0. Mutant K = 0.25, so ΔG = −2.494 ln 0.25 ≈ +3.46 kJ/mol. The mutation disfavors insertion in this assay.

  2. 10 min
  3. 15 min
  4. 30 min

B. 10 min

120/960 = 1/8 = (1/2)^3. Three half-times occupy 30 min; each is 10 min. The exit rate is about 0.0693 min⁻¹.

  2. 368 units
  3. 632 units
  4. 950 units

B. 368 units

E = 1000 exp(−0.05 × 20) = 1000/e ≈ 368. The 632 lost units have exited the ER under the assumptions, not necessarily reached the medium.

37. Numerical or quantitative — An ER cohort has k_e = 0.03 and k_d = 0.07 min⁻¹. What eventual fraction is exported in the competing first-order model?

  2. 0.07
  3. 0.30
  4. 0.70

C. 0.30

The export probability is k_e/(k_e+k_d) = 0.03/0.10 = 0.30. The total disappearance rate is 0.10 min⁻¹, which alone does not specify destination.

  4. Both 80%

B. A 75%, B 25%

Each complete span exchanges sides. Five is odd, so the C terminus is opposite the N terminus. This assumes other spans remain correctly inserted.

  4. The fusion is glycosylated

Glycan and protection indicate lumenal access. After controlling glycan mass, a larger chain supports retention of the signal segment. Direct N-terminal analysis would confirm cleavage.

47. Experimental or integrative — A type I reporter lacking its stop-transfer helix is fully protected in intact microsomes and no longer carbonate-resistant. Which interpretation fits?

  1. It probably became a soluble lumenal product
  2. It necessarily failed targeting
  3. It has two membrane spans
  4. Its ribosome entered the lumen
Answer and explanation

A. It probably became a soluble lumenal product

The initial signal still permits entry. Loss of the retained anchor can make the downstream chain lumenal and soluble; eventual secretion depends on later maturation and sorting.

48. Experimental or integrative — A protein has an N-terminal epitope protected in intact microsomes, C-terminal epitope digested, and an uncleaved single signal anchor. Which topology fits?

  1. Type II
  2. Type III
  3. A soluble lumenal protein
  4. A soluble cytosolic protein
Answer and explanation

B. Type III

N lumenal/C cytosolic plus one uncleaved anchor fits type III. A type I precursor instead has a separate cleavable signal; soluble proteins do not normally have this opposite-sidedness.

49. Experimental or integrative — A four-span reporter starts cytosolic. A validated glycosylation site between helices 2 and 3 is modified. What should you conclude?

  1. The assumed ideal topology may be wrong
  2. Every sequon is always modified
  3. The N terminus must be extracellular in all cells
  4. The glycan proves secretion
Answer and explanation

A. The assumed ideal topology may be wrong

The ideal model predicts that loop to be cytosolic. Positive glycosylation suggests lumenal access, so reconsider span insertion, orientation or reporter-induced changes.

50. Experimental or integrative — A mutant reporter enters ER normally but stays BiP-associated and is poorly exported; an independently measured unrelated cargo is exported normally. What is most likely?

  1. Global COPII failure
  2. A substrate-specific folding or assembly defect
  3. Complete SRP depletion
  4. No translation of the reporter
Answer and explanation

B. A substrate-specific folding or assembly defect

The early entry marker and unaffected cargo argue against a general targeting/export failure. Persistent BiP association suggests delayed maturation, although other substrate-specific interactions could contribute.

51. Experimental or integrative — A glycoprotein is cleaved and protected, remains Endo H sensitive, and fails to appear in the medium. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. It never entered ER
  2. It is definitely lysosomal
  3. An early maturation/export defect is consistent, but the exact stage is unresolved
  4. It must have reached the surface
Answer and explanation

C. An early maturation/export defect is consistent, but the exact stage is unresolved

Initial processing supports entry. For a validated reporter, continued sensitivity may suggest delayed Golgi processing; it does not distinguish folding from export machinery without further assays.

52. Experimental or integrative — A reporter with a functional KDEL signal becomes more abundant in the medium after receptor inactivation, but early cleavage remains normal. What changed?

  1. Initial aminoacylation
  2. Post-entry retention/retrieval
  3. The genetic code
  4. Signal peptide sequence
Answer and explanation

B. Post-entry retention/retrieval

Normal entry and cleavage separate early targeting from later localisation. Disrupting retrieval can increase escape; it need not alter translation or signal recognition.

53. Experimental or integrative — A secretory enzyme has acquired Endo H resistance, but remains intracellular after acute exocytosis inhibition. Which conclusion follows?

  1. It certainly never reached ER
  2. Later processing occurred despite failure of final release
  3. The glycan was attached in cytosol
  4. A targeting sequence must be deleted
Answer and explanation

B. Later processing occurred despite failure of final release

For the validated substrate, resistance supports passage to a later processing state. Failure of secretion can occur after Golgi transit.

54. Experimental or integrative — A short chase after inhibiting new protein synthesis still shows stimulus-dependent hormone release. Which explanation is strongest?

  1. No proteins were previously made
  2. Stored granules contain already matured cargo
  3. Translation now occurs in extracellular fluid
  4. The hormone has no secretory pathway
Answer and explanation

B. Stored granules contain already matured cargo

A pre-existing releasable pool separates final exocytosis from new synthesis. Extended inhibition may eventually deplete the store.

55. Experimental or integrative — A lysosomal hydrolase is normally retained intracellularly but a sorting mutant releases more into medium; ER entry and glycan transfer are normal. What should be investigated first?

  1. Failure of lysosomal address recognition or generation
  2. Lack of ribosomal decoding
  3. Failure of all signal peptides
  4. Nuclear pore diameter
Answer and explanation

A. Failure of lysosomal address recognition or generation

Later lysosomal sorting can fail despite normal entry. Check the relevant carbohydrate address and receptor-dependent trafficking; increased extracellular enzyme is misdelivery, not evidence of improved lysosomal function.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Rough ER has its own ribosome type
ER-bound and free cytosolic ribosomes are the same population.
Ribosomes enter ER lumen
Only the chain passes through the conducting route.
All hydrophobic sequences are cleaved
Signal anchors are retained; cleavage depends on context.
Stop-transfer stops translation
It stops downstream lumenal transfer, not elongation.
Sec61 hydrolyses one ATP per residue
Sec61 is a channel; synthesis and targeting use separate energy-dependent factors.
Positive-inside is absolute
Local charges bias orientation but other determinants matter.
A pellet proves membrane integration
Lumenal cargo, peripheral proteins and aggregates can also pellet.
A sequon always receives a glycan
Accessibility and location influence occupancy.
No glycan means cytosolic
Absence alone is not conclusive.
KDEL prevents all ER exit
Retrieval recaptures escaped proteins.
Endo H resistance proves secretion
It identifies a processing state, not final location.
Rapid disappearance proves export
Degradation may contribute to disappearance.
All ER entry is cotranslational
Tail anchors and some post-translational clients are exceptions.
All extracellular protein uses ER
Unconventional release and damage are alternatives.
