1. From a folded chain to a working molecular machine

Imagine a chain of 250 amino acids emerging from a ribosome. The chain contains helices, strands and flexible loops, but it cannot become a useful enzyme merely by acquiring those local shapes. A catalytic histidine near residue 40 might have to meet an aspartate near residue 100 and a serine near residue 200. The chain must arrange its distant regions so that the correct atoms meet in three-dimensional space. Now imagine two copies of that enzyme coming together to create a pocket that neither chain can form alone. We have moved from secondary structure to tertiary structure, and then from tertiary structure to quaternary organisation.

Primary structure tells us the covalent order of residues. Secondary structure describes recurring local backbone conformations, especially helices and sheets. Tertiary structure describes the overall three-dimensional arrangement of one polypeptide, including the positions of side chains and the relative arrangement of its structural regions. Quaternary structure describes how two or more polypeptide subunits associate in a biological complex. The distinction depends on the number of chains, not on size or complexity: a huge single-chain enzyme can have an elaborate tertiary structure without possessing quaternary structure.

Do not imagine these levels as four isolated events that always occur in a fixed temporal order. Local structure, long-range contacts and assembly can develop together during synthesis or folding. The hierarchy is primarily a way to describe the organisation we observe. A chain may retain local structure without possessing a single stable global fold; certain proteins remain disordered until a partner binds. Others gain their final shape as they join an oligomer. This node concentrates on the architecture and associations of proteins; the kinetic routes by which proteins reach such states belong to 1.C.04.

Learning checkpoint

When a single polypeptide folds into two compact lobes, the result is still tertiary structure. When separate polypeptides associate, the interchain arrangement belongs to quaternary structure, even when a disulfide bond links the chains.

Learning goals: recognise the architecture of common folds, distinguish motifs from domains and subunits, explain the chemical and energetic basis of packing, quantify association equilibria, interpret symmetry and stoichiometry, and use experimental evidence to decide whether a proposed assembly is biological.

2. Motifs: the small recurring patterns that build a fold

The secondary-structure elements discussed in 1.C.02 are not placed randomly. Connecting two beta strands by a short reverse turn generates a hairpin. Connecting a strand to a helix and then to another strand creates a beta-alpha-beta arrangement. Such recurring combinations are often called structural motifs or supersecondary structures. A motif is a recognisable local pattern; it is not necessarily independently stable or functional on its own.

Beta hairpin, Greek key and beta-alpha-beta

A beta hairpin normally consists of two adjacent antiparallel strands joined by a short turn or loop. Hydrogen bonds run between the backbones, while side chains project to alternating faces of the sheet. A Greek-key motif is a characteristic four-strand arrangement within an antiparallel sheet. In one conventional orientation the strand order across the sheet is 4–1–2–3, with chain connectivity running 1→2→3→4. The longer connection from 3 to 4 passes around the earlier strands. This pattern helps build many larger beta-rich folds, including the immunoglobulin-like family of beta sandwiches, although individual Greek-key motifs are not synonymous with an entire immunoglobulin domain.

A beta-alpha-beta motif has two sequence-successive strands separated by an alpha helix and connecting loops. The strands commonly lie parallel in a sheet and the helix packs against one face; most such crossovers are right-handed. Its geometry is especially common in alpha/beta enzymes. Repeating the motif can build larger frameworks in which a sheet creates a stable base and loops at one edge assemble the active site. Local topology is therefore a useful clue about the geometry of a catalytic pocket.

Helical motifs and metal-stabilised motifs

A helix-turn-helix (HTH) contains two helices connected by a short turn. In many bacterial DNA-binding regulators, one helix lies in the major groove and contributes sequence recognition, while the other positions it. This is distinct from a helix-loop-helix (HLH): the longer loop allows flexible helix positioning, and many basic HLH proteins dimerise via the HLH region while adjacent basic segments contact DNA. An EF-hand is another helix-loop-helix arrangement, but its loop coordinates calcium. Oxygen-containing ligands from side chains and backbone groups help hold Ca²⁺. Calmodulin contains four EF-hands arranged in two lobes; calcium binding shifts the conformational ensemble and changes target recognition.

In a classical Cys₂His₂ zinc finger, two cysteines and two histidines coordinate a Zn²⁺ ion, supporting a compact beta-beta-alpha structure. The metal commonly serves a structural rather than catalytic role. The helix often reads bases in the major groove; not every zinc-binding motif binds DNA. Different zinc-binding architectures use other ligand sets, such as Cys₄ or Cys₃His. Removing zinc can therefore disrupt the fold even when the chain's peptide bonds remain intact.

Coiled coils form when two or more helices wrap around a common axis. In the classical heptad repeat (abcdefg), positions a and d often carry hydrophobic residues that fit into neighbouring hydrophobic grooves. Electrostatic interactions at e and g may help determine partner selection and orientation. A leucine zipper is a particular coiled-coil dimerisation motif, frequently with leucines at d positions; in basic leucine zipper transcription factors, the neighbouring basic region contacts DNA. Coiled coils may be parallel or antiparallel, and their oligomeric state depends on the sequence and packing geometry.

A sequence motif, by contrast, is a recurring amino-acid pattern such as a conserved metal-binding or phosphorylation sequence. It need not form a fixed local three-dimensional motif. Conversely, similar structural motifs may be built from quite different sequences. A short sequence match alone is insufficient evidence that two proteins share a complete fold or biochemical function.

3. Domains, modules, linkers and multidomain proteins

Consider a protein that must recognise a phosphorylated receptor, bind a second partner and catalyse a reaction. A single compact region might not easily accommodate all three tasks. Evolution can combine relatively independent structural units into one chain. A domain is a region with a distinct structural organisation, often forming a compact and relatively independently folding unit. Domains typically have internal contacts more extensive than their contacts with other regions, although some depend strongly on their neighbours, ligands or partners.

Structural, functional and evolutionary domain definitions overlap but do not always match. A functional domain may require two structural regions; a structural domain may have no isolated biochemical activity. A sequence-defined domain is inferred from similarities or a profile model. A domain can be continuous in sequence or discontinuous: residues 1–70 and 180–250 might fold together, while residues 71–179 form another inserted domain. Cutting a sequence into equal segments would destroy this organisation. Domain boundary assignment therefore uses contacts, geometry, conservation and experimental evidence, not simply a fixed length or a visible linker.

A linker can be short and rigid, fixing relative domain orientation, or long and flexible, letting domains move independently. Some linkers contain regulatory sites. In many kinases, a catalytic domain is associated with regulatory domains that constrain its activity; release of an intramolecular interaction can expose the catalytic machinery. In antibodies, immunoglobulin domains provide compact frameworks, while hinge flexibility lets antigen-binding arms sample different positions. A modular protein can thus combine recognition, catalysis and mechanical movement without inventing a new fold for every task.

How modularity evolves

Gene duplication, fusion, recombination and exon shuffling can create new arrangements of existing domains. A single-domain ancestral protein may be duplicated; the copies can specialise as catalytic and regulatory units, or the genes may fuse to encode a multidomain protein. Repeated domains can create multivalent recognition. SH2 domains commonly recognise phosphotyrosine-containing motifs, SH3 domains often recognise proline-rich motifs, and PH domains form a versatile family whose members can bind particular phosphoinositides or protein partners. Their occurrence provides clues, not a guarantee of one particular ligand.

A conserved domain with a recognisable catalytic residue pattern may suggest an enzyme family. Nevertheless, domain composition, active-site integrity, substrate-binding loops and cellular context must all be considered. Two proteins with the same domains in a different order may couple their functions differently. Likewise, two domains with the same fold may catalyse different reactions. A fold is a structural framework, not a complete functional annotation.

Distinguish five terms

A motif is a recurring local pattern; a domain is a structural or functional unit; a fold is a characteristic overall topology; a chain is a covalently connected polypeptide; a subunit is a polypeptide participating in a multichain assembly. A structural repeat may be smaller than a domain or may repeat entire domains.

4. Tertiary architecture: bringing distant residues together

Local helices and sheets become useful when their positions relative to one another generate a specific surface. Consider a serine protease such as chymotrypsin: the catalytic triad uses Ser195, His57 and Asp102 (chymotrypsin numbering). The positions are far apart in sequence yet close in the native three-dimensional active site. Asp helps orient and tune His; His exchanges protons with Ser during catalysis. A mutation in a buried packing residue far from the triad could reduce catalysis by disturbing the geometry, even though the catalytic residues themselves are unchanged.

When looking at a folded structure, distinguish the hydrophobic core, solvent-exposed surface, buried pockets, flexible loops and domain interfaces. In many soluble proteins, nonpolar side chains are concentrated inside, while many polar or charged side chains contact water. This is a statistical preference, not an absolute rule. Hydrophobic surface patches can be essential for protein binding; polar residues may be buried when they form carefully satisfied hydrogen bonds or coordinate a cofactor. An active-site cavity can contain ordered water molecules. An exposed loop may be more mobile than the core, but a loop participating in metal coordination or a rigid interaction network can be highly ordered.

Protein structures are often visualised in ribbon cartoons, which emphasise backbone organisation but hide most atomic detail. A space-filling representation reveals packing and solvent accessibility. A molecular surface shows pockets and exposed patches. A contact map displays residue pairs that are near one another in three dimensions; its axes are sequence positions. Contacts close to the diagonal often reflect local structure, while distant off-diagonal contacts reveal long-range packing. For an antiparallel sheet, an off-diagonal strip may have negative slope; for parallel strands the corresponding strip typically has positive slope. The exact pattern depends on contact definitions and geometry.

For transmembrane proteins the environment changes the usual packing rule. Lipid-facing surfaces of membrane-spanning helices can be hydrophobic, whereas a water-filled channel may expose polar groups to its pore. Beta-barrel membrane proteins may have alternating inward and outward side chains, often with nonpolar residues facing the bilayer. A water-soluble domain attached to the same membrane protein can still have the ordinary soluble-protein core. Predicting which residues should be exposed always requires identifying what they are exposed to.

Not every biologically important region forms a compact structure. Intrinsically disordered proteins or regions lack a single persistent tertiary conformation under relevant conditions and sample ensembles. They can mediate regulation, flexible connections, or binding coupled to folding. Their existence does not contradict the relationship between structure and function; rather, it reminds us that an ensemble of conformations can be functional. A structure drawing is a snapshot or model, not a claim that every atom is permanently fixed.

5. The physical chemistry of tertiary packing

The folded state is a result of many competing energetic contributions. Individual hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts, electrostatic interactions and hydrophobic effects are not independently additive in a simple universal way, because changing one contact changes solvent exposure, neighbouring geometry and flexibility. We should ask how a proposed structural change alters the whole protein–solvent system rather than count attractive contacts as if each were an identical reward.

Hydrophobic effect and solvent exclusion

When nonpolar surface is buried on folding or binding, the solvent reorganises. The hydrophobic effect can favour burial of nonpolar groups in water. It is a solvent-mediated free-energy effect rather than a special attractive covalent bond between nonpolar side chains. Its enthalpic and entropic components vary with temperature, length scale and molecular environment. A leucine-to-aspartate mutation buried in a dry core can be destabilising because it removes favourable packing and introduces a polar or charged group whose interactions are not adequately satisfied. A leucine-to-valine substitution may be more conservative but can still produce a cavity or change packing.

Van der Waals contacts and steric complementarity

Atoms attract weakly at appropriate separations through dispersion interactions, but strong repulsion occurs at excessively short distance. A Lennard-Jones-type model uses U(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)12 − (σ/r)6], with r the atomic separation, ε the well depth and σ a distance parameter at which this model's potential is zero. The minimum occurs at r = 21/6σ. This is an illustrative pair potential, not a complete protein force field. A well-packed core avoids atomic overlap while achieving many close contacts. Replacing a small buried residue by a bulky one may cause clashes rather than improve stability.

Hydrogen bonding and desolvation

Hydrogen bonds require suitable donor–acceptor geometry and commonly involve backbone carbonyl oxygens and amide hydrogens or polar side chains. Buried hydrogen bonds can select a precise fold by satisfying polar groups where water is excluded. But the unfolded chain can also hydrogen-bond to water; the energetic gain depends on what interactions are replaced. An unsatisfied buried backbone carbonyl is usually unfavourable, and unusual structures often retain internal water molecules to satisfy such groups.

Electrostatics, salt bridges and local pKa

A salt bridge pairs oppositely charged groups such as an aspartate carboxylate and lysine ammonium group. Their Coulomb interaction is approximately proportional to q₁q₂/(εrr), where the relative permittivity εr describes dielectric screening. Electrostatics are longer range than typical direct packing contacts, but dissolved ions screen them, and protein interiors are not uniform dielectrics. A buried salt bridge may be favourable if it offsets the dehydration cost of both ions; it may be net destabilising when desolvation dominates. The local environment changes residue protonation and apparent pKa, so pH can influence structural stability and partner selection. Increasing salt can weaken favourable electrostatics, shield unfavourable repulsion or affect solubility; the net response is not universal.

Disulfides, metals and cofactors

Oxidation of two cysteine thiols creates a covalent disulfide (–S–S–) bridge. Intrachain disulfides constrain tertiary architecture; interchain disulfides constrain quaternary architecture. Disulfides can stabilise a folded state by limiting the conformational freedom of the unfolded chain; geometrically strained disulfides can have other effects. Extracellular and secretory-pathway proteins often contain these bridges because those environments favour their formation; the reducing cytosol of many cells typically does not, although exceptions exist. A reducing reagent such as DTT can cleave a disulfide without hydrolysing peptide bonds.

Metal coordination may help organise a protein around Zn²⁺, Ca²⁺, Fe or other ions. Cofactors such as heme can complete a binding pocket and change structural stability. An apoprotein lacks its required cofactor, whereas a holoprotein contains it. Removing a metal with a chelator can destroy a structurally essential binding site, but metal removal does not always unfold the entire chain. Assess the structural role and affinity of the metal independently of its possible catalytic role.

Important exam distinction

Hydrophobic interactions are not covalent bonds. Hydrogen bonds can be intra- or intermolecular. Disulfides are covalent but can connect regions of one chain or different chains. Removing a single interaction does not automatically destroy all other structural levels.

6. Protein folds: the recurring architecture of nature

Although many different amino-acid sequences exist, proteins repeatedly use successful three-dimensional frameworks. A protein fold is usually described by the arrangement and connectivity of its core secondary-structure elements. Two proteins may have the same secondary-structure content but different folds, much as two buildings may have the same number of floors yet different floor plans. Distinguishing arrangement from connectivity is essential for analysing structures.

All-alpha architectures

Myoglobin has the globin fold: a compact helical arrangement around a heme-binding pocket. Four-helix bundles use closely packed helices, with hydrophobic side chains facing the interior of the bundle. Helical bundles can form within one chain, between multiple chains, or through a mixture of both. Alpha-solenoid proteins such as some HEAT-repeat proteins contain successive helical repeats that generate curved scaffolds rather than a small globular core. A long coiled coil is a different helical architecture from a compact globin fold, even if both are predominantly alpha-helical.

All-beta architectures

An immunoglobulin-like domain contains two packed beta sheets forming a sandwich, with a core between them; antibody variable domains use loops on that framework for recognition. A beta barrel forms when a sheet closes around an axis so that the first and last strands participate in sheet closure. Soluble beta barrels may contain hydrophobic interiors or ligand cavities, while outer-membrane beta barrels often expose nonpolar residues to lipids and may contain a pore. A beta propeller comprises repeated blades arranged around a central axis; many blades consist of four antiparallel strands. WD-repeat proteins frequently form such scaffolds. The term barrel describes a structural geometry, not a guarantee that it is a channel.

Alpha/beta and alpha+beta folds

In classic SCOP terminology, alpha/beta domains frequently alternate helices and strands along the chain and often have a parallel or mixed sheet. Alpha+beta domains generally contain more segregated helical and beta regions, often with antiparallel sheets. These are descriptive tendencies rather than inflexible rules. CATH combines mixed alpha/beta architectures at its broad Class level and distinguishes them further by architecture and topology.

The Rossmann fold is a classic nucleotide-binding framework with a central mostly parallel beta sheet flanked by helices. Many NAD-binding enzymes share Rossmann-like architecture and an N-terminal glycine-rich region that accommodates nucleotide phosphates, but not every Rossmann-like domain binds NAD and not every nucleotide-binding protein has this fold. Specific binding depends on loop conformations, hydrogen-bond networks and the chemical details of the pocket.

The TIM barrel, named after triose phosphate isomerase, contains eight beta strands forming an internal parallel barrel, surrounded by eight alpha helices in a common idealised description. The chain follows repeated beta-alpha connections. Catalytic residues often occur in loops at the C-terminal ends of the beta strands. Many enzymes use this topology but perform very different chemical reactions. Other alpha/beta folds include the flavodoxin-like fold and many P-loop NTPase frameworks; similar nucleotide requirements do not imply identical global topology.

Fold recognition is valuable because structural similarity can persist after sequence identity has become weak. However, structural convergence can occur. To infer common ancestry, combine topology with sequence profiles, conserved rare features, catalytic residues and phylogenetic evidence rather than treating every similar shape as homologous.

7. Structural classification and the evidence for evolutionary relationships

A classification system turns the three-dimensional variety of proteins into navigable groups. CATH and SCOP classify domains rather than assuming an entire multidomain chain belongs to one structural class. CATH uses four major levels: Class, Architecture, Topology and Homologous superfamily. The hierarchy separates what a domain looks like from how its parts connect and whether evidence supports common ancestry. For example, two proteins with similar proportions of helices and sheets may share a Class while differing at every lower level.

CATH levelQuestion answered
C — ClassWhat is the broad secondary-structure composition (mainly α, mainly β, mixed, few secondary structures)?

T — TopologyHow are secondary-structure elements arranged and connected?
H — Homologous superfamilyIs there sufficient evidence for shared evolutionary ancestry?

SCOP (Structural Classification of Proteins) traditionally uses class, fold, superfamily and family as key levels. A fold groups domains sharing a structural framework. A superfamily expresses inferred evolutionary relationship, even where sequence similarity is weak; a family typically brings together more clearly related members. CATH and SCOP use different classification decisions, so labels should not be expected to translate one-to-one. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) is an archive of macromolecular structural models, not itself the same type of classification as CATH or SCOP. Pfam provides sequence-profile-based family/domain annotation, and InterPro integrates several predictive resources.

Suppose two domains contain six beta strands and five helices. If their arrangement looks alike but the order of strand connections differs, they may share a CATH architecture but not topology. If the topology also matches, one may suspect common ancestry, yet the classification into a homologous superfamily requires more evidence. Comparing the conserved core is often more informative than comparing variable surface loops. Structural alignments quantify similarity after superposition: RMSD = √[Σdᵢ²/n], where dᵢ is the distance between each of n matched atom pairs after alignment. Low RMSD over only a tiny aligned fragment is weak evidence for full-domain similarity; report aligned length, coverage and which atoms were selected.

Protein topology must not be confused with DNA linking number from 1.B.05. In fold classification the word refers to how secondary-structure elements are connected and arranged. Some protein chains contain genuine knots or slipknots, but they are not implied by an ordinary CATH topology assignment. A ribbon diagram can also appear crossed because it is projected onto a screen. A crossing in a two-dimensional picture is not proof of a molecular knot.

Exam trap

Identical function does not prove identical fold, and identical fold does not prove identical function or common ancestry. Homology is an evolutionary relationship, not a percentage. Sequence identity and structural similarity can be quantified; ancestry is inferred.

8. Quaternary organisation: when separate chains form a functional unit

Once we have a folded chain, a new possibility appears: its surface may be shaped to recognise another chain. The resulting biological assembly can contain two, three, four or many subunits. A monomer is a single polypeptide unit; a dimer contains two, a trimer three, a tetramer four, a hexamer six and an octamer eight. A homooligomer consists of copies of the same type of chain; a heterooligomer contains at least two distinct types. The term protomer is used in different ways, often for the repeating unit of a larger oligomer, so a protomer can itself be a pair of unlike chains.

Adult human haemoglobin A (HbA) is an α₂β₂ heterotetramer; its α and β chains are related but not identical. Myoglobin is a monomeric heme protein with a related globin fold. The two proteins illustrate how a similar tertiary framework can participate in different higher-order arrangements. Haemoglobin's detailed oxygen binding and allosteric models will be developed in 1.C.05. Here the important fact is that its interfaces permit coordinated changes between subunits, a capability that the individual globin fold does not by itself supply.

Lactate dehydrogenase in mammals provides another important illustration. Its H and M subunit types form tetramers with five common stoichiometric isoenzyme classes: H₄, H₃M, H₂M₂, HM₃ and M₄. Differences in composition can alter tissue distribution and catalytic properties. There can be multiple geometric arrangements of a given stoichiometry; the five isoenzyme classes do not necessarily enumerate every microscopic arrangement. An oligomeric protein is not necessarily an allosteric protein: subunits may act independently or exchange conformational information depending on their interfaces.

Antibodies have two heavy and two light chains, associated by noncovalent contacts and disulfide bonds in conventional IgG. Each chain contains more than one domain, and a recognition site is assembled from the variable domains of a heavy and a light chain. The disulfide linkage is an interchain covalent stabiliser, not a reason to describe the entire assembly as one polypeptide. In contrast, fusion of two genes into a continuous polypeptide changes chain count and can transform an interchain association into an intrachain domain interface. A single-chain engineered antibody fragment is a useful conceptual example.

Do not equate oligomeric state with the number of distinct genes. A homotetramer uses four copies of a single chain type, whereas an α₂β₂ tetramer uses two types and four physical chains. Nor should cofactors such as heme, zinc or ATP be counted as polypeptide subunits. A larger molecular complex may also contain RNA, lipid or carbohydrates; its protein chain arrangement is part of the larger assembly, but those components are not extra polypeptides.

9. How interfaces recognise and stabilise partners

Association requires a compatible interface. Two protein surfaces must fit geometrically and chemically while avoiding steric clashes. Hydrophobic patches can become buried, polar groups can form interchain hydrogen bonds, oppositely charged residues can form salt bridges, and multiple weak dispersion interactions can accumulate. Specificity does not arise merely from making the strongest conceivable interaction: the desired partner must be favoured over competing partners, which may require excluding incorrectly shaped or chemically incompatible surfaces.

Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) is the area traced by the centre of a solvent-sized probe over the molecular surface. The total buried SASA when A and B associate is ΔSASA = SASA(A) + SASA(B) − SASA(AB), evaluated with consistent coordinates and probe definition. Some software reports interface area as half this total, corresponding to an average per partner. Always inspect the convention. A large interface is often consistent with biological association, but crystal contacts can be extensive and some real interfaces are small. Area alone cannot prove whether a dimer is physiological.

Many interfaces contain energetic hot spots, a small subset of residues whose mutations produce relatively large changes in binding free energy. Alanine scanning replaces selected side chains by alanine to assess their contribution while leaving the backbone largely intact. A mutation that weakens association might remove a direct contact, perturb a nearby structural region or disrupt folding of the monomer. An independent folding assay is therefore necessary before interpreting a loss of binding as an interface-specific effect.

Permanent, transient, obligate and non-obligate assemblies

A transient association can form and dissociate during normal cell activity, such as many signalling interactions. A permanent association is long-lived under physiological conditions. An obligate oligomer depends on its association for native stability or function; non-obligate partners can exist as independently folded species. These distinctions describe different properties: a folded monomer can form a long-lived complex, and an obligate assembly may still dissociate under altered conditions. Cellular concentration, localisation, phosphorylation, ligand binding, salt and pH can affect the population of complexes.

Interchain beta sheets may extend one chain's sheet through backbone hydrogen bonds with another chain. Helical interfaces may form coiled coils or compact bundles. Domain swapping is a special arrangement in which equivalent structural elements from separate chains exchange positions: a segment from A completes a domain-like structure in B and vice versa. It can produce dimers or higher oligomers and is distinct from merely contacting external surfaces. It can couple normal association to alternative conformations and in some cases aggregation; not every oligomer is domain-swapped.

Membrane protein interfaces can involve residues shielded from lipids, tightly packed helix surfaces and bound lipid molecules that contribute to assembly. A detergent-solubilised membrane protein may exhibit a different oligomeric state from its state in a membrane. For such proteins, the lipid or detergent environment and the protein concentration should be reported along with the measured assembly.

10. Quantifying association: affinity, concentration and free energy

Protein association is often reversible. For a simple heterodimer, A + B ⇌ AB, define Kd = [A][B]/[AB], where brackets refer to free equilibrium concentrations and Kd has concentration units for this 1:1 reaction. Ka = 1/Kd has reciprocal concentration units. A smaller Kd means stronger affinity under the same conditions. For elementary one-step binding, Kd = koff/kon, with koff in s⁻¹ and kon in M⁻¹ s⁻¹. More complex mechanisms can give apparent rate constants that do not obey this simple interpretation.

Using standard concentration c° = 1 M, the standard binding free energy is ΔG° = RT ln(Kd/c°). R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, a Kd of 1 µM gives ΔG° ≈ 2.478 kJ mol⁻¹ × ln(10⁻⁶) ≈ −34.2 kJ mol⁻¹. A tenfold weakening gives ΔΔG° = RT ln(10) ≈ +5.7 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. A positive difference for mutant minus wild type means weaker binding under the measured conditions.

The identity ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° accounts for enthalpic and entropic contributions from both proteins and solvent. Association usually restricts the relative translational and rotational motion of partners and may reduce conformational entropy. Hydration changes and release of ions or water can compensate for these costs. A favourable ΔH° alone cannot ensure tight binding. Similarly, a negative standard free energy does not imply that all molecules must be bound at every concentration: the actual reaction free energy depends on concentrations.

Heterodimer occupancy

If free B is buffered at a fixed concentration and A has one independent binding site, the fraction of A bound is θ = [B]/(Kd + [B]). At [B] = Kd, half of A is bound. This statement uses free B, not total B. If both components are supplied at comparable concentrations and binding appreciably depletes B, use mass balance. Let AT = [A] + [AB] and BT = [B] + [AB]. With x = [AB], x solves x² − (AT + BT + Kd)x + ATBT = 0. The physical solution is the smaller root. For AT = BT = 1 µM and Kd = 1 µM, x = (3 − √5)/2 µM ≈ 0.382 µM, so 38.2% rather than 50% of A is bound.

Homodimer equilibrium and mass balance

For 2M ⇌ D, Kd = [M]²/[D]. The total concentration CT expressed in monomer equivalents is [M] + 2[D]. Substituting [D] = [M]²/Kd gives 2[M]² + Kd[M] − KdCT = 0. Thus [M] = (−Kd + √(Kd² + 8KdCT))/4. The fraction of chains in dimers is 2[D]/CT; the fraction of molecular particles that are dimers is [D]/([M]+[D]). These fractions are different because each dimer contains two chains.

Example: Kd = 10 µM and CT = 30 µM. Solving gives [M] = 10 µM and [D] = 10 µM. Therefore 20 of the 30 µM monomer equivalents are in dimers (66.7%), although dimers account for only half of the molecular particles. At CT = Kd, half of chains are in dimers because [M] = Kd/2 and [D] = Kd/4. Dilution generally shifts this simple equilibrium toward monomer; dilution does not necessarily change Kd itself.

Higher-order association

A direct ideal association nM ⇌ Mn gives a concentration-form dissociation quotient [M]n/[Mn] with units of concentration to the power n−1. It must not be treated as a conventional 1:1 micromolar Kd. Many tetramers form through dimeric intermediates, with different stepwise constants. Use the correct stoichiometry and mass-balance equations; an apparent single midpoint may conceal multiple assembly processes.

Quantitative rule

Always identify free versus total concentration, the reaction stoichiometry, and whether the requested fraction counts chains or molecular particles. Standard-state free energies require dimensionless equilibrium constants.

11. Symmetry, stoichiometry and higher-order architectures

Repeated subunits allow nature to build large structures from a limited set of sequences. If identical subunits use compatible interfaces, assembly can generate rotational symmetry. Cyclic symmetry Cn has a principal n-fold rotational axis. Rotation by 360°/n brings equivalent positions into coincidence. A C₂ homodimer rotates by 180°, a C₃ trimer by 120°, and a C₆ ring by 60°. Dihedral symmetry Dn adds n twofold axes perpendicular to the principal n-fold axis and has 2n proper rotational symmetry operations. A homotetramer can have D₂ symmetry, but not every tetramer has D₂ symmetry: C₄ or asymmetric arrangements are also possible.

Some large assemblies use higher point symmetries: tetrahedral, octahedral or icosahedral. Icosahedral rotational symmetry has 60 proper rotational operations. In the simplest ideal capsid, 60 equivalent copies of a protein occupy symmetry-related positions; many icosahedral viral capsids contain 60T subunits in quasi-equivalent environments, with triangulation number T = h² + hk + k² for nonnegative integers h and k (classical Caspar–Klug construction). Not all capsids obey this simple description; some have multiple protein types, deviations or distinct architecture. A T=3 ideal capsid has 180 subunits, not three subunits.

Helical symmetry differs from point symmetry. Translation along the axis is coupled to rotation about it, allowing long assemblies such as protein filaments. Actin polymerises into polar filaments; tubulin αβ heterodimers assemble into protofilaments that form microtubules, typically with 13 protofilaments. These polymers do not have one fixed small oligomeric stoichiometry. Their growth and disassembly are controlled by subunit concentrations, nucleotide state and regulatory factors. A repeated protomer does not make the entire filament a single covalent chain.

Protein cages provide another strategy. Ferritin commonly assembles into a 24-subunit shell that stores iron in a protected interior; the shell has channels through which iron can enter or leave. GroEL contains two stacked seven-subunit rings, forming a tetradecamer; GroES is a seven-subunit cap that associates during the chaperonin cycle. The geometry illustrates how repeated subunits create a central chamber, while details of folding assistance belong to 1.C.04. Proteasomes, ribosomes and ATP synthase contain multiple components with distinct roles; they illustrate modular assembly rather than one universal symmetry rule.

Sometimes symmetry is approximate rather than exact (pseudosymmetry). Related but distinct subunits can occupy similar positions, and a symmetric assembly may become asymmetric when only one site binds a ligand or during a catalytic cycle. A static symmetry label therefore does not imply that every functional state must have the same symmetry. An assembly may also be conformationally heterogeneous, with several subunits adopting different states at the same moment.

12. Why build an oligomer instead of a bigger single chain?

One advantage is genetic economy: identical subunits can generate repeated surfaces without requiring a gene encoding every copy. Another is composite function. A catalytic site may span a subunit interface, or one subunit may bind a substrate while a second supplies a catalytic group. Mutating the interface can eliminate activity without destroying the individual tertiary folds. Conversely, if all active sites are self-contained and subunits work independently, dissociation may not eliminate intrinsic catalytic function even though it changes the complex's size.

Multivalent assemblies increase opportunities for binding. A single weak contact can dissociate rapidly, but multiple simultaneous contacts can create high apparent binding strength, called avidity. Avidity is not identical to intrinsic affinity of one binding site. An antibody with two recognition sites may bind a multivalent antigen more strongly overall than an isolated Fab fragment, even if the microscopic affinity of each site is unchanged. The spacing and orientation of sites determine whether they can bind simultaneously.

Allostery occurs when a perturbation at one site changes the behaviour of another. Oligomeric interfaces can transmit changes in packing, electrostatics, flexibility or domain orientation. Haemoglobin is the classical example of subunit communication, but allostery also occurs in monomers, so the existence of quaternary structure is neither necessary nor sufficient for allostery. Cooperativity describes how occupancy at one site affects occupancy of other sites; it is one type of coupled binding behaviour. A Hill coefficient greater than one can indicate positive cooperativity in suitable binding data, but it should not be read directly as the number of subunits. Detailed binding models are reserved for 1.C.05.

Dynamic assembly is itself a regulatory mechanism. Phosphorylation can create a docking motif, change charge balance or expose a previously hidden interface. Ligand binding may stabilise an oligomeric state. Membrane localisation can increase effective local concentration and promote association. A chain that is inactive as a monomer can become active after dimerisation; another protein may be inhibited by assembly into a storage complex. The direction of functional change must be established experimentally, rather than assumed from the change in mass.

Higher-order interactions have disease consequences. The Glu6Val substitution in the mature β-globin chain of sickle haemoglobin creates a nonpolar surface patch that favours intermolecular contacts in the deoxygenated state and can drive polymerisation. The fundamental lesson is that a surface mutation can profoundly affect association without requiring global unfolding of the monomer. A different kind of aberrant association occurs in amyloid fibrils, where repeated cross-beta architecture may be formed from partially folded or disordered proteins. Amyloid is a specific ordered aggregate architecture, not a synonym for every protein aggregate. Folding and aggregation mechanisms will be studied in the next node.

Mechanism versus appearance

A multimer can be active, inactive, regulatory, structural or pathological. A high-mass band or large particle is not sufficient evidence for a useful physiological oligomer.

13. How experimentalists determine structure and assembly

To infer quaternary structure correctly, combine information about individual chains with evidence about the intact native complex. An experimental estimate of molecular mass is not automatically a chain count unless the chain masses are known. Likewise, a beautiful crystal structure may display a packing contact that does not occur in solution. Different methods answer different questions, and agreement between orthogonal methods is more persuasive than any single observation.

Denaturing and native electrophoresis

SDS-PAGE commonly unfolds proteins and coats them with negatively charged detergent, so migration mainly reflects polypeptide mass. Reducing SDS-PAGE additionally breaks disulfide bonds. Nonreducing SDS-PAGE generally disrupts noncovalent interfaces but can preserve covalently linked chains. Suppose a protein migrates at about 80 kDa without reducing reagent and at 40 kDa after reduction. A reasonable hypothesis is an interchain disulfide-linked dimer; confirmation requires checking chain identity and possible anomalies. If both lanes show 40 kDa, the native sample may still be an 80-kDa noncovalent dimer. Standard SDS-PAGE cannot reliably determine the native oligomeric state alone.

Native PAGE preserves more native interactions but migration depends on mass, charge, shape and electrophoresis conditions. A slow band may be large, poorly charged or differently shaped. Blue native PAGE can help analyse complexes, including membrane complexes, but the dye, detergents and conditions can influence the measured species. Use carefully chosen standards and independent evidence.

Size exclusion and absolute mass methods

Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) separates primarily by hydrodynamic size: larger particles access less pore volume and elute earlier. An elongated monomer can elute at a volume expected for a larger globular protein. SEC calibrated with globular standards therefore yields an apparent molecular mass. Coupling SEC with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) and concentration detection estimates molar mass without assuming a globular shape; it can distinguish an elongated 40-kDa monomer from a compact 80-kDa dimer. Correct concentration determination and sample quality remain important, particularly for detergent-bound proteins or glycoproteins.

Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) offers complementary information. Sedimentation velocity measures movement in a centrifugal field and yields distributions related to mass, shape and friction. Sedimentation equilibrium can estimate molar mass and association equilibria from concentration gradients under suitable models. The Svedberg equation is M = RTs/[D(1 − v̄ρ)], where s is the sedimentation coefficient, D the translational diffusion coefficient, v̄ partial specific volume and ρ solvent density. Use consistent units; 1 S = 10⁻¹³ s. Sedimentation coefficients are not simply additive with subunit number.

Native mass spectrometry can directly resolve intact assembly masses and distinguish stoichiometric species. Transfer into the gas phase can alter some interactions, so solution-based confirmation is valuable. Chemical crosslinking can capture neighbouring chains or residues, but crosslinks depend on accessible reactive groups and geometry and can trap transient contacts. FRET measures distance-dependent energy transfer between appropriate labels; for a simple donor–acceptor pair E = 1/[1 + (r/R₀)6], where R₀ is the distance at 50% efficiency. A FRET signal suggests proximity under model assumptions, not by itself a precise oligomer stoichiometry.

Atomic structures and solution ensembles

X-ray crystallography can give atomic models when good crystals and data are available. The asymmetric unit is the smallest unique content of the crystal used with symmetry operations to generate the lattice. The biologically relevant assembly may be smaller, equal or larger than the asymmetric unit. RCSB PDB provides biological assembly annotations; inspect the interface, solution data and relevant literature rather than counting chains in the asymmetric unit alone.

NMR spectroscopy can characterise structures and dynamics in solution; inter-residue and interchain restraints can identify contacts, although size and sample behaviour constrain the experiments. Cryo-electron microscopy is powerful for large assemblies and multiple conformational states; local resolution and conformational heterogeneity affect detail. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) gives information about overall size, shape and radius of gyration in solution but usually does not uniquely determine atomic coordinates. Prediction tools can propose plausible monomer and multimer structures; a confident predicted fold or interface is not direct proof of its physiological existence or exact affinity.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) can estimate affinity, binding stoichiometry and enthalpy when the experiment is appropriately designed and fitted. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) can provide binding and kinetic measurements, with potential artefacts from immobilisation, mass transport and rebinding. Comparing multiple concentrations is essential for identifying reversible association rather than treating a concentration-dependent mixture as a permanently fixed oligomer.

Experimental logic

First measure chain masses, then native mass and its concentration dependence, then test interface-specific mutations and assess whether the monomer remains folded. Finally compare a proposed structure with the independent solution evidence.

14. A complete worked investigation: is this protein a functional dimer?

Imagine that a newly purified enzyme X has a 35-kDa chain by reducing and nonreducing SDS-PAGE. SEC calibrated with globular proteins gives an apparent mass of 92 kDa. SEC-MALS measures 69–71 kDa across the main peak at high loading concentration. At low concentration, it detects a population near 35 kDa. The crystal asymmetric unit contains one chain, but applying a crystallographic twofold operation produces a pair with complementary surfaces. An interface residue, Leu88, is replaced by alanine. The mutant has a main 35-kDa SEC-MALS peak and greatly reduced catalytic activity.

Start with the SDS result. The individual chains have a mass of about 35 kDa, and there is no detected disulfide-linked oligomer that survives nonreducing SDS treatment. The native 70-kDa mass suggests two chains. The 92-kDa apparent SEC result does not require a third chain: the dimer may be elongated or have other hydrodynamic differences from the calibration standards. Its dilution-dependent mass suggests a reversible monomer–dimer equilibrium. The one-chain asymmetric unit does not rule out the dimer because the other chain may be generated by crystal symmetry.

Now examine the mutation. Leu88 may contribute favourable nonpolar packing or interface shape. The mutant's lower native mass is consistent with weakened association. But its reduced catalysis alone does not establish that the catalytic site spans the interface. It might misfold subtly, lose a metal ion or perturb a nearby active-site loop. Compare wild type and mutant by circular dichroism (secondary-structure-sensitive), thermal or chemical stability measurements, ligand binding or higher-resolution structural tests. CD alone cannot exclude a tertiary packing change. If the mutant is demonstrably folded and still loses assembly and activity, the evidence for assembly-dependent function becomes stronger.

To quantify affinity, measure equilibrium populations across a range of total concentrations and fit the 2M ⇌ D mass balance. If the monomer and dimer exchange rapidly during SEC, a single peak may show a concentration-dependent weight-average molar mass. Slow exchange may give distinct peaks. Interpret the measurement using the relevant response model instead of automatically converting one intermediate mass to a simple mole fraction. If other oligomers appear, extend the equilibrium model.

Consider a second experiment with protein Y: reducing SDS-PAGE has bands at 20 and 30 kDa, while a native method gives 100 kDa. If Y consists only of these two types in positive integer amounts, solve 20a + 30b = 100. Dividing by ten gives 2a + 3b = 10. The positive integer solution is a = 2, b = 2, so A₂B₂ is the only such composition under those assumptions. If a type is absent, an A₅ homomer also gives 100 kDa. Mixtures, modifications and mass uncertainty change the inference. Chain identity, band stoichiometry or native MS can confirm the result.

This investigative approach connects every earlier idea: a folded framework positions residues, interface chemistry selects compatible partners, association equilibrium determines the population, and experimental evidence decides which assembly is biologically meaningful. It also shows why memorising a list of protein examples without learning the physical logic is not enough for CSIR-NET Part C.

15. CSIR-NET Part B — conceptual practice

These 18 original single-correct questions are written for CSIR-NET and SET preparation. They are not claimed to be previous-year questions. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

1. A single chain contains three independently folding domains. Which highest classical structural level is established?

  1. Secondary
  2. Tertiary
  3. Quaternary
  4. Primary
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Three domains of one chain give tertiary organisation; quaternary requires more than one polypeptide.

2. A beta-alpha-beta motif commonly connects which strands?

  1. Parallel
  2. Necessarily antiparallel
  3. Unrelated chains only
  4. No beta strands
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. The common motif links two parallel strands through an intervening helix.

3. What principally distinguishes CATH Architecture from Topology?

  1. pH
  2. Chain molecular mass
  3. Connectivity of secondary elements
  4. Number of genes
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Architecture describes spatial arrangement; topology includes connectivity.

4. Which statement concerning a protein domain is correct?

  1. All domains are exactly 100 residues
  2. A domain must be a separate chain
  3. Domains may be discontinuous in sequence
  4. All domains bind DNA
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Inserted domains can separate sequence segments that form one structural domain.

5. An EF-hand most commonly coordinates which ion?

  1. Na⁺
  2. Ca²⁺
  3. Cl⁻
  4. I⁻
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The EF-hand loop coordinates calcium through oxygen ligands.

6. Which is characteristic of a classical Cys₂His₂ zinc finger?

  1. Zinc-supported ββα organisation
  2. Eight parallel β strands
  3. Four identical chains
  4. Heme binding
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Zinc coordinates cysteine and histidine side chains, supporting the local fold.

7. Which feature is typical of a TIM barrel?

  1. Eight inner parallel β strands surrounded by helices
  2. Four separate chains by definition
  3. Two antiparallel sheets only
  4. One long coiled coil
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. The repeated beta-alpha arrangement forms the characteristic barrel.

8. Which is most accurate about buried polar groups?

  1. Always absent
  2. Always destabilising
  3. Often satisfied by hydrogen bonds, ions or water
  4. Always charged
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Burial is context-dependent; internal hydrogen-bonding can satisfy polar groups.

9. Which treatment is most directly intended to cleave a disulfide?

  1. DTT
  2. A neutral buffer alone
  3. Increasing protein concentration
  4. Native SEC
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. DTT is a reducing reagent; it does not directly hydrolyse peptide bonds.

10. The normal adult HbA assembly is:

  1. α₄
  2. β₄
  3. α₂β₂
  4. αβ
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. It is a heterotetramer of two alpha and two beta globins.

11. A C₆ cyclic assembly has a principal rotation angle of:

  1. 30°
  2. 45°
  3. 60°
  4. 120°
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. 360°/6 = 60°.

12. Which statement about hydrophobic effects is correct?

  1. They are covalent bonds
  2. They reflect solvent-mediated free energy
  3. They require cysteine
  4. They favour every exposed hydrophobic residue
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Solvent reorganisation is central; environment determines the effect.

13. Which experiment best separates hydrodynamic size from absolute solution mass among the choices?

  1. Globular-standard SEC alone
  2. SDS-PAGE alone
  3. SEC-MALS
  4. Isoelectric focusing
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. MALS coupled to concentration detection estimates molar mass independently of SEC calibration shape assumptions.

14. Which statement is correct concerning allostery?

  1. Every dimer is allosteric
  2. Monomers cannot be allosteric
  3. Allostery may occur in a single chain
  4. Every tetramer binds four ligands cooperatively
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Allostery is energetic coupling between sites, not a compulsory feature of oligomerisation.

15. What does a smaller dissociation constant mean for a simple binding equilibrium under identical conditions?

  1. Weaker affinity
  2. Stronger affinity
  3. Larger chain mass
  4. More disulfides
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Less free concentration is needed to reach a comparable bound population.

16. Which statement about an asymmetric unit is correct?

  1. It is always the native oligomer
  2. It is necessarily a monomer
  3. It is crystallographically unique content, not necessarily the biological assembly
  4. It contains only ligand
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Biological assemblies can be generated using symmetry or may occupy a subset of an asymmetric unit.

17. A leucine zipper commonly uses which periodic arrangement?

  1. Heptad repeat
  2. Codon triplet repeat only
  3. Ten-residue nucleotide repeat
  4. Alternating disulfides
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Coiled-coil packing often follows the seven-position pattern with hydrophobic a and d positions.

18. Which is true of structurally similar folds?

  1. Always same function
  2. Always homologous
  3. May arise by divergence or convergence
  4. Always identical sequence
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. A similar structural solution alone is not sufficient proof of ancestry or function.

16. CSIR-NET Part C — analytical and data interpretation practice

These 20 original problems use experimental interpretation, stoichiometry and quantitative equilibria. Show your assumptions and units before selecting an answer.

1. A chain is 24 kDa by reducing SDS-PAGE and 48 kDa by nonreducing SDS-PAGE. Native SEC-MALS gives 96 kDa. Which model best fits?

  1. Four chains arranged as two disulfide-linked pairs
  2. One 96-kDa polypeptide
  3. Two 24-kDa chains only
  4. Four chains joined by peptide bonds
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Reduction gives 24-kDa chains; nonreducing detergent retains 48-kDa covalent pairs; native mass supports four chains. This assumes clean samples and accurate mass.

2. For 2M ⇌ D, [M] = 4 µM and [D] = 8 µM. Find Kd and fraction of chains in dimers.

  1. 2 µM and 80%
  2. 2 µM and 66.7%
  3. 0.5 µM and 80%
  4. 8 µM and 40%
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Kd = 16/8 = 2 µM. Total chain equivalents = 4 + 16 = 20 µM; fraction = 16/20.

3. Wild type Kd = 10 nM; mutant Kd = 1 µM. At 298 K, what is mutant minus wild-type standard binding free energy?

  1. −11.4 kJ/mol
  2. +11.4 kJ/mol
  3. +5.7 kJ/mol
  4. Zero
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Kd ratio = 100; RT ln100 ≈ 2.478 × 4.605 = 11.4 kJ/mol. Binding is weakened.

4. A 40-kDa chain elutes from globular-calibrated SEC at an apparent mass of 80 kDa, but SEC-MALS gives 41 kDa. Best interpretation?

  1. Stable dimer proven
  2. Likely monomer with unusual hydrodynamic size
  3. Covalent tetramer
  4. SEC-MALS establishes exact tertiary fold
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Absolute mass is close to one chain; elongated shape or related effects can explain the SEC discrepancy.

5. A 1:1 receptor binds free ligand with Kd = 2 µM. At free ligand 6 µM, occupancy is:

  1. 25%
  2. 50%
  3. 75%
  4. 100%
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. θ = 6/(2+6) = 0.75. The specified concentration is free ligand.

6. Equal total A and B concentrations are 2 µM each, and Kd = 2 µM. Find [AB].

  1. 1 µM
  2. Approximately 0.764 µM
  3. 2 µM
  4. 0.25 µM
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. x² − 6x + 4 = 0 gives x = (6−√20)/2 ≈ 0.764 µM. Free B is depleted.

7. A 2M ⇌ D system has Kd = 4 µM, and total chain concentration = 12 µM. Find [D].

  1. 2 µM
  2. 4 µM
  3. 6 µM
  4. 8 µM
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. [M]=4 µM satisfies 4²/4=4 µM dimer; mass balance 4+2×4=12.

8. SASA(A)=6000 Å², SASA(B)=5000 Å², SASA(AB)=9200 Å². What are total buried SASA and the half-total convention?

  1. 900 and 1800 Å²
  2. 1800 and 900 Å²
  3. 11000 and 5500 Å²
  4. 9200 and 4600 Å²
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 6000+5000−9200 = 1800; half-total is 900 Å².

9. Two domains have similar secondary composition and spatial arrangement but different strand connections. In CATH they may share:

  1. Architecture but not topology
  2. Homologous superfamily necessarily
  3. Topology but not architecture
  4. Sequence identity necessarily
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Connectivity differentiates the topology level from the architecture level.

10. A 30-kDa chain forms a native 180-kDa complex of only that chain type. If the complex has cyclic symmetry, what is the principal rotational angle?

  1. 30°
  2. 60°
  3. 90°
  4. 180°
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 180/30 = six subunits, and a C₆ assembly has rotations in increments of 60°. Mass alone would not establish C₆ symmetry.

11. A mutation changes buried Leu to Asp. Both activity and soluble recovery decrease. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Leu was certainly a catalytic acid
  2. Asp necessarily strengthens packing
  3. Core destabilisation is plausible; measure folding and binding separately
  4. Quaternary structure certainly remains unchanged
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The mutation introduces polar/charged chemistry in a hydrophobic environment; multiple indirect mechanisms can explain the data.

12. A crystal asymmetric unit has one 50-kDa chain. A crystallographic twofold partner makes a plausible interface. AUC supports a 100-kDa species. Which inference is strongest?

  1. A biological dimer is supported
  2. The protein must be monomeric
  3. A tetramer is proven
  4. The chains must be covalent
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Independent native mass and symmetry-generated geometry support a dimer; covalency requires separate evidence.

13. A native complex contains only chain types A (18 kDa) and B (27 kDa). Both are present, total mass 90 kDa. Which integer stoichiometry fits?

  1. A₂B₂
  2. A₃B₁
  3. A₁B₃
  4. A₄B₁
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. 2×18+2×27=90. Solving 2a+3b=10 yields a=b=2 for positive integers.

14. For a one-step interaction, kon = 2×10⁵ M⁻¹s⁻¹ and koff = 0.02 s⁻¹. What is Kd?

  1. 10 nM
  2. 100 nM
  3. 1 mM
  4. 4 µM
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 0.02/(2×10⁵) = 1×10⁻⁷ M = 100 nM.

15. FRET efficiency is 0.5 for a calibrated label pair with R₀ = 5 nm. Under the simple model, distance is:

  1. 2.5 nm
  2. 5 nm
  3. 10 nm
  4. Impossible even with model assumptions
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. E=1/[1+(r/R₀)⁶]; E=0.5 means r=R₀.

16. A classical icosahedral capsid uses h=1, k=2. What is its ideal T and subunit count?

  1. T=3, 180
  2. T=5, 300
  3. T=7, 420
  4. T=6, 360
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. T=1²+1×2+2²=7; 60T=420 in the ideal Caspar–Klug description.

17. An interface mutant has Kd unchanged but k_off decreases tenfold. Under a single-step model, what happened to k_on?

  1. Increased tenfold
  2. Decreased tenfold
  3. Unchanged
  4. Increased hundredfold
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Because Kd=koff/kon, equal proportional decreases preserve Kd. Residence time increases, despite unchanged equilibrium affinity.

18. An oligomer has two sites, each with unchanged microscopic affinity after mutation, but the bivalent ligand dissociates much faster. Which is most plausible?

  1. Loss of favourable spacing and avidity
  2. Peptide bonds have necessarily broken
  3. Primary sequence is identical
  4. Microscopic affinity must have increased
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Changing geometry can prevent simultaneous binding or rebinding without altering a single site's intrinsic affinity.

19. A sample is 30-kDa monomer and 60-kDa dimer; at equilibrium, [M]=[D]. Which statement is true?

  1. Half the chains are dimeric
  2. Two-thirds of chains are dimeric
  3. One-third of particles are dimers
  4. All chains are monomeric
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Equal molecule concentrations yield dimer particle fraction 1/2, but chain fraction 2/(1+2)=2/3.

20. A 4-subunit protein shows a Hill coefficient of 1 in ligand-binding measurements. What can be concluded?

  1. It must actually be a monomer
  2. Four binding sites are proven
  3. No net cooperativity is indicated by the Hill slope under those conditions, but stoichiometry requires independent evidence
  4. All subunits are covalently linked
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. A Hill slope near one is compatible with independent sites and does not reveal the number of chains or binding sites.

17. Consolidation, misconceptions and references

Rebuild the learning sequence in your own words: secondary elements combine into motifs; domains provide structural and functional modules; the fold describes how the core is organised; tertiary architecture creates specific surfaces and pockets; quaternary organisation emerges when chains assemble through complementary interfaces. Association is controlled by free energy and concentration. Symmetry and stoichiometry describe the resulting geometry, whereas experimental validation determines whether the proposed state is biologically relevant.
