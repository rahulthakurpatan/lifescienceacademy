1. Why a cell must manufacture and recycle nucleotides

Imagine a cell preparing for division. It already contains DNA, but the nucleotides locked in its chromosomes cannot simply be taken out to build the second copy: doing so would destroy the information that needs to be inherited. The cell must manufacture a new supply of building blocks while retaining the old genome. Even a non-dividing cell has this problem on a smaller scale because RNA is continually synthesised and degraded, DNA damage must be repaired, and many nucleotide-containing molecules participate in ordinary metabolism. Nucleotide metabolism is therefore the management of a reusable but continuously changing molecular inventory.

Recall from the nucleotide chemistry chapter that a nitrogenous base alone is not a nucleotide. Attaching a pentose to a base creates a nucleoside; phosphorylation produces a nucleotide. Adenine, adenosine and AMP are consequently three chemically different substances. The base determines much of the information-carrying identity, the sugar provides the scaffold, and phosphate groups enable reactions such as polymerisation and phosphoryl transfer. Purines (adenine and guanine) have two fused rings, whereas pyrimidines (cytosine, uracil and thymine) have one six-membered ring. Their different ring architectures are the reason their biosynthetic strategies are not identical.

The ribonucleotide triphosphates ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP are substrates for RNA synthesis. Their deoxy counterparts are dATP, dGTP, dCTP and dTTP, the normal DNA substrates. The distinction is not just structural: the 2′-hydroxyl of ribose must be removed by a specialised reduction reaction to provide deoxyribose in nucleotide form. Thymine also contains a methyl group that uracil lacks. These two modifications explain why the DNA precursor pathway cannot be described merely as phosphorylation of RNA precursors.

ATP is used in energy coupling, GTP in translation and signalling, UTP in activation of sugars for glycogen and glycoconjugate synthesis, and CTP in phospholipid synthesis. NAD, FAD and coenzyme A contain nucleotide-derived structural elements. Thus a change in nucleotide supply can affect many reactions even before DNA replication slows. Some ATP hydrolysis simply converts ATP into ADP, preserving the purine ring; oxidative phosphorylation can recharge ADP without building a new adenine molecule. This is a critical distinction between energy metabolism, which changes phosphorylation state, and de novo synthesis, which creates new ring-containing material.

The cell has two complementary ways to fill its pools. De novo synthesis constructs nucleotides using simpler precursors, whereas salvage recovers pre-existing bases or nucleosides. Degradation removes material when it is no longer useful. The three routes are linked by mass balance: if the rate of synthesis plus salvage equals utilisation plus degradation, the amount in the pool can remain constant even while individual molecules turn over rapidly. The goal is not maximal production of every nucleotide, but sufficient, proportionate production of each. An excess of one DNA precursor can be as problematic as a shortage of another because polymerases compete for chemically similar substrates.

By the end of this chapter, the learner should be able to reconstruct both de novo pathways, predict the fate of labelled atoms, compare the energy requirements of synthesis and salvage, explain how pathway blocks alter metabolites, and use feedback logic to interpret changes in nucleotide pools. The preceding amino acid chapter supplies the nitrogen donors, and the next vitamin chapter will develop the coenzyme chemistry that supports this network.

2. PRPP: the common activated sugar and the cost of activation

Both classes of nucleotide require a ribose-phosphate scaffold. Glucose metabolism provides ribose-5-phosphate through the oxidative or nonoxidative pentose phosphate pathway, depending on whether reducing power and ribose are needed in the same proportions. Ribose-5-phosphate is not itself an efficient donor of a ribosyl group to a base or nitrogen nucleophile. Its anomeric carbon has a hydroxyl group, a poor leaving group under physiological conditions. The cell solves this chemical problem by transferring a diphosphoryl group to carbon 1.

PRPP synthetase, also called ribose-phosphate pyrophosphokinase, catalyses ribose-5-phosphate + ATP → 5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate (PRPP) + AMP. Magnesium is required. ATP is split to AMP rather than ADP because both terminal phosphate groups are transferred together. The original 5-phosphate on ribose remains in place. PRPP consequently carries a leaving group at carbon 1 and a phosphate at carbon 5, so it is chemically prepared for the formation of an N-glycosidic bond and does not require a separate initial 5-phosphorylation.

When PRPP participates in phosphoribosyl transfer, pyrophosphate (PPi) leaves. In many cellular settings inorganic pyrophosphatase hydrolyses PPi to two inorganic phosphates. Removal of a reaction product lowers its activity and favours forward transfer. This is an illustration of thermodynamic coupling rather than a claim that a particular bond contains energy independently of its surroundings. ATP → AMP plus regeneration of ATP from AMP requires two phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. The PRPP-producing reaction therefore costs two ATP equivalents even though it consumes one ATP molecule.

PRPP is shared by purine de novo synthesis, purine base salvage and orotate conversion in pyrimidine synthesis. It also serves other biosynthetic routes in certain organisms, including histidine biosynthesis in microbes. It is not the committed purine intermediate. Commitment refers to a reaction whose product is directed mainly toward a particular pathway; a shared substrate cannot identify which downstream route will consume it.

In mammalian cells PRPP synthetase responds to the cellular phosphate and nucleotide environment: inorganic phosphate supports activity, while ADP and GDP can inhibit the major enzyme isoforms. Increased PRPP availability promotes purine commitment and stimulates mammalian CPS II, as described later. A change in PRPP therefore connects carbohydrate availability, salvage capacity and both synthetic pathways. However, elevated PRPP does not guarantee elevated final product if a downstream enzyme or an amino acid donor is limiting.

Consider 3 μmol of ribose-5-phosphate fully converted into PRPP. The synthetase consumes 3 μmol ATP and produces 3 μmol AMP. Rebuilding that ATP pool from AMP requires 6 μmol of high-energy phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. If those PRPP molecules are used to salvage 3 μmol hypoxanthine, there is no additional direct ATP hydrolysis in the transferase reaction itself, although the pre-existing PRPP was not free to manufacture. This distinction is essential whenever an examination question asks for energy cost from ribose-5-phosphate rather than from PRPP.

3. Where every atom of the purine ring comes from

Before memorising ten enzymatic reactions, identify the materials that the pathway must assemble. A purine ring contains nine atoms: five carbon atoms and four nitrogen atoms. Numbering follows the conventional fused-ring system, and numbering must not be confused with the positions of the pentose, which carry primes. The N9 atom of the purine binds carbon 1′ of ribose. Unlike pyrimidine synthesis, the first nitrogen is attached to ribose before the rest of the ring exists. The remaining atoms are added in an orderly sequence to the growing scaffold.

Glutamine contributes its side-chain amide nitrogen to N9 and N3. Its α-amino nitrogen is not the nitrogen directly transferred by these two amidotransferases. Glycine supplies a consecutive three-atom segment, C4, C5 and N7, so the whole glycine backbone becomes part of the fused ring. Aspartate supplies N1, but its carbon skeleton is later released as fumarate. Bicarbonate or carbon dioxide contributes C6. Two separate molecules of N¹⁰-formyltetrahydrofolate provide C8 and C2, each as a one-carbon formyl unit. The folate cofactor is recovered in its tetrahydrofolate form and can be reloaded with a one-carbon unit.

These assignments were established through classical precursor labelling studies of purine biosynthesis, including the work of John Buchanan and colleagues. The logic of an isotope tracer is simple: a labelled atom retains its nuclear identity through ordinary chemical reactions even when the surrounding bonds change. If labelled glycine is incorporated without scrambling, the position of its labelled atoms in the final purine reveals the contribution of glycine. A labelled atom can also travel through an intermediate metabolic pathway before entering the ring, however. Whole-cell incorporation alone does not establish a direct donation unless alternative routes are controlled or positional information is obtained.

A useful reasoning test is to consider purified enzymes supplied with glycine labelled at both carbon atoms and its nitrogen. Each newly assembled purine ring would carry three heavy atoms from that glycine molecule, provided the other donors are unlabelled and no exchange occurs. An intact nucleotide could carry additional isotope labels in the ribose if glucose or ribose were labelled; ring atom accounting and whole-nucleotide mass accounting are therefore different tasks.

The fused ring is not synthesised by attaching adenine or guanine to PRPP during the de novo route. That description belongs to salvage. In de novo synthesis the ring first appears in its complete form in inosine monophosphate, whose base is hypoxanthine. Adenine and guanine nucleotide identities are generated afterwards by chemically modifying that common branch-point nucleotide.

4. Building IMP: the ten reactions of de novo purine synthesis

The conventional mammalian pathway occurs mainly in the cytosol. The first committed enzyme, glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase (PPAT), hydrolyses glutamine to glutamate and channels ammonia to a phosphoribosyl-transfer active site. The nitrogen substitutes for the diphosphate group of PRPP, producing 5-phosphoribosylamine (PRA). This establishes N9 and commits the ribose scaffold toward purines. PPi is released; no additional ATP is directly hydrolysed in this step. PRA is unstable in aqueous solution, so prompt transfer into the next reaction is biologically useful.

The second enzyme, GAR synthetase, uses ATP to couple glycine to PRA. ATP-dependent activation of the glycine carboxyl group permits the formation of an amide linkage to the amino group of PRA. The resulting glycinamide ribonucleotide (GAR) now contains the future N9 plus the C4, C5 and N7 atoms contributed by glycine. GAR transformylase then transfers a formyl group from N¹⁰-formyl-THF to GAR, yielding formylglycinamide ribonucleotide (FGAR). This supplies C8, which will be located between N7 and N9 in the five-member ring.

At this point the eventual five-member ring still lacks the appropriate bonding arrangement for closure. FGAM synthetase uses ATP and a second glutamine to replace a carbonyl oxygen with nitrogen, forming formylglycinamidine ribonucleotide (FGAM). The introduced nitrogen will become N3 of the six-member ring, not another nitrogen of the five-member ring. AIR synthetase then uses ATP to drive cyclisation and dehydration of FGAM, forming aminoimidazole ribonucleotide (AIR). The five-member imidazole ring is now present, but the molecule is not yet a complete purine. The N3-containing substituent is positioned for assembly of the second ring.

The next task is to add C6. In the mammalian route AIR carboxylase introduces carbon dioxide to form carboxyaminoimidazole ribonucleotide (CAIR). The direct mammalian reaction does not require a separate ATP hydrolysis. In many bacteria, by contrast, an ATP-dependent N5-CAIR synthetase reaction produces a carboxylated intermediate which a mutase rearranges to CAIR. This alternative is why textbooks sometimes differ in the number of ATP-consuming steps or the number of intermediates. One must state which organism and which pathway boundaries are being counted.

SAICAR synthetase activates the carboxyl group of CAIR with ATP and joins it to the amino group of aspartate, forming succinylaminoimidazolecarboxamide ribonucleotide (SAICAR). Aspartate does not contribute four carbons to the purine ring. Adenylosuccinate lyase cleaves SAICAR to aminoimidazolecarboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) and fumarate. The remaining nitrogen, now positioned as a carboxamide group, will become N1. Fumarate can return to central carbon metabolism through malate and oxaloacetate; the cost and fate of those carbon atoms must not be confused with the atom balance of the purine ring.

The final pair of reactions uses another N¹⁰-formyl-THF molecule. AICAR transformylase forms FAICAR, donating C2. IMP cyclohydrolase closes the second ring by cyclodehydration to give inosine monophosphate (IMP). In mammals these two activities reside in the bifunctional ATIC protein. Other activities also occur in multifunctional proteins such as GART and PAICS. For examination reasoning the order of chemical reactions remains the same whether the activities belong to separate proteins or domains of one protein.

IMP is the first fully formed purine ribonucleotide of the pathway. Its phosphate is already on ribose carbon 5′ and its base is hypoxanthine. It is neither free hypoxanthine nor inosine (the corresponding nucleoside). From IMP the cell can choose adenine or guanine nucleotide production. The important conceptual advance is that all of the expensive ring-building steps have been shared. Regulation can therefore act first on total purine output and then separately on the distribution between the two branches.

Reading the pathway backwards can help diagnose a block. If the supply of formyl-THF falls, GAR and AICAR transformylation may become limiting. If adenylosuccinate lyase is defective, both the SAICAR-to-AICAR step and the IMP-to-AMP branch are affected because the same enzyme participates twice. If glutamine availability falls, the two glutamine-dependent nitrogen transfers are affected, and pyrimidine synthesis is also likely to be compromised. These predictions follow from specific reactions, not from memorising a list of deficiencies.

5. IMP at the crossroads: AMP, GMP and their triphosphates

The branches leading from IMP change the substituents of its ring while retaining the common purine framework. Adenylosuccinate synthetase uses GTP to activate IMP and joins aspartate at the C6 position. The resulting adenylosuccinate is then cleaved by adenylosuccinate lyase to give AMP and fumarate. The amino nitrogen of this second aspartate becomes the exocyclic amino group of adenine. This nitrogen must be distinguished from N1, which was donated by a different aspartate during common ring construction. Thus an AMP molecule made de novo requires the contribution of two aspartate nitrogens at different stages.

The guanine branch begins by oxidising IMP to xanthosine monophosphate (XMP) with IMP dehydrogenase. NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH during formation of a carbonyl at C2. GMP synthetase then uses glutamine as an amide nitrogen donor and ATP to activate XMP. The carbonyl oxygen at C2 is replaced by an amino group through an adenylated intermediate; ATP is converted to AMP and PPi. The new amino group is exocyclic, not one of the original ring nitrogens. This is why the number of glutamine molecules directly supplying nitrogen differs between AMP and GMP production: two are required for the common ring and another for the GMP branch.

The use of GTP for AMP synthesis and ATP for GMP synthesis is a meaningful example of reciprocal energy coupling. When one purine triphosphate is abundant, it is available to support production of the other. This arrangement supports balance but does not on its own guarantee an exact ratio, because branch enzymes have their own regulation and the ATP/GTP pools are used by many processes. Feedback from AMP and GMP to their respective branch enzymes works alongside the reciprocal energy requirement.

Once AMP and GMP exist, phosphate-transfer enzymes can generate diphosphates and triphosphates. Adenylate kinase catalyses ATP + AMP ⇌ 2 ADP. Guanylate kinase catalyses ATP + GMP ⇌ ADP + GDP. Nucleoside diphosphate kinase transfers terminal phosphate between nucleoside triphosphates and diphosphates, for example ATP + GDP ⇌ ADP + GTP. In the adenylate reaction the two ADP molecules are chemically indistinguishable; regeneration through ATP-producing metabolism maintains the desired net ATP supply. These are phosphoryl-transfer reactions, not ring synthesis.

Nucleotide nomenclature gives a quick way to check a proposed reaction. A nucleotide monophosphate cannot directly serve as a standard substrate for RNA polymerase merely because it contains the correct base. The polymerase uses a triphosphate, whose cleavage enables chain extension. Similarly GMP formation cannot replace GTP use in translation until kinase reactions supply GTP. All these transformations reuse the base and sugar, linking nucleotide pool size to the energy state of the cell.

6. Counting ATP correctly: synthesis is not the same as phosphorylation

Energy questions in nucleotide metabolism often appear contradictory because authors count different quantities. There are three separate matters: the number of ATP molecules consumed, the number of phosphoanhydride-equivalent transfers needed to regenerate them, and the metabolic cost of making precursors such as glycine or formyl-THF. A fair comparison states the start and end compounds and whether the organism uses direct AIR carboxylation. The following ledger begins with ribose-5-phosphate, assumes mammalian direct carboxylation, ignores costs of precursor production and counts ATP → AMP as two ATP equivalents, ATP or GTP → diphosphate as one.

PRPP formation consumes one ATP to AMP, which costs two equivalents. During IMP synthesis, GAR synthetase, FGAM synthetase, AIR synthetase and SAICAR synthetase each convert one ATP to ADP. Adding four to the two spent on PRPP gives six equivalents for IMP, from five ATP molecules. If the question starts with preformed PRPP, four equivalents are spent. An organism using an additional ATP-dependent AIR carboxylation route adds one to these totals. More inclusive biochemical accounting can include other costs and obtain higher values; those are not inconsistent if the accounting boundaries differ.

Conversion of IMP to AMP requires one GTP to GDP. Thus the direct cost from ribose-5-phosphate to AMP is seven equivalents, from five ATP molecules and one GTP. For GMP, GMP synthetase consumes one ATP to AMP, adding two equivalents; oxidation by IMP dehydrogenase generates NADH but does not directly require ATP. The direct GMP synthesis cost is eight equivalents. Do not subtract a fixed ATP yield for the NADH unless the problem specifies that reducing equivalents are oxidised under particular conditions; cytosolic NADH fate is context dependent.

To convert an NMP into its NTP, two additional phosphoryl-transfer equivalents are required in the net accounting. Consequently AMP to ATP or GMP to GTP adds two. This is the reason it is misleading to state that a cell can obtain net energy simply by manufacturing ATP through purine de novo synthesis. Making the molecular scaffold costs energy; energy metabolism subsequently cycles that scaffold between ATP and ADP.

Worked example: a cell forms 4 μmol AMP and 6 μmol GMP from ribose-5-phosphate using the mammalian route. The common IMP stage makes 10 μmol IMP at six equivalents each, giving 60 μmol equivalents. The AMP branch adds 4 μmol GTP equivalents; the GMP branch adds 12 μmol ATP equivalents. The total is 76 μmol of high-energy phosphoryl-transfer equivalents. If all ten nucleotide monophosphates are converted to triphosphates, 20 more equivalents are needed, bringing the total to 96 μmol. This calculation excludes precursor and reducing-power generation. It is dimensionally consistent: a count of equivalents per molecule multiplied by an amount of molecules gives an amount of equivalents.

Compare with salvage: synthesis of AMP from pre-existing adenine and PRPP requires no further direct ATP expenditure for ring construction. If PRPP is made from ribose-5-phosphate, the direct cost is two equivalents rather than seven. Saving five equivalents per AMP is substantial, but a cell cannot depend exclusively on salvage when the supply of recoverable purine rings is insufficient. This is why both strategies coexist rather than one replacing the other.

7. Feedback regulation and the problem of unequal purine demand

If all purine production proceeded continuously whenever ribose was available, the cell would unnecessarily consume nitrogen, folate and ATP. The committed reaction is therefore an important control point. Purine nucleotides inhibit glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase through feedback, while PRPP availability promotes flux. In standard textbook descriptions AMP, GMP and IMP contribute inhibitory signals; the precise sensitivity and cooperative effects differ among organisms and enzyme forms. The general principle is that an accumulation of downstream purines reduces new ring production.

The first branch step for AMP formation, adenylosuccinate synthetase, is inhibited by adenine nucleotide end-product signals; the GMP branch enzyme IMP dehydrogenase responds to guanine nucleotide feedback. Suppose GMP becomes abundant while AMP remains relatively scarce. Inhibition of the guanine branch reduces diversion of IMP toward XMP, leaving more IMP available for AMP formation. GTP, if abundant, can also support the AMP branch. However, overall purine feedback may reduce common synthesis, and the actual flux will depend on substrate availability and consumption. Branch feedback should not be interpreted as a simple mathematical switch.

The same logic explains why the concentration of an intermediate need not reflect flux through it. If IMP production slows from ten to five units per minute while consumption also falls from ten to five, the IMP pool can remain approximately unchanged. Conversely, a blocked downstream reaction may cause IMP accumulation even if the rate of its synthesis has fallen. An examination may give only metabolite concentrations; to infer the rate of pathway activity one needs a tracer, a time course, a measured reaction rate, or other evidence.

PRPP synthetase is an earlier control point shared across pathways. An increase in the amount of available PRPP can promote both purine synthesis and pyrimidine production, but committed-step feedback can oppose this effect. Salvage provides an additional regulatory layer. By consuming PRPP and generating feedback-active nucleotide products it simultaneously decreases a stimulus for de novo synthesis and increases its inhibitory signal. Losing salvage can therefore have a larger effect than merely losing one source of IMP or GMP.

A simple mass-balance example makes this clear. A cell initially produces 12 nmol IMP per minute by de novo synthesis and salvages 8 nmol per minute. If utilisation is 17 and degradation is 3 nmol per minute, the net change is zero. Suppose salvage suddenly stops before the other rates adjust: net change = 12 − 17 − 3 = −8 nmol per minute. Over time, increased PRPP availability and reduced product feedback may increase de novo flux. The initial decline does not tell us the eventual steady-state concentration. The cell's regulation responds to the imbalance; it does not invalidate mass balance.

8. Purine salvage: retaining an expensive ring

Cellular nucleic acid turnover releases oligonucleotides, nucleotides, nucleosides and bases through nucleases and phosphatases. A free base has already acquired the elaborate ring structure that de novo synthesis would otherwise have to construct. Salvage recovers this chemical investment by attaching a fresh activated ribose-phosphate group. Two particularly important mammalian enzymes are adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT) and hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT, also called HPRT).

APRT catalyses adenine + PRPP → AMP + PPi. HGPRT catalyses hypoxanthine + PRPP → IMP + PPi or guanine + PRPP → GMP + PPi. These reactions replace the leaving diphosphate at ribose C1 with the purine base, preserving the base ring. They are not phosphorylations of a nucleoside. A base has no sugar hydroxyl to phosphorylate; it needs a phosphoribosyl transfer reaction. In contrast, adenosine kinase uses ATP to phosphorylate adenosine to AMP, because adenosine already possesses ribose.

Purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) catalyses a reversible phosphorolysis reaction, for example inosine + Pi ⇌ hypoxanthine + ribose-1-phosphate. Guanosine can similarly form guanine. Depending on substrate concentrations, the reaction may support salvage or degradation. It should not be confused with a phosphatase that removes a phosphate from a nucleotide, or with a kinase that adds phosphate to a nucleoside. The names of the compounds immediately before and after a reaction often reveal what type of enzyme is required.

Tissues differ in their reliance on de novo and salvage reactions, partly because they express different enzyme levels and have different demands. Salvage is especially useful when ATP and precursor supply constrain new ring synthesis. A labelled adenine supplied to cells can rapidly appear as labelled AMP without labelling the common purine biosynthetic intermediates. This is evidence consistent with salvage, especially if APRT activity is required. Nevertheless, incorporation into AMP alone does not prove which enzyme was involved if alternate reactions and tracer exchange are possible.

HGPRT deficiency illustrates the consequences of lost salvage. Hypoxanthine and guanine that are no longer efficiently recycled become available for degradation. PRPP is consumed less by HGPRT, while feedback from salvaged IMP and GMP decreases. The increase in PRPP and diminished feedback can stimulate de novo synthesis, creating more purines that may ultimately be degraded. Severe inherited HPRT deficiency causes Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, characterised biochemically by urate overproduction and associated neurological and behavioural features. Those neurological manifestations should not be attributed solely to urate crystals; their mechanism is more complex than the purine mass balance.

APRT deficiency provides a different example: unrecovered adenine may be oxidised to poorly soluble 2,8-dihydroxyadenine, which can precipitate in the urinary tract. The important examination principle is that both deficiencies impair base recycling but produce different substrate and product patterns. One should distinguish loss of a salvage enzyme from loss of a downstream catabolic enzyme.

9. Purine degradation: why urate is the endpoint in humans

A purine nucleotide contains phosphate, sugar and base, so its degradation can proceed through removal of phosphate, modification of the base and cleavage of the glycosidic bond. AMP has two commonly taught routes toward hypoxanthine. In the first, 5′-nucleotidase converts AMP to adenosine; adenosine deaminase (ADA) then hydrolytically deaminates adenosine to inosine. PNP removes ribose by phosphorolysis to yield hypoxanthine. In the second route AMP deaminase directly converts AMP to IMP, which is dephosphorylated to inosine before PNP acts. Both routes converge at hypoxanthine.

GMP is dephosphorylated to guanosine, which PNP converts to guanine and ribose-1-phosphate. Guanine deaminase converts guanine into xanthine. Hypoxanthine is oxidised to xanthine by xanthine oxidoreductase, which further oxidises xanthine to uric acid. The two steps add oxygen-containing groups to the purine ring; the ring remains intact in the human final product. Xanthine oxidoreductase can function in dehydrogenase and oxidase forms, using different electron acceptors. The oxidase form can reduce molecular oxygen and generate reactive oxygen species. The core examination distinction is that deamination removes an amino nitrogen whereas these terminal oxidation reactions change the oxidation state of the ring.

At physiological pH, uric acid is largely present as urate. Humans and other great apes lack functional urate oxidase (uricase), which in many other mammals converts urate toward more soluble allantoin through intermediate reactions. Some organisms degrade purines further. Thus urate is not a universal endpoint of purine catabolism. In humans, renal and intestinal handling determines how rapidly urate leaves the body. An elevated urate concentration may arise from excessive production, inadequate excretion, or both; measuring urate alone does not identify the causal enzyme.

If urate exceeds its effective solubility under local conditions, monosodium urate crystals can form and trigger inflammation in joints, producing gout. This is a physicochemical consequence of the terminal metabolite, not evidence that nucleotides themselves crystallise in joints. Xanthine oxidoreductase inhibition by allopurinol (through its active metabolite oxypurinol) or febuxostat reduces urate production; hypoxanthine and xanthine can increase, and hypoxanthine can be salvaged when HGPRT is functional. A block at the terminal oxidation step predicts lower downstream formation and greater availability of upstream substrates, but pool concentrations also depend on excretion and salvage.

ADA deficiency presents a different mechanistic problem. Loss of ADA activity permits deoxyadenosine and adenosine accumulation; phosphorylation of deoxyadenosine can raise dATP, inhibiting ribonucleotide reductase and disturbing deoxynucleotide pools. Developing lymphocytes are particularly vulnerable, and severe ADA deficiency can cause severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID). PNP deficiency also disrupts lymphocyte function, with accumulation of deoxyguanosine-derived dGTP especially affecting T cells. These are examples of toxic imbalance caused by a catabolic block rather than simple energy failure.

In a short incubation of an enzyme preparation converting hypoxanthine to urate, suppose 100 nmol hypoxanthine disappears and 60 nmol xanthine accumulates, with no salvage or other loss. The remaining 40 nmol must have progressed to urate if initial xanthine and urate are zero and the reactions are the only ones operating. The ring count is conserved even though oxygen and nitrogen-containing groups change in other reactions. This numerical reasoning is useful for experiments with labelled purine carbon.

10. Pyrimidine ring construction: carbamoyl phosphate to orotate

Pyrimidines use the same general classes of precursors as purines but solve the assembly problem differently. The six-member ring is built first and then attached to PRPP. The two principal nitrogen sources are glutamine and aspartate; the latter supplies most of the ring framework. The pathway begins by generating an activated carbamoyl group, which can be transferred to an amino acid and subsequently cyclised.

Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II (CPS II) catalyses the cytosolic synthesis of carbamoyl phosphate in mammals. Bicarbonate is activated using ATP, glutamine is hydrolysed to glutamate, and the resulting ammonia reacts with the activated bicarbonate to form carbamate. A second ATP phosphorylates carbamate, yielding carbamoyl phosphate. Both ATP molecules become ADP. A simplified net reaction is glutamine + bicarbonate + 2 ATP + H₂O → glutamate + carbamoyl phosphate + 2 ADP + Pi, omitting proton bookkeeping.

This reaction resembles the mitochondrial carbamoyl phosphate synthesis used in the urea cycle, but the enzymes must not be interchanged. Mammalian CPS I is mitochondrial, uses free ammonia as the nitrogen source and requires N-acetylglutamate for activation. CPS II is cytosolic, uses glutamine as the immediate nitrogen donor and is regulated by pyrimidine demand and PRPP. Both consume two ATP per carbamoyl phosphate. Compartmentation prevents the normal nitrogen-disposal route from competing directly for the same local carbamoyl phosphate pool. A mitochondrial urea cycle defect can nevertheless lead to excess carbamoyl phosphate entering the pyrimidine pathway, as discussed later.

Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) transfers the carbamoyl group from carbamoyl phosphate to the α-amino group of aspartate, releasing inorganic phosphate and making carbamoyl aspartate. The transfer links the bicarbonate-derived carbon and glutamine-derived nitrogen to the aspartate-derived framework. Dihydroorotase then catalyses ring closure by dehydration to form dihydroorotate. These two reactions do not directly require additional ATP; the activation energy investment was already made in carbamoyl phosphate formation.

Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) oxidises dihydroorotate to orotate, establishing the unsaturated ring. In mammalian cells this enzyme is associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane, and electrons are transferred through its flavin cofactor to ubiquinone. The ubiquinone pool must be reoxidised by the respiratory chain to sustain this reaction. Some bacterial DHODH enzymes have different electron acceptors and locations, so the mammalian mitochondrial requirement should not be generalised to all organisms.

The first three mammalian activities, CPS II, ATCase and dihydroorotase, are domains of a single multifunctional protein called CAD. This molecular organisation allows the early reactions to be coordinated, but three different chemical transformations still occur. In bacteria these activities are often separate proteins; in the classic Escherichia coli regulatory example ATCase is the major feedback-controlled committed step because carbamoyl phosphate also serves arginine synthesis.

At the orotate stage the ring has been made but the molecule is not yet a nucleotide. One of the four aspartate carbons remains as an external carboxyl group attached to the ring. It will be eliminated later, meaning a carbon-balance calculation performed at orotate gives a different answer from one performed at UMP.

11. From orotate to UMP, UTP and CTP

Orotate phosphoribosyltransferase catalyses orotate + PRPP → orotidine 5′-monophosphate (OMP) + PPi. The N1 of the pyrimidine ring forms a glycosidic bond with ribose carbon 1′. The enzyme is a phosphoribosyltransferase, not a kinase, because it transfers the activated ribose-phosphate moiety of PRPP to the ring. OMP decarboxylase then removes the external carboxyl group as CO₂ and forms uridine monophosphate (UMP). Mammalian UMP synthase contains both activities. The decarboxylation is an unusually efficient enzyme-catalysed transformation despite requiring neither ATP nor an additional organic cofactor in the direct reaction.

The pyrimidine ring has six positions: N1, C2, N3, C4, C5 and C6. N1, C4, C5 and C6 come from aspartate; C2 comes from bicarbonate; N3 comes from glutamine amide nitrogen. The fourth aspartate carbon, originally the α-carboxyl carbon, is the carboxyl group lost during OMP decarboxylation. Uniformly carbon-labelled aspartate therefore leaves three labelled carbons in the final ring, whereas specifically α-carboxyl-labelled aspartate releases that label as CO₂ in a direct, unscrambled route. Pyrimidine atom numbering should not be inferred from the numbering of the amino acid itself.

UMP kinase transfers a phosphate from ATP to UMP to form UDP. Nucleoside diphosphate kinase then transfers another phosphoryl group to form UTP. CTP synthetase acts on UTP rather than free uracil or UMP in the standard pathway. It uses ATP to activate the oxygen-containing C4 position and glutamine as the usual amide nitrogen donor, replacing the oxygen with an amino group to produce CTP. The exocyclic amino nitrogen in cytosine is thus donated by a second glutamine molecule, while the two ring nitrogens remain unchanged. CTP synthetase can use ammonia under appropriate experimental conditions; in intact mammalian metabolism glutamine is the standard donor.

From ribose-5-phosphate to UMP the direct cost is four equivalents: two for PRPP and two for CPS II. No extra ATP is directly hydrolysed in ring closure, DHODH oxidation, phosphoribosyl transfer or OMP decarboxylation. UMP to UTP requires two more, and UTP to CTP requires one more, giving seven equivalents from ribose-5-phosphate to CTP, excluding feeder pathway costs. If the question asks for CMP rather than CTP, the pathway still passes through CTP before CMP is obtained by phosphate removal; it is not equivalent to direct conversion of UMP to CMP.

Pyrimidine nucleotide phosphorylation supports processes beyond nucleic acid synthesis. UTP can be used to form UDP-glucose, an activated sugar for glycogen formation and other glycosyl transfer reactions. CTP can activate lipid intermediates such as phosphocholine or diacylglycerol-related pathways through CDP-linked compounds. A loss of pyrimidine synthesis can thus limit membrane construction and carbohydrate metabolism as well as RNA production. The nucleotide product is a junction between biosynthesis of informational polymers and biosynthesis of other cellular structures.

12. Regulating pyrimidines and connecting the pathway to respiration

Mammalian CPS II activity is inhibited by UTP and stimulated by PRPP. The logic matches the common purine pathway: if the final nucleotide pool is abundant, the first major pathway step is restrained before more resources are consumed. If the activated ribose substrate is abundant, the cell can increase pyrimidine production in coordination with purine production. CTP synthetase is also feedback inhibited by CTP; this limits conversion of the uracil nucleotide pool to cytosine nucleotides when cytosine demand is already met. GTP stimulates the glutaminase reaction of CTP synthetase in classical biochemical descriptions, helping couple purine and pyrimidine status.

The textbook E. coli ATCase example is slightly different. ATP acts as an allosteric activator and CTP as an inhibitor. ATP signals adequate energy and a relatively abundant purine pool, so pyrimidine production is favoured; CTP signals sufficient pyrimidine supply. The enzyme has separate catalytic and regulatory subunits, and binding at regulatory sites changes the conformational equilibrium between lower-activity and higher-activity states. Aspartate saturation can be sigmoidal, a manifestation of cooperativity rather than the hyperbola expected for a simple Michaelis–Menten enzyme. This is a direct connection to enzyme regulation studied in Subunit 1.D.

Suppose a bacterial ATCase preparation is tested at the same sub-saturating aspartate concentration with excess carbamoyl phosphate. Control activity is 30 units; addition of ATP gives 55 units, and addition of CTP gives 12 units. These illustrative values support activation and inhibition under the stated conditions. They do not show that ATP competes with aspartate for the active site. To establish how the curve changes, measure rates across a range of aspartate concentrations with each effector. Allostery is supported by an effect mediated through regulatory sites, not merely by observing a lower reaction rate.

In mammals the link to mitochondrial respiration occurs at DHODH. If ubiquinone cannot be efficiently reoxidised, the oxidation of dihydroorotate may become restricted. The cells can then experience an insufficiency of pyrimidines even if glycolysis maintains some ATP. Providing uridine under suitable cell-culture conditions can bypass de novo ring synthesis by salvage. If growth improves, the experiment supports a pyrimidine supply limitation, although it does not prove that this is the only function of respiration. ATP shortage, redox disturbance and other respiratory consequences still need to be considered.

An inhibition experiment is especially informative if pathway intermediates and products are both measured. An increased dihydroorotate/orotate ratio with reduced downstream UMP production is consistent with a DHODH block. A CPS II block should instead decrease the supply of early intermediates. In either case, accumulation is not guaranteed at all times because feedback and other removal processes change. Initial rates in a controlled assay are easier to interpret than metabolite levels after a long period of adaptation.

13. Pyrimidine salvage, ring degradation and biochemical disorders

Mammalian pyrimidine salvage commonly occurs at the nucleoside level. Uridine-cytidine kinases use ATP to phosphorylate uridine or cytidine to UMP or CMP, respectively. Thymidine kinases phosphorylate thymidine to dTMP; deoxycytidine kinase supports recovery of deoxycytidine and certain other deoxynucleosides. These substrates already have a sugar, so a kinase can add phosphate at carbon 5′ without constructing the ring or using PRPP directly. Some organisms also possess pyrimidine base phosphoribosyltransferases, such as uracil phosphoribosyltransferase, but this is not the dominant textbook mammalian route.

Pyrimidine nucleotides are degraded by dephosphorylation and cleavage or modification of nucleosides. Cytidine can be deaminated to uridine, and cytosine-derived material generally converges on uracil degradation. Thymidine yields thymine. Uridine phosphorylase and thymidine phosphorylase catalyse phosphorolytic cleavage of their corresponding nucleosides. The subsequent pyrimidine ring pathway differs fundamentally from purine oxidation because the ring is opened and fragmented to small soluble metabolites.

Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) uses NADPH to reduce the double bond of uracil or thymine, producing dihydrouracil or dihydrothymine. Dihydropyrimidinase hydrolytically opens the reduced ring, forming N-carbamoyl-β-alanine or N-carbamoyl-β-aminoisobutyrate. β-Ureidopropionase then releases carbon dioxide and ammonia, forming β-alanine from uracil or β-aminoisobutyrate from thymine. These compounds may be metabolised further or excreted. Unlike urate, the characteristic pyrimidine degradation products do not retain an intact heterocyclic ring.

The carbon accounting reflects the original structures. Uracil has four ring carbons: three remain in β-alanine and one leaves as CO₂. Thymine has an additional methyl carbon: four remain in β-aminoisobutyrate and one leaves as CO₂. In both cases the two ring nitrogens are accounted for as one amino nitrogen retained in the β-amino acid and one nitrogen released as ammonia in the final hydrolysis. A tracer question should therefore specify whether the starting pyrimidine is uracil or thymine and whether a labelled atom resides in the retained fragment.

DPD deficiency impairs the first major reductive step and can lead to elevated uracil and thymine. It also alters breakdown of the pyrimidine analogue 5-fluorouracil, explaining why deficiency is associated with severe toxicity from that compound. This is a mechanistic application of enzyme specificity, not a recommendation about drug treatment. It illustrates how an enzyme evolved for a normal metabolite may also act on a structurally related analogue.

Hereditary orotic aciduria due to UMP synthase deficiency illustrates a synthesis defect instead of a degradation defect. Orotate accumulates because its conversion to UMP is impaired; insufficient pyrimidine supply can produce megaloblastic anaemia. Uridine salvage bypasses the defective conversion and can restore UMP production. The resulting nucleotide feedback also suppresses upstream pyrimidine synthesis. An upstream urea cycle defect, ornithine transcarbamoylase (OTC) deficiency, can also increase orotate: excess mitochondrial carbamoyl phosphate becomes available for cytosolic pyrimidine synthesis. Unlike primary UMP synthase deficiency, OTC deficiency is associated with hyperammonaemia. Both can produce elevated orotate but for different reasons, so the metabolite alone is not a unique diagnosis.

14. Ribonucleotide reductase: supplying the deoxy sugar

A cell can produce RNA precursors without producing DNA precursors. The central chemical difference is the oxygen atom at carbon 2′ of the pentose. Removing that oxygen from a ribonucleotide requires a reduction rather than a kinase reaction. Ribonucleotide reductase (RNR) supplies the deoxyribonucleotide pool by converting ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides. The mammalian class I enzyme acts on ribonucleoside diphosphates: ADP, GDP, CDP and UDP become dADP, dGDP, dCDP and dUDP. It does not normally reduce free ribose, nucleosides or NMPs. Some other classes and organisms use different nucleotide phosphorylation states and radical-generating systems, so the substrate rule must be associated with the class being studied.

The reaction presents a chemical challenge: the 2′-hydroxyl is part of a stable carbon–oxygen bond. RNR solves it through controlled radical chemistry. In mammalian class I RNR, an iron-associated radical system in the small subunit helps generate a transient cysteine thiyl radical in the catalytic large subunit. This radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from the ribose, creating a substrate radical that enables elimination of the 2′ oxygen as water. Catalytic cysteines donate reducing equivalents and form a disulfide bond. Subsequent hydrogen transfer restores the sugar framework with a hydrogen at the 2′ position instead of the hydroxyl. The radical is regenerated, allowing further catalytic cycles.

The disulfide formed during reduction must be returned to its reduced thiol state for the enzyme to continue working. Thioredoxin or glutaredoxin systems provide reducing equivalents; thioredoxin reductase and glutathione-dependent systems ultimately draw on NADPH. The overall conceptual balance is NDP + NADPH + H⁺ → dNDP + NADP⁺ + H₂O, with the intermediate electron carriers omitted. This is an idealised net accounting; actual protonation states depend on pH. The important result is one net two-electron reduction per sugar. The pentose phosphate pathway therefore contributes both ribose precursors for nucleotide biosynthesis and NADPH for deoxynucleotide supply.

RNR activity must be tightly regulated because the DNA polymerase must have access to the four precursors in suitable proportions. The enzyme has two conceptually distinct regulatory sites. An overall activity site responds to ATP as an activating signal and dATP as an inhibitory signal in mammalian RNR. The specificity site changes the enzyme's preference among its different ribonucleotide substrates according to the bound effector. ATP or dATP at the specificity site favours CDP and UDP reduction; dTTP favours GDP reduction; dGTP favours ADP reduction. The resulting dATP can inhibit overall activity at the separate activity site. The apparent contradiction that dATP can both favour certain substrates and inhibit overall turnover is resolved by recognising the different sites and conditions.

A rise in total dATP can therefore suppress RNR even when dGTP, dCTP or dTTP is scarce, as illustrated by ADA deficiency. In contrast, a specificity effector may alter the relative amounts produced without shutting off the entire enzyme. In rapidly dividing mammalian cells, regulation of enzyme subunit abundance also increases deoxynucleotide supply around S phase. DNA repair in non-dividing cells still requires deoxynucleotides, and specialised small-subunit forms help maintain a smaller pool. This avoids the incorrect claim that RNR is active only when cells divide.

Hydroxyurea inhibits class I RNR by disrupting its radical chemistry. An early prediction is a decline in de novo deoxyribonucleotide formation while ribonucleotide formation can continue. Exogenous deoxynucleosides may partially bypass the block if transport and salvage phosphorylation are adequate, but providing ordinary ribonucleosides alone cannot solve the chemical reduction problem. When interpreting rescue experiments, the identity of the supplied sugar is as important as the base identity.

15. Making dTMP: one-carbon metabolism and the folate cycle

RNR generates dUDP, but DNA normally contains thymine rather than uracil. The distinction is chemically important because spontaneous deamination of cytosine creates uracil in DNA. A repair system can recognise uracil as an abnormal DNA base when thymine is the normal partner of adenine. This does not mean DNA never contains uracil; misincorporation and damage still occur, and some viral genomes have unusual base compositions. For standard cellular DNA metabolism the goal is to maintain a sufficient dTTP pool while keeping dUTP comparatively low.

One route from dUDP involves phosphorylation to dUTP followed by dUTPase hydrolysis: dUTP + H₂O → dUMP + PPi. dUTPase has two linked functions: it lowers the amount of dUTP available for accidental incorporation into DNA and supplies dUMP for thymidylate synthesis. Another route produces dUMP through dCMP deamination, after dCDP has been converted to dCMP. The relative importance of these routes varies among species and cell types. Both provide deoxyuridine monophosphate, the actual substrate for the next reaction.

Thymidylate synthase catalyses dUMP + N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF → dTMP + dihydrofolate (DHF). The methylene group provides the new methyl carbon at C5 of uracil and the folate derivative provides reducing power to complete formation of the methyl group. An active-site cysteine temporarily forms a covalent bond with the substrate, enabling carbon transfer and reduction. The crucial distinction from purine formylation is that thymidylate synthase oxidises its folate donor to DHF, whereas the formyl-transfer steps in purine synthesis return THF without this same oxidation to DHF.

Dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) regenerates tetrahydrofolate using NADPH: DHF + NADPH + H⁺ → THF + NADP⁺. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase, a pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent enzyme, transfers a one-carbon unit from serine to THF to regenerate methylene-THF, producing glycine. Therefore folate acts as a recyclable carrier, serine supplies one carbon in this regeneration route, and NADPH provides reducing power needed to sustain the cycle. The purine pathway also uses folate derivatives, so interference with the folate pool can affect both purine synthesis and thymidylate production.

Combining the three idealised reactions eliminates the folate intermediates: dUMP + serine + NADPH + H⁺ → dTMP + glycine + NADP⁺ + H₂O, with water shown for the serine-to-glycine one-carbon transfer balance and proton conventions simplified. This follows by cancelling methylene-THF, DHF and THF on opposite sides. It shows that one carbon from serine becomes the thymine methyl group when this is the one-carbon supply route, and one NADPH is used to regenerate THF per dTMP produced. If a question begins with UDP instead of dUMP, RNR also requires one NADPH equivalent, making two reducing equivalents for the sugar and folate-cycle steps together, before accounting for other precursor-generating processes.

Thymidylate kinase converts dTMP to dTDP, and nucleoside diphosphate kinase supplies dTTP. Thymidine salvage can bypass thymidylate synthase by phosphorylation to dTMP. Inhibiting DHFR with methotrexate restricts regeneration of reduced folate, while the 5-fluorouracil metabolite FdUMP inhibits thymidylate synthase by forming a stable ternary complex with enzyme and methylene-THF. Both can reduce thymidylate supply, but their immediate targets and effects on other folate reactions differ. FdUMP is not the only active metabolite of 5-fluorouracil; RNA-directed effects can also occur. Rescue by thymidine can support a dTMP limitation but may not reverse every effect of a drug with multiple targets.

Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency can indirectly restrict usable folate forms through the methyl-folate trap, linking one-carbon metabolism to megaloblastic changes in proliferating cells. It is not because B₁₂ itself donates the methyl carbon to dUMP. This distinction provides a direct bridge to the following chapter on vitamins and coenzymes.

16. Integrating the pathways: precursor supply, labelling and metabolic diagnosis

Nucleotide synthesis is best understood as a network fed by central metabolism rather than a pair of isolated pathways. Ribose-5-phosphate comes from the pentose phosphate pathway. Glycine and serine connect purine and one-carbon metabolism to glycolytic intermediates. Aspartate is commonly produced by transamination of oxaloacetate, while glutamine transports reduced nitrogen and supplies amide groups. ATP from cellular energy metabolism supports activation, and NADPH supports reductive chemistry. Consequently a defect in an upstream pathway can reduce nucleotide synthesis even when every enzyme of the purine and pyrimidine pathways is present.

Consider a dividing cell in which glutamine amide nitrogen is specifically labelled with ¹⁵N. If there is no isotope dilution or redistribution, IMP acquires two labels, AMP retains two, GMP acquires a third, UMP acquires one, and CTP acquires a second. This prediction follows directly from the donor assignments, rather than from a memorised labelling pattern. For a mixture in which 50% of the glutamine amide nitrogen is labelled and each of the two IMP donations is independent, the probability of two labelled positions is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. The probability of exactly one label is 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.50 and of zero is 0.25. If donor enrichment differs between steps or labels redistribute through other amino acids, the simple binomial calculation no longer applies.

Compare a culture given uniformly labelled glucose. A nucleotide may contain labelled ribose through the PPP and labelled ring carbons through glycine, serine-derived formyl groups or aspartate. Therefore a mass increase of five units in a nucleotide is not sufficient by itself to prove that all five labels occupy the ribose. Fragmentation or positional data would be needed. Classical tracer reasoning remains useful even when modern measurements are available: establish which atom is labelled, which chemical transformation can transfer it, what other pathways might redistribute it and what experimental evidence distinguishes competing explanations.

A pathway block is most convincingly investigated by combining metabolite measurements, enzyme activity and bypass experiments. Suppose a cultured mammalian cell has low UTP, high orotate and near-normal dihydroorotate oxidation. Addition of uridine restores UTP and growth. This is compatible with a UMP synthase defect or inhibition because uridine kinase bypasses orotate conversion; it is less consistent with an isolated RNR block, which should directly restrict deoxy rather than ribonucleotide production. However the rescue alone cannot distinguish the two UMP synthase activities. Measuring OMP can help: impaired phosphoribosyl transfer limits OMP formation, whereas impaired decarboxylation permits OMP accumulation, subject to secondary metabolic effects.

In a second hypothetical experiment, a cell line with impaired HGPRT shows high PRPP, reduced incorporation of labelled hypoxanthine into IMP and increased labelled glycine incorporation into purine rings. The three observations together support reduced salvage and increased de novo activity, provided the enrichment and precursor pool sizes are controlled. Increased glycine labelling alone would not prove that flux rose because increased fractional enrichment could be caused by reduced dilution by unlabelled salvaged bases. A flux calculation requires information about pool size, enrichment and time.

Finally, an integrated accounting exercise: a double-stranded DNA segment of 1000 base pairs has 60% GC content. There are 2000 nucleotides in the duplex, of which 1200 are G or C. Complementarity gives 600 G and 600 C; the remaining 800 consist of 400 A and 400 T. If a cell synthesises the entire duplex from monomers, it needs 2000 dNTPs. If the question instead specifies copying an existing duplex semiconservatively, the two new strands together also contain 2000 nucleotides for one complete replication of this segment, not 4000. The RNR-equivalent reduction cost is one reducing equivalent per newly synthesised deoxy sugar if all are derived de novo from ribonucleotide precursors; additional folate-cycle regeneration for the 400 thymine nucleotides adds 400 NADPH equivalents. The simplified direct total is therefore 2400 NADPH equivalents under these assumptions. Salvage of deoxynucleosides, pre-existing pools and alternate thymidylate supply change the actual metabolic cost.

The unifying lesson is that balanced nucleotide supply depends on coordinated precursor generation, pathway-specific feedback, the redox state and salvage. It also explains why vitamin deficiency, defects of amino acid metabolism, inhibition of mitochondrial respiration and mutations in nucleotide enzymes can converge on impaired nucleic acid production while producing different biochemical signatures.

17. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason practice

Original examination-style questions. Attempt each before revealing the explanation.

1. Which product marks commitment of PRPP to purine biosynthesis? A. Ribose-5-phosphate; B. PRPP; C. PRA; D. IMP.

Explanation: C. PPAT makes PRA, the first committed intermediate. Ribose-5-phosphate and PRPP are shared; IMP is the later completed common purine nucleotide.

2. Which donor directly supplies purine C8 and C2? A. Aspartate; B. Glycine; C. N¹⁰-formyl-THF; D. Bicarbonate.

Explanation: C. Two separate formyl transfers provide these carbons. Glycine gives C4 and C5 plus N7; bicarbonate C6; aspartate N1.

3. The direct mammalian conversion of IMP to AMP uses which nucleotide as energy donor? A. ATP; B. GTP; C. CTP; D. UTP.

Explanation: B. Adenylosuccinate synthetase hydrolyses GTP. ATP-to-AMP cleavage instead occurs in the GMP synthetase branch; CTP and UTP are not direct donors here.

4. Which enzyme participates in both common IMP synthesis and AMP formation? A. PPAT; B. IMP dehydrogenase; C. Adenylosuccinate lyase; D. APRT.

Explanation: C. It removes fumarate from SAICAR and adenylosuccinate. The others catalyse commitment, the GMP branch and adenine salvage respectively.

5. Which reaction requires PRPP in mammalian pyrimidine de novo synthesis? A. CPS II; B. ATCase; C. DHODH; D. Orotate phosphoribosyltransferase.

Explanation: D. Orotate is attached to ribose-phosphate after its ring is formed. The first three enzymes operate on earlier ring precursors.

6. Which pairing is correct? A. CPS I–glutamine; B. CPS II–mitochondrial urea cycle; C. CPS I–N-acetylglutamate activation; D. CPS II–urate.

Explanation: C. Mammalian CPS I uses free ammonia in the mitochondrial urea cycle. CPS II uses glutamine in cytosolic pyrimidine synthesis. Urate is a purine degradation product.

7. What is the normal substrate of mammalian RNR? A. Adenine; B. AMP; C. ADP; D. DNA.

Explanation: C. Mammalian class I RNR reduces NDPs. Free base, monophosphate and polymerised DNA are not the normal substrates.

8. Which direct enzyme product is DHF? A. GAR transformylase; B. Thymidylate synthase; C. DHFR; D. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase.

Explanation: B. Thymidylate synthase oxidises methylene-THF to DHF. GAR transformylase releases THF, DHFR reduces DHF, and SHMT reloads THF.

9. Assertion: HGPRT loss can increase purine de novo synthesis. Reason: Loss of salvage can increase PRPP and diminish purine feedback. Choose: A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason does not explain; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true.

Explanation: A. Both substrate activation and loss of product inhibition explain increased common pathway flux; the effect is not merely the missing salvage product.

10. Assertion: Humans normally degrade purine rings completely to β-alanine. Reason: Pyrimidine rings can undergo reductive opening. Use the assertion–reason choices above.

Explanation: D. The assertion is false: human purine catabolism ends at urate. The reason is true but describes pyrimidine degradation.

11. Which enzyme directly prevents accumulation of dUTP and generates dUMP? A. dUTPase; B. DHFR; C. UMP synthase; D. Adenylate kinase.

Explanation: A. dUTPase hydrolyses dUTP to dUMP and PPi. The other enzymes regenerate folate, produce UMP and interconvert adenine phosphate states.

18. Part C: quantitative and analytical practice

Original examination-style questions. Attempt each before revealing the explanation.

1. An isolated mammalian IMP pathway starts with 8 μmol ribose-5-phosphate. Calculate ATP molecules consumed and ATP equivalents.

Explanation: 5 ATP molecules per IMP (one to AMP and four to ADP); 8 × 5 = 40 μmol ATP. Equivalents = 8 × (2 + 4) = 48 μmol. Starting with PRPP would remove 16 μmol equivalents.

2. Three μmol AMP and two μmol GMP are produced from ribose-5-phosphate. Compare direct energy expenditure to salvage of the same amounts from adenine and guanine.

Explanation: De novo = 3 × 7 + 2 × 8 = 37 μmol equivalents. Salvage with newly formed PRPP = 5 × 2 = 10 μmol. Difference = 27 μmol, excluding precursor generation and phosphorylation beyond NMP.

3. Glutamine amide nitrogen is 20% labelled; independent direct donations and no scrambling. Find IMP molecules with exactly one label.

Explanation: 2 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.32 or 32%. Zero labels = 64%, two labels = 4%; totals 100%. Do not count the third glutamine donor, which enters only the GMP branch.

4. Uniformly ¹³C-labelled aspartate is used with unlabelled carbamoyl phosphate and PRPP. How many labelled carbons remain in UMP?

Explanation: Three. Aspartate provides C4, C5, C6 and N1; its α-carboxyl carbon leaves from OMP as CO₂. Four labelled carbons would describe orotate before decarboxylation.

5. A mutant accumulates SAICAR and adenylosuccinate. Which single enzyme defect explains both, and why is a PPAT defect less plausible?

Explanation: Adenylosuccinate lyase acts on both substrates. PPAT lies before either compound and its isolated inhibition would reduce supply rather than directly block their consumption.

6. In a mammalian cell culture orotate rises while UMP falls. Uridine rescues growth. Which enzymes should be assayed, and what extra measurement separates the two activities?

Explanation: Assay the two UMP synthase activities and measure OMP. Low formation of OMP suggests transferase impairment; accumulation with impaired conversion suggests decarboxylase impairment. Rescue does not establish the exact molecular lesion.

7. A patient-derived cell model accumulates urate and PRPP, and labelled hypoxanthine incorporation into IMP is low. Predict the mechanism and the response of de novo purine synthesis.

Explanation: HGPRT impairment is consistent with the observations. Reduced base salvage increases substrate availability for degradation and PRPP availability, while reducing salvaged product feedback. De novo synthesis may rise; confirm with controlled tracer flux rather than urate alone.

8. Under fixed substrate concentrations an E. coli ATCase assay has initial rates 24, 48 and 6 units without effector, with ATP and with CTP, respectively. Quantify the effects and identify the type of inference.

Explanation: ATP gives 48/24 = 2-fold activation; CTP gives 6/24 = 0.25 of control, a 75% decrease. These data support differential effector action but do not alone prove the binding sites or kinetic model; binding and substrate saturation data are needed.

9. A respiratory inhibitor lowers pyrimidines while cells maintain ATP through glycolysis. Uridine partially rescues proliferation. Which reaction supplies a mechanistic explanation?

Explanation: Mammalian DHODH transfers electrons to ubiquinone; impaired respiratory reoxidation limits dihydroorotate oxidation. Uridine salvage bypasses the ring-building pathway. Partial rescue does not rule out other mitochondrial defects.

10. A nucleotide pool has 20 nmol initially. Input is 9 nmol/min de novo and 4 salvage; output is 10 utilisation and 2 degradation. What is the amount after 4 min if rates are fixed?

Explanation: Net = 9 + 4 − 10 − 2 = 1 nmol/min. Amount = 20 + 4 = 24 nmol. This assumes rates and volume stay fixed; feedback may change real rates.

11. An RNR preparation is supplied separately with specificity-site dTTP or dGTP at otherwise permissive activity. Predict which reduction is favoured.

Explanation: dTTP favours GDP reduction; dGTP favours ADP reduction. These are specificity effects, not the same as overall inhibition by dATP at the activity site.

12. A synthesis assay consumes 7 μmol dUMP, with THF fully recycled by DHFR and serine hydroxymethyltransferase. Calculate the direct serine and NADPH requirements and glycine production.

Explanation: 7 μmol each, by one-carbon transfer and one DHFR reduction per dTMP. If the starting substrate is UDP and every sugar must be reduced, another 7 μmol NADPH equivalents are needed for RNR.

13. Two enzyme inhibitors separately block DHFR and thymidylate synthase. Predict differences in folate intermediates and common effects on DNA precursors.

Explanation: DHFR block prevents DHF reduction and can deplete usable reduced folate for purines as well as dTMP. Thymidylate synthase block directly limits dUMP methylation, so dTMP/dTTP decline; DHF generation by that reaction also falls. Both can impair DNA synthesis; pool changes depend on other pathways.

14. A duplex of 2500 base pairs has 36% GC. How many dATP and dGTP are needed to make its two complementary strands?

Explanation: 5000 nucleotides; 1800 are G+C, giving 900 dGTP and 900 dCTP. Remaining 3200 give 1600 dATP and 1600 dTTP. Do not apply Chargaff equality to a single arbitrary strand.

15. In a purified catabolic assay 50 nmol uracil and 30 nmol thymine are degraded completely through the standard reductive pathway. Calculate β-amino acid and CO₂ yields.

Explanation: 50 nmol β-alanine, 30 nmol β-aminoisobutyrate and 80 nmol CO₂. Carbon balance: 50×4 + 30×5 = 350 nmol carbon atoms; products 50×3 + 30×4 + 80 = 350.

16. A compound inhibits RNR but not RNA synthesis enzymes. Ordinary uridine fails to rescue DNA replication, while a mixture of all four deoxynucleosides partly rescues it. Explain.

Explanation: Uridine supplies ribonucleotides, still requiring RNR for the deoxy sugar. Deoxynucleosides already have deoxyribose and can be phosphorylated through salvage. Partial rescue depends on transport, kinase activities and balanced precursor pools; it does not prove a single target independently.

19. Consolidation and transition to vitamin metabolism

Begin your revision with the shared precursor rather than the product list. Ribose-5-phosphate is activated to PRPP; the purine ring is assembled on that scaffold and the pyrimidine ring is assembled before attachment. Reconstruct IMP synthesis by identifying the donor of each new atom, then follow the two branches to AMP and GMP. Reconstruct UMP synthesis by separating CPS II activation, ring closure, oxidation, PRPP attachment and decarboxylation. If you can explain the purpose of each step and trace the fate of aspartate carbon, the intermediate names become meaningful rather than arbitrary.

The recurring regulatory principle is feedback at costly early reactions and at branch points. Salvage reduces the need for de novo synthesis, while degradation disposes of excess ring-containing material. Human purines end in urate; pyrimidines are opened into soluble fragments. DNA precursor generation imposes additional requirements for NADPH-dependent reduction and the folate-dependent conversion of dUMP to dTMP. Keep the names of nucleotide pools and the phosphorylation states precise, because many wrong examination answers interchange bases, nucleosides and nucleotides.

The next chapter will bring these relationships together through the vitamin-derived coenzymes: folate carries one-carbon groups; PLP facilitates amino-acid reactions; niacin-derived NAD and NADP transfer electrons; and riboflavin-derived flavins participate in redox chemistry. The nucleotide pathway thus provides a concrete example of why micronutrients are required for biosynthesis even when they are not themselves consumed as major fuel molecules.

Revisit nucleotide chemistry, the pentose phosphate pathway and enzyme regulation when revising the chemical foundations.
