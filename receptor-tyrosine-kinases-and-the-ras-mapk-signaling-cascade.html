1. Why growth factors need a membrane-to-nucleus relay

A multicellular organism must coordinate the behavior of cells that are separated from one another. A cell in a repairing epithelium, for example, needs to distinguish a local instruction to proliferate from the ordinary presence of nutrients. Glucose can provide carbon and energy for synthesis, but its availability does not by itself tell every cell to divide. A growth factor provides a different kind of information. By binding a receptor, it changes the probability that particular intracellular reactions will take place. The receiving cell interprets this information through its existing proteins and its physiological state. A growth factor is therefore an instruction rather than simply an additional metabolic substrate.

Many growth factors are polypeptides. Their hydrated, charged molecular surfaces are poorly suited to passive passage through the hydrophobic interior of a membrane. The cell does not need to transport the whole growth factor into the cytoplasm to respond to it. Instead, a membrane receptor has an external region that recognizes the ligand and an internal region whose activity changes when recognition occurs. This is an example of transduction: an extracellular event is converted into a different, intracellular molecular event. The sequence and amount of ligand need not be reproduced on the intracellular side. What matters is that the receptor changes the activity of the appropriate cellular machinery.

Two problems arise as soon as the signal crosses the membrane. First, the receptor must distinguish the correct molecular target from thousands of proteins in the cytoplasm. Second, the limited number of active receptors must influence enough target molecules to alter cell behavior. A receptor that only changed its own shape would have little effect unless other proteins could recognize the change. Receptor tyrosine kinases solve these problems through phosphorylation, selective binding and enzyme cascades. Their intracellular domains can catalyze reactions, and the products of those reactions create opportunities for other signaling proteins to assemble. This gives a physical basis for amplification and specificity.

Consider a cell expressing both a receptor for epidermal growth factor and a receptor for a different growth factor. Both receptors can stimulate ERK under suitable conditions, although they recognize different external ligands. Their outputs may overlap because they recruit common intracellular components. The cell can nevertheless respond differently if one receptor stimulates ERK for a short period while the other maintains its activity, or if the second receptor also strongly activates another branch. This is why the identity of the final kinase is not enough to predict the response. The pattern of its activation, the availability of substrates and the state of the cell all contribute.

A mitogen is a signal that promotes entry into a proliferative program under appropriate conditions. A survival factor reduces the probability of death through regulated mechanisms. These functions are not mutually exclusive: a particular growth factor can have both effects in a responsive cell. Nor does the name growth factor guarantee that the cell will increase in size, divide or survive. Receptor expression, ligand concentration and other environmental inputs matter. Throughout this chapter, the Ras–ERK branch is used to explain how extracellular information reaches intracellular enzymes and gene regulation, whereas the PI3K–Akt branch illustrates how a related receptor can support survival and anabolic growth. Their downstream physiological outcomes are not identical to the activity of the signaling enzymes themselves.

2. Architecture and catalytic chemistry of receptor tyrosine kinases

A receptor tyrosine kinase, abbreviated RTK, is a membrane receptor with intrinsic protein tyrosine kinase activity. The word intrinsic means that the receptor polypeptide contains the catalytic machinery rather than merely attaching to a separate protein kinase. Typical animal RTKs have an extracellular ligand-binding region, a single transmembrane alpha helix and a cytoplasmic region that contains a tyrosine kinase domain. The extracellular structures vary among receptor families, so the shared organization does not imply that all ligands bind in the same way. The intracellular arrangement also differs in the placement of regulatory segments and phosphorylation sites.

The transmembrane helix is more than a passive anchor. It constrains the relative positions of the extracellular and cytoplasmic domains. A change in the orientation or association of receptor molecules outside the cell can therefore change the interactions of their internal regions. One must not imagine the membrane as a rigid barrier that transmits a simple mechanical push through a fixed rod. Receptor molecules move laterally, rotate and sample conformations in a fluid lipid bilayer. Ligand binding can favor a subset of conformations or associations that allow productive kinase interactions. The resulting shift in molecular populations can be strong enough to alter phosphorylation without any ligand entering the cell.

A protein tyrosine kinase transfers the terminal phosphoryl group of ATP to the hydroxyl group on a tyrosine side chain. In simplified notation, protein–Tyr–OH + ATP yields protein–Tyr–O–phosphate + ADP. The phosphorylated residue has altered charge and interaction properties. Magnesium ions help coordinate nucleotide phosphates in the catalytic site, and the kinase positions the acceptor hydroxyl relative to the donor. The reaction is not the incorporation of an intact ATP molecule into a protein. ADP leaves after transfer, while the protein retains the phosphate group as a covalent modification.

Tyrosine differs from serine and threonine because its hydroxyl group is part of an aromatic phenolic side chain. A kinase's substrate selectivity depends on its active site and its interaction with the surrounding substrate sequence, not merely on whether an amino acid has an oxygen atom. A protein tyrosine kinase and a protein serine/threonine kinase can both use ATP, yet they generally recognize distinct types of acceptor. In the Ras–MAPK pathway this distinction is essential: the receptor phosphorylates tyrosine sites, Raf acts chiefly on serine residues in MEK, and MEK has the dual specificity required to phosphorylate both threonine and tyrosine in ERK. ERK then predominantly phosphorylates serine/threonine sites in its own substrates.

Phosphorylation does not have one universal biochemical meaning. A phosphate near a catalytic site can favor an active conformation. A phosphate on a flexible receptor tail can instead provide a binding site, with little direct effect on catalytic turnover. On a different protein, phosphorylation can favor inhibition or relocation. The functional interpretation depends on the residue, its location and the proteins that read it. This distinction is especially useful when analyzing mutant receptors: a mutation that destroys catalytic activity and a mutation that removes one docking site can produce very different patterns of downstream signals.

Removal of a phosphate is catalyzed by a phosphatase. This hydrolysis uses water and releases inorganic phosphate; it does not normally reconstruct ATP from ADP in the signaling reaction. The kinase and phosphatase therefore form an energy-consuming cycle when both are active. A steady level of phosphorylated receptor can hide substantial continuing turnover. The fraction of receptors bearing a phosphate is determined by the relative rates of addition and removal and by the receptor's accessibility to the relevant enzymes. A high phosphorylation measurement may result from stronger kinase action, weaker phosphatase action or both. The amount of total receptor need not have changed.

Intrinsic kinase activity distinguishes an RTK from several other types of cell-surface receptor. A conventional seven-transmembrane GPCR promotes nucleotide exchange on a heterotrimeric G protein rather than acting as a receptor tyrosine kinase. A standard cytokine receptor recruits an associated JAK family kinase, while a transforming growth factor beta receptor is principally a receptor serine/threonine kinase. These categories help predict the earliest intracellular reaction, but they do not establish that downstream pathways are completely separate. In some contexts, GPCR stimulation can indirectly activate an RTK or converge on ERK through other routes. A classification should describe the molecular identity of the receptor rather than assert an exclusive final output.

3. Ligand-dependent activation and trans-autophosphorylation

A receptor kinase must remain sufficiently quiet when the corresponding growth signal is absent. Basal activity need not be exactly zero, but an unregulated receptor would weaken the connection between extracellular information and cell behavior. Many RTKs are restrained by interactions that make their kinase domains less catalytically effective in the unstimulated state. Ligand binding changes the association or conformation of receptor molecules, reducing these restraints or favoring a productive kinase arrangement. This increases the ability of one receptor molecule to phosphorylate another within a receptor complex.

The term dimer refers to an association of two receptor subunits. In a simplified model, two receptors initially diffuse independently in the plasma membrane. Ligand binding makes an associated state more favorable, and two intracellular kinase domains become positioned so that phosphorylation can proceed efficiently. The ligand may itself provide two binding surfaces, as in a dimeric growth factor, or it may change receptor conformation to reveal an interface through which two ligand-bound receptors associate. The mechanistic distinction is important because the presence of two receptor molecules does not automatically establish that a ligand bridges them directly.

Trans-autophosphorylation describes transfer of phosphate from ATP by the kinase domain of one receptor chain to a tyrosine residue on the partner chain in the same receptor complex. The prefix trans indicates that the acceptor site is on another polypeptide. Autophosphorylation refers to modification of the receptor system by its own kinase activity, not to an obligatory reaction within a single chain. This arrangement creates a physical connection between receptor association and catalytic output. It also makes a kinase-dead receptor capable of being phosphorylated by a catalytically competent partner, if the association and substrate sites remain appropriate.

To understand that last result, distinguish the role of an enzyme from the role of a substrate. Suppose receptor A has an intact catalytic site and receptor B has a defective catalytic site but retains several tyrosines. When A and B form a productive complex, A can potentially phosphorylate B. The observation of phosphorylated B does not establish that B has recovered its kinase activity. B may still fail to phosphorylate A or other substrates. This simple logic explains why receptor immunoprecipitation followed by detection of phosphotyrosine must be interpreted in conjunction with measurements of catalytic function and the composition of receptor complexes.

Many RTKs gain catalytic activity through phosphorylation of tyrosines in an activation loop, a mobile segment within the kinase domain. In an inactive conformation, this loop can obstruct proper access of the substrate or prevent residues in the catalytic site from adopting a productive arrangement. Phosphorylation changes its interactions and may stabilize a more active conformation. Once activation has begun, additional receptor residues outside the kinase domain can become phosphorylated. Activation-loop sites and docking sites therefore perform distinguishable tasks, even though both can be detected by a general phosphotyrosine antibody.

The epidermal growth factor receptor is an important qualification to the usual textbook model. Ligand binding promotes a receptor association in which the kinase domains form a functionally asymmetric pair. One kinase domain acts as an allosteric activator of the other. The interaction resembles the way an activating protein can alter the conformation of an enzyme without supplying a covalent phosphate at the activating interface. Receptor-tail phosphorylation follows kinase activation and is essential for recruiting downstream components, but the initial activation of EGFR cannot be accurately described as an obligatory activation-loop phosphorylation event identical to that of all other RTKs.

The insulin receptor provides a second qualification. Its mature receptor is a disulfide-linked complex containing two extracellular alpha subunits and two beta subunits with transmembrane and intracellular kinase regions. Insulin does not need to assemble two completely separate mature receptor monomers into this complex, because the subunits are already associated. Instead ligand binding reorganizes the existing receptor so that the kinase domains can become activated and phosphorylate relevant sites. The general principle is ligand-dependent regulation of kinase interactions, not universal ligand-induced association of previously separate proteins.

A third distinction concerns signaling from a receptor after ligand removal. Ligand occupancy, kinase activity, receptor phosphorylation and downstream activity are different measurable variables. A receptor may remain phosphorylated for a short time after ligand has dissociated, while a kinase farther downstream may remain active even after receptor phosphorylation falls. Conversely, phosphatases may rapidly suppress phosphorylation despite substantial receptor occupancy. A student should therefore avoid drawing conclusions about the entire pathway from the measured state of one component. Different steps have different rates and can continue to influence one another after the original external stimulus changes.

4. Phosphotyrosine docking and adaptor-mediated recruitment

The phosphorylation of a receptor tail creates a molecular address that specific proteins can read. A common reader is the Src homology 2 domain, abbreviated SH2. This compact protein domain contains a binding surface for phosphotyrosine and additional contacts that discriminate among neighboring amino acids. The negative charge and geometry of the phosphate contribute to recognition, but the phosphate is not the sole determinant of affinity. Different SH2-containing proteins can consequently prefer different phosphotyrosine-containing sequences on the same receptor.

This sequence-dependent recognition explains why receptors with several phosphorylatable tyrosines can branch into distinct outputs. One phosphorylated site may favor recruitment of an adaptor connected to Ras; another may bind a regulatory subunit of PI3K or a signaling enzyme such as PLC gamma. Mutating one site to phenylalanine removes the hydroxyl group required for phosphorylation while conserving much of the aromatic character of the original side chain. Such a mutation can separate a specific recruitment function from the broader ability of the receptor to phosphorylate other residues. The result is informative only if receptor expression, surface delivery and kinase activity are also checked.

Not every SH2-containing protein is an enzyme. Growth factor receptor-bound protein 2, commonly written Grb2, is an adaptor with a central SH2 domain and two SH3 domains. It links proteins together. The SH2 domain can attach Grb2 to an appropriate phosphorylated receptor site or an intervening phosphorylated docking protein, while the SH3 domains recognize proline-rich sequences in associated proteins. Grb2 does not itself replace GDP with GTP on Ras, hydrolyze ATP to phosphorylate ERK or generate a lipid messenger. Its function is to organize a productive encounter between molecules that might otherwise be separated.

One major binding partner of Grb2 is the guanine nucleotide exchange factor SOS, named Son of sevenless. SOS possesses regions that interact with Grb2 and a catalytic region that acts on Ras. Ras is associated with the cytoplasmic face of membranes through lipid modifications and other targeting determinants. Recruitment of the Grb2–SOS complex to a phosphorylated receptor places SOS in a location where it can interact efficiently with membrane-associated Ras. This arrangement illustrates how a change in local concentration can regulate a reaction without changing the total cellular amount of either reactant.

Some RTKs recruit Grb2 directly, while others use an intermediate such as Shc. In an indirect route, the activated receptor recruits and phosphorylates Shc; a phosphorylated site on Shc then binds Grb2. The rest of the connection to SOS and Ras can be similar. A mutation of a direct Grb2 docking site on a particular receptor may have little effect if an indirect recruitment route is available. This is why the existence of a biochemical binding interaction does not establish that it is the only route used in intact cells. Mutations at several sites, or combined loss of alternative adaptors, may be required to demonstrate necessity.

Adaptor proteins can also organize multiple branches. A receptor-associated docking protein may contain several sites that are phosphorylated and recruit different SH2-containing partners. Rather than being a simple one-dimensional chain, the receptor neighborhood is an assembly of proteins whose occupancy depends on affinity, concentration, phosphorylation and competition. The same site generally cannot accommodate two competing proteins simultaneously, whereas two different sites can be occupied together if their geometry permits. A mutation in one docking motif may therefore redistribute the binding of proteins even when the total number of phosphorylated sites is only slightly reduced.

Protein association should not be confused with catalytic activity. In a co-immunoprecipitation experiment, an antibody against the receptor can recover associated Grb2 after ligand treatment. This establishes that the proteins occur in the same recoverable complex under the experimental conditions; it does not prove that they bind each other directly or that Grb2's interaction is necessary for signaling. A purified phosphopeptide-binding experiment can test direct binding, and a mutant-reconstitution experiment can test whether a particular binding event is functionally necessary. Each experiment answers a different question. Together, they connect recognition, localization and the subsequent activation of Ras.

5. Ras as a regulated guanine nucleotide switch

Ras belongs to a family of small monomeric GTPases. The term GTPase indicates that the protein can hydrolyze GTP to GDP and inorganic phosphate. The small GTPase is not a heterotrimeric G protein; it is a single polypeptide with its own nucleotide-binding and regulatory surfaces. Mammalian Ras proteins include H-Ras, N-Ras and K-Ras. Their shared switch mechanism makes them useful textbook models, although differences in membrane targeting and regulation can influence their biological roles.

Ras binds guanine nucleotides tightly. In its GDP-bound form it generally interacts poorly with the effectors responsible for the growth-factor response. When it binds GTP, the additional terminal phosphate makes contacts that stabilize different conformations of the switch I and switch II regions. These conformational changes make the effector-binding surface more compatible with proteins such as Raf. The conversion of Ras-GDP to Ras-GTP is therefore not a protein phosphorylation reaction. GTP is held noncovalently in the nucleotide-binding pocket, and the identity of the bound nucleotide changes the protein's conformation.

SOS increases the rate at which GDP leaves Ras. When the nucleotide-binding pocket is temporarily empty, either GDP or GTP can bind. Because the cytosolic guanine nucleotide pool usually favors GTP and because binding occurs in the relevant molecular environment, exchange promotes accumulation of Ras-GTP. SOS is called a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, or GEF, rather than a kinase. It does not synthesize GTP, transfer phosphate from ATP to GDP or convert bound GDP chemically into bound GTP. Its primary catalytic contribution is to facilitate nucleotide release and exchange.

Active Ras does not necessarily remain active. It possesses intrinsic GTP hydrolysis activity, returning itself to the GDP-bound state and releasing inorganic phosphate. The intrinsic reaction is relatively slow. GTPase-activating proteins, abbreviated GAPs, accelerate it. A GAP is thus a positive regulator of the GTPase chemical reaction and ordinarily a negative regulator of Ras signaling. The apparent contradiction arises only when enzyme activity and signaling activity are confused. Faster hydrolysis means a shorter lifetime for the effector-binding GTP state.

Membrane association places Ras in the vicinity of recruited SOS and relevant effectors. In mammalian Ras proteins, lipid modification of the carboxyl-terminal region and additional targeting features promote membrane interaction. Different isoforms use different combinations of targeting features. The lipid anchor does not itself switch Ras into the GTP state; instead it helps determine where exchange and effector recruitment can occur. A mutant Ras that is able to bind GTP but unable to reach the appropriate membrane may therefore transmit a weaker signal than a correctly localized Ras, even if both proteins have similar catalytic chemistry in solution.

A Ras mutant with impaired GTP hydrolysis may remain in the GTP-bound state longer than wild-type Ras. Such a mutant can signal when growth-factor stimulation is weak or absent, provided it can load GTP and interact with appropriate effectors. It is often called constitutively active, but this expression describes a tendency to maintain signaling in a particular context rather than implying that every molecule is GTP-bound at all times. A GDP-preferring or nucleotide-exchange-defective mutant has a different effect. Some dominant-negative Ras constructs can interfere with signaling by trapping exchange factors, reducing activation of endogenous Ras. The distinction between loss of one protein's activity and interference with a shared regulator is central to genetic interpretation.
