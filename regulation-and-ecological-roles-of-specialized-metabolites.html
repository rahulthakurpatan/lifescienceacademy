1. From pathways to ecological function

A leaf may contain hundreds of specialized metabolites, yet their abundance changes as the leaf ages, encounters an insect or receives sunlight. Earlier chapters traced the biosynthetic origins of terpenoids, phenolics, alkaloids and other nitrogen-containing compounds. Here the question is why particular compounds are made in particular places and at particular times, and what happens when another organism encounters them.

Specialized metabolites are not simply metabolic waste. Chemical traits can influence survival and reproduction, but finding a molecule in a plant does not establish that it evolved for defense. A compound may contribute to multiple functions: anthocyanins, for example, can influence visual signaling and responses to environmental stress. The effects depend on the species, tissue, developmental stage and ecological context.

Historical perspective and evidence

Natural-product chemistry initially emphasized isolation, structure and medicinal properties. Chemical ecology instead examines interactions among organisms. Ehrlich and Raven's 1964 work on butterflies and plants provided an influential framework for considering how plant chemistry and herbivore adaptation might shape diversification. Modern experiments employ biosynthetic mutants, chemical complementation, isotope tracers and behavioral assays to test causal connections rather than assume an ecological function from chemical structure.

Three levels of explanation are essential. Regulation determines pathway activity. Deployment determines the chemical form and site of accumulation or release. Ecological function concerns effects on the plant or an interacting organism. A high transcript level does not guarantee a large metabolite pool, and a large pool does not guarantee exposure of a target organism.

Learning objectives

Explain spatial and temporal regulation, distinguish constitutive and induced defenses, interpret JA signaling and flux data, compare antagonism and mutualism, and design experiments that test allelopathy and chemical ecological hypotheses.

2. Tissue-specific synthesis, storage and transport

Specialized products are distributed unevenly. Epidermal tissues are well positioned to accumulate UV-absorbing flavonoids. Glandular trichomes on leaves or flowers produce or store lipophilic and volatile compounds. Roots release metabolites into the rhizosphere. Young leaves, reproductive tissues and developing seeds may receive substantial protection, although distributions differ with species and environment.

Cellular division of labor

Plastidial MEP and cytosolic mevalonate pathways provide precursors for different terpenoid branches. Many phenylpropanoid reactions occur in the cytosol or involve enzymes associated with the endoplasmic reticulum, including cytochrome P450s. Final products may accumulate in vacuoles, cell walls, secretory cavities or external secretions rather than at the site of synthesis. Compartmentation creates distinct substrate concentrations, pH conditions and enzyme access, and can reduce damage to the producing cell.

Nicotine is predominantly synthesized in tobacco roots, transported through the xylem and accumulated in aerial tissues. Mint glandular trichomes contain specialized cells that synthesize essential oils, which accumulate in a space beneath the cuticle. Anthocyanins commonly accumulate as glycosides in vacuoles. Lignin is deposited into cell walls where it provides structural reinforcement. These examples separate biosynthesis from deployment.

Membrane transport and chemical modification

ABC transporters couple transport to ATP hydrolysis. MATE family transporters commonly exploit electrochemical gradients. Substrate specificity and direction depend on the particular protein and membrane. Some glutathione S-transferase proteins assist anthocyanin trafficking without necessarily forming a permanent glutathione conjugate. Glycosylation can change solubility, reactivity and transport; it is not universally a detoxification mechanism. A stored glycoside may subsequently be hydrolyzed by an appropriate enzyme.

Experimental demonstration

Label a root-supplied precursor and monitor labeled product in roots and shoots over time. Controls must distinguish transport of the product from transport of its precursor followed by local synthesis. Compare a transporter mutant with wild type and a complemented line. If total plant content remains similar but the mutant accumulates more product in roots and less in shoots, a distribution defect is more plausible than complete abolition of synthesis. Promoter reporters locate gene expression, whereas mass spectrometry and chemical imaging locate metabolites. Neither alone establishes all steps.

3. Environmental and developmental regulation

Transcription and metabolic flux

Transcription factors activate or repress genes encoding pathway enzymes. The MYB–bHLH–WD40 complex controls many anthocyanin biosynthesis genes; different MYB proteins can favor different flavonoid branches. Enzyme activity also depends on substrate, cofactors, feedback, redox conditions and protein turnover. Increasing one enzyme at a branch point may redirect precursor from a competing pathway, but it may have little effect if another reaction limits flux.

For metabolite M, dM/dt = vsynthesis + vimport − vdegradation − vexport. With a fixed tissue mass, terms can be expressed in nmol g−1 h−1. If tissue grows, dilution also matters for concentration. At steady state net accumulation is zero, not necessarily the individual reaction rates.

Worked example: a pool increases from 40 to 70 nmol/g in three hours. Average net accumulation is 10 nmol/g/h. If degradation plus export is 6 nmol/g/h and import is negligible, average synthesis is 16 nmol/g/h. This estimate is not a direct measurement of the activity of a single enzyme.

Light and UV-B

UV-B perception by UVR8 initiates signaling involving COP1 and transcriptional regulators including HY5. Increased expression of selected flavonoid biosynthetic genes can enhance accumulation of UV-screening flavonols in exposed tissues. Visible light also influences anthocyanin production through photoreceptor-dependent signaling. A UV-protection function can be tested using flavonoid-deficient and complemented lines exposed to controlled UV-B, with equivalent visible light and developmental conditions.

Temperature, water and nutrients

Stress may stimulate a particular pathway at moderate intensity but suppress it at severe intensity when photosynthesis and cell integrity decline. Nitrogen availability can alter allocation between nitrogen-containing metabolites and carbon-rich phenolics. Carbon–nutrient balance is a conditional hypothesis, not a universal law. Reduced biomass can increase concentration even if total production does not rise. Measure content per organ, concentration per dry mass and new synthesis independently.

Developmental and circadian control

Flower scent can be timed to pollinator activity; ripening fruits alter pigmentation, aromas and deterrent compounds as their ecological roles change. A scent rhythm persisting under constant environmental conditions supports an endogenous timing mechanism, whereas a rhythm measured only under day/night cycles could be a direct response to illumination. A developmental time course should compare equivalent organs and sampling times.

4. Constitutive, induced and damage-activated defense

Constitutive defenses are available before attack. Induced defenses increase or are newly synthesized after a stimulus. Both strategies may coexist in a plant. Pre-existing tannins or lignin provide immediate barriers, whereas inducible antimicrobial compounds allow flexible allocation. Induction can take time, and constitutive defenses may carry maintenance or opportunity costs.

Stored precursors activated on damage

Glucosinolates and myrosinase form a classic damage-activated system. Tissue disruption can bring enzyme and substrate together, producing hydrolysis products such as isothiocyanates or nitriles, depending on the chemical context. Cyanogenic glycosides may similarly release hydrogen cyanide after enzyme-mediated breakdown. These systems illustrate the distinction between preformed precursor, rapid activation and new biosynthesis. An active product appearing seconds after injury does not demonstrate that its precursor was synthesized after injury.

Phytoalexins and phytoanticipins

Phytoalexins are antimicrobial compounds produced or accumulated de novo following challenge. Camalexin in Arabidopsis and resveratrol in grapevine are representative examples. Phytoanticipins are present before challenge or are produced from pre-existing constituents following infection. Classification depends on the history of production, not solely on structure. Pathogens may tolerate or detoxify defensive compounds, so a phytoalexin-deficient plant may be more susceptible to one pathogen but not another.

Priming

Priming is an altered physiological state that enables a faster or stronger response to a subsequent challenge. Basal concentrations may remain unchanged. Compare treated and untreated plants before and after a standardized challenge. A greater challenge-dependent increase in the pretreated group supports priming, whereas a consistently elevated prechallenge pool supports constitutive elevation. These patterns may coexist.

5. Jasmonate, salicylate and hormonal crosstalk

Plants perceive damage-associated cues, herbivore-associated elicitors and microbial molecules. Calcium signals, reactive oxygen species and kinase cascades help initiate defense responses. The resulting chemical profile varies according to cue, genotype, tissue and developmental stage.

Core jasmonate pathway

Jasmonate biosynthesis begins with fatty acid-derived precursors in chloroplasts, includes OPDA and continues through peroxisomal reactions leading to jasmonic acid (JA). Conjugation with isoleucine generates JA-Ile, a major active signal. JA-Ile promotes interaction between the F-box protein COI1 and JAZ repressors. The SCFCOI1 ubiquitin ligase complex targets JAZ proteins for proteasomal degradation. This releases transcription factors such as MYC2 from repression and changes expression of defense-related genes.

Jasmonate signaling can regulate nicotine production in tobacco and numerous other alkaloid, terpene and phenolic pathways in other species. An exogenous methyl jasmonate treatment can serve as an elicitor, but the compound may be converted into other jasmonates. Effects depend on dose and treatment. A COI1-deficient mutant may produce JA-Ile normally but cannot efficiently transduce the canonical signal. Adding additional ligand does not repair a missing functional receptor component; a biosynthesis-deficient plant with intact perception may respond to a downstream active hormone.

SA and ethylene

Salicylic acid (SA), with regulators including NPR1, is important in many responses against biotrophic pathogens and in systemic acquired resistance. JA and ethylene are commonly involved in responses to herbivores and necrotrophs. These are tendencies rather than exclusive assignments. Crosstalk can be antagonistic, additive or synergistic. Ethylene can alter the relative contribution of different JA-regulated transcriptional programs. Concurrent insect and pathogen attack may therefore produce a chemical response different from the sum of single challenges.

Local and systemic signaling

A wounded leaf can influence other leaves and roots through electrical, calcium and vascular chemical signaling. Increased defense expression in a distant leaf does not identify the transported molecule: hormone synthesis at the destination may be triggered by a different mobile signal. Reciprocal grafting, isotope tracing and tissue-specific mutations can separate signal production, movement and perception.

6. Plant interactions with herbivores, pollinators and microbes

Direct herbivore defense

Alkaloids can interfere with physiological targets in susceptible insects. Tannins may affect feeding or digestion, while terpenoids may alter behavior or physiology. Choice assays measure preference; no-choice assays measure performance under forced exposure. A feeding deterrent need not be toxic, and a toxic compound may not be recognized before ingestion. Measure consumption, growth and survival separately.

Specialists can evolve detoxification, target-site tolerance, excretion or sequestration. Monarch caterpillars can tolerate and sequester certain milkweed cardenolides, which inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase in susceptible animals. The same compound may deter generalists while providing a cue or protective resource to adapted specialists. Ecological function therefore depends on properties of both the producer and receiver.

Indirect defense and volatile signals

Herbivore-induced plant volatiles include green-leaf volatiles and terpenoids. They can influence parasitoids and predators that locate herbivores. In a tritrophic interaction, plant, herbivore and natural enemy are linked by chemical cues. Attraction in an olfactometer shows a behavioral preference but does not alone prove that the plant benefits. To show indirect defense, test whether natural enemies reduce herbivore damage and improve an appropriate fitness measure under ecologically relevant conditions.

Pollination and seed dispersal

Anthocyanins and carotenoids contribute to flower colors; flavonoids may create UV patterns visible to certain insects. Floral scent can help pollinators locate flowers. Fruit pigments and odors can signal ripeness to dispersers. The identity, timing and concentration of chemicals matter more than a universal measure of attractiveness. Demonstrate a reproductive role by controlling for flower size, nectar availability and other cues while measuring visitation and seed production.

Root–microbe communication

Legume root flavonoids can activate compatible rhizobial NodD regulatory proteins, inducing bacterial nod gene expression. The resulting bacterial Nod factors are lipochitooligosaccharide signals that trigger host responses involved in nodulation. The plant-derived flavonoid and bacterial Nod factor are distinct signals. Root-exuded strigolactones stimulate hyphal branching of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, facilitating contact, but can also stimulate germination of certain parasitic plants. A signal beneficial in one interaction can be exploited in another.

Root exudates can also influence microbial community composition, nutrient mobilization and disease resistance. Microorganisms may degrade or transform exuded molecules, so profiles obtained in sterile culture may not persist in natural soil. To demonstrate an effect on microbiome-mediated plant performance, compare defined microbial communities and sterile controls, measure candidate compounds and restore missing chemical or microbial components.

7. Allelopathy and chemical interactions between plants

Allelopathy describes chemical effects of one organism on another in its environment; plant examples include inhibitory or stimulatory effects mediated by released compounds. It differs from competition for light, water and mineral nutrients, although both mechanisms may coexist. Suppressed growth near a donor plant is insufficient evidence of chemical interference.

Environmental fate and examples

Compounds may be released by root exudation, leaf leaching, volatilization or decomposing residues. Concentration at the receiver depends on release, dilution, diffusion, soil adsorption, microbial transformation and chemical degradation. Juglone associated with walnut is a traditional example of a potentially phytotoxic naphthoquinone. Sorghum produces sorgoleone, a hydrophobic root-associated compound capable of interfering with photosynthetic electron transport in experimental systems. Rice momilactones are associated with inhibition of some neighboring plants. Effects depend on exposure and environmental conditions.

Experimental controls

Compare receiver growth with and without a donor while matching nutrients, water, pH, light and osmotic conditions. Conditioned medium or a suitable root barrier can help distinguish chemical transfer from resource competition. Identify molecules using chromatography and mass spectrometry, then use realistic concentration series and solvent controls. A biosynthetic donor mutant and chemical rescue provide stronger causal evidence. Activated carbon may adsorb organics but also alters nutrient availability or microbial communities; carbon alone is not definitive proof.

If control root length is 8 cm and treated length is 5 cm, relative inhibition = (8 − 5)/8 × 100 = 37.5%. The calculation demonstrates inhibition in the tested conditions, not that a naturally occurring concentration produces this effect. Replication and uncertainty estimates are needed to assess reproducibility.

8. Resource allocation, growth–defense trade-offs and evolution

Specialized metabolism draws on fixed carbon, nitrogen, ATP, reducing equivalents and enzyme synthesis. Allocating resources to one process can limit another, but there is no universal fixed growth–defense budget. A protective trait can preserve leaf area and increase lifetime carbon fixation. Some products have several physiological roles. Evaluate costs and benefits under relevant conditions rather than assuming all defense reduces growth.

Optimal defense theory predicts relatively greater protection where tissues have high fitness value and probability of attack, conditional on defense effectiveness and cost. Young leaves and reproductive tissues may be valuable, but replacement rates, herbivore preferences and resource availability affect the prediction. Constitutive and inducible defenses can complement each other. Coevolution can generate escalating chemical defenses and counteradaptations but does not imply that each species will always evolve stronger defenses.

In this simplified example the defended genotype produces 10 fewer seeds without herbivory but 35 more seeds with herbivory. If attack occurs with probability p, expected defended yield = 90 − 15p; expected deficient yield = 100 − 60p. Equality occurs at p = 10/45 ≈ 0.222. The defense is favored by this model above an attack probability of approximately 22%. Real populations experience variable attack intensity, multiple traits and environmental interactions, so the threshold is illustrative rather than universal.

To test defense costs, compare isogenic or complemented lines under both attack and no-attack conditions. Measure reproductive output or another appropriate fitness proxy rather than only biomass. A slow-growing mutant may carry unrelated developmental defects; chemical or genetic restoration strengthens causal inference.

9. Experimental evidence and data interpretation

Metabolomics and isotope tracing

LC–MS is widely used for nonvolatile compounds; GC–MS can analyze volatiles and appropriately derivatized molecules. Peak areas cannot automatically be compared as molar concentrations because detection responses differ. Internal standards, calibration curves and extraction controls help quantify abundance. Isotope incorporation into newly produced molecules can distinguish new synthesis from pre-existing stores, subject to precursor labeling and appropriate kinetic interpretation.

The transcript peaks before the metabolite, consistent with a delay between transcription, enzyme production and accumulation. However, temporal ordering alone does not prove causation. Measure enzyme abundance, activity, precursor labeling and metabolite turnover; compare a loss-of-function mutant and a complemented line. Include untreated time-matched controls, since circadian and developmental processes can alter the chemical profile.

Factorial elicitor experiment

To distinguish mechanical injury from herbivore-associated elicitors, include untreated plants, standardized mechanical wounds, wounds treated with insect oral secretion and secretion without wounds where feasible. Use matched solvents and equivalent injury, then quantify metabolites, transcription and insect performance. A larger response to wounded-plus-secretion plants than wounded plants supports an additional elicitor effect. Randomize samples, use independent biological replicates and normalize to appropriate biomass.

Causal reasoning

A biosynthetic mutant may reduce the total product pool; a transporter mutant may redistribute it; a receiver insensitive to the compound may lose the ecological response despite unchanged plant chemistry. Complementation or chemical rescue must restore relevant form, location and concentration. Distinguish rescue of a biochemical phenotype, rescue of an interacting organism's behavior and restoration of plant fitness. A single assay rarely establishes all three.

Evidence ladder

Association → controlled perturbation → phenotype restoration → realistic exposure → fitness consequence.

13. Assertion–reason and application practice

For each assertion–reason pair: A = both true and reason explains assertion; B = both true but reason does not explain assertion; C = assertion true, reason false; D = assertion false, reason true.

1. A: Stored glucosinolates support rapid defense after damage. R: Injury can bring glucosinolates into contact with myrosinase.

Solution

A. Rapid enzyme-mediated activation explains the response.

2. A: Constant metabolite content proves synthesis has stopped. R: Inputs can balance losses at steady state.

Solution

D. Net zero accumulation does not imply zero flux.

3. A: A compound may deter a generalist but attract a specialist. R: Receivers differ in perception and detoxification.

Solution

A. Receiver-specific traits explain distinct ecological effects.

4. Numerical: in the seed yield model, calculate expected yields when p = 0.4.

Solution

Defended = 84; deficient = 76. The modeled advantage is 8 seeds.

5. A leaf concentration doubles from 20 to 40 nmol/g while dry mass falls from 2 to 1 g. What happens to total content?

Solution

Both totals are 40 nmol. Concentration doubled without net increase in content.

6. Design an experiment testing whether a flavonoid stimulates rhizobial nod expression rather than simply bacterial growth.

Solution

Use a nod-promoter reporter and normalize activity per bacterial cell; compare solvent control, NodD-deficient and complemented strains across concentrations. Measure growth independently.
