1. The biological problem: a genome without its own cell

A virus carries genetic information but does not possess the complete cellular apparatus required to express that information independently. The extracellular infectious particle is called a virion. It contains a genome protected by a protein coat, and in some viruses an additional membrane envelope. It may also contain enzymes or other proteins needed at the beginning of an intracellular phase. The virion is not a miniature cell. In particular, it lacks a complete functional ribosome system for manufacturing its own proteins. The distinction between the genetic instructions in the particle and the molecular activities of an infected cell is the starting point for understanding viral gene expression.

A host cell already contains ribosomes, transfer RNAs, amino acids, nucleotide pools, ATP, GTP and many enzymes. These resources normally maintain the host's own metabolism and gene expression. When a viral genome becomes available inside a cell, some of its information may be expressed using these resources. The existence of suitable resources, however, does not guarantee expression of every viral gene. The genome must first be made accessible in an appropriate compartment, its information must be presented in a molecular form recognized by the relevant machinery, and the resulting messages must be translated or otherwise used. Each transition introduces a distinct point at which expression may be regulated.

Why should different viral proteins appear at different times? Consider a genome that encodes enzymes needed to produce more nucleic acid and proteins needed to form the protective coat of a particle. The enzymes are useful before the pool of new genomes has expanded. Many coat molecules become especially useful when genomes are available for packaging. Producing every protein at an identical rate is not necessarily an efficient allocation of the host's resources. The required order and quantity of proteins differ according to the biological task. This is a reason why several viruses exhibit a recognizable temporal program, although the timing and mechanism of that program vary considerably.

An important distinction is between gene expression and genome replication. Gene expression produces functional RNA or protein from genetic information. Replication increases the number of genome molecules. A viral messenger RNA and a viral progeny genome may differ in size, end structure, polarity, associated proteins and biological role. Even when the two molecules have the same sequence polarity, their functions need not be identical. An increase in viral nucleic acid therefore cannot automatically be interpreted as increased production of a particular protein.

Three questions organize the topic. What is the chemical form of the genome? How does the virus obtain a messenger RNA compatible with the host's translation machinery? How does the amount, timing and use of that messenger RNA change? These questions connect the principles of transcription and translation studied earlier with the special restrictions imposed by viral dependence on host cells.

2. From the hereditary role of RNA to a molecular theory of viral expression

The recognition that a viral genome could consist of RNA was a major conceptual development in genetics. The familiar cellular distinction between DNA as the hereditary material and RNA as an intermediate in gene expression had emerged from studies of cells and DNA-containing viruses. It was not obvious in the middle of the twentieth century that RNA itself could supply the inherited information of a virus. Tobacco mosaic virus became important in addressing this question because its chemical composition included RNA and protein. The issue was whether the protein coat or the nucleic acid determined the inherited properties of the virus.

Historical investigations by Heinz Fraenkel-Conrat, Robley Williams, Bea Singer and others in the 1950s helped demonstrate that the RNA carried the viral genetic information. The conclusion rested on the distinction between the physical components of a particle and the inherited information that determines the properties of subsequent generations. In work involving different strains of TMV, the inherited characteristics followed the source of the RNA rather than the source of the associated coat protein. This was evidence about heredity, not simply an observation that the virus contained nucleic acid. The original coat protein was a physical component, while the RNA specified information relevant to the formation of later viral products.

This distinction can be understood without describing the laboratory methods. A structural protein can be part of a particle without being the inherited template for producing that same protein in another cell. The chemical structure of the protein depends on its amino acid sequence. If the sequence of the subsequently produced protein follows the information in the RNA, the RNA is the relevant genetic instruction. The experiment therefore separated the material that carried the instruction from a material whose properties could be specified by that instruction. This is the same kind of distinction that must be made when considering DNA and its encoded proteins in a cell.

A related question emerged once the genetic role of RNA had been established. Was RNA inherited by every virus through a direct RNA-to-RNA route, or were there systems that passed through another molecular form? The discoveries of RNA-directed DNA synthesis in 1970 by David Baltimore and by Howard Temin and Satoshi Mizutani provided evidence for an RNA-to-DNA route in certain animal viruses. That finding expanded the recognized routes of nucleic acid information transfer. It did not overturn the principle that protein sequence information is not used to reconstruct a specific nucleic acid sequence by a reverse process of translation. The discovery concerned copying between nucleic acids.

The 1971 expression-based framework placed these observations into a common conceptual system. This historical sequence explains why the genome-expression classes are useful rather than arbitrary. They represent experimentally established distinctions in the ways genetic information becomes accessible to the cellular expression machinery. In 1977, the adenovirus work on split genes added another lesson: even when the viral genome is DNA and the host makes RNA from it, the mature message can be assembled from discontinuous regions of the primary transcript. Together these discoveries changed the way molecular biologists understood the relationship between heredity, RNA and protein output.

A recurring feature of the history is the importance of comparing different kinds of evidence. Chemical composition establishes which macromolecules are present. Hereditary observations establish which molecules carry information. Enzyme activity establishes the possible direction of copying. The structure of a mature RNA reveals something about the route by which the information is presented to a ribosome. No single observation answers all four questions. Students can use this framework when evaluating statements about viruses: a claim about the inherited material, a claim about the source of an RNA and a claim about the production of a protein require different supporting evidence.

3. Physical organization of viral genomes and expression substrates

A genome is more than an abstract string of letters. Its chemical form determines the kinds of molecular interactions available to it. In double-stranded DNA, complementary bases occupy opposite strands and the information for transcription must be read from one strand at a particular region. The strand used for one gene need not be the same as that used for another gene elsewhere in the genome. Transcriptional orientation is therefore a local feature of a gene and its regulatory region rather than a single property that must apply uniformly to an entire double-stranded DNA molecule.

In single-stranded RNA, the bases can pair with complementary regions of the same molecule. Such internal pairing can give the RNA a folded structure. The structure is dynamic because weak noncovalent interactions can form and break, and because proteins can influence which conformations are favored. The secondary structure of a region near an initiation site can affect whether that site is available to the translation apparatus. A short RNA region that participates in base pairing is not necessarily permanently inaccessible. Accessibility depends on the competing interactions and on the molecular context. This principle was encountered earlier when considering ordinary cellular messenger RNAs and riboswitches, and it applies equally to viral RNAs.

Another relevant distinction is between the chemical identity of a template and its physical availability. In a virion the genome is associated with a protective structure. A protected genome is advantageous during transfer between cells, but its information is not necessarily accessible to a ribosome or a polymerase in that state. The physical relationships change as the intracellular phase begins. Depending on the virus, the genome may become accessible as a nucleic acid associated with residual viral proteins, as a component of a transcriptionally active structure, or in association with host components. The common requirement is appropriate access to the information, not complete release of naked nucleic acid in every case.

The need to store information compactly also creates opportunities for multiple expression outcomes. A coding region is a sequence that contributes to the amino acid order of a protein, while a regulatory sequence influences when or how some molecular process occurs. These functions are conceptually distinct but need not occupy completely separate physical regions in compact genomes. A sequence can have coding significance in one context and structural or regulatory significance in another. Therefore, a change to a nucleic acid sequence may have consequences that are not predictable solely from the amino acid sequence encoded by it.

Overlapping coding regions illustrate compact information storage. Two reading frames can partially occupy the same nucleotide region while specifying different amino acid sequences. A nucleotide substitution that is synonymous in one reading frame may be nonsynonymous in the other. A substitution can also affect an RNA structural feature without changing the encoded protein sequence. This is why the phrase silent mutation must be used with care. It normally describes the coding consequence in a specified reading frame; it does not guarantee the absence of all biological effects. In a NET-style question, the precise meaning of silent should be defined before drawing conclusions about expression.

The distinction between structural proteins and nonstructural proteins is also important. A structural protein contributes to the physical architecture of a virus particle. A nonstructural protein is generally discussed for its intracellular function rather than its role as a major architectural component. These functional labels do not establish whether a protein is an enzyme. Some components associated with particles can possess enzymatic activity, and a protein that acts in an intracellular process need not be retained as an abundant component of the mature particle. The expression problem is consequently not simply to make one class of protein before the other. It is to supply different functional activities and molecular components in the appropriate context.

These physical distinctions clarify why apparently similar expression diagrams can represent different biological systems. A DNA molecule in the nucleus, an RNA associated with a viral protein, and a freely accessible cytoplasmic message are not equivalent substrates, even though all carry nucleotide sequences. Before following a reaction arrow, identify what the molecule is made of, whether its sequence is in the appropriate orientation, and whether the relevant enzyme can act on it. This approach prevents errors that arise when genetic information is treated as independent of its molecular form.

4. Genome polarity and the common requirement for messenger RNA

The term positive sense denotes a relationship between a nucleic acid sequence and a coding message. In an RNA genome of positive polarity, the relevant coding region has the orientation required for reading by ribosomes. A negative-sense RNA genome is complementary to the corresponding messenger RNA and must first be copied into positive-sense RNA. The labels positive and negative do not refer to the electrical charge of the RNA. Both types possess the negatively charged phosphate backbone characteristic of nucleic acids. Nor do the labels indicate whether the effect of an RNA on the host is beneficial or harmful.

A ribosome reads messenger RNA in the direction from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end. The order of nucleotides, the choice of an initiation site and the grouping of nucleotides into codons determine the protein that can be produced. A complementary RNA contains a different sequence and cannot generally supply the same codon information. Thus an incoming negative-sense genome does not become a functional messenger simply because the cell contains ribosomes. The correct RNA must be produced first by an appropriate polymerase.

A polymerase is classified according to both the template it reads and the product it makes. A DNA-dependent RNA polymerase reads DNA and produces RNA. An RNA-dependent RNA polymerase reads RNA and produces RNA. A reverse transcriptase is able to synthesize DNA using an RNA template. These activities are related through their use of complementary base pairing, but they are not interchangeable. The enzyme normally responsible for nuclear cellular messenger RNA transcription is not automatically able to transcribe an incoming RNA viral genome. This difference creates a fundamental constraint on the initial gene-expression route.

Positive polarity is a necessary sequence relationship for ordinary translation, but it is not sufficient evidence that a molecule is efficiently translated. A message must also have a suitable physical structure and be available to initiation factors and ribosomes. The RNA may be masked by proteins, folded in ways that restrict recognition, or located where translation is not occurring. A genomic positive-sense RNA can therefore serve as a messenger under one set of conditions and as a template for a different process under another.

The distinction between template and product is particularly useful when following diagrams of viral RNA expression. An arrow labelled RNA synthesis does not identify the enzyme unless the template is also specified. An arrow from RNA to protein refers to translation rather than RNA synthesis. An arrow from RNA to complementary RNA can represent the production of a message or a replication intermediate, depending on the length and role of the product. Keep these possibilities separate before interpreting the biological outcome.

5. The historical insight of the Baltimore framework

A useful way to organize viruses emerged when researchers recognized that different viral genome types could nevertheless be related by their routes to messenger RNA. David Baltimore's 1971 article on the expression of animal virus genomes presented this information-flow perspective. Its significance was that the functional problem of making a readable message could be used to compare apparently very different kinds of viruses. The framework subsequently came to include seven conventional genome-expression classes. Its value in molecular biology is not primarily the memorization of numbered categories but the ability to infer the required information-transfer steps.

Double-stranded DNA viruses can in principle supply an appropriate DNA template for transcription. Single-stranded DNA viruses generally require formation of a double-stranded DNA form before ordinary transcription of their genes. For double-stranded RNA viruses, production of positive-sense transcripts from the appropriate strands is required. Positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses can commonly translate the genome itself at an initial stage, whereas negative-sense RNA viruses require synthesis of positive-sense messages before corresponding proteins can appear. RNA viruses that use reverse transcription have a DNA intermediate from which messenger RNAs are transcribed. DNA viruses that use reverse transcription include an RNA intermediate in genome reproduction, while their messages are produced from a DNA form.

These categories describe routes of information transfer, not evolutionary families. Two viruses may use a related expression route while having quite different structures, hosts or evolutionary histories. Conversely, related molecular activities can participate in different places within expression and replication schemes. For example, the presence of reverse transcription does not mean that a ribosome translates DNA. In the conventional reverse-transcribing RNA virus route, RNA is copied into DNA and RNA messages are then produced from DNA. Ribosomes still read RNA. The apparent reversal of the familiar DNA-to-RNA direction occurs in nucleic acid synthesis, not in the transfer of sequence information from protein back to nucleic acid.

This framework can be applied to plants as well as animals, since the chemical problem is shared. Plant and animal cells possess eukaryotic translation machinery, though their cellular environment and many regulatory responses differ. The presence of a cell wall in a plant does not change the chemistry of codon reading by cytoplasmic ribosomes. Accordingly, an examination question that identifies only the host as a plant provides insufficient information to predict the type of polymerase required for viral transcription. The genome type and expression route must be considered.

The framework also does not establish a universal temporal program. It tells us how messenger RNA can arise, not necessarily which genes are expressed first or how strongly a particular message is translated. Additional information about promoter use, RNA processing, accessibility, translation and protein stability is required to infer changes in gene output. This limitation is important when moving from broad classification to the actual control of expression.

6. Chemical directionality, energy and molecular specificity

The reactions involved in gene expression have different kinds of chemical substrates and products. During RNA synthesis, an RNA chain grows through addition of nucleotides. The nucleotide sequence is determined by complementarity with an appropriate template. During protein synthesis, a growing chain receives amino acids delivered by transfer RNAs. The sequence relationship is established through codons and anticodons rather than through direct chemical copying of the amino acid chain from a DNA molecule. These differences in chemistry explain why a polymerase cannot substitute for a ribosome.

Nucleotide polymerization depends on activated nucleotide substrates. RNA polymerases use ribonucleoside triphosphates, while DNA polymerases use deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates. The difference in the sugars and the catalytic specificity of the enzymes determine the chemical form of the product. The release of pyrophosphate accompanies nucleotide incorporation, and the cellular handling of this product contributes to the directionality of the overall process. A viral genome does not supply these substrates simply by carrying the information required to specify a polymerase.

RNA-dependent RNA polymerases and DNA-dependent RNA polymerases share the broad requirement to align incoming nucleotides with a template, but their substrate recognition differs. A molecule can be complementary to a target RNA without being an appropriate template for every polymerase. The enzyme must bind and position its substrate in a manner compatible with catalysis. The specification of template type therefore contains information about the biochemical constraints on the reaction. A student should not infer that all RNA-producing enzymes can copy any nucleic acid molecule merely because they make the same kind of product.

Protein synthesis has its own energetic requirements. Transfer RNA molecules become linked to amino acids through reactions catalyzed by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases. The activation of amino acids requires energy, and the movement of ribosomal complexes through stages of translation also depends on nucleotide hydrolysis. These energy-dependent activities are provided by the cellular system on which viral expression relies. The virus may encode proteins that participate in its gene-expression program, but encoding a protein is not equivalent to independently supplying the complete set of energetic and chemical resources needed to synthesize it.

RNA structures and protein–nucleic acid complexes involve weaker interactions that influence molecular accessibility. Base pairing and stacking contribute to RNA structure. Protein binding can be favored by electrostatic and other noncovalent interactions, among them hydrogen bonding and interactions with the molecular surface. The ability of a molecule to adopt an appropriate conformation affects its interaction with enzymes. Changes in binding need not correspond to changes in covalent sequence, and changes in covalent sequence need not have the same effect on binding in every structural context.

These principles distinguish the thermodynamic possibility of an interaction from the rate at which it occurs. Two complementary regions may be capable of pairing, but accessibility and molecular motion influence the rate at which they encounter one another. A bound complex may persist for a measurable time even when other states are possible. The existence of a stable RNA structure does not prove that it cannot be rearranged during gene expression. The kinetic and energetic context matters. No universal quantitative affinity or energy value can be assigned to viral expression as a whole.

For examination purposes, the appropriate mathematical treatment of these chemical principles usually involves familiar ideas such as mass balance, rate, stoichiometry and the interpretation of relative changes. The specificity of an individual viral polymerase or the detailed energetics of a particular regulatory complex would require additional experimental information. The aim is not to derive artificial constants but to identify which substrates, products and cellular resources are relevant. When a question proposes that viral protein synthesis can continue independently of amino acid charging or ribosomal function, this biochemical framework immediately reveals the flaw.

7. DNA-virus expression and the early-to-late transition

For a representative nuclear double-stranded DNA virus, expression begins when the viral DNA can be used as a template by transcription machinery. The DNA sequence includes regions with regulatory functions as well as sequences ultimately represented in RNA products. A promoter is a region of DNA associated with the initiation of transcription. Different promoters within a viral genome can have different dependencies on regulatory proteins and different patterns of activity during infection. In this respect the principles resemble those of ordinary cellular genes, even though the overall program is coordinated in a viral context.

Adenovirus provides a classical example of a nuclear DNA virus in which host RNA polymerase II transcribes viral messenger RNA precursors. Its early phase involves production of regulatory and other nonstructural proteins, while a major later phase includes substantial expression of proteins associated with particle structure. These categories describe predominant expression programs and should not be interpreted as perfectly sharp temporal boundaries. Some transcripts can remain detectable across phases. The existence of late expression also does not imply that every structural component must be transcribed exclusively after genome replication.

A useful conceptual distinction separates template abundance from transcription per template. At an early stage there may be relatively few copies of a viral DNA molecule. Later, after genome reproduction, many more templates may be present. Even if each template produced RNA at an unchanged average rate, the total RNA production across the cell could increase. A second possibility is that the transcriptional activity per available template also changes. A third possibility is that processing or lifetime of the RNA changes. All three can increase the measured abundance of a late transcript, but they describe different regulatory events.

If a late RNA is less abundant when genome replication is prevented, the observation supports a relationship between DNA replication and late expression in that system. It does not, on its own, prove a particular direct molecular interaction between the replication machinery and the promoter. A smaller number of DNA templates is one possible explanation. A requirement for a different state of viral DNA, for additional viral factors or for altered RNA maturation is another. The student must distinguish what is established by an observation from a proposed mechanism that may account for it.

Nuclear location is relevant because it provides access to cellular transcription and RNA-processing machinery. Some DNA viruses, however, have expression strategies that depend on virus-encoded transcription components rather than ordinary nuclear transcription. The mere statement that the genome is DNA therefore does not establish which enzyme transcribes it or where the reaction occurs. The appropriate generalization is that DNA is a template for DNA-dependent RNA synthesis, while the cellular source of the enzyme and its location depend on the particular virus.

The early and late labels are most useful as functional descriptions of changing output. Early products are commonly associated with establishing conditions needed for subsequent expression and genome reproduction. Late products often include proteins required in larger numbers when particles are formed. Neither the sequence position of a gene on a DNA diagram nor the direction of transcription alone determines whether it is early or late. Regulatory dependencies must be inferred from evidence rather than from the physical order of the genes.

8. Nuclear transcription, processing and compartmental boundaries

Many animal DNA viruses use the nuclear transcription system of the host. This imposes the same basic chemical requirements that are encountered in cellular transcription. The polymerase must interact with an appropriate DNA template, ribonucleoside triphosphates must be available, and the transcription reaction must be coordinated with other events that make the RNA a functional product. The viral DNA does not provide a free source of energy. The substrates and the cellular environment are part of the expression system.

RNA polymerase II produces the precursor of many eukaryotic messenger RNAs. The enzyme's catalytic role should be distinguished from the roles of proteins that help regulate which DNA regions are transcribed. A regulator can influence the probability of initiation or other stages without directly catalyzing RNA synthesis. Consequently, a virus may depend on cellular polymerase activity while using additional viral gene products to influence expression. Calling transcription host dependent does not mean that no viral factor can influence it, just as calling a reaction virus encoded does not mean that host resources are irrelevant.

Transcription begins at a defined position relative to a regulatory region, and the transcript extends away from that position in a particular direction. The start of transcription and the start of translation are distinct locations. A transcript can contain a five-prime untranslated region before its first translated codon. At its other end it may contain sequence downstream from the stop codon. These untranslated regions can participate in message stability, processing or recognition. The start of an open reading frame is therefore not a substitute for identifying a transcription start site.

Processing introduces another important distinction between primary and mature products. A primary transcript may contain sequences not present in the final messenger RNA. Mature messages can also possess chemical or structural features associated with their ends that are not represented as an identical continuous sequence in the original DNA. An investigator who compares genomic DNA with mature RNA must take processing into account. A difference between the molecules is not automatically evidence of a change in the genetic template.

A nuclear RNA must become available to cytoplasmic ribosomes if it is to participate in the usual cytoplasmic translation of a protein. The movement of RNA from one compartment to another introduces an additional potential regulatory level. Suppose that a cell contains a large amount of a particular mature RNA in its nucleus but little of that RNA in the cytoplasm. Even if nuclear transcription and processing have occurred efficiently, the cytoplasmic translation output may be limited. A total-cell RNA measurement alone would fail to distinguish this situation from one in which the message is distributed evenly between the compartments.

This reasoning explains why a nuclear DNA-virus transcript can be associated with several successive requirements. Template availability, transcription, processing, movement into the cytoplasm and translation are not one reaction but a chain of activities. A defect in an early step can reduce the amount of material reaching every subsequent step. A defect in a later step can instead leave upstream intermediates detectable while reducing final protein output. In a simplified problem, the pattern of accumulated intermediates may help identify where a block lies. In real systems, feedback between steps can complicate that interpretation.

Not every animal DNA virus uses the nucleus for the same transcriptional program. Poxviruses are a conventional example of DNA viruses with a cytoplasmic expression strategy that includes viral transcription machinery. This exception is particularly informative because it separates the properties of the template from the origin of the polymerase. The DNA still provides the information used to make RNA, but the necessary activities are available outside the conventional nuclear context. The generalization that all DNA viruses use host nuclear RNA polymerase II is therefore false, even though adenovirus is a useful representative example of such dependence.

The compartment distinction also provides a way to understand why host cells cannot be reduced to passive bags of nucleotides. Cellular organization establishes where enzymes and substrates meet, where RNAs mature and where ribosomes are available. Viral gene expression must operate within these physical constraints. In a comparative question, two systems that both make RNA from DNA may nonetheless differ in the cellular location and machinery involved, so the pathway must be specified before assigning a universal response to a perturbation.

9. Adenovirus and the evidence for RNA splicing

The discovery of interrupted genes is closely connected with studies of viral messenger RNA. In 1977, Susan Berget, Claire Moore and Phillip Sharp reported evidence that the five-prime region of a mature adenovirus late message corresponded to separated regions of the viral DNA. Work by other researchers, including Louise Chow and Richard Roberts and their colleagues, independently revealed the discontinuous relationship between viral RNA and DNA. These findings challenged the expectation that a mature eukaryotic messenger RNA necessarily corresponded to one uninterrupted segment of genomic DNA. They were part of the discovery of split genes recognized by the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

The logic of an RNA–DNA hybrid makes the observation understandable. A single-stranded RNA can pair with complementary DNA. If a mature RNA corresponds continuously to one uninterrupted region, the pairing relationship is correspondingly continuous along that region. If portions of the DNA have no complementary sequence in the mature RNA because intervening segments were removed from the RNA during maturation, the pattern differs. Separated complementary regions may be juxtaposed within the mature RNA while intervening DNA regions remain unpaired. The spatial relationships visible in the classical electron-microscopy observations provided evidence for discontinuous gene organization and RNA processing.

The observation did not imply that the DNA itself had been permanently cut into a new sequence during every act of expression. Instead, a larger primary RNA transcript could undergo processing in which intervening segments, termed introns, are removed and the retained portions, termed exons, are joined. The same DNA template can be transcribed repeatedly. Processing changes the RNA product without requiring a new arrangement of the original DNA. This distinction is essential for understanding how cells and some viruses produce multiple related messages.

Alternative splicing is the use of different combinations of splice sites or retained segments during RNA maturation. It can lead to mature RNAs that contain different combinations of exons. When the affected sequences alter coding regions, the resulting proteins may differ. When they alter untranslated regions, the proteins may have the same amino acid sequence but their production can differ because the messages have different properties. Not every difference among RNA species is therefore evidence for different transcription start sites, and not every splice difference changes a protein's sequence.

Adenovirus illustrates the usefulness of RNA processing in a compact genome. Multiple RNA products can share parts of a precursor while differing in the regions retained in their mature forms. In a broad model, changing the balance among processing outcomes changes the relative amounts of different mature messages without requiring an equivalent change in the initial transcription rate. The gene output depends on both the production of the common precursor and the allocation of that precursor among processing alternatives.

A splice junction is a feature of the mature RNA where previously separated regions have become adjacent. Detecting such a junction provides evidence about RNA processing, but detecting a mature RNA does not directly demonstrate how much protein it produces. To make that inference one must also know whether the message is translated and how long its product persists. The historical importance of adenovirus studies is that they exposed an additional regulatory level that is now central to the understanding of eukaryotic gene expression generally, not a process unique to adenovirus.

10. Alternative RNA-processing outcomes and coding consequences

Alternative splicing can be understood through the distinction between an RNA molecule's sequence and the protein sequence that it can specify. Suppose an abstract primary RNA contains three exons, A, B and C, separated by introns. One mature RNA retains A, B and C, whereas another retains A and C. These products differ in their sequence even though they originated from the same underlying DNA region. If exon B contributes to the translated region, the proteins produced from the two RNAs may differ. If it lies entirely within an untranslated portion of the message, the proteins may instead have identical primary amino acid sequences.

Removal of a coding segment does not always produce a simple protein with one shorter region. If the boundaries alter the grouping of nucleotides into codons, the reading frame downstream can change. In the simplest hypothetical case, removing a coding sequence whose length is divisible by three preserves the downstream grouping, whereas removing a sequence of one or two nucleotides beyond a multiple of three changes it. This arithmetic rule must be applied to the specific coding region; it cannot be used indiscriminately for untranslated segments. Nor does preservation of a reading frame establish that a shortened protein folds or functions normally.

For regulatory interpretation, it is useful to distinguish the amount of precursor from the fractions that enter different processing pathways. Imagine that 200 molecules of a hypothetical precursor have two possible mature outcomes. If 150 yield form X and 50 yield form Y during a defined interval, the fractions are 0.75 and 0.25. If a second observation shows 100 of each mature form produced from the same number of precursors, the allocation has changed even though precursor production has not. This abstract example describes the logic of alternative processing and is not a quantitative measurement of adenovirus.

The fractions of processing products formed in an interval are not necessarily equal to the fractions found in a steady pool of mature RNAs. If one mature RNA survives for longer than the other, it can become disproportionately abundant. In the preceding hypothetical model, if form X is produced three times as rapidly as Y but has a removal constant three times as large, both may attain the same steady abundance. An abundance ratio measured after processing therefore reflects both the production of each RNA form and its subsequent removal. This distinction is often missed when students infer splice-site choice directly from a ratio of accumulated mature products.

Alternative processing and alternative initiation can both generate multiple RNAs from a single genomic region, but they make different predictions about the relationships among the molecules. Alternative initiation changes where synthesis begins, whereas alternative splicing changes the pattern of segments retained in the mature RNA. Alternative cleavage and polyadenylation can affect where a mature RNA ends. These mechanisms can coexist, and the presence of one does not exclude another. An accurate description specifies the molecular feature that differs, such as a distinct start, end or splice junction.

The adenovirus late transcription system exemplifies why the identification of shared RNA segments matters. A group of mature messages can share leader sequences while differing in the coding portions associated with those leaders. The same upstream information can therefore be present in several RNA products. The important examination-level inference is that a common sequence in mature messages need not correspond to independent, identical transcription starts adjacent to every protein-coding region. Processing of longer precursors can account for shared features of multiple products.

A conceptual difficulty arises when students compare RNA splicing with protein cleavage. Both processes can remove material from a larger precursor, but their molecular consequences differ. RNA splicing alters the nucleotide sequence that is presented to the ribosome. Protein cleavage acts after translation and alters the set of polypeptide products derived from the translated precursor. A shortened RNA can influence which amino acids are incorporated during translation; a cleaved protein does not retroactively change the messenger RNA or the original codon sequence. This distinction prepares the ground for comparing splicing and polyprotein processing as different ways of expressing compact genomes.

The historical discovery of RNA splicing also illustrates how an unexpected physical observation can reveal a molecular mechanism that had not been anticipated by the simplest textbook model. The existence of separated complementary DNA segments corresponding to one mature RNA was incompatible with a purely uninterrupted mapping. The proposed processing mechanism accounted for that discrepancy and provided a framework that could be tested in other eukaryotic systems. The scientific value lies not merely in remembering the discovery date but in understanding what pattern was inconsistent with the earlier model.

11. RNA viruses: different routes to a translatable message

The usual RNA expression route of a positive-sense RNA virus begins with the availability of an incoming genome that can function as messenger RNA. Protein synthesis from that RNA can occur through the host translation system, provided the appropriate conditions for recognition are met. A viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase may then be produced among the initial viral proteins. The distinction between a polymerase encoded by the genome and one present in the incoming particle matters because the first event must be possible before additional viral protein synthesis has taken place.

In a negative-sense RNA virus, the incoming RNA is not an ordinary messenger for its encoded proteins. Production of positive-sense messages requires an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Such a virus must provide the appropriate transcriptional activity at the outset, typically through polymerase associated with the entering viral particle. It would be circular to propose that the first functional polymerase is made by direct translation of the incoming negative-sense genome, since the positive-sense messenger necessary for that translation is precisely what the polymerase must make.

A double-stranded RNA genome imposes another access constraint. The relevant positive-sense sequences exist within a double-stranded molecule, but the intact duplex is not equivalent to freely available messenger RNA. Positive-sense transcripts can be generated from the complementary strands by viral transcription machinery. In representative double-stranded RNA viruses, this production is associated with viral core structures. The physical association of a template with proteins can distinguish transcriptionally useful genome molecules from RNA messages available to ribosomes.

The ordinary host ribosome does not distinguish the organismal origin of an RNA by reading a label that says viral or cellular. It responds to the molecular properties that allow initiation and subsequent decoding. Consequently, different classes of viral RNA can all converge on host-mediated translation despite their very different preceding steps. In an exam question, the crucial point is often to identify whether a hypothetical defect affects the creation of messenger RNA or the utilization of messenger RNA. A loss of transcriptional activity and a loss of translation activity can both reduce protein output, but the intervening RNA patterns may differ.

RNA genome replication is related to RNA transcription because both may involve RNA-dependent RNA synthesis. However, a genome-length copy and a messenger RNA may have different ends, lengths and associated proteins. Some RNA viruses make additional messages of less than genome length for particular proteins. Other strategies produce a larger translated protein precursor that is subsequently cleaved. Thus the fact that a genome is a single RNA molecule does not imply that it always functions as a single conventional monocistronic messenger for all its genes. The structure of the expression pathway must be learned rather than assumed from the physical number of genome molecules.

12. Reverse-transcribing genomes and the distinction between RNA function and RNA identity

The occurrence of RNA-directed DNA synthesis provides a particularly instructive exception to the simplified description of information flow often introduced in school biology. Reverse transcriptase can synthesize DNA using RNA as a template. This is a copying reaction in which the product is a nucleic acid. It must be distinguished from an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, whose product is RNA, and from a ribosome, whose principal role is the formation of a polypeptide based on codon information.

In the conventional expression scheme for retroviruses, the inherited RNA passes through a DNA form. Viral messenger RNAs are subsequently transcribed from DNA using cellular transcription machinery. The existence of positive-sense RNA in the particle does not mean that the initial gene-expression route is identical to the direct use of an incoming genome by a conventional positive-sense RNA virus. The molecular context of the entering genome and the role of the DNA form are essential features of the route. The same sequence polarity can therefore occur in systems with different requirements at the beginning of expression.

A provirus is viral DNA that has become integrated into cellular DNA in the retroviral context. The resulting DNA can provide a template for the production of viral RNAs. Because cellular transcription acts on DNA, the production of RNA from this DNA form is chemically an ordinary DNA-directed transcription reaction, even though the DNA itself was derived from an RNA genome. Reverse transcription and subsequent transcription are two separate reactions with different products. A statement that the RNA genome is directly transcribed into messenger RNA by reverse transcriptase confuses these two steps.

The expression products of a DNA form can include both RNAs that act as messages and RNAs that have genomic functions in later particles. Their biological roles depend on the way they are processed, associated with other molecules and used. The term genomic RNA does not necessarily describe a molecule incapable of translation. Conversely, the presence of an RNA in the cell does not guarantee that it will be inherited as part of a particle. Molecular function is defined by what happens to the RNA in the relevant biological context.

A different reverse-transcribing scheme is represented by the DNA viruses that pass through an RNA intermediate during genome reproduction. Their expression of protein-coding information still requires RNA transcripts from a DNA template. The fact that the system includes RNA-directed DNA synthesis does not make all its transcription RNA directed. In this class, one must distinguish the RNA intermediate associated with production of DNA genomes from the messenger RNAs used for protein production. Some RNA species can participate in more than one role, but the reaction by which each function is fulfilled must remain clear.

These two broad reverse-transcribing categories illustrate why the Baltimore framework should be interpreted as a map of information flow rather than as a list of labels. A student who knows only that a virus has reverse transcriptase cannot infer its inherited nucleic acid type without knowing the rest of the scheme. The enzyme can participate in the route from an inherited RNA genome to a DNA form or in the route from an RNA intermediate to a DNA genome. The direction of the enzyme's reaction is the same, but its position within the broader expression and replication relationship differs.

There is also an important distinction between a persistent DNA template and ongoing expression from that template. A DNA molecule can be present without every one of its associated genes being actively transcribed. Its presence therefore does not provide direct evidence for the simultaneous production of all corresponding proteins. This principle is familiar from ordinary cellular genes. It also helps explain why an examination problem involving viral DNA detection cannot be answered by assuming that every viral protein must be present. The relevant molecular activity must be specified.

Detailed regulation of particular retroviral systems, integration mechanisms and clinical consequences are separate topics. For present purposes the reverse-transcribing examples establish three essential distinctions: inherited genome type versus the form used for transcription, the chemical activity of the enzyme versus its role in the larger pathway, and nucleic acid presence versus active expression. These distinctions are more useful than memorizing every named virus associated with the categories.

13. A plant-virus example: tobacco mosaic virus and subgenomic messages

Tobacco mosaic virus, commonly abbreviated TMV, is a useful textbook example of expression from a positive-sense plant viral RNA genome. Its expression strategy demonstrates why an RNA that carries several coding regions need not be translated at each of those regions with identical efficiency. The ordinary scanning model of eukaryotic translation favors initiation at an appropriate site encountered by the scanning ribosomal complex. Coding regions located farther along a long RNA may therefore need a different means of becoming available for translation. The issue is one of message organization and access, not an inability of plant ribosomes to recognize the genetic code.

The genome of a representative TMV contains coding information for replication-associated proteins, a movement protein and a coat protein. The full-length genomic RNA is used in the expression of replication-associated proteins. The movement and coat protein genes are expressed from shorter viral RNAs called subgenomic messenger RNAs. These RNAs represent portions of the viral genetic information rather than independent complete copies of the whole genome. By making shorter messages, the system presents particular coding regions in a context suited for their translation.

The coat protein contributes to the protective structure associated with the RNA genome. The movement protein has a role in movement between plant cells. Replication-associated proteins participate in RNA synthesis. The functions are distinct, and the production of their messages can follow different patterns. An understanding of these roles helps explain why an abundance of coat protein need not be proportional to the abundance of a replication-associated protein. Different classes of RNA can contribute independently to the protein output.

A subgenomic messenger RNA must not be confused with a genome segment. In a segmented genome, different molecules collectively carry the heritable genetic complement. A subgenomic RNA may instead be a shorter expression product made within the infected cell from information also represented in a longer genome molecule. Tobacco mosaic virus is conventionally described as having a nonsegmented positive-sense RNA genome. Its production of shorter expression messages is therefore not evidence that the genome is divided into separate inherited segments.

A second possible strategy used by other plant RNA viruses is the production of a polyprotein, a long polypeptide precursor that is cleaved to yield individual proteins. This differs fundamentally from production of a subgenomic RNA. In the former strategy, diversification occurs at the protein processing level. In the latter, different RNA templates are generated for translation. Some viral systems use more than one strategy, but a student should not attribute the same combination of strategies to every virus merely because it possesses positive-sense RNA.

The plant example also illustrates a shared biological constraint. Although plants and animals differ in cell structure and physiology, neither type of virus has a complete independent ribosome system. The expression of viral protein remains dependent on cellular translation machinery. The organization of viral messenger RNA helps determine which parts of the genome can be expressed by that machinery, while the host environment contributes the resources and conditions needed for translation.

14. Polyprotein processing as a distinct expression strategy

A protein-coding message does not necessarily correspond to one final functional polypeptide. In a polyprotein expression strategy, a ribosome translates a continuous open reading frame to produce a long precursor. Subsequent cleavage events separate portions of that precursor into smaller protein products. The larger molecule is called a polyprotein because it contains regions that will become several distinct products, not because it is an aggregate of unrelated proteins that were synthesized separately.

Cleavage is a chemical reaction involving peptide bonds. Proteases catalyze the hydrolysis of such bonds and thereby convert a precursor into smaller polypeptide chains. The amino acids of the products were already incorporated during translation of the original message. In contrast to RNA splicing, the process does not remove introns or create a new messenger RNA. In contrast to an alternative initiation event, it does not require a separate start of translation for every protein ultimately obtained from the precursor. The stages of synthesis and maturation should remain conceptually distinct.

A simple analogy can be made with the ordinary processing of cellular protein precursors. Cells can synthesize an inactive or incompletely matured precursor that is converted into another form after cleavage. The viral polyprotein strategy uses the same broad chemical principle of polypeptide processing, although the biological functions and organization are different. It offers a way to encode multiple products within a continuously translated region, and is commonly described in textbook accounts of certain animal and plant RNA viruses. It is not a property of all RNA viruses.

Consider a hypothetical precursor containing three regions that are separated by cleavage to form proteins A, B and C. If each completely processed precursor yields one molecule of each protein, the immediate theoretical yield from 100 completed precursors is 100 molecules of A, 100 of B and 100 of C. This stoichiometric statement is limited to the specified idealized processing event. If one product is degraded more rapidly than the others, the observed amounts at a later time need not remain equal. If incomplete processing occurs, some material may remain in larger intermediates rather than in the three final forms. Counting only the mature products would underestimate the amount of precursor synthesized.

The strategy can be distinguished from expression through subgenomic messages. Suppose an abstract genome makes two independent short messages, each translated separately to form one protein. The relative protein yields can vary according to the amount and translation of each message. A polyprotein precursor instead provides the initial regions within one translated chain, after which processing and product stability influence their individual availability. The difference is one of the molecular stage at which initially linked information becomes separate functional products.

One should also distinguish a single long open reading frame from a polycistronic messenger in the usual bacterial sense. A polycistronic messenger contains multiple coding regions that may be translated through separate initiation events. A polyprotein-encoding region can yield multiple final products after one continuous translation event. Both strategies can express multiple proteins from a single RNA molecule, but they are not mechanistically identical. The words polycistronic and polyprotein should not be treated as interchangeable.

The properties of the final products also differ from those of the precursor. Some structural or functional features may become apparent only after the relevant polypeptide region has been separated from adjacent regions. Other protein functions may reside in a precursor or intermediate rather than exclusively in the final cleaved products. Thus incomplete processing is not always equivalent to a universal absence of function. An examination problem should specify whether it concerns translation of the precursor, conversion into mature products or the biological activity of one of those products.

The comparison with the TMV subgenomic message strategy illustrates a central lesson in viral gene regulation. Different genomes face the common problem of expressing multiple functions using a limited amount of genetic material, but the solutions may operate at different levels. RNA synthesis can provide separate messages. RNA processing can rearrange the retained sequences in messages. Protein processing can generate different mature products from a translated chain. None of these solutions requires abandoning the basic direction of information transfer from messenger RNA to protein.

15. Selective translation and the meaning of host shutoff

Translation is not determined solely by the total concentration of messenger RNA. A message must be available to ribosomes and compatible with the initiation machinery. Many eukaryotic cellular messages carry a modified five-prime cap that participates in recognition by translation-initiation factors. A three-prime polyadenylated region can also influence stability and translation in many messages. These features are not universal requirements for every translation event, however, and not every viral messenger resembles a typical cellular messenger in every structural detail.

The phrase selective translation means that some RNA species contribute disproportionately to protein synthesis relative to others under the same cellular conditions. Selectivity can arise because two classes of RNA differ in availability, stability, association with proteins or dependence on a particular component of translation. An RNA may remain abundant but contribute little to protein synthesis if it is unavailable for efficient initiation. Conversely, a relatively small pool of efficiently used messages can sustain considerable protein production. This is a direct application of the distinction between RNA abundance and translation efficiency.

Host shutoff is a broad descriptive term for reduced host gene expression during certain viral infections. The reduction may reflect changes in cellular RNA production, RNA processing, RNA lifetime, translation or a combination of these. It is not synonymous with destruction of all host ribosomes, and it does not occur to the same extent in every infection. A cell undergoing host shutoff may still retain substantial translation machinery, although the distribution of its activity among messages may differ from the uninfected state.

A decrease in cellular protein synthesis is not sufficient evidence to conclude that cellular RNA has been destroyed. The cellular messages may still be present but may be poorly translated. Likewise, a decline in cellular RNA abundance does not establish that translation initiation has been directly inhibited. These alternatives concern separate stages of gene expression and can be distinguished conceptually by comparing RNA abundance with protein synthesis. A change in both quantities may still be difficult to assign to a unique cause, because loss of one molecular activity can indirectly affect the other.

Selective translation should also be distinguished from selective accumulation of proteins. A stable protein may accumulate even when its current synthesis rate is modest. A newly produced but rapidly degraded protein may remain scarce despite substantial translation. The amount of protein detected at a given time reflects production and removal. Thus observations of total protein abundance, rates of new protein synthesis and message abundance are complementary measurements rather than interchangeable proxies.

The general concept of host shutoff is relevant here because it demonstrates that viral expression occurs in a shared molecular environment. Host and viral messages draw on overlapping cellular resources, and changes in the cellular state can alter the outcome of gene expression. The detailed mechanisms by which particular pathogens interfere with host defence or cause disease belong to other topics; the important principle for this chapter is the separation of message production, message availability, translation and protein lifetime.

16. Why translation initiation is an important regulatory step

Translation initiation establishes the relationship between the ribosome and the coding message. The ribosome must be placed in an appropriate position so that the coding sequence is read in the correct frame. A change in the number of successful initiation events can alter the amount of protein synthesized even if the elongation process proceeds normally once initiation has occurred. The first stage of translation therefore supplies an important point of regulation for both cellular and viral messages.

In the familiar cap-dependent initiation scheme of eukaryotic cells, proteins recognize features associated with the five-prime end of an RNA and help recruit the small ribosomal subunit and its associated initiation machinery. The complex can scan the RNA to identify a suitable initiation site. Recognition depends on more than the presence of the three nucleotides of a start codon. The local molecular context and availability of the RNA influence whether initiation occurs. This is why the number of start codons in a message is not equivalent to the number of proteins that will be made.

Some messages are translated through routes that differ from the common cap-dependent scheme. An internal ribosome entry site, abbreviated IRES, is an RNA element associated with initiation that does not depend on the usual requirement for recruitment from a capped five-prime end. The details of the relevant factor requirements differ among systems. The term should not be interpreted to mean that the RNA synthesizes protein without a ribosome or that every cellular initiation factor is dispensable. It describes an alternative relationship between the message and the initiation machinery.

Different kinds of alternative initiation must not be conflated. Internal recruitment and the scanning of a message from its end are distinct concepts. A failure to initiate at one possible start codon may allow another start to be used in some scanning contexts, while internal recruitment involves a different route of positioning the machinery. The fact that a protein originates from a downstream coding region does not, by itself, establish an internal ribosome entry mechanism. Subgenomic RNA production, RNA processing or other expression routes can also present a downstream coding region for translation. Evidence is needed to distinguish the molecular route.

The three-prime end of an RNA can also be relevant to its expression. A polyadenylated end and its associated proteins can contribute to the stability or translation of many cellular messages. However, not every biologically functional RNA has a conventional polyadenylated end. The absence of a particular end feature does not permit an immediate conclusion that the message cannot be translated. The question must be framed in terms of the actual requirements of the message and the cellular machinery involved. Broad variation in the structures of viral messages makes this point especially clear.

It is useful to distinguish initiation from elongation when interpreting protein synthesis measurements. If fewer ribosomes begin translation per unit time, the total number of completed products can decline. If elongation is slow, a message may have ribosomes associated with it for longer while the production of completed chains decreases. Therefore, a high occupancy of a message by ribosomes is not always equivalent to a high output of completed protein. This general limitation applies to the interpretation of cellular translation measurements as well as to studies of viral gene expression.

A student should also recognize the limits of the term selective translation. It does not mean that the genetic code changes to favor a particular genome, and it does not necessarily imply that every other message is completely excluded from the translation machinery. It describes relative differences in the use of messages. Because initiation, elongation and message lifetime all influence protein production, the observation that one protein is synthesized in larger quantities than another does not identify a unique translation-control mechanism. Identification of the relevant stage requires a more specific observation.

17. Quantitative reasoning: distinguishing abundance from flux

A simple mathematical model makes the distinction between RNA production and RNA abundance precise. Let M represent the average number of molecules of a specified messenger RNA in a cell. Let s represent the rate of production of that message, measured in molecules per cell per unit time. Let d be a first-order removal constant, measured in inverse time. If the RNA population is well represented by a single constant removal probability per unit time, its rate of change is dM/dt = s − dM. The product of the constant d and the abundance M is a removal rate, in molecules per cell per unit time, rather than a dimensionless fraction.

When the production and removal rates are equal, M remains constant and the model gives M = s/d. This is a steady state, not a condition in which all molecular activity has stopped. Individual molecules can still be produced and lost. The model assumes approximately constant production and first-order loss over the interval being considered, and it ignores detailed compartmentalization and discrete fluctuations. These assumptions are useful in a hypothetical data problem but are not universal properties of infected cells.

Suppose an abstract RNA species A has a production rate of 30 molecules per minute and a removal constant of 0.3 per minute. Its predicted steady abundance is 100 molecules. If another RNA species B has a production rate of 15 molecules per minute and a removal constant of 0.15 per minute, its predicted steady abundance is also 100 molecules. Equal abundance therefore does not imply equal production rates. The two species differ in turnover and will not necessarily respond at the same speed when their production rates change. These values are hypothetical illustrations of mass balance, not measurements of a virus.

If the production of an RNA stops completely, the same model becomes dM/dt = −dM. Integration gives M(t) = M(0)e^(−dt), with t measured in the time unit reciprocal to d. The half-life is obtained by setting M(t) equal to half its initial value, giving t half = ln(2)/d. For d = 0.3 per minute, the half-life is approximately 2.31 minutes. For d = 0.15 per minute it is approximately 4.62 minutes. A message that persists for longer after production ceases may support continued protein production, provided translation remains functional.

Protein output can be represented by a separate balance. Let P be the amount of a protein, k the average protein production rate per available messenger and g a first-order protein removal constant. Then dP/dt = kM − gP. Even if two messages have identical abundance, the resulting protein concentrations may differ because k or g differs. This model explains why a comparison of early and late protein amounts cannot by itself establish a transcriptional switch. A student should first identify which quantities have been measured and which remain unknown.

For another hypothetical comparison, assume two groups of cells contain 40 and 80 copies of an abstract DNA template, respectively. Their newly produced RNA counts during the same short observation interval are 120 and 240. The normalized output is three RNA molecules per template in both groups during the interval. Total RNA output is twice as high in the second group, but the data do not demonstrate a change in average output per template. This distinction helps interpret temporal changes in expression without confusing increasing genome copy number with direct transcriptional activation.

18. Interpreting temporal expression curves and regulatory models

A temporal expression diagram is most informative when its vertical axis is identified precisely. A plot of messenger RNA molecules per cell differs from a plot of newly synthesized RNA per minute. A plot of protein concentration differs from a plot of protein synthesis rate. These quantities have different physical units, so they cannot be compared as if they measure the same property. Before interpreting the shape of an early or late curve, first determine which variable is shown.

A curve can increase because an input becomes larger, because removal becomes smaller or because both happen together. This principle follows from conservation of material. If an RNA population gains molecules faster than it loses them, its abundance rises. If losses exceed production, abundance falls. A peak in abundance occurs when the net rate changes from positive to negative, passing through zero in a smooth model. It does not necessarily occur when production is maximal. This is why the time of greatest RNA abundance and the time of greatest RNA synthesis can differ.

An early RNA curve and a late RNA curve may cross. At the crossing point the two plotted abundances are equal if they share an absolute scale. The crossing point does not imply equal transcription rates, equal degradation rates, equal translation efficiency or equal biological importance. If the curves were separately normalized to their own maximum values, even equality of absolute abundance cannot be inferred. Normalized plots are useful for comparing relative changes over time but can conceal differences in total amounts.

Consider a hypothetical illustration with an early RNA that rises from zero to a relative value of 100 and then declines to 20. A late RNA remains initially low, rises to 100 on its own normalized scale and remains high during the later observation. The different shapes support temporal differences in relative RNA abundance. They do not demonstrate that both RNAs reached the same absolute concentration. The normalized peak for each RNA corresponds to its own maximum, which could represent very different molecule counts. An examination problem that omits the normalization method is insufficient for an absolute quantitative comparison.

A change in template abundance is particularly relevant to DNA-virus expression. Let N be the number of available templates and a the average number of completed transcripts produced per template per unit time. Under a simple independent-template approximation, total production is s = Na. The units of a are transcripts per template per minute if time is measured in minutes, and the units of s are transcripts per minute. This model describes a decomposition of total production, not a universal law that every template is identical. The average a can itself depend on cellular state and template accessibility.

For a generic numerical example, a group with ten templates each producing an average of four messages per minute has a total rate of 40 messages per minute. A second group with twenty templates each producing two messages per minute has the same total rate. The increased template number is exactly offset by reduced average activity. Conversely, a doubling of total production can occur through doubling N at constant a, doubling a at constant N or through several intermediate combinations. A measurement of total output cannot uniquely determine the factors without additional information.

This decomposition also clarifies why increasing numbers of viral genomes do not necessarily produce proportional amounts of a particular transcript. Some templates may not be transcriptionally available, and the cellular machinery is not unlimited. A simple proportional relation is useful only when the average activity per counted template remains comparable. If many nucleic acid copies are physically unsuitable for the reaction, total genome counts may overestimate the number of active templates. In a hypothetical exercise, it is important to state whether the count represents all genome molecules or the subset available to the expression system.

The timing of protein accumulation introduces another delay. A message must be translated before its product appears, and its protein products may persist after the message has declined. In a minimal model the protein pool integrates the history of RNA availability and translation over time. A late peak in protein abundance can therefore be a consequence of earlier synthesis and slower removal rather than evidence that the corresponding transcription event occurred at the same late time. Measurements of rates and stocks answer different questions.

A comparison between early and late programs is consequently best framed as a collection of linked observations. Which messages become available at different stages? Which protein products are newly made? How do template availability and cellular conditions change? Are the plotted values absolute or normalized? These questions are not specific to virology. They are the same questions that arise when analyzing cell differentiation, responses to signaling and changes in cellular metabolism. The viral context provides a useful example of how the distinctions can be integrated in a single biological system.

19. Limits of simple models and the role of cellular resources

The mass balance models used in the preceding sections are deliberately simple. A population of messenger RNA molecules in a real cell may be distributed among several states. Some molecules may be in the nucleus, some in the cytoplasm and some associated with proteins in complexes. These populations need not have equal access to ribosomes or equal rates of removal. A single first-order loss constant is an approximation that represents an average behavior over a chosen interval.

The use of a constant translation coefficient likewise assumes that each relevant message makes protein at an approximately constant average rate. This approximation can fail when the availability of ribosomes, amino acids or cellular energy changes. Translation is a resource-dependent molecular process. Ribosomal movement and the associated reactions use energy, and the supply of charged transfer RNAs depends on cellular metabolism. In a virus-infected cell, the availability of the necessary resources can change over time. Thus the rate per message cannot be assumed constant simply because the underlying coding sequence is unchanged.

A distinction must also be made between a protein being synthesized and a protein being functional. Newly synthesized proteins may require folding or association with other molecules. A processing step may be needed before a mature product is available. A protein may be present but inactive because a necessary binding partner is missing. The simple concentration variable P does not distinguish these states unless the model explicitly defines it as a measure of functional protein. An activity assay and a measurement of total protein can therefore provide different information even when both are accurate.

The availability of cellular machinery is one reason why an extracellular virus particle does not continuously make all of its encoded proteins. Genetic information alone cannot sustain the chemical reactions involved in expression. Without a suitable system for nucleotide synthesis, RNA production, aminoacylation, ribosomal decoding and energy supply, possession of a nucleic acid sequence does not result in autonomous biosynthesis. This limitation is a central feature of viruses and should not be confused with the fact that some viral genomes encode several enzymes of their own.

The host environment is not constant across different organisms or different cell types. Plant and animal cells share the central principles of eukaryotic translation, but their physiological organization and regulatory systems differ. Even cells in one organism can have different metabolic states and levels of particular cellular proteins. A broad viral genome class therefore cannot predict every expression outcome in every possible cellular environment. The relationship between genome information and gene output is conditional on the biological context.

A mathematical model used for examination reasoning must identify its domain of validity. If a problem states that the removal constant is fixed, a calculation of steady abundance from production divided by removal is appropriate. If production and removal both vary continuously, the same steady-state expression may not describe the observed time series. If the system has not reached steady state, dividing the instantaneous production rate by the loss constant gives a predicted steady amount under the assumed conditions, not necessarily the actual current amount. Such qualifications are not unnecessary technicalities. They determine what the calculation actually means.

20. Predictive reasoning at different levels of an expression pathway

An examination question often presents a hypothetical change in one component of a molecular pathway and asks for the likely outcome. The key is to establish the order of dependence among the steps. If production of a messenger RNA is required before synthesis of a protein, the absence of that message prevents new protein synthesis through that route. If the protein already exists, however, its immediate disappearance does not necessarily follow. The remaining amount depends on its lifetime and other removal processes. The same reasoning applies to many pathways in cell biology.

Consider an abstract system in which DNA-directed transcription generates a precursor RNA, processing converts the precursor into a mature message, and the message is translated into a protein. If the total amount of precursor remains unchanged but mature RNA becomes less abundant, a change at the processing stage is one possible explanation. Increased removal of mature RNA is another. If precursor production and removal have both changed in compensating ways, an unchanged precursor pool could conceal an altered transcription rate. The problem must specify whether the measurements represent amounts or production rates before a unique conclusion is justified.

A different hypothetical observation is that the amounts of precursor and mature message remain comparable, but the rate of appearance of newly synthesized protein declines. This pattern points toward a problem at or downstream of message utilization rather than immediately supporting a transcriptional explanation. It does not establish that initiation is the specific step affected, since slower completion of chains or changes in message accessibility could also contribute. If only total protein abundance declines, accelerated removal becomes an additional possibility. The most specific justified conclusion depends on the measurement provided.

The concept of a loss of function is distinct from the loss of a molecule. An enzyme may be present in normal quantity but unable to catalyze the reaction required in the system. Conversely, a modest reduction in its abundance may produce little change in output if the available activity remains greater than the requirement. Thus an immunological measurement of protein quantity is not equivalent to a direct measurement of catalytic activity. This distinction is especially useful when interpreting hypothetical mutations in regulatory proteins or polymerases at the level of a written examination question.

A cis-regulatory effect is associated with the nucleic acid molecule on which the relevant sequence acts. A trans-acting factor is a diffusible or otherwise separately produced molecular activity that can act on an appropriate target. These terms describe the relationship between a regulatory element and its target, not simply the chemical distinction between DNA and protein. In compact genomes, RNA sequences can also have cis functions. The question of whether a regulatory activity can act on another molecule must be answered from the mechanism, rather than assumed from the identity of the molecule as nucleic acid or protein.

Care must be taken when using the words required and sufficient. An activity is required for a defined outcome if that outcome cannot occur through the specified pathway without it. This does not imply that the activity alone is sufficient to generate the outcome. An RNA polymerase cannot produce a particular transcript without an appropriate template and nucleotide substrates. A mature coding RNA cannot produce a polypeptide without translation machinery. Several required components may jointly support an output, so showing that one component is necessary does not show that it is the only determinant.

This distinction also clarifies interpretation of comparisons between host-dependent and virus-encoded activities. The presence of an encoded polymerase can establish the potential for a particular type of RNA synthesis, but it does not establish that the enzyme is currently active or that all other requirements are satisfied. In a generalized expression scheme, the correct answer to a question about blocking one activity should identify the immediate product of that activity and then reason about dependent downstream products. It should not jump from the molecular change to a prediction about a whole organism without additional evidence.

The purpose of this form of reasoning is to identify the limits of an inference. A well-constructed analytical question may include more than one biologically plausible explanation and ask which is supported by the data. The answer can be that no unique mechanism is identified. Students who learn to distinguish an observation, its immediate molecular interpretation and its broader biological implications are better prepared for unfamiliar questions than students who memorize a list of virus names and expected outputs.

Consider first a representative nuclear DNA-expression scheme in which host DNA-directed RNA synthesis is the source of new viral messages. Loss of that transcriptional activity prevents the formation of additional messages through this route. It does not immediately destroy all existing mature messages, so translation of an already available message may continue for some period if the necessary machinery remains functional. The distinction between production and persistence explains why a transcriptional block and an immediate decline in every protein are not equivalent predictions. The outcome is also not identical to blocking all cellular translation, which prevents formation of new polypeptides even when intact messages remain.

In the elementary negative-sense RNA-expression scheme, the initial RNA-to-RNA transcriptional activity has a different template and product relationship from host DNA-directed transcription. The source of the required messenger must be identified from the pathway. If the relevant RNA-directed synthesis is absent in the idealized scheme, the negative-sense template cannot supply its corresponding new positive-sense messenger through that route. A loss of translation, in contrast, affects the utilization of an available message. These are qualitative deductions about the order of the information-flow steps; they do not establish specific responses to real substances or quantitative effects in particular viruses.

The same distinction can be drawn when comparing two translation products formed through different expression routes. If a larger precursor polypeptide is detectable but its mature product is not, incomplete conversion or subsequent loss of that product is consistent with the observation. If a shorter expression RNA is missing while a longer RNA that contains the same sequence is detected, the difference may concern the production or lifetime of the shorter RNA. These patterns call attention to the particular intermediate that has been measured. An examination answer should avoid using the general phrase expression failed when the evidence permits a more precise description of which molecular form is absent.

21. Reading scientific evidence without overinterpreting it

A time series provides information about when a molecule becomes detectable and how its amount changes. It does not automatically provide the causal relationships among molecules that appear at successive times. If an early protein appears before a late RNA, the ordering is compatible with a role for that protein in the later expression program. The ordering alone is insufficient to prove that the protein directly regulates the late RNA. Both might depend on a third variable that changes over time, such as genome availability or the general state of the cell.

Consider an abstract teaching dataset in which an early RNA is readily detected before a late RNA, followed by an increase in late protein. The first observation indicates an earlier appearance of the first measured RNA relative to the second. The second is consistent with a later increase in translation from the late message. Without measurements of protein lifetime and rates of new synthesis, however, the accumulation of the late protein might also reflect a change in its stability. A careful interpretation states the observations first and then identifies possible mechanisms in decreasing order of direct support.

The interpretation of RNA hybridization depends on which nucleic acid species is detected. A complementary probe may recognize sequences shared by more than one RNA molecule. Thus a signal for a given coding region might include full-length genomic RNA, a precursor and a shorter message, depending on the system. Separation by molecular size or identification of a characteristic processing junction can help distinguish RNA species conceptually. An examination problem that gives only a single total signal should not be read as if every individual RNA species had been measured separately.

The classic adenovirus observations provide an example in which molecular geometry furnished evidence more specific than a total RNA measurement. The discontinuity between mature RNA and genomic DNA suggested removal of intervening RNA segments. The conclusion was strengthened by the ability to account for separated regions of sequence correspondence. Nevertheless, the evidence for RNA splicing is a distinct matter from the question of what controls the relative abundance of different splice products during viral gene expression. A structural observation and a regulatory explanation are related, but the second does not follow in full from the first.

Control comparisons matter whenever a change in an observed output is attributed to a particular process. If every message, including ordinary cellular messages, changes in the same direction, a global change in the cell's physiological state must be considered. If one RNA class changes while another remains stable, the observation is consistent with a more selective event, but the relevant level of regulation still has to be identified. Appropriate normalization and clear definitions of what is measured are essential for distinguishing a change in total material from a change in a particular molecular process.

The main lesson for examination reasoning is to avoid treating the first plausible mechanism as the only possible mechanism. The correct conclusion may be that the available measurements do not distinguish transcriptional regulation from altered RNA stability. A quantitative result can be exact within a model while the biological interpretation remains uncertain. Recognizing that difference is part of scientific mastery rather than a weakness in the answer.

22. Independent measurements and the hierarchy of scientific conclusions

The molecular measurements commonly discussed in gene-expression studies address different properties of nucleic acids and proteins. A hybridization signal reports the presence of molecules capable of interacting with a complementary probe under the stated conditions. A measurement of RNA size gives information about the length of the detected molecules. A measurement associated with a particular junction can distinguish a processed RNA from a molecule lacking that junction. None of these measurements directly establishes the amount of functional protein produced.

A protein measurement likewise requires a clear definition of its target. A signal recognizing a protein sequence could reflect the mature product, a larger precursor that contains the same sequence or a smaller fragment that retains it. If these forms are not distinguished, a combined signal may be misleading. In the abstract polyprotein example, a larger band and two smaller bands might represent a precursor and processed products. The observation must be interpreted in relation to the expected molecular identities, not only the total signal intensity. Protein size alone is not conclusive evidence for the sequence or activity of the molecule.

Comparisons of molecular signals often use a reference to account for differences in the amount of material analyzed. The choice of reference matters. A reference RNA whose abundance changes under the condition being studied cannot automatically be assumed to remain a constant denominator. If the numerator and denominator both decline in equal proportion, their ratio can remain unchanged even though their absolute amounts have fallen. An apparently stable normalized signal can therefore conceal a global change. Conversely, a change in a ratio can arise from a change in the numerator, the denominator or both.

For example, suppose a purely hypothetical cell sample has 60 arbitrary units of target RNA and 30 units of a reference RNA. A second sample has 30 units of target and 15 units of reference. The target-to-reference ratio is two in both samples. A conclusion of unchanged absolute target abundance would be incorrect, since the stated target signal has fallen by half. If the measurement scales are comparable, the ratio establishes only the relative relationship to that reference. The example is a generic illustration of measurement logic and not a dataset from a viral infection.

Measurements made from a group of cells may also conceal differences among individual cells. An average of 100 molecules per cell could arise from relatively uniform expression or from a mixture of cells with high expression and cells with little detectable expression. A gradual change in the population mean does not prove that each individual cell changes gradually. In some contexts, the changing proportion of cells in different expression states can contribute to the time series. A question based only on population averages should not be interpreted as direct evidence about every individual cell.

Finally, correlation between two expression products is not the same as evidence that one directly controls the other. A common upstream condition can affect both. The order of their appearance may suggest a hypothesis, but temporal ordering does not identify a binding interaction or a direct causal connection. The quality of an explanation depends on the match between the type of evidence and the type of claim. This is particularly important for broad models of early and late viral expression, because several cellular and viral processes can change during the same interval.

The historical examples in this chapter show how molecular evidence can overcome these ambiguities when the observation specifically addresses the question. Discontinuous RNA–DNA correspondence supplied evidence for RNA processing; hereditary properties that followed RNA supplied evidence about genetic information. These are stronger than a statement that two molecular quantities were merely correlated. When reading a new data problem, identify the exact relationship that was measured and ask whether the proposed conclusion requires an additional unobserved step.

23. Comparative biology: shared cellular constraints and different genomic solutions

Plant and animal cells belong to different multicellular lineages, but their basic cytoplasmic translation systems are homologous. Both use eukaryotic ribosomes and the general codon-based relationship between messenger RNA and amino acid sequence. The genetic code is substantially conserved across organisms, with specific exceptions that are not relevant to the standard cytoplasmic examples here. The conserved relationship explains why a viral expression strategy can be described using the same broad biochemical vocabulary in plant and animal systems.

The physical organization of the host nevertheless matters. Plant cells have a wall surrounding their plasma membrane and communicate with neighboring cells through specialized channels known as plasmodesmata. Animal cells do not have plant cell walls or plasmodesmata. A protein described as a movement protein in a plant virus has a function in this distinct biological environment. The expression of such a protein belongs to the same general process of RNA production and translation as the expression of a coat protein, but its physiological role is different. The molecular properties of the gene product must be interpreted in relation to the host in which it acts.

The contrast between TMV and other plant RNA viruses illustrates diversity within one broad genome category. Members of the plant virus family Potyviridae use a major polyprotein as part of their expression strategy. TMV provides a familiar example in which shorter RNA messages allow the expression of particular downstream genes. These examples both involve positive-sense RNA and cellular translation, yet they differ in the stage at which separate protein products become available. This is evidence against the mistaken idea that one type of inherited nucleic acid implies one unique way of organizing every gene.

The comparison with adenovirus is equally instructive. In the representative adenovirus scheme, information is transcribed from DNA and processed to produce a collection of mature RNA messages. In the representative TMV scheme, additional RNA species are synthesized from viral RNA templates rather than by the ordinary nuclear DNA-directed transcription pathway. Both systems can make multiple messages, but the chemical template and the means of generating those messages differ. The similarity in final output should not obscure the differences in mechanism.

These strategies illustrate the relationship between coding capacity and regulatory complexity. A compact genome may encode multiple functions without devoting a separate large regulatory region and independently inherited molecule to each final product. Overlapping coding regions, processing alternatives and precursor molecules are different ways in which a limited nucleotide sequence can give rise to a variety of expression products. This does not mean that all viruses have very small genomes or that compactness alone determines their regulatory strategy. Genome sizes and expression arrangements vary widely. A comparison must be made at the level of the particular mechanism.

The evolutionary interpretation also requires caution. Similar regulatory strategies can arise in distinct biological lineages because they address comparable molecular constraints. The existence of positive-sense RNA in two viruses does not by itself establish that they share a recent common ancestor. Conversely, viruses that share aspects of molecular ancestry may differ in the details of their expression programs. The Baltimore classes describe routes of information flow rather than a fully resolved evolutionary tree. Classification by mechanism and classification by ancestry serve different purposes.

The study of viral expression has broader importance beyond knowledge of individual viruses. Viral model systems helped uncover cellular processes such as splicing and provided examples of the flexibility of RNA organization. Understanding their expression strategies also provides a foundation for interpreting research on the biological actions of antiviral interventions and host responses at a conceptual level. However, the study of therapeutic development, disease severity, detailed host defence and viral transmission belongs to other syllabus units. The present chapter retains the molecular focus needed to interpret regulation of RNA and protein production.

24. Connecting viral expression to cellular gene control

The molecular principles considered in this chapter are not restricted to viruses. Cellular genes also depend on template accessibility, polymerase specificity, RNA maturation and the regulation of translation. Viral systems make certain distinctions especially clear because their genomes vary in chemical form and have a restricted dependence on cellular resources. The requirement to generate a readable RNA is shared by protein-coding genes across these systems, even when the path to that RNA differs.

The temporal dimension illustrates a general aspect of gene regulation. A gene product may be needed before another process can occur, creating an ordered relationship among molecular activities. Other products may be required in greater quantities later. The amounts of the relevant RNAs and proteins reflect the rates of synthesis and removal at each stage. This provides a framework for comparing early and late expression without presuming that every system follows an identical sequence or that all observed changes occur at the level of transcription.

RNA processing illustrates another general principle: the sequence of a mature message can differ from the continuous sequence represented by a DNA template. By varying processing outcomes, biological systems can change the distribution of mature messages produced from a precursor. By changing translation or protein lifetime, they can alter the final output even when those messages remain unchanged. Each molecular layer can contribute independently, and each layer is constrained by the properties of the molecules and enzymes involved.

The comparison between plant and animal viruses also teaches the limits of generalization. Host type does not uniquely determine the genome-expression class. Genome polarity does not determine every detail of RNA processing or translation. The presence of an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase does not imply that all RNA products have the same role. A short viral RNA is not necessarily a separate genome segment. Each statement should be evaluated with reference to the relevant molecular form and biological task.

These principles lead naturally to the study of eukaryotic transcriptional regulation in the next curriculum node. A cellular genome contains regulatory DNA elements and genes whose expression varies across conditions. The central distinction between the amount of a DNA template and the activity of that template will remain important. The viral examples have shown why a promoter, a mature RNA and a translated protein must be considered separate levels of the same information-flow system.

Part C — Analytical practice

Original practice questions. All numerical datasets are hypothetical.

21. An abstract RNA is produced at 48 molecules/min and removed with a first-order constant of 0.4/min. What is the predicted steady amount?

  1. 19.2 molecules.
  2. 48 molecules.
  3. 120 molecules.
  4. 0.0083 molecules.
Answer and explanation

C. 120 molecules. At steady state s = dM, hence M = 48/0.4 = 120 molecules. Multiplication or division in the opposite order has the wrong dimensions.

22. Two samples have target/reference signals 80/40 and 40/20. Which inference is justified?

  1. The target amount is unchanged.
  2. The ratio is unchanged but the target signal is halved.
  3. The second ratio is half the first.
  4. The reference increased twofold.
Answer and explanation

B. The ratio is unchanged but the target signal is halved. Both ratios equal 2; the numerator and denominator each halve. A stable ratio is not evidence for stable absolute abundance.

23. An abstract RNA has a half-life of 3 min. Its production stops at 160 molecules. What is the modeled amount after 6 min?

  1. 80 molecules.
  2. 20 molecules.
  3. 120 molecules.
  4. 40 molecules.
Answer and explanation

D. 40 molecules. Six minutes equals two half-lives, giving 160 × (1/2)^2 = 40. The exponential model does not subtract a fixed number each minute.

24. Samples contain 20 and 50 templates and produce 100 and 250 new RNAs in the same interval. Which is supported?

  1. Average output per counted template is equal.
  2. The second template is 2.5 times as active.
  3. Transcription has stopped in the first sample.
  4. The second sample necessarily has slower RNA removal.
Answer and explanation

A. Average output per counted template is equal. The normalized production is 5 transcripts per template in both. Removal is not specified and total counts alone do not show activation.

25. A common precursor yields 90 molecules of X and 30 of Y. Assuming these are newly formed products and there are no other outcomes, what fraction is X?

  1. 0.25.
  2. 0.75.
  3. 0.33.
  4. 3.0.
Answer and explanation

B. 0.75. There are 120 total outcomes; 90/120 = 0.75. The ratio X/Y is 3, not the fraction of all outcomes.

26. Two mature RNAs are produced at 18 and 6 molecules/min and removed with constants of 0.3 and 0.1/min. What are their steady amounts?

  1. 60 and 60 molecules.
  2. 6 and 18 molecules.
  3. 54 and 60 molecules.
  4. 18 and 6 molecules.
Answer and explanation

A. 60 and 60 molecules. Divide each production rate by its own removal constant. Equal stocks conceal a threefold production difference.

27. A coding exon of 12 nucleotides is skipped in a hypothetical message without altering the reading-frame start. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The frame must shift by one nucleotide.
  2. The downstream protein is necessarily fully functional.
  3. The downstream reading frame remains aligned, but function need not be preserved.
  4. The protein loses twelve amino acids.
Answer and explanation

C. The downstream reading frame remains aligned, but function need not be preserved. Twelve nucleotides are four codons. Frame preservation does not establish folding or activity.

  1. 25 of each.
  2. 225 of each.
  3. 75 products in total.
  4. 75 of each.
Answer and explanation

D. 75 of each. Each precursor yields one copy of each region, giving 225 molecules total. Equal initial yield does not guarantee equal later abundance.

  1. A change in message utilization or chain production is consistent with the data.
  2. Transcription must have stopped.
  3. Every message has been destroyed.
  4. Protein degradation is proven to be the sole cause.
Answer and explanation

A. A change in message utilization or chain production is consistent with the data. The rate of newly synthesized protein is distinct from total protein abundance. Stable RNA does not imply normal translation.

30. A mature RNA signal contains a junction between exon A and exon C that are separated in genomic DNA. Which process best accounts for the stated relationship?

  1. Direct translation of genomic DNA.
  2. Protein hydrolysis.
  3. A change in the electrical charge of RNA.
  4. RNA processing that removes intervening sequence.
Answer and explanation

D. RNA processing that removes intervening sequence. A splice junction is a nucleotide-level relation. Protein processing cannot create such a junction in RNA.

31. A sample contains 10 templates at 6 RNAs/min/template. A second has 15 templates at 4 RNAs/min/template. What are the production rates?

  1. 6 and 4 RNAs/min.
  2. 60 and 60 RNAs/min.
  3. 16 and 19 RNAs/min.

B. 60 and 60 RNAs/min. Multiplying template number by average output gives equal rates. Neither per-template rate alone is total output.

  2. Their production rates are identical.

C. Both are at half of their own maxima. Separate normalization removes the information needed for direct absolute comparison.

  2. 24 molecules.
  3. 12 molecules.
  4. 120 molecules.
Answer and explanation

D. 120 molecules. The same mass-balance relation gives P = 24/0.2 = 120. The units are molecules, not molecules per minute.

34. A sample has a larger nuclear RNA pool but the same cytoplasmic RNA pool as a control. Which inference is defensible?

  1. The total protein output must double.
  2. Nuclear RNA is directly decoded by ordinary cytoplasmic ribosomes.
  3. The total RNA increase alone does not establish increased cytoplasmic translation.

C. The total RNA increase alone does not establish increased cytoplasmic translation. Compartment-specific availability matters; an increase in nuclear RNA need not increase the pool accessible to cytoplasmic ribosomes.

  4. 4:1.

A. 2:1. P = synthesis/removal. Halving removal doubles abundance under the specified assumptions.

  3. The molecule has a positive net charge.

B. Coding polarity alone does not guarantee translation. Sequence orientation and physical access are independent properties.

37. An early RNA appears before a late RNA in a time series. Which claim is unsupported by ordering alone?

  1. The first RNA is detected earlier in the reported series.
  2. The early RNA directly activates transcription of the late RNA.
  3. The two RNA profiles differ in timing.
  4. Further evidence is required for a causal mechanism.
Answer and explanation

B. The early RNA directly activates transcription of the late RNA. Temporal precedence is compatible with causation but does not prove a direct regulatory interaction.

38. A hypothetical population has 50 cells with 200 copies of a message and 50 with zero. What is the mean?

  1. 200 copies/cell.
  2. 50 copies/cell.
  3. 0 copies/cell.
  4. 100 copies/cell.
Answer and explanation

D. 100 copies/cell. The total is 10,000 over 100 cells. The average does not represent the count in either cell class.

39. A protein-reactive measurement detects a precursor and a processed product without distinguishing them. Which inference is unsafe?

  1. The signal can include more than one molecular form.
  2. Size information may help distinguish forms.

  4. The signal alone does not establish activity.
Answer and explanation

C. All measured signal represents mature functional protein. Shared sequence features can cause the same assay to detect both forms; biological activity requires additional evidence.

40. In an abstract pathway, a protein is normally produced from a mature RNA. New protein production ceases immediately, but the pre-existing pool remains. Which property accounts for persistence?

Answer and explanation

A. Production and removal rates are momentarily equal. dM/dt = 0 implies s = dM; both rates may be nonzero.

50. Integrated reasoning: which pair of observations most directly distinguishes a difference in protein synthesis from a difference in protein accumulation?

  1. Genome polarity and cell-wall composition.
  2. Rate of new chain production and total protein amount.
  3. Only a single total RNA measurement.
  4. Only the position of a gene on a genome map.
Answer and explanation

B. Rate of new chain production and total protein amount. Comparing flux with stock helps distinguish production from retention; the other options do not measure the required protein variables.

54. A cell population contains a nuclear DNA-derived transcript and an unrelated already available cytoplasmic coding RNA. Suppose DNA-directed transcription stops but translation initially remains functional. Which immediate statement is valid?

Answer and explanation

Examination traps

All viruses encode DNA.
Some have inherited RNA genomes; distinguish genetic material from the form used for expression. Why the confusion occurs: Students often generalize from cellular heredity to all genetic systems. Examination angle: Ask which genome type supplies the inherited information in a representative RNA virus.
Positive RNA is electrically positive.
Polarity describes sequence orientation, not the phosphate backbone's charge. Why the confusion occurs: The words positive and negative are familiar from electrochemistry. Examination angle: Test whether an RNA with positive polarity still has a negatively charged backbone.
All positive-sense genomes are immediately translated in the same way.
Sequence polarity and physical accessibility are distinct; reverse-transcribing RNA viruses also have a different initial route. Why the confusion occurs: Textbook diagrams often compress the first translation step into a single arrow. Examination angle: Ask whether coding polarity alone establishes access to the ribosome.
Every RNA polymerase can read any nucleic acid template.
Enzyme names specify template and product; recognition and catalysis are substrate dependent. Why the confusion occurs: Enzymes are sometimes named only by the product, obscuring template specificity. Examination angle: Give RNA and DNA templates and ask which activity yields the stated product.
All DNA viruses use host nuclear Pol II.
Adenovirus is representative, not universal; some DNA viruses use cytoplasmic viral transcription machinery. Why the confusion occurs: A common nuclear DNA-virus example is mistaken for a universal rule. Examination angle: Compare a nuclear DNA-virus expression scheme with a cytoplasmic DNA-virus scheme.
A viral genome is identical to its mature message.
Processing and production of shorter messages can alter the molecular relationship. Why the confusion occurs: Genomic RNA and messenger RNA may have the same polarity, which obscures their different roles. Examination angle: Ask whether a detected coding-region sequence identifies a full-length genome or a shorter message.
A late gene must lie at the end of a genome.
Late refers to temporal expression rather than sequence coordinate. Why the confusion occurs: Linear gene diagrams can be mistaken for temporal timelines. Examination angle: Give two promoter-expression profiles rather than gene positions and ask which is early.
A rise in total transcripts proves promoter activation.
More counted templates or slower removal can change abundance. Why the confusion occurs: Students often confuse total output with activity per template. Examination angle: Provide total output and template count and require normalization.
All early and late phases are mutually exclusive.
Expression patterns can overlap and vary with the system. Why the confusion occurs: Simplified textbook phases may be drawn as separate blocks. Examination angle: Provide overlapping curves and ask whether an early RNA must have disappeared.
Every exon encodes amino acids.
Exons can include untranslated sequences. Why the confusion occurs: Exon is often incorrectly used as a synonym for coding sequence. Examination angle: Ask whether a retained untranslated exon contributes amino acids.
RNA splicing changes the genomic DNA.
Splicing changes the RNA product. Why the confusion occurs: Removal of an intron is easily mistaken for deletion from DNA. Examination angle: Ask whether the genomic sequence must change after each splicing event.
A subgenomic RNA proves genome segmentation.
A shorter expression product need not be an independent inherited segment. Why the confusion occurs: Short RNA molecules can resemble physically separate genomic molecules. Examination angle: Ask whether a shorter expression product is necessarily inherited independently.
A polyprotein is a polycistronic RNA.
A continuous translated precursor is a polypeptide, not multiple independently initiated coding regions. Why the confusion occurs: Both concepts are associated with production of multiple proteins. Examination angle: Distinguish separate translation-initiation events from cleavage of one translated chain.
IRES-mediated initiation requires no ribosomes.
Alternative recruitment still uses ribosomal decoding. Why the confusion occurs: The phrase cap-independent is mistaken for machinery-independent. Examination angle: Ask which component remains essential for codon decoding.
Host shutoff destroys all host translation machinery.
The term refers to reduced output; different stages may be involved. Why the confusion occurs: The word shutoff sounds like total cessation of every process. Examination angle: Ask whether a decrease in host output establishes destruction of ribosomes.
A high protein concentration proves rapid ongoing synthesis.
Protein lifetime and prior production can account for accumulation. Why the confusion occurs: An accumulated protein is mistaken for a current synthesis measurement. Examination angle: Compare samples with different protein lifetimes and equal synthesis rates.
Equal normalized peaks prove equal abundance.
Separate normalization discards absolute scale information. Why the confusion occurs: A shared graph scale is mistakenly assumed to represent absolute amounts. Examination angle: Use curves separately normalized to their maxima and ask what equality means.
A synonymous substitution is always biologically silent.
The same nucleotides can have structural or other coding functions. Why the confusion occurs: The effect on one coding frame is confused with every possible molecular role of a nucleotide. Examination angle: Ask whether an unchanged amino acid sequence excludes an RNA structural consequence.

Revision and mastery

Every protein-producing viral expression pathway must make a message usable by cellular ribosomes. Genome form and polarity determine the upstream route, while RNA synthesis, processing, translation and product stability determine the final output. The temporal programs of representative DNA viruses and the shorter messages of TMV illustrate distinct solutions.

Essential equations

  • dM/dt = s − dM; M at steady state = s/d.
  • After production stops, M(t) = M(0)e^(−dt); half-life = ln(2)/d.
  • Total production s = Na for average output a per counted template N.
  • dP/dt = kM − gP; P at steady state = kM/g.
