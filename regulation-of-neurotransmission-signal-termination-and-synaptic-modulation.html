1. Why a chemical message must end

A motor neuron can cause a muscle fibre to contract and then allow it to relax. A sensory neuron can send a succession of messages that remain distinguishable from one another. Both examples depend on an often overlooked property of communication: a useful signal must have an ending. The arrival of an action potential at a nerve terminal explains why neurotransmitter release begins, but it does not explain why the receiving cell stops responding. Once the transmitter is outside the presynaptic cell, repolarizing that cell cannot directly retrieve molecules already released. Chemical signals require additional mechanisms to constrain their duration and spatial reach. Without these mechanisms, separate impulses could merge into a prolonged stimulus and the relationship between neuronal activity and its effect on a target would become unreliable.

A chemical synapse is an interface between a releasing cell and a responding cell. The presynaptic terminal contains release machinery, stores of transmitter and membrane proteins that control both processes. A narrow extracellular space, the synaptic cleft, separates the two membranes. The postsynaptic cell contains receptors whose activity depends on the local transmitter concentration. The term synaptic transmission refers to the transfer of information across that interface. Regulation includes processes that alter either the transmitter concentration or the relationship between concentration and response. Termination is a particular aspect of regulation: it describes how a signal ceases to act after it has served its immediate purpose. A mechanism that reduces the next release event is regulatory even if it does not remove any transmitter from the preceding event.

Imagine two terminals that release identical quantities of the same chemical at the same instant. One terminal is surrounded by cells that efficiently remove the chemical, whereas the other has poor clearance. The initial concentrations near their release sites may be similar. A short time later the concentrations diverge because removal differs. Receptors at the second synapse may remain exposed for longer, even though the releasing neuron did nothing unusual. Now consider a different comparison in which transmitter concentration is identical at two synapses but one postsynaptic membrane carries fewer receptors. The electrical responses differ because the receiving machinery differs. These comparisons establish two independent dimensions of control: availability of the chemical message and responsiveness of its target.

The duration of transmitter availability is also distinct from the duration of the electrical or biochemical response. A ligand may dissociate from a receptor before a channel closes completely, and a receptor that activates a G protein can initiate an intracellular process that persists beyond ligand binding. A postsynaptic voltage response may continue while the membrane capacitance discharges even when the synaptic current has ended. Conversely, sustained exposure can produce a falling current because receptors enter less responsive states. Thus extracellular concentration, receptor occupancy, ion current, membrane voltage and downstream biochemical effects are related variables, not interchangeable measurements. A time course must be interpreted in terms of the particular variable measured.

The idea of a precisely timed signal does not imply that every neurotransmitter is removed in a few milliseconds. Some synapses generate rapid, localized currents. Other release sites influence receptors outside the immediate apposition of pre- and postsynaptic membranes. Monoamines and peptides can act over longer intervals than a typical fast amino acid synapse. The physiological purpose determines the desirable temporal profile. A fast motor command needs a reliable association between impulses and muscle activation; a modulatory chemical can change the responsiveness of a network over a longer interval. Regulation means achieving an appropriate time course, not necessarily the fastest possible clearance.

A useful framework separates six causal events: production of transmitter, loading into a releasable compartment, stimulus-dependent release, binding to receptors, removal of extracellular transmitter and restoration of release competence. Each event has constraints imposed by the available molecules, membrane organization and energy supply. The extracellular chemical can be destroyed or transported away; the presynaptic cell can reduce further secretion; the postsynaptic cell can alter receptor function; and supporting glial cells can influence the chemical environment. These processes operate together rather than as a universal linear sequence. During repetitive stimulation, removal of one pulse overlaps preparation of the next pulse.

A synapse is therefore not simply a pipe that empties transmitter whenever electricity arrives. Its strength is a variable biological property. In experimental questions, a large response can mean more vesicles released, more transmitter per vesicle, increased receptor sensitivity, altered ion gradients or slower termination. A small response can arise from opposite changes or from a failure to replenish vesicles. Identifying which mechanism is responsible requires measurements that discriminate among these possibilities. The most useful habit is to ask whether a proposed perturbation changes the source, the extracellular path, the target or the recovery process, and then predict an independent observation that could distinguish the alternatives.

2. The transmitter cycle and the problem of replenishment

A transmitter molecule must be available inside a neuron before it can enter a vesicle. Small-molecule neurotransmitters are commonly made by enzymes that act on metabolic precursors in or near the nerve terminal. A neuron producing acetylcholine uses choline acetyltransferase to transfer an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to choline. A glutamatergic neuron can obtain glutamate through cellular metabolism and from glutamine supplied by other cells. GABAergic neurons synthesize gamma-aminobutyric acid from glutamate using glutamate decarboxylase, which requires pyridoxal phosphate. The identities of these enzymes matter because blocking production affects the supply of new transmitter, whereas blocking removal affects extracellular transmitter that has already been released. The early and late consequences of these perturbations therefore need not be the same.

The cytoplasm of a terminal and the interior of a synaptic vesicle are distinct compartments. Their separation allows a neuron to keep a pool of transmitter ready for release without maintaining a high extracellular concentration. Most small-molecule transmitters are accumulated by vesicular transport proteins powered indirectly by ATP. A vacuolar proton ATPase in the vesicle membrane hydrolyses ATP and transports protons into the vesicle lumen. The resulting proton electrochemical gradient has both a concentration component and an electrical component. Vesicular transporters exploit these components in different proportions. For example, vesicular monoamine and acetylcholine transporters depend strongly on proton exchange, while vesicular glutamate transport is strongly influenced by the electrical component of the gradient. It is incorrect to assume that every vesicular transporter exchanges identical numbers of ions in the same direction.

This compartmentalization makes it possible to distinguish transmitter uptake at the plasma membrane from packaging at a vesicle membrane. A plasma membrane transporter clears transmitter from extracellular space into a cell, using ion gradients across the cell surface. A vesicular transporter moves transmitter from the cytoplasm into a small intracellular compartment using the energy stored across the vesicle membrane. Both proteins are membrane transporters, but the direction relative to the cell and the energy source are different. A defect in vesicular loading can leave transmitter in the cytoplasm without supplying enough molecules to the releasable vesicle pool. A defect in extracellular uptake can prolong transmitter exposure even while vesicular loading initially remains normal.

When an action potential reaches the presynaptic membrane, voltage-dependent calcium channels open and calcium enters from the extracellular fluid. Local calcium elevation near a prepared vesicle raises the likelihood of membrane fusion. The earlier release chapter examines the machinery in detail; here the important consequence is that the neuron produces a brief source of extracellular molecules rather than a sustained leak through an open receptor. After repolarization, calcium channels close and pumps, exchangers and buffers lower free cytoplasmic calcium. This reduction decreases the drive for further fusion. Calcium removal from a terminal is not, however, removal of transmitter from a cleft. The first process controls secretion and the second controls receptor exposure. A question that mentions calcium chelation must be interpreted with that distinction in mind.

Once a vesicle fuses, its soluble contents can enter the extracellular space and move toward receptors. The number of molecules that reach any receptor depends on the initial release, the three-dimensional arrangement of membranes, diffusion, binding and competing removal mechanisms. The postsynaptic membrane does not ordinarily capture every released molecule permanently. Binding is reversible, and receptor activity can be initiated by molecules that later dissociate. Some dissociated molecules can bind again if they remain nearby. A rapid removal system limits opportunities for repeated binding and helps return the receptor population toward its resting distribution of states.

A neuronal terminal must also recover the membrane and proteins of vesicles that have fused. Endocytosis retrieves membrane material, which can return to the vesicle pool directly or through intermediate compartments. The recovered compartment must regain the appropriate internal acidity and transmitter content before it functions as a filled vesicle. Thus restoration of vesicle number and restoration of transmitter content are not identical. A membrane compartment may be present but functionally empty; alternatively, a normal filled vesicle may be unable to reach a release site because docking or priming is impaired. These distinctions become especially important when the first stimulus produces a normal response but later stimuli fail.

Neuropeptides follow a somewhat different economy. They originate from larger precursor proteins synthesized and processed in the secretory pathway, and dense-core vesicles carry the processed products toward release sites. The intact peptide is generally not recovered by the small-molecule plasma membrane transporters that handle amino acid or monoamine transmitters. Extracellular peptidases and diffusion can limit peptide signals, while replenishment depends on biosynthesis and trafficking. A peptide released together with a small-molecule transmitter may therefore exhibit a different time course and recovery pattern. It is not necessary for the two chemicals to have identical release thresholds or identical extracellular fates.

A terminal can operate effectively only when supply, utilization and recovery remain sufficiently matched to its activity. A resting terminal can accumulate reserves that conceal a slow production defect for a time. During sustained stimulation, those reserves may be consumed faster than they are restored, producing progressively smaller release events. The converse is also possible: slowing removal can temporarily enhance exposure while gradually impairing the recovery of transmitter precursors. The first response after a perturbation is not always representative of its steady-state effect. Biological reasoning should therefore distinguish acute observations, repetitive responses and recovery after a rest interval.

3. How experiments separated release, receptor action and clearance

The investigation of chemical signaling began with the problem of whether the influence of one cell on another could be conveyed by a transferable substance. Otto Loewi's work on frog hearts in 1921 provided a classical answer. Fluid associated with stimulation of a vagus nerve could slow another heart, supporting the existence of a diffusible mediator rather than a purely electrical influence. The mediator was subsequently identified with acetylcholine. This experiment established the chemical nature of the message, but it did not by itself resolve how long the substance remained active at individual synapses or how many molecules were released by each stimulus. Those questions required measurements on a much smaller spatial and temporal scale.

The neuromuscular junction became a particularly useful preparation because the target muscle fibre could be impaled with a microelectrode and its membrane voltage monitored while the associated motor nerve was stimulated. A nerve impulse produces an end-plate potential that may initiate a muscle action potential. At rest, small spontaneous depolarizations also occur. Katz and colleagues interpreted these miniature end-plate potentials as responses to discrete packets of transmitter. This explanation connected the electrical signal at the target with a probabilistic release process at the nerve ending. The frequency of miniature events and their average size provide different kinds of evidence: frequency concerns how often spontaneous packets are released, while amplitude reflects the response to a packet under the recording conditions.

A miniature event is not a direct count of individual acetylcholine molecules. It is an electrical effect resulting from the combined action of molecules released together, their subsequent movement and the response of receptors. If the transmitter is cleared more slowly, a miniature current can become longer without requiring more release events. If the postsynaptic receptors are reduced in number, the average miniature amplitude may decline while the presynaptic release probability remains unchanged. Such possibilities motivated experiments comparing transmitter release, receptor behavior and removal rather than treating the waveform as a complete description of one molecular process.

Katz and Miledi's 1973 paper, The binding of acetylcholine to receptors and its removal from the synaptic cleft, used measurements of miniature potentials and fluctuations associated with acetylcholine action to investigate these distinctions. Their study examined the consequences of suppressing acetylcholine hydrolysis. The important conclusion for the present chapter is that the lifetime of acetylcholine action depends on more than the time required for a freely diffusing molecule to leave an idealized open space. Interactions with receptors and the geometry of the synapse influence removal. Their evidence helps explain why eliminating an enzymatic route cannot always be modeled simply as an instantaneous transition to unrestricted diffusion. The precise experimental estimates from that preparation should not be applied indiscriminately to different synapses.

An experiment can be designed around the same logic using a hypothetical preparation. First record a reproducible postsynaptic current after a brief nerve stimulus. Then apply an intervention expected to reduce an extracellular degrading enzyme while holding stimulus strength, recording voltage and extracellular ions constant. If the falling phase of the current becomes slower, this observation is consistent with prolonged transmitter action. It does not prove that the enzyme alone determines decay, because the current depends on receptor kinetics and the intervention may have other effects. An additional measurement of extracellular transmitter, or a direct test of receptor responses to a brief controlled transmitter pulse, strengthens the interpretation. An untreated time-matched preparation distinguishes the intervention from deterioration during recording.

Inhibiting extracellular breakdown and preventing vesicle loading create especially informative contrasting predictions. Breakdown inhibition should primarily affect the lifetime of released transmitter while vesicles still contain their original transmitter. A loading defect can reduce the amount released by a subsequent vesicle without directly prolonging extracellular survival. A single peak-amplitude measurement may not separate them if both conditions also affect receptor occupancy. Measuring event frequency, quantal amplitude, current decay and recovery during repeated stimuli gives a more discriminating set of observations. These are general principles of experimental inference rather than an assertion that each intervention has identical effects at every synapse.

4. Diffusion, geometry and the spatial boundary of a message

Diffusion arises from random thermal motion of molecules. Although a single molecule can move in any direction, a group of molecules tends to spread away from a region of high concentration toward regions of lower concentration. For a synapse, the release event creates a local concentration gradient in extracellular fluid. Molecules move across the cleft and laterally beyond the region facing the release site. Diffusion alone redistributes molecules; it does not destroy them. A molecule leaving the cleft can reach a receptor outside the synapse, encounter a transporter or remain in the larger extracellular space. The terms clearance and degradation are therefore not synonyms.

Fick's first law expresses the diffusive flux in one dimension as J = −D dC/dx. Here J is amount per unit area per unit time, D is the diffusion coefficient with dimensions of area per unit time, and C is amount per unit volume. The negative sign indicates net movement down the concentration gradient. In three dimensions the corresponding gradient is a vector. A steep concentration gradient produces a larger net flux for a given D, but real boundaries complicate how concentration changes over time. There is no requirement for a molecule to travel along a straight path from release site to receptor.

For free diffusion in three dimensions, the mean squared displacement of a molecule is 6Dt. Its root mean square displacement is consequently the square root of 6Dt. This is an ensemble statistic, not a promised travel distance for each molecule. In a hypothetical aqueous medium with D = 0.5 square micrometres per millisecond, a time of 0.1 millisecond corresponds to a root mean square displacement of about 0.55 micrometres. Such an estimate illustrates why diffusion can be important over microscopic distances. It is not a measured synaptic clearance time. A narrow cleft has reflecting or interacting boundaries, receptors, enzymes and transporters, and its lateral escape geometry is not an unbounded three-dimensional solution.

The extracellular volume into which molecules can distribute influences the local concentration attained after release. A small accessible volume can produce a high transient concentration for a given number of molecules. A larger extracellular space can dilute the same release, reducing the probability that any nearby receptor binds the transmitter. Shape is as important as volume: surrounding glial processes, the distance between release sites and the distribution of transporter-bearing surfaces determine the paths available to molecules. Consequently, an increase in the total volume of extracellular fluid does not imply a simple proportional change in every local synaptic concentration.

Spillover describes activation of targets beyond the immediate release region by molecules that spread through extracellular space. It need not indicate a malfunction. Some signaling systems employ receptors outside classical synaptic contacts and chemical communication can be less spatially confined than a textbook drawing suggests. Nevertheless, excessive or poorly controlled spillover can reduce the independence of nearby synapses. If a glutamate pulse reaches receptors normally influenced by another terminal, the receiving cell may no longer distinguish which input produced the chemical exposure. High transporter activity at appropriately located membranes can restrict the spread, whereas blocking transport can increase activation of more distant receptors.

The role of diffusion differs according to the question asked. It can be a delivery mechanism when molecules travel from release sites toward receptors and a clearance mechanism when molecules leave the relevant receptor neighborhood. In either case the underlying physical process is the same. Describing diffusion as a loss term is appropriate only when the region of interest is defined, for example the cleft or a small perisynaptic compartment. If the entire tissue is considered, diffusion is redistribution rather than net chemical loss. Uptake and degradation change the amount of free extracellular chemical in ways that diffusion alone cannot reproduce in a closed domain.

An important analytical trap is assuming that an unchanged peak current excludes altered clearance. If receptor sites near the release site become nearly saturated during the brief initial pulse, slowing escape can have a small effect on peak occupancy but a conspicuous effect on later binding and the decay of current. At more distant receptors, where concentration is lower, the same perturbation may alter both peak and duration. A single recording from one postsynaptic location does not characterize the complete spatial distribution of transmitter. Combining a local recording with a second measurement at a more distant site can therefore test whether a perturbation primarily changes the range of a chemical signal.

5. Enzymatic termination at cholinergic synapses

Acetylcholine is an ester formed from acetic acid and the alcohol group of choline. Its quaternary ammonium group carries a permanent positive charge, and the molecule does not readily cross a lipid bilayer unaided. The ester bond supplies a chemical opportunity for terminating its action: hydrolysis separates the acetyl portion from choline. The enzyme acetylcholinesterase accelerates this reaction enormously. At a vertebrate neuromuscular junction, much of the relevant enzyme is anchored to the extracellular basal lamina through a collagen-like protein called collagen Q. The placement of the enzyme is essential to its physiological function because acetylcholine released from the nerve must encounter it before spreading extensively through the extracellular space or repeatedly rebinding receptors. The basal lamina is an extracellular matrix, not a third plasma membrane.

The chemical reaction can be represented as acetylcholine plus water yielding choline and acetate, with protonation states depending on the pH. At physiological pH, acetic acid is predominantly present as acetate. Neither product is equivalent to intact acetylcholine in activating the normal neuromuscular nicotinic receptor. Consequently, one catalytic turnover eliminates one molecule of active substrate from the pool that could continue stimulating the receptor. Choline is not simply discarded: it can enter the presynaptic cell through a high-affinity choline transporter and be used again by choline acetyltransferase. The acetyl group must be supplied anew from acetyl-CoA. The chemical bond destroyed outside the cell must therefore be reconstructed inside the cell. Recycling of one constituent does not constitute reuptake of the original transmitter.

Acetylcholinesterase belongs to the serine hydrolases. In its active site, a serine hydroxyl group participates in nucleophilic attack on the ester carbonyl of acetylcholine. Histidine and a glutamate residue help organize the catalytic chemistry. The substrate-binding environment positions the positively charged portion of acetylcholine while bringing the ester close to the catalytic serine. The enzyme forms a transient acetylated intermediate after release of choline. Water then participates in hydrolysis of the acetyl-enzyme intermediate, freeing acetate and restoring the serine group for another reaction. The catalyst is regenerated; it is not consumed stoichiometrically alongside the transmitter. This distinction explains how a localized population of enzyme molecules can act repeatedly on successive pulses.

The site at which acetylcholine is synthesized is physically separated from the site at which acetylcholinesterase removes it. Synthesis takes place inside the cholinergic neuron, where acetyl-CoA and choline are available. Hydrolysis takes place at accessible extracellular enzyme molecules after release. If both reactions occurred unrestrained in the same compartment, newly synthesized transmitter could be destroyed before packaging. Compartmentalization lets the cell maintain ready supplies while allowing rapid removal of released material. The organization is an example of a general principle of metabolism: enzymes with opposing physiological consequences can coexist because their access to substrates is controlled in space.

The consequence of impaired acetylcholinesterase depends on the physiological preparation and the extent of the perturbation. In an uncomplicated acute experiment, reducing catalytic activity can prolong the exposure of nicotinic receptors to acetylcholine. The declining phase of an end-plate current may become slower, and acetylcholine can activate additional receptor sites after the initial release. A larger peak is possible if additional receptors remain available, but it is not a universal prediction because receptor occupancy can already be high. When stimulation is prolonged, receptor desensitization and persistent depolarization can complicate the relationship between extracellular transmitter and muscle activation. Persistent chemical excitation need not mean sustained effective contraction. If voltage-gated sodium channels cannot recover appropriately from inactivation, new action potentials can become difficult to generate. This illustrates why stronger receptor exposure and stronger physiological output must not be treated as identical.

A deficiency of the anchoring protein collagen Q gives a useful conceptual contrast with a mutation that reduces the catalytic capacity of individual acetylcholinesterase molecules. Both changes can decrease effective hydrolysis near the junction. In the first case enzyme molecules may retain catalytic activity in a biochemical assay yet be poorly concentrated where the transmitter is released. In the second case correctly localized enzyme may turn over substrate inefficiently. A measurement of total activity in an extracted tissue sample may fail to distinguish these alternatives because it does not report the original spatial distribution. Immunolocalization combined with an enzyme activity assay can address the question more directly. The important lesson is that enzymatic rate in a tissue depends on localization as well as catalytic properties.

At a cholinergic junction, choline uptake constitutes a further point of regulation. The high-affinity choline transporter at the plasma membrane uses the sodium gradient to support uptake; transporter availability helps sustain transmitter production. Blocking it need not immediately lengthen the lifetime of acetylcholine, because the esterase can still hydrolyse released molecules. Instead, repeated release consumes the presynaptic stock of transmitter precursors, and later vesicles may become less fully loaded. This temporal separation distinguishes a choline uptake defect from an acetylcholinesterase defect. If a preparation exhibits normal first responses but reduced responses during an extended train, substrate supply is one plausible explanation, though release machinery and recycling must also be considered.

The major extracellular degrading enzyme at the neuromuscular junction should not be confused with butyrylcholinesterase, a related enzyme capable of hydrolysing several esters. The proteins have overlapping biochemical capabilities but different distributions and substrate preferences. Nor should acetylcholinesterase be confused with choline acetyltransferase, despite the fact that both names contain acetylcholine-related terminology. The direction and cellular location of the chemical transformation identify each enzyme more reliably than the similarity of names. An examination statement that claims acetylcholinesterase converts choline and acetyl-CoA into acetylcholine has exchanged the degradative and synthetic enzymes.

6. Reuptake and the energy behind membrane transport

For many transmitters, the neuron need not destroy the entire molecule after every release. A membrane transporter can bind extracellular substrate, carry it across the cell surface and release it into the cytoplasm. The transmitter may subsequently be repackaged or metabolized. This process is called uptake, or reuptake when a releasing cell recovers the transmitter it previously released. Uptake by a neighboring glial cell removes extracellular transmitter just as effectively from the standpoint of a receptor, although the destination differs. The physiological question is not simply whether the molecule is preserved but whether its local extracellular concentration decreases at an appropriate rate.

A plasma membrane transporter differs from a receptor in its principal operation. A receptor converts ligand binding into a response, such as ion conduction or intracellular signaling. A transporter transfers a substrate between compartments through an ordered conformational cycle. Its binding site becomes accessible to one side of the membrane and subsequently to the other. When the protein closes around substrate during the transition, both sides need not be connected by a continuously open aqueous passage. An ion channel, by contrast, can provide a conducting pathway that allows many ions to cross during a single opening. Real proteins can combine transport and channel properties, but the alternating-access model is useful for understanding coupled uptake without imagining that neurotransmitter simply pours through an open pore.

An important group of plasma membrane uptake proteins belongs to the SLC6 family. Familiar examples are the dopamine transporter DAT, the serotonin transporter SERT, the norepinephrine transporter NET, GABA transporters and glycine transporters. Their operation typically depends on sodium and chloride, although detailed coupling ratios and return steps differ among family members. The high concentration of sodium outside most resting neurons and the negative electrical potential of the cell interior favor movement of sodium into the cytoplasm. Coupling substrate translocation to ion movement can make uptake thermodynamically favorable even when accumulation of substrate alone would be unfavorable. It is therefore a form of secondary active transport. The transporter itself does not ordinarily hydrolyse an ATP molecule in each cycle of substrate uptake.

The sodium gradient is maintained in large part by the sodium-potassium ATPase, which uses ATP hydrolysis to move three sodium ions outward and two potassium ions inward per cycle. This primary active transport provides energy indirectly for many uptake processes. The distinction is central to cell physiology: inhibition of ATP production need not directly interact with a neurotransmitter transporter to impair its ability to clear the transmitter. As the supporting ion gradients weaken, the coupled transport cycle loses its driving force. The time required depends on the initial gradients, ion leaks, metabolic reserves and activity of the cell. An immediate effect and a delayed effect should not automatically be assigned the same mechanism.

The energetic contribution of an ion moving from outside to inside can be written as ΔG = RT ln([X]in/[X]out) + zFΔψ, where R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, z is the signed ionic charge, F is Faraday's constant and Δψ is the electrical potential of the inside relative to the outside. Concentrations approximate chemical activities in this elementary treatment. The logarithm is dimensionless because it contains a ratio of concentrations in the same units. The chemical term favors movement toward lower concentration, whereas the electrical term favors movement of positive ions toward a negative interior. The total free-energy change of a coupled transport cycle is the sum of the terms for all transported species, including any species moved in the opposite direction. Net inward transport is favorable if the total free-energy change for the defined forward cycle is negative.

For an illustrative sodium ion entering a cell at 310 kelvin with intracellular sodium of 15 millimolar, extracellular sodium of 145 millimolar and membrane potential of −60 millivolts, the chemical contribution is approximately −5.85 kilojoules per mole. The electrical contribution is about −5.79 kilojoules per mole. The combined change is −11.64 kilojoules per mole of inward sodium. This hypothetical calculation shows why sodium entry can drive the uptake of a transmitter against its own gradient. It does not specify the energetic budget of a particular transporter until its full stoichiometry, substrate charge and all accompanying ion movements have been included. Omitting a chloride or potassium term can reverse a conclusion about the driving force.

The number of transporter molecules on the cell surface helps set the capacity for uptake. Each molecule requires a finite interval to bind, translocate, release and return to a configuration that can accept extracellular substrate again. If the extracellular concentration becomes sufficiently high, increasing concentration cannot indefinitely increase the turnover per transporter. This gives a saturable dependence of uptake on substrate concentration. A simple initial-rate model is v = Vmax C/(Km + C), where v is the rate of uptake, C is extracellular substrate concentration, Vmax is maximal uptake rate under the specified conditions and Km is the concentration that produces half the maximal rate in this model. The equation is phenomenological for a transporter measured at fixed ion gradients; Km need not be the true equilibrium dissociation constant for substrate binding.

At low substrate concentration, C is much smaller than Km and uptake approaches (Vmax/Km)C. Under these conditions a twofold increase in concentration approximately doubles the rate. At high concentration, C is much larger than Km and uptake approaches Vmax. If a release pulse drives the nearby transporters toward saturation, doubling release can cause a disproportionately large prolongation of extracellular exposure because the rate of removal cannot double throughout the high-concentration phase. The degree of nonlinearity depends on how much additional loss occurs by diffusion, enzymatic breakdown or other transporters. A single idealized saturation curve should not be fitted to an intact synapse without considering these routes.

Reduced transporter density and altered substrate affinity are experimentally distinguishable in principle. A decrease in the number of otherwise unchanged transporters reduces maximal uptake capacity and can leave the apparent Km unchanged. A change that affects substrate recognition can shift the concentration dependence and alter apparent Km. Mutations may also alter conformational transition rates, coupling or delivery of proteins to the plasma membrane, so the simple alternatives are not exhaustive. Measuring uptake at one concentration cannot uniquely estimate both Vmax and Km. A concentration series together with a surface protein measurement helps separate a trafficking defect from a catalytic-cycle defect.

Transporter direction is not an immutable property of the amino acid sequence. The net direction of a reversible coupled cycle depends on the overall free-energy change imposed by the gradients. During major disturbances of ionic homeostasis, a transporter that normally clears extracellular transmitter may lose uptake capacity or, in some circumstances, support outward movement. This does not imply that a simple uptake blocker necessarily reverses the transporter; inhibition and reversal are different physical phenomena. Inhibition reduces the rate of transport through a particular route, whereas reversal changes the net direction of transport under suitable conditions. Predicting either requires information about the ion and substrate gradients and the interaction of the intervention with the protein.

7. Comparing the clearance of monoamines, GABA and glycine

Dopamine, serotonin and norepinephrine illustrate chemical messages whose extracellular availability can be shaped strongly by selective plasma membrane transporters. The abbreviation DAT identifies the dopamine transporter, SERT identifies the serotonin transporter and NET identifies the norepinephrine transporter. The assignment of a name to a preferred substrate does not imply that transport is absolutely exclusive under all circumstances. In some brain regions, for example, norepinephrine transporters can contribute appreciably to dopamine clearance. The relative contribution of each route depends on transporter distribution, density and the location of release sites. The most appropriate NET-level prediction is based on a specified preparation, not a claim that one named protein is the sole clearance mechanism everywhere in the nervous system.

Monoamine metabolism and plasma membrane uptake also need to be separated. Monoamine oxidase acts mainly in association with the outer mitochondrial membrane and can metabolize monoamines that enter the intracellular space. Catechol-O-methyltransferase contributes to the metabolism of catecholamines such as dopamine and norepinephrine. The presence of metabolic enzymes does not eliminate the need to move extracellular transmitter away from receptors. In many monoaminergic signaling regions, transport into cells constitutes an important immediate removal route, with metabolism influencing the subsequent fate of the recovered molecules. The location of the reaction determines whether a given enzyme has direct access to the extracellular transmitter pool. Acetylcholinesterase at a neuromuscular junction is a useful contrast because its extracellular location permits direct destruction of released acetylcholine.

An acute selective reduction in a monoamine uptake route can increase the extracellular lifetime of the transmitter, assuming release remains unchanged and alternative removal routes are insufficient to compensate. This increases exposure of some receptors but does not predict an identical response in every target cell. Different receptor subtypes can activate distinct signaling systems; receptor density, occupancy and desensitization also affect the output. The same change in concentration may increase a particular response while decreasing another through a feedback mechanism. Consequently, the phrase increased monoamine signaling is a shorthand that must be unpacked into effects on particular receptors or measured physiological variables.

GABA is a major inhibitory transmitter in the mature mammalian central nervous system, although the electrical consequence of a GABA receptor depends on ion gradients and receptor subtype. Several GABA transporters are expressed in neurons and glia. Their substrate is GABA itself, not a degradation product of extracellular GABA. Once internalized, GABA may contribute to transmitter pools or enter metabolic pathways involving GABA transaminase. Inhibiting uptake can prolong GABA exposure and in some contexts increase tonic activation of extrasynaptic receptors. The phasic synaptic response and the tonic background conductance need not change by the same factor because they experience different extracellular concentration profiles and express different receptor populations.

The use of glycine as a transmitter provides another instructive distinction. Glycine receptors mediate inhibitory chloride conductance in several regions including spinal cord and brainstem, while glycine also serves as a co-agonist at NMDA-type glutamate receptors. Glycine transporters help regulate extracellular glycine availability. Therefore, a manipulation increasing extracellular glycine cannot automatically be classified as exclusively increasing inhibitory signaling. Its consequence depends on which receptors are present, their occupancy and the electrical state of the cell. This is an example of how a single chemical can take part in more than one communication mechanism without changing its molecular identity.

A transporter blocker may initially increase extracellular transmitter and later alter the amount available for vesicular reuse. In addition, higher extracellular concentration can activate inhibitory autoreceptors. As release becomes reduced by feedback, the concentration increase predicted from clearance alone may be partially offset. These coupled effects do not invalidate the simple prediction that reduced uptake tends to prolong a fixed pulse. They show that the assumptions of that prediction must be tested when interpreting a living system with continuing release. For this reason, pulse experiments at fixed input and measurements of ongoing spontaneous transmitter levels answer different questions. A well-designed analytical problem specifies which conditions are being held constant.

8. Astrocytes, glutamate clearance and the glutamate–glutamine cycle

The major excitatory transmitter in much of the vertebrate central nervous system is glutamate. It also participates in cellular metabolism, so the presence of glutamate inside a cell does not by itself identify a glutamatergic neuron. What matters for chemical signaling is its regulated loading, release and recognition by glutamate receptors. Extracellular glutamate is normally kept far below the concentrations found inside many cells. Maintaining that difference is a continuous task because glutamate is released from active synapses and can escape into surrounding extracellular fluid. A cell that responds to glutamate must be protected from inappropriate stimulation without preventing a brief useful transmitter pulse.

Astrocytes are glial cells whose processes often extend near synapses and blood vessels. The idea of a tripartite synapse emphasizes that the releasing neuron, the responding neuron and nearby glial processes can cooperate in communication. It does not assert that every synapse is enclosed by an identical astrocytic sheath. The extent of glial contact varies among regions and synapse types. A transporter situated on an astrocytic surface near the release site competes effectively for molecules that diffuse toward that surface. A transporter located at a greater distance may still contribute to long-term extracellular homeostasis but encounter fewer molecules during the earliest phase of a pulse.

Excitatory amino acid transporters, abbreviated EAATs, are membrane proteins that accumulate glutamate and related acidic amino acids. EAAT1 and EAAT2 are strongly associated with astrocytes in many mammalian brain regions, while other family members are predominantly neuronal or show restricted distributions. EAAT2 is often a major contributor to glutamate uptake in forebrain tissue, but relative expression differs between tissues and developmental states. The conventional names GLAST and GLT-1 are widely used for rodent counterparts of EAAT1 and EAAT2, respectively. These transporters belong to the SLC1 family, not to the SLC6 monoamine transporter family. Their coupling mechanism is also distinct.

For EAAT1, EAAT2 and EAAT3, a normal coupled transport cycle takes one glutamate anion, three sodium ions and one proton inward while moving one potassium ion outward. The incoming positive charges total four; the incoming glutamate contributes one negative charge; the outgoing potassium subtracts one positive charge from the interior. The net coupled movement is therefore equivalent to two positive elementary charges entering the cell per glutamate transported. This net charge transfer means that membrane voltage contributes to the energetics of uptake. A negative interior favors the net inward movement. EAAT proteins can also support an anion conductance that is not fixed stoichiometrically to glutamate translocation; a measured transporter-associated current is consequently not always a direct count of transported glutamate molecules.

The driving forces can be analyzed using the free-energy relationship introduced for sodium-coupled uptake. For the forward cycle, the chemical contributions include the inward concentration ratios for glutamate, sodium and protons and the outward concentration ratio for potassium. The electrical contribution is the net charge transfer of two positive charges multiplied by the membrane potential difference and Faraday's constant. The energy is favorable when the complete sum is negative. A physiological consequence is that the combined energy stored in several ion gradients can sustain an extremely large intracellular-to-extracellular glutamate concentration ratio. A sodium gradient alone is not sufficient to describe the entire cycle, and the effect of altered potassium concentration should be considered with the other gradients.

Astrocytes must handle the glutamate after uptake. If transport simply accumulated glutamate indefinitely inside a finite cell, the driving force for additional accumulation would diminish and cellular metabolism would be disturbed. One important route converts glutamate into glutamine through glutamine synthetase. The enzyme uses ATP to incorporate ammonium into the side-chain carboxyl group of glutamate, producing glutamine. The product differs chemically from glutamate: it has an amide rather than a second carboxylate group in its side chain. It is not a substitute agonist for the standard fast postsynaptic glutamate response. This transformation links transmitter recovery to nitrogen metabolism and reduces the intracellular pool of free glutamate available for outward movement.

Glutamine can leave the astrocyte through appropriate amino acid transport systems and enter neurons through other transporters. In neurons, phosphate-activated glutaminase can hydrolyse glutamine to glutamate and ammonium. The recovered glutamate can enter vesicles by means of vesicular glutamate transporters. GABAergic neurons can also use glutamate derived from glutamine as the substrate for glutamate decarboxylase. The sequence is a metabolic shuttle rather than a claim that each individual transmitter molecule is returned unchanged to exactly the same terminal. Carbon and nitrogen can enter other metabolic pathways, and the participating pools can receive additional substrates.

The shuttle places demands on the energy budgets of both neurons and astrocytes. ATP supports the ion pumps that maintain uptake gradients, glutamine synthesis consumes ATP, and vesicle loading requires energy stored in a proton gradient. The breakdown of the ion gradients after a severe metabolic disturbance can therefore compromise extracellular clearance as well as the ability of a terminal to prepare new vesicles. A brief reduction in energy supply may have several consequences at once. If extracellular glutamate rises under such a condition, it is necessary to distinguish increased release from reduced uptake or reversal of transport. Measuring only the extracellular concentration does not identify which of these mechanisms occurred.

Excessive glutamate receptor activation can disrupt cellular ion homeostasis and cause injury, a phenomenon called excitotoxicity. The term describes a damaging outcome of excessive excitatory signaling rather than a special chemical form of glutamate. Prolonged activation of receptors that permit calcium entry, together with depolarization and secondary changes in ion transport, can burden calcium regulation and metabolism. Efficient glial uptake thus serves both information processing and cellular protection. The appropriate conclusion is not that glutamate is inherently toxic, since physiological pulses are indispensable to normal signaling. The concentration, duration, localization and receptor context determine whether an exposure is useful or harmful.

A selective reduction of astrocytic EAAT capacity yields a different immediate prediction from inhibition of astrocytic glutamine synthetase. In the former condition extracellular glutamate removal can slow even if intracellular conversion pathways remain intact. In the latter condition membrane uptake can initially persist because existing ion gradients and metabolic pathways still operate, but the balance of intracellular glutamate and glutamine production changes. Over longer intervals, transmitter replenishment and metabolic handling may be affected. This distinction shows why a transporter assay and an enzyme assay are complementary. A genetic rescue restoring the appropriate transporter specifically to astrocytes would test whether the cellular location of uptake is necessary for a measured extracellular phenotype.

A comparison with the neuromuscular junction reveals a useful generalization. Both astrocytic glutamate handling and extracellular acetylcholine hydrolysis involve components outside the presynaptic neuron. The acetylcholine pathway destroys the original transmitter chemically before a constituent returns to the neuron. The glutamate pathway frequently moves the intact transmitter across a glial membrane before conversion into a transportable precursor. One uses a localized extracellular enzyme as a principal early termination mechanism; the other uses ion-coupled transport and intercellular metabolism. Both achieve a separation between the extracellular signaling pool and the intracellular storage or precursor pools, but their mechanisms and energetic requirements differ.

9. Autoreceptors and presynaptic feedback

A cell can regulate its output by sensing the product of its own activity. In chemical neurotransmission, receptors expressed by the releasing neuron can detect its own transmitter. Such receptors are called autoreceptors. Their identity is defined by the relationship between the transmitter and the neuron that releases it, not by a unique receptor structure. A receptor for GABA on a GABAergic terminal may be an autoreceptor, whereas the same receptor subtype on a glutamatergic terminal can respond to GABA from another cell and function as a heteroreceptor. The distinction requires knowledge of the transmitter phenotype of the presynaptic cell. Receptor name alone is insufficient.

Many familiar autoreceptors provide inhibitory feedback. Consider a terminal that releases increasing quantities of transmitter during a train of impulses. Some extracellular transmitter encounters receptors on the terminal itself. If receptor activation decreases the probability that the next impulse causes secretion, the rate of further release falls relative to a terminal without feedback. The system thereby couples its recent output to its future output. A rise in transmitter concentration activates a mechanism opposing further elevation. This is a negative-feedback loop. It differs from enzymatic removal because the feedback receptor need not destroy the transmitter molecules that activate it.

Representative inhibitory autoreceptors include D2-family dopamine receptors on appropriate dopaminergic neurons, alpha-2 adrenergic receptors on noradrenergic terminals, certain serotonin receptors such as terminal 5-HT1B autoreceptors, and GABA-B receptors on GABAergic terminals. Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors can also act as inhibitory autoreceptors in appropriate cholinergic systems. These are examples rather than a claim that each subtype occurs on every terminal of a transmitter class. Some autoreceptors operate on dendrites or cell bodies and change neuronal firing, while others are located at release terminals and change secretion more directly. The term presynaptic autoreceptor in the local synaptic sense refers especially to receptors regulating terminal output.

Many inhibitory presynaptic metabotropic receptors couple to Gi/o-family G proteins. Ligand binding changes receptor conformation, promotes activation of the heterotrimeric G protein and enables its activated components to regulate effectors. One common effector is the presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channel. G protein beta-gamma components can reduce calcium channel activity, so an arriving action potential generates less calcium entry near vesicles. Because vesicle fusion depends strongly on local calcium concentration, a moderate reduction of calcium entry can produce a substantial decrease in release probability. This pathway controls a chemical message through an electrical-to-chemical conversion step without requiring a change in the postsynaptic receptor population.

Other pathways can reduce secretion at a step after calcium entry. G protein components may interact with parts of the vesicle fusion machinery, affecting the ability of calcium-bound sensors to trigger exocytosis. Some inhibitory receptors also influence potassium channels or cyclic AMP signaling, changing terminal excitability or the state of release proteins. These mechanisms are not interchangeable experimental explanations. A fall in transmitter secretion accompanied by a fall in presynaptic calcium suggests a channel-dependent contribution; a fall in secretion with no measurable reduction in local calcium can motivate a test of downstream release machinery. Neither observation alone identifies a molecular target with certainty, because the spatial resolution of calcium measurement and the reliability of secretion assays matter.

The G protein signal itself has an ending. GTP hydrolysis returns the G protein toward an inactive state, and receptor occupancy falls as extracellular transmitter is cleared. Regulators of G protein signaling can accelerate GTP hydrolysis in relevant systems. The time course of feedback thus depends both on transmitter exposure and on molecular recovery of the intracellular pathway. A brief transmitter pulse can be followed by a longer period of reduced release probability if receptor-linked signaling persists. This temporal feature allows a terminal to integrate recent activity over a period rather than treating each impulse as independent.

A simple experiment can compare responses before and after reducing an inhibitory autoreceptor's activity. If presynaptic calcium entry increases and evoked quantal content rises, while the mean miniature response remains unchanged, the observations support a presynaptic change in secretion. A direct application of transmitter to the postsynaptic membrane should give a similar response if target sensitivity has not changed. It is important to control for whether the intervention also reaches postsynaptic receptors of the same subtype. Receptor expression is not necessarily restricted to the presynaptic membrane, so a bath-applied ligand can act at more than one site. Selective genetic manipulation or a preparation in which presynaptic and postsynaptic compartments can be manipulated separately provides stronger evidence about locus.

Feedback becomes especially relevant when a transmitter uptake route is inhibited. If the same amount is initially released but its extracellular concentration decays more slowly, inhibitory autoreceptors can experience prolonged activation. The next release event can then be smaller, offsetting some of the concentration increase expected from the loss of uptake. This is why a clearance perturbation may influence both the duration and amplitude of a series of responses. If autoreceptor feedback is also disabled, the compensation can be lost. A comparison of control, uptake-deficient, autoreceptor-deficient and double-perturbed preparations can help determine whether a reduction in release arises through the feedback pathway. The prediction requires confirmation that the interventions are selective and do not substantially alter baseline transmitter supply.

Not every autoreceptor produces inhibition. Some receptors and cellular contexts can enhance secretion through positive feedback or indirectly increase excitability. A positive-feedback mechanism tends to amplify an initial change until constrained by other processes, such as saturation, transmitter clearance or vesicle depletion. The specific receptor's effector coupling and its location determine the direction of the effect. In exam questions, the word autoreceptor establishes that a neuron responds to its own transmitter; it does not mathematically guarantee a negative sign. When inhibitory coupling is specified, negative feedback becomes a justified prediction. When coupling is unspecified, several outcomes may be possible.

10. Presynaptic inhibition and facilitation by other inputs

A releasing terminal can also respond to signals originating from other neurons. Such local control is often described as presynaptic modulation. One neuron may form a contact near another neuron's terminal or release a diffusible substance that reaches receptors on it. This organization allows a third cell to modify communication between a sender and a receiver without necessarily generating a large direct postsynaptic potential in the receiver. For example, suppressing release from an excitatory terminal can reduce the excitatory input to a target. Suppressing release from an inhibitory terminal can instead disinhibit the target. Presynaptic inhibition describes an effect on secretion, not a universal decrease in activity of the final receiving cell.

The term axo-axonic contact refers to a connection in which an axon acts on another axonal region. Some classical examples of presynaptic inhibition occur in sensory pathways, where local signaling regulates transmitter output from incoming afferent terminals. The anatomical contact is not by itself a complete mechanism. It is necessary to identify which receptors are present and what conductance or signaling changes follow their activation. A metabotropic receptor that inhibits calcium channels operates differently from an ionotropic receptor that alters the axonal membrane's conductance.

GABA-B receptors provide a common example of metabotropic presynaptic inhibition. In an appropriate terminal, GABA-B activation can reduce voltage-gated calcium channel function and lower vesicle release probability. The presynaptic neuron need not produce GABA itself; if it releases glutamate, the GABA receptor senses a heterologous signal. GABA-A receptors, in contrast, are ligand-gated anion channels. In certain sensory axons the chloride gradient permits GABA-A receptor activation to depolarize the local membrane while still decreasing effective transmitter release under some conditions. Increased conductance can shunt depolarizing current, and sustained depolarization can alter voltage-gated channel availability. In other conditions a depolarizing action can facilitate transmission or evoke activity. Thus membrane depolarization is not synonymous with increased synaptic output.

To understand the relation between calcium and secretion, suppose an illustrative release probability is proportional to the fourth power of a normalized local calcium signal over a limited unsaturated range. A reduction in calcium signal from 1.0 to 0.8 then gives a relative release probability of 0.8 to the fourth power, or 0.4096. The predicted release is about 41 percent of control if the number of available vesicles and their contents remain fixed. The exponent is an empirical approximation, not a universal stoichiometric count of calcium ions required at every synapse. Release sensors, the distance between channels and vesicles, saturation and cooperative binding all affect the relationship. The model is useful for understanding amplification of a small upstream change.

Facilitation is an increase in the effectiveness of release or the resulting synaptic response. A modulatory receptor can increase secretion by changing calcium entry, the state of the release machinery or the supply of vesicles at release sites. In some terminals, activation of receptors linked to Gs-family G proteins stimulates adenylyl cyclase and increases cyclic AMP, which can influence proteins involved in transmitter output. Other receptors use different signaling routes. A widening of the presynaptic action potential can also allow calcium channels to remain active longer, increasing entry in suitable conditions. The expected effect depends on whether calcium entry was initially limiting and whether vesicles remain available.

When two pathways converge on the same terminal, their combined effects need not add linearly. If one inhibitory receptor nearly suppresses calcium entry, a second inhibitor acting through the same limited calcium-dependent step may have little additional effect. If the second acts directly on fusion machinery, an additional suppression can occur even at the remaining calcium concentration. Saturation and shared effectors can create occlusion, in which the combined effect is smaller than the arithmetic sum of individual effects. However, observing occlusion does not prove that the two receptors act on exactly the same protein. A common floor in measurable secretion or a ceiling in receptor activation can also produce it. Independent measurements of intermediate steps are required to interpret pathway convergence.

Presynaptic regulation can be important even when a target receives many simultaneous signals. Selective reduction of release from one group of terminals changes the relative contribution of that input without imposing a global alteration of postsynaptic receptor responsiveness. A sensory input, for example, can be gated by local signaling while other inputs to the same target remain effective. Conversely, an alteration of postsynaptic membrane resistance can influence responses to several inputs at once. The input specificity of an effect is therefore informative, although some postsynaptic changes are locally restricted to particular receptor clusters. An experiment comparing two independent input pathways converging onto one cell can exploit this distinction.

An important boundary separates modulation at a synapse from modulation of action potential generation at the cell body. A receptor that hyperpolarizes the soma can reduce the number of impulses reaching all terminals of the neuron, indirectly lowering transmitter output. A receptor located near one terminal can reduce the amount released per impulse without necessarily changing the firing rate recorded near the soma. These two mechanisms can produce a similar decrease in extracellular transmitter in a tissue sample. Recording presynaptic action potentials and measuring release under controlled stimulation can separate changes in impulse delivery from changes in secretion per delivered impulse. The most precise explanation identifies which variable the intervention changes.

11. Postsynaptic responsiveness: sensitivity, saturation and desensitization

A synapse can change its response while the extracellular transmitter pulse remains unaltered. The mechanisms responsible are located in the responding cell, and may involve the number of receptors at its surface, their molecular state, the conductance generated by each activated channel or the electrical and biochemical conditions in which receptors operate. The concentration of transmitter outside the cell is consequently only one of several determinants of synaptic strength. This observation is essential for interpreting pharmacological or genetic experiments: an intervention that increases the amplitude of a postsynaptic response does not necessarily increase transmitter secretion.

For a simple population of identical independent receptors with reversible ligand binding, the equilibrium fraction occupied can be written as θ = C/(Kd + C). The concentration C is the free ligand concentration adjacent to the receptors; Kd is the equilibrium dissociation constant, expressed in the same concentration units; θ is dimensionless. This equation follows from equating the rate of binding, proportional to free ligand and unoccupied receptor concentrations, with the rate of dissociation, proportional to occupied receptors. At C = Kd, half the receptors are occupied. A receptor population with a smaller Kd achieves greater fractional occupancy at the same free ligand concentration, provided that the simple assumptions hold.

A hypothetical receptor population with Kd = 10 micromolar is 50 percent occupied at 10 micromolar ligand and approximately 91 percent occupied at 100 micromolar. Doubling ligand concentration from 100 to 200 micromolar increases occupancy only to about 95 percent. If an intervention prolongs ligand exposure without altering a peak that was already well above Kd, the earliest response may change relatively little while the later part of the response changes markedly. This illustrates the nonlinear dependence of receptor occupancy on concentration. Real ligand-gated receptors can have multiple binding sites, cooperative activation, different conformational states and nonequilibrium kinetics. The simple equation is not a universal model of their conductance.

Equilibrium binding cannot be assumed when transmitter concentration changes faster than the receptor population approaches equilibrium. A convenient kinetic model for a single binding site is dθ/dt = kon C(1 − θ) − koff θ. Here kon has units of reciprocal concentration per unit time and koff has units of reciprocal time. The first term represents binding to free sites and the second represents dissociation. After an ideal instantaneous washout, the equation predicts exponential loss of occupancy with a time constant 1/koff. During a real synaptic pulse, C changes continuously, and molecules that dissociate may encounter other receptors or rebind before clearance. A receptor with slow dissociation can remain occupied even after the concentration of free extracellular transmitter has become low. Measuring the decline of binding therefore does not necessarily reveal the decline of free ligand.

Channel activation introduces another distinction. An occupied receptor can adopt an open conducting state, a closed state that is capable of opening, or a desensitized state that responds poorly despite the continued presence of ligand. These states do not describe three different proteins; they describe alternative conformations or functional states of a receptor population. Deactivation refers to the decline of receptor activity when ligand is removed, whereas desensitization refers to declining responsiveness during continued agonist exposure. They may occur over different time scales and can overlap in an actual experiment. An abrupt decline of current during prolonged transmitter application is consistent with desensitization only if changes in local ligand concentration, electrical driving force and recording stability have been adequately controlled.

For a ligand-gated channel population, a simplified current equation is I = g(Vm − Erev). The total activated conductance g depends on channel number, single-channel conductance and open probability; Vm is membrane voltage and Erev is the reversal potential. With this convention, a negative current corresponds to net inward movement of positive charge or outward movement of negative charge. The expression explains why increasing receptor activation does not invariably increase the magnitude of the electrical response in a straightforward way. If membrane voltage is close to the channel's reversal potential, a large conductance can be accompanied by a small net current. In a current-clamp experiment the same conductance can decrease membrane resistance and influence responses to other inputs even when it causes little direct voltage deflection.

This distinction is particularly useful for inhibitory anion channels. If a receptor-mediated conductance increases while the reversal potential is near resting membrane voltage, the resulting voltage change can be small. However, the increase in conductance allows current produced by a simultaneous excitatory input to dissipate more readily, reducing the excitatory voltage response. This is shunting inhibition. Moving the anion reversal potential through a change in chloride regulation can alter the physiological consequence of a given amount of GABA receptor activation. A receptor population that is unchanged in number and binding properties can therefore generate a different response because the ion gradients have changed. The appropriate comparison between two cells must specify their membrane potentials and reversal potentials.

Metabotropic receptors have additional levels of responsiveness. Ligand binding activates intracellular pathways, which may regulate ion channels, enzymes or other signaling proteins. A receptor can become less effective at activating its G protein, and intracellular signaling can decline because of GTP hydrolysis, degradation of second messengers or reversal of protein phosphorylation. Receptor phosphorylation and interactions with regulatory proteins can reduce coupling; receptors may also be removed from the surface through endocytosis and subsequently recycled or degraded. These changes are mechanistically distinct from enzymatic hydrolysis or membrane uptake of transmitter outside the cell. Thus desensitization of a GPCR pathway does not prove that extracellular transmitter has been cleared.

Some changes in postsynaptic responsiveness operate over longer periods than those needed to terminate a single pulse. The insertion or removal of receptor proteins changes the number available to respond to subsequent pulses. An increase in the number of functional receptors can amplify a subsaturating transmitter response without affecting the quantity of transmitter released. Changes in receptor phosphorylation can influence channel function or receptor interactions without changing total receptor protein. Such regulation is important for understanding why a measurement of total protein from a tissue extract does not necessarily predict synaptic conductance. A surface-specific measurement and electrophysiological test provide different information, and both may be needed to locate the cause of a changed response.

One useful experimental approach compares a postsynaptic response evoked by nerve stimulation with the response produced by a controlled direct application of transmitter. Suppose a manipulation decreases both responses to a similar extent while presynaptic calcium entry and the measured extracellular transmitter pulse remain stable. This pattern is consistent with a postsynaptic contribution. If only the nerve-evoked response declines and direct application produces a normal response, reduced release becomes a more plausible explanation. Neither comparison is definitive unless the applied transmitter reaches the same receptor population and reproduces a sufficiently relevant concentration profile. Direct application can activate extrasynaptic receptors or bypass the rapid concentration transients of a natural synapse.

Finally, the distinction between availability and sensitivity is important when studying repeated stimulation. A receptor may not have recovered fully from desensitization by the time a second transmitter pulse arrives. In that case the second electrical response can be smaller even if the two pulses contain the same amount of transmitter. The result resembles presynaptic depression caused by depletion of releasable vesicles. Measuring the amount released, or testing receptor recovery with appropriately timed controlled applications, helps distinguish the two. The same observable reduction in response can be produced by mechanisms on either side of the synapse, and a sound explanation must be supported by a measurement that addresses the proposed locus.

12. Synaptic vesicle recovery and repeated transmission

A presynaptic terminal contains a finite amount of membrane and a finite number of vesicles prepared to release transmitter. When a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, its soluble contents are discharged and its membrane joins, or transiently communicates with, the cell surface. If every action potential caused new membrane addition without retrieval, the terminal would progressively change its surface area and lose the organized vesicle population required for later transmission. Endocytosis and vesicle regeneration solve this material balance problem. They permit the same presynaptic machinery to perform successive release events while maintaining a relatively stable terminal architecture.

The readily releasable pool is a functional group of vesicles that can undergo fusion promptly upon suitable stimulation. Other vesicles can be recruited to replenish this pool. The terms recycling pool and reserve pool are useful operational descriptions, but their sizes and relationships depend on stimulation conditions and the experimental definitions used. A vesicle observed by microscopy is not necessarily ready for rapid release. It may contain little transmitter, lack an appropriate position near calcium channels or require additional molecular preparation before fusion. Functional pool size is therefore not identical to the total vesicle count in an electron micrograph.

Vesicle retrieval can proceed through more than one route. In a classical description, clathrin and adaptor proteins participate in selection of membrane components and formation of coated intermediates; the GTPase dynamin can contribute to membrane scission. Retrieved compartments subsequently lose their coat and regenerate vesicles. Other modes include rapid retrieval of membrane followed by formation of vesicles from an endosomal intermediate, and transient fusion events in which the vesicle can reseal. The relative importance of these modes varies with cell type, temperature and stimulation pattern. At examination level the central lesson is that exocytosis is balanced by retrieval and that clathrin-mediated recovery is an important mechanism but not the sole universal explanation for every vesicle.

The recovered membrane must be converted into a compartment capable of storing transmitter. When a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, its previously acidic lumen is exposed to extracellular conditions. Following retrieval, the vacuolar proton ATPase rebuilds an electrochemical proton gradient. Appropriate vesicular transporters then accumulate their substrates. A monoaminergic terminal depends on a vesicular monoamine transporter, a cholinergic terminal on a vesicular acetylcholine transporter, and a glutamatergic terminal on vesicular glutamate transporters. Recovery of membrane area alone therefore cannot establish that normal transmitter loading has resumed. A treatment affecting the proton ATPase can allow membrane retrieval to proceed yet impair the functional filling of recycled vesicles.

Consider two hypothetical terminals with identical initial stores of filled vesicles. The first loses endocytic efficiency after stimulation, and the second loses vesicle acidification after retrieval. Both may initially release transmitter normally from their preexisting pool. With continued stimulation, the first terminal encounters a shortage of newly recovered vesicle membrane, whereas the second may generate compartments that cannot accumulate normal transmitter content. The first mechanism can reduce the number of release-ready vesicles; the second can reduce the chemical content of vesicles that still undergo fusion. Recording exocytosis independently from postsynaptic quantal amplitude can distinguish the mechanisms. A normal number of fusion events combined with a lower quantal response supports a loading defect, although postsynaptic responsiveness must also be tested.

A pH-sensitive fluorescent reporter associated with a vesicle membrane protein provides one conceptual means of following these events. Its fluorescence can be relatively weak in the acidic vesicle lumen and increase when exocytosis exposes it to a more neutral extracellular environment. After internalization and reacidification, the fluorescence decreases again. The falling fluorescence signal is therefore influenced by both membrane retrieval and proton-dependent acidification. It cannot be assumed to be an isolated measurement of endocytosis. Additional experimental controls that discriminate surface-exposed from internalized reporter can help separate the processes. A defect in acidification could prolong the signal even if endocytosis occurred at the normal rate.

The speed of recovery is especially important during trains of action potentials. A terminal with a large initial releasable pool but slow replenishment can transmit its first few messages strongly and then show depression. A terminal with a smaller initial pool and rapid replenishment may have a lower initial output but sustain transmission more consistently. This trade-off cannot be assessed by counting the total vesicles alone. Release probability, the fraction of vesicles in a prepared state, recruitment rate and stimulus frequency collectively determine the pattern of use. The same terminal can exhibit different behavior when tested with widely spaced impulses rather than a high-frequency train.

The time scales of extracellular clearance and vesicle recovery must not be confused. The transmitter released during a single brief event may be removed from the receptor neighborhood long before the vesicle membrane and contents are fully restored. Removal ends the immediate extracellular signal; recycling prepares the terminal to produce future signals. Blocking membrane recovery need not prolong transmitter action from the first vesicle. Conversely, inhibiting extracellular degradation need not immediately impair endocytosis. When a treatment affects both processes indirectly, time-resolved measurements are needed to distinguish primary from secondary consequences. This separation completes the picture of a synapse as a system with both a communication cycle and a material recovery cycle.

13. Short-term synaptic facilitation and depression

Two identical nerve impulses delivered close together need not produce identical chemical signals. The first impulse changes the internal state of the terminal and sometimes the state of the postsynaptic cell. If the second impulse arrives before those changes have recovered, the second response can be enhanced or reduced. Short-term synaptic plasticity refers to activity-dependent changes in synaptic effectiveness that persist over brief intervals, often milliseconds to seconds and in some forms longer. The present discussion concerns how immediate transmitter output and receptor responsiveness depend on recent activity, rather than the long-term mechanisms underlying learning and memory.

Facilitation often reflects residual calcium remaining in the presynaptic terminal after a previous impulse. The local calcium concentration at a release sensor during an action potential rises rapidly and declines as calcium diffuses, binds buffers and is extruded or sequestered. The residual elevation after the peak is smaller and more spatially distributed. Nevertheless, it can alter subsequent release, directly or through calcium-sensitive proteins that influence the release process. The next impulse then encounters a terminal whose biochemical state differs from that before the first impulse. The additional calcium-dependent drive can increase the probability of vesicle fusion. Residual calcium should not be understood as an unchanged copy of the brief high-calcium microdomain around an open channel; the two signals have different spatial and temporal properties.

A synapse with low initial release probability can show pronounced paired-pulse facilitation. Relatively few vesicles are consumed by the first stimulus, leaving many available for the second. If residual calcium increases the probability of fusion, the increase in release can outweigh the small reduction in available vesicles. A synapse with high initial release probability may instead release a large fraction of the readily releasable pool on its first stimulus. Even if calcium-dependent enhancement remains present, the second stimulus has fewer available vesicles. Depletion can dominate and the second response becomes smaller. This reasoning explains why the existence of residual calcium does not guarantee facilitation in every preparation.

A useful simple model treats the mean response magnitude as A = Npq, where N is the number of independent available release sites or equivalent release opportunities, p is their probability of releasing one quantum in response to the stimulus, and q is the mean response contributed by one quantum. The model assumes that responses sum linearly, that the sites can be represented by identical release probabilities and that postsynaptic saturation or other nonlinearities are negligible. In a real terminal, sites may differ in release probability and more than one vesicle can contribute at a site. The simplified relationship is still valuable for distinguishing three broad classes of modulation: changes in available release opportunities, changes in fusion probability and changes in quantal response magnitude.

For example, suppose 100 sites are initially available, each has release probability 0.1 and q is one arbitrary unit. The mean first response is 10 units. If each successful site temporarily becomes unavailable, about 90 sites remain immediately afterward. Suppose the next impulse arrives before replenishment and residual calcium raises the release probability to 0.2. The predicted second response is 18 units. The paired-pulse ratio, defined as the second response divided by the first, is 1.8. If the initial probability had instead been 0.7 and no change in probability occurred on the second stimulus, 30 remaining sites would produce a mean response of 21 units compared with 70 initially. The ratio would be 0.3. These are illustrative expected values from the simple model rather than measurements of a particular animal.

Depression can also arise without exhausting the releasable pool. Repeated stimulation may decrease calcium channel availability, alter the state of release machinery, activate inhibitory autoreceptors or reduce postsynaptic receptor sensitivity. In a receptor population that desensitizes after the first transmitter pulse, a second pulse can evoke less current even when presynaptic release is unchanged. Changes in vesicle content can also modify q during sustained activity if loading cannot keep pace with recycling. A paired-pulse ratio by itself does not uniquely identify which factor changed. Interpretation is strongest when multiple independent measurements are consistent with a mechanism and when alternatives are explicitly tested.

Recovery from depression supplies another informative measurement. If vesicle availability is the limiting factor, increasing the interval between stimuli allows more sites to regain a release-ready vesicle. In a simple recovery model, the deficit from a fully available pool declines approximately exponentially with time when replenishment has a constant probability per unit time for each vacant site. At an interval equal to the recovery time constant, approximately 63 percent of the initial deficit has been replenished. This prediction is conditional: if recruitment depends on activity or more than one pool is involved, recovery can contain several time constants or depart from a single exponential. A fitted time constant is a description of the observed recovery curve, not automatic proof of a single molecular reaction.

The functional significance of short-term plasticity depends on the signal being conveyed. A facilitating terminal can favor closely spaced impulses, making a burst more influential than isolated impulses. A depressing terminal may transmit the beginning of a stimulus train particularly effectively while reducing the impact of subsequent impulses. These properties give synapses a form of temporal filtering. They arise from local biological variables such as calcium, receptor state and vesicle availability. The existence of such filtering does not require a persistent change in gene expression or long-term restructuring of the synapse. The short-term mechanisms can operate while the anatomical connection remains unchanged.

An experimental comparison can exploit the predicted dependence on stimulus interval. If facilitation depends on residual calcium, it should generally decrease as the interval becomes sufficiently long for the relevant calcium-dependent state to dissipate. If depression is caused by depleted vesicles, recovery should increase as more time becomes available for replenishment. Both effects may coexist and generate a biphasic interval dependence. A short interval can reveal strong depression, an intermediate interval facilitation, and a longer interval a return toward the control response. Rather than treating such a curve as contradictory, one should consider how at least two processes with distinct signs and recovery time scales could produce it. Direct calcium measurements and tests of vesicle availability can make this explanation experimentally testable.

14. Quantitative reasoning about the duration of a transmitter pulse

A quantitative model of clearance must begin by defining the region whose concentration is being measured. Let C denote the concentration of free transmitter in a hypothetical well-mixed extracellular compartment of constant volume. Suppose secretion adds transmitter at a rate s(t), expressed as concentration per unit time. If losses from this region are proportional to the concentration, then the total balance is dC/dt = s(t) − kC. The loss coefficient k has dimensions of reciprocal time. This mathematical statement says that the concentration increases when input exceeds removal and decreases when removal exceeds input. It is a model for a specified compartment, not a description of every molecular trajectory in an anatomically complex synapse.

After an instantaneous release pulse, assume secretion ceases and the initial concentration is C0. Separating the variables gives dC/C = −k dt. Integrating from the initial time to time t gives ln(C/C0) = −kt. Exponentiating yields C(t) = C0 exp(−kt). The time constant τ = 1/k is the time at which concentration falls to approximately 36.8 percent of its initial value. The half-life is the time needed to reach half the initial concentration, and substituting C/C0 = 1/2 gives t½ = ln(2)/k. The time constant and half-life are therefore different quantities even though both describe the same exponential decay. In an exam, a time constant of 10 milliseconds corresponds to a half-life of approximately 6.93 milliseconds, not 10 milliseconds.

If several independent first-order routes remove transmitter from the compartment, their rates add. For example, a hypothetical diffusion loss coefficient of 0.10 per millisecond, a transporter contribution of 0.25 per millisecond and a degradative contribution of 0.05 per millisecond produce a total k of 0.40 per millisecond. The resulting time constant is 2.5 milliseconds. Removing the transporter contribution leaves k = 0.15 per millisecond, giving a time constant of about 6.67 milliseconds. If initial concentration is held at 80 micromolar, the concentration after five milliseconds is approximately 10.8 micromolar in control and 37.8 micromolar after transport inhibition. This illustrative dataset makes clear that eliminating a major route does not require the concentration to remain permanently high; the remaining routes still contribute to loss.

The area under a concentration-time curve summarizes cumulative exposure in this model. Integrating C0 exp(−kt) from zero to infinity gives C0/k. Its dimensions are concentration multiplied by time. In the preceding example the area is 200 micromolar milliseconds for control and approximately 533 micromolar milliseconds after uptake inhibition. The peak is identical by construction, but cumulative exposure is increased by a factor of about 2.67. The model thereby provides an explicit case in which an unchanged peak conceals a substantial increase in exposure. Receptor response cannot be calculated from this area alone unless receptor kinetics and concentration dependence are also known. For a saturating receptor population, a doubling of integrated exposure need not produce a doubling of integrated current.

During continuing low-level release, the same model predicts a steady concentration Css = s/k when s is constant, because the input and removal rates become equal. This result is not valid if transmitter supply changes with concentration, as occurs when inhibitory autoreceptors alter release. To see why, imagine a feedback law s(C) = s0/(1 + C/K), where s0 is the maximum input rate at low concentration and K is the concentration giving half that input in the chosen model. At steady state, kC = s0/(1 + C/K). Rearranging produces a quadratic relation instead of the simple ratio s0/k. Lowering k still tends to raise the equilibrium concentration in this model, but increased concentration reduces input and moderates the increase. The feedback model is hypothetical; it is not a fitted dose-response relationship for a particular autoreceptor.

A finite interval between impulses creates another important consequence of slow clearance. If a pulse adds an amount corresponding to a concentration increment Q and exponential clearance operates for an interval Δ, the fraction remaining before the next impulse is exp(−kΔ). With a sufficiently long sequence of identical pulses and no feedback, the concentration immediately after each pulse approaches Q/[1 − exp(−kΔ)]. This follows from the geometric sum of the current pulse and the residual contributions from earlier pulses. At low stimulation frequency, the residual factor becomes small and the concentration after each pulse approaches Q. When impulses arrive much closer together relative to the clearance time, the residual factor approaches one and considerable accumulation can occur. Saturation of uptake and receptor feedback can invalidate this linear accumulation model, so the formula is most useful as a baseline against which observed deviations are interpreted.

Transport-mediated removal is not always first order. When the transporter follows an initial-rate saturation model, the local loss can be written as Vmax C/(Km + C) in concentration per unit time. In the absence of other routes the low-concentration limit behaves approximately exponentially with k = Vmax/Km. At a sufficiently high concentration the rate approaches Vmax, making the early decrease approximately linear in time. For a high substrate concentration, a fixed amount rather than a fixed fraction is removed per unit time while the transport system remains near saturation. A plot on semilogarithmic axes may consequently curve instead of forming a straight line. A straight-line fit over the entire time course can obscure the difference between high-concentration and low-concentration regimes.

The mathematical separation of free concentration, receptor occupancy and current is also useful for identifying what a measured decay constant represents. If clearance is rapid compared with receptor dissociation and deactivation, the receptor kinetics may dominate the current's falling phase. If receptor activity follows concentration closely and clearance is slow, transmitter removal may dominate. If the membrane voltage is measured rather than current, the electrical properties of the membrane add another time scale. A single exponential fitted to a voltage response is not necessarily an estimate of transporter turnover, catalytic activity or diffusion coefficient. A good analysis identifies the measured variable first, states which steps are being approximated and then asks whether those approximations are justified.

15. Identifying regulatory mechanisms through converging evidence

Suppose a hypothetical synapse produces an evoked current of 100 picoamperes under control conditions. After an experimental perturbation the current peaks at 100 picoamperes again, but its fitted decay time increases from 5 to 12 milliseconds. A researcher might propose that transmitter clearance has slowed. This is a reasonable hypothesis, especially if the initial receptor population was near saturation, but receptor deactivation could also have slowed. A direct sensor for extracellular transmitter near the release site can test the predicted prolongation of concentration. A rapid application and removal of transmitter in an isolated receptor preparation can test deactivation independently. If the sensor reports prolonged transmitter exposure and receptor deactivation after a controlled pulse remains unchanged, the evidence more strongly favors altered clearance.

In another hypothetical experiment, a nerve-evoked response falls from 100 to 50 picoamperes after an inhibitory receptor agonist is introduced. The mean miniature event remains 10 picoamperes, and the postsynaptic response to directly applied transmitter remains unchanged. A presynaptic effect is plausible because two independent measurements of target responsiveness are stable. The mean quantal content estimated from the ratio of mean evoked response to mean miniature response would fall from ten to five under linear summation assumptions. This does not identify whether the number of available sites or release probability changed. Measuring release failures, variability or presynaptic calcium can help separate these alternatives. A reduced calcium signal would support an upstream contribution; unchanged calcium with reduced release would direct attention toward the fusion or vesicle recruitment steps.

Changes in failure frequency offer another simple line of evidence. In a model with N independent equivalent sites and release probability p at each, the probability of no release is P0 = (1 − p)^N. With four sites and p = 0.5, P0 is 0.0625. If probability declines to 0.25 without a change in site number, P0 becomes approximately 0.316. An increase in apparent failures can also occur when postsynaptic quantal responses become so small that events fall below detection threshold. Thus failure counts must be interpreted together with noise, detection sensitivity and measurements of q. A technical artifact in detecting small currents can imitate a biological change in secretion. This is one reason to avoid inferring an exact release probability from a single statistic unless the model assumptions and detection limits are clear.

A particularly informative way to test extracellular enzyme function is to compare wild-type tissue with tissue expressing an enzyme variant that retains activity but is misplaced relative to synapses. If total enzyme activity in extracts is normal but transmitter clearance near the release site is impaired, the localization hypothesis gains support. A further rescue that restores the correct anchoring without changing the catalytic sequence would provide more targeted evidence. The interpretation depends on whether tissue development or compensatory expression of other proteins also changed. An acute manipulation and a genetic manipulation have complementary strengths: the acute manipulation minimizes some developmental adaptations but may have nonspecific effects, while the genetic manipulation can be targeted to a molecule or cell type but may provoke compensation.

The logic of perturbation and rescue is equally relevant to astrocytic transport. Consider a preparation in which extracellular glutamate after a standardized stimulus is prolonged after reduction of an astrocyte-enriched transporter. Restoring that transporter selectively in astrocytes and recovering a normal concentration decay would support a causal glial contribution. The design should include a control construct, a check of surface localization and a measurement of the amount of transmitter released. If the genetic manipulation also changes presynaptic development, a smaller or larger release pulse could otherwise be mistaken for a clearance effect. A rescue that restores protein expression but fails to restore its transport function is a useful additional control because it helps distinguish the catalytic role of the transporter from other effects of its presence.

For paired-pulse experiments, the appropriate readout is the ratio of second to first response under carefully matched recording conditions. Suppose control responses are 40 and 60 picoamperes, whereas treatment responses are 20 and 40 picoamperes. The ratios are 1.5 and 2.0, respectively. The ratio increases even though both absolute responses decrease. This is compatible with a decrease in initial release probability combined with less vesicle depletion, but it does not demonstrate that the second pulse became absolutely stronger. The result is also potentially influenced by residual postsynaptic receptor state or changes in stimulus delivery. Reporting only ratios discards information contained in absolute amplitudes. Reporting both and testing an independent measure of secretion produces a more informative analysis.

The broader principle is to match the measurement to the proposed mechanism. An extracellular sensor measures a chemical exposure near its location, not necessarily the exact concentration at every receptor. A postsynaptic current reports the net effect of receptor conductance and driving force, not a direct vesicle count. A membrane reporter can indicate trafficking but may also respond to acidification. A biochemical assay measures activity under its own substrate and ionic conditions, not necessarily in the original tissue geometry. Each method answers a particular question and has a characteristic limitation. Strong mechanistic interpretation comes from agreement between methods whose limitations differ, not from treating several indirect readings as interchangeable measures of the same event.

In this way, regulation of neurotransmission integrates the principles of cellular communication studied throughout the subunit. It uses membrane transport and ion gradients, enzyme catalysis, extracellular matrix organization, receptor-linked signaling and intracellular trafficking. All of these processes are subject to feedback and depend on metabolic energy. A comparison with the next topic, haematopoiesis, illustrates that regulation also operates on much longer time scales in other systems. Synaptic modulation adjusts communication over intervals associated with neuronal activity, whereas regulation of blood-cell production changes cell survival, proliferation and differentiation in response to tissue demands. Both require signals that can be turned on, sensed, limited and adjusted. The molecules and biological outputs differ, but the logic of separating signal production, transmission, reception and feedback remains valuable.

Worked numerical examples

1. Clearance time constant

Given k = 0.4 ms⁻¹, τ = 1/0.4 = 2.5 ms and half-life = 0.693/0.4 = 1.73 ms. These are different measures of the same first-order decay.

2. Residual concentration

C₀ = 80 µM; after 5 ms at k = 0.4 ms⁻¹, C = 80e⁻² = 10.83 µM. At k = 0.15 ms⁻¹, C = 80e⁻⁰·⁷⁵ = 37.79 µM. The initial concentration is fixed; slower clearance changes subsequent exposure.

3. Transport capacity

With Vmax = 24 µM/ms and Km = 6 µM, uptake at C = 6 µM is 12 µM/ms; at C = 18 µM, it is 18 µM/ms. Tripling concentration raises rate only 1.5-fold because the transporter approaches saturation.

4. Sodium electrochemical energy

At 310 K, [Na⁺]in = 15 mM, [Na⁺]out = 145 mM and Δψ = −0.060 V: RT ln(15/145) ≈ −5.85 kJ/mol; FΔψ ≈ −5.79 kJ/mol; total ≈ −11.64 kJ/mol. This is the sodium contribution only.

5. Calcium-dependent release

If release is proportional to Ca⁴ over a limited unsaturated range, a 20% decrease in the calcium signal gives 0.8⁴ = 0.4096 of control secretion. The exponent is a model assumption.

6. Paired-pulse ratio

A₁ = 20 pA, A₂ = 40 pA gives PPR = 2.0. Control 40 and 60 pA gives 1.5. Both treated amplitudes are smaller despite an increased ratio.

7. Quantal content and failures

Under linear summation A/q = 50/10 = 5 quanta on average. Separately, for N = 4 independent sites and p = 0.25, P₀ = 0.75⁴ = 0.3164. These models require equivalent sites and reliable detection.

8. Pulse accumulation

If each pulse adds 10 µM and one-half remains at the next pulse, steady peak = 10/(1−0.5) = 20 µM. This is valid for linear clearance, identical pulses and no feedback.
