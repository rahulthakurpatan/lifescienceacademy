1. The biological problem: using lactose only when it is needed

A bacterium cannot carry out every possible biochemical reaction at the same rate simply because its genome contains the corresponding instructions. Making a protein requires amino acids, ribosomes, messenger RNA, chemical energy and time. These resources are also needed to reproduce the cell, maintain its membrane and repair damaged molecules. For a cell that encounters a changing food supply, the ability to alter the distribution of these resources is valuable. Escherichia coli provides a particularly clear example because it can exploit several different carbon compounds, including lactose. The lactose system demonstrates how a change outside the cell can produce a selective change in the amount of a particular group of proteins, without requiring any alteration in the inherited genome.

Lactose is a disaccharide containing galactose and glucose joined through a beta-1,4 glycosidic linkage. Its presence in the extracellular environment does not automatically make either monosaccharide available to the cytoplasm. The inner membrane presents a barrier to the free movement of many hydrophilic solutes, and a suitable enzyme must cleave the glycosidic bond before the products can enter the relevant metabolic pathways. The bacterium therefore faces two coordinated requirements: bring lactose into the cell and catalyse its utilization. It would be inefficient to manufacture a large amount of a lactose transporter when no lactose is available, and increasing transport alone would not supply the enzymes needed to metabolize the imported sugar.

A gene encoding a lactose-associated enzyme can remain present in a bacterium even when enzyme synthesis is strongly reduced. A change in enzyme abundance following the addition of lactose is thus a phenomenon of gene expression rather than a change in the genetic code. This distinction can be tested by withdrawing lactose. If the same cell lineage ceases to produce large amounts of the enzyme when lactose is removed and resumes production when lactose is restored, reversible regulation provides a plausible explanation. A mutation that permanently alters an enzyme's amino acid sequence cannot by itself account for repeated, reversible changes of this sort. Natural selection can change the composition of a culture over generations, however, so a population experiment must also consider whether the apparent response reflects changing cell types.

In a growing bacterium, the amount of an enzyme per cell depends on both the rate at which it is made and the rate at which it is lost or diluted by growth. If the production of a lactose enzyme suddenly decreases, the enzyme molecules that already exist do not necessarily disappear at once. A biochemical assay might therefore detect activity even after transcription of the associated gene has returned to a low level. Conversely, a newly activated promoter may produce detectable messenger RNA before enough functional enzyme has accumulated to alter the growth of the organism. Throughout this chapter it is important to keep gene transcription, protein abundance, catalytic activity and bacterial growth separate as measurable outcomes.

The lactose operon is an example of a negatively regulated inducible system. The word negative describes the effect of a regulatory protein when it occupies its relevant DNA target: transcription is reduced. The word inducible describes the overall response to an appropriate signal: expression increases. These terms describe different aspects of regulation and are not alternatives. An inducer can increase transcription by weakening an inhibitor rather than by directly stimulating RNA polymerase. This double relationship is the key to understanding why a protein called a repressor is central to a system that turns on in response to a nutrient.

A second regulatory influence arises from the general carbon status of the bacterium. The cell may prefer another available carbon source even when lactose is present, so release of the lactose-specific repressor does not always guarantee maximal expression. This chapter isolates the lactose-sensitive component of the switch. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, comparisons between induced and uninduced conditions assume that the background carbon conditions permit transcription and are held constant. The detailed regulation involving cyclic AMP and CRP, also called CAP, is reserved for the subsequent chapter on positive control. Separating these two inputs is essential for identifying what a mutation has changed.

The usefulness of the switch is not that the bacterium knows in advance whether a particular protein will be needed. A molecular arrangement that produces more transporter and enzyme under appropriate conditions and less under inappropriate conditions can confer a fitness advantage over many generations. The underlying process is the interaction of physical molecules: sugar, protein, DNA and RNA polymerase. No central decision-making organ directs the response. Understanding the behavior of these molecules, and how their interactions can be experimentally separated, explains the apparent coordination of the whole cell.

2. From enzyme adaptation to the genetic switch

When early studies of bacterial metabolism compared cultures grown on different carbon sources, the amount of a particular enzyme was found to depend on the previous nutritional conditions. The phenomenon was historically described in terms of enzyme adaptation. This description did not specify whether the environment modified an enzyme already present, selected cells with greater enzymatic capacity, or caused the synthesis of new enzyme molecules. Those possibilities predict different responses when the environmental signal changes. The study of lactose utilization became a way of addressing this broader question about the relation between genes and proteins.

The availability of mutants that could not efficiently metabolize lactose made it possible to examine the problem genetically. A mutant lacking functional beta-galactosidase could fail to use lactose even when its regulation was normal. Another mutant could contain normal enzyme-coding sequences but synthesize the enzyme in conditions in which a wild-type cell produced very little. The first kind of defect concerns the function of the metabolic machinery; the second suggests that the machinery is no longer subject to its usual control. The distinction between these classes led investigators to distinguish genes specifying metabolic proteins from genetic elements determining when those proteins were produced.

In 1959, Arthur Pardee, François Jacob and Jacques Monod published a study of the genetic control of beta-galactosidase inducibility. The experiment is often referred to as PaJaMo. Bacterial conjugation provided a way to introduce particular genetic information into a recipient with a different regulatory history. The value of the approach was that an incoming functional enzyme gene could encounter cytoplasm that did not yet contain the regulatory product expected from the newly introduced genotype. The experiment thus separated the immediate availability of genetic information from the later accumulation of its product.

In an important arrangement, the recipient lacked functional inducibility control and functional beta-galactosidase, whereas the donor could transfer functional genetic information for both. Following transfer, enzyme synthesis could initially occur without added inducer. Subsequently, synthesis declined as the functional regulatory system became established. The interpretation was that a wild-type regulatory gene gives rise to a product that can accumulate in the cell and inhibit expression when inducer is absent. The observation was particularly informative because the incoming enzyme gene could be expressed before the inhibitory influence of the incoming regulatory information was established. This explanation did not require the regulatory gene to be physically part of the enzyme molecule.

Consider an alternative hypothesis in which the regulatory region is merely a fixed piece of DNA that changes the coding sequence of beta-galactosidase. Under that hypothesis, the transfer of a normal enzyme gene would restore the ability to make the normal enzyme but would not by itself explain delayed establishment of a diffusible inhibitory effect. Another hypothesis could propose that an inducer is a required chemical substrate of RNA polymerase. Such a proposal has difficulty explaining enzyme synthesis in the absence of inducer when the relevant negative regulator is missing. Genetic and physiological observations together favored a model in which removal of inhibition permits synthesis.

This reasoning also helps explain why the rate of enzyme synthesis is more informative than total accumulated enzyme in such experiments. If existing enzyme is stable, its activity may remain high even after the formation of new enzyme slows. A curve of accumulated enzyme could continue to rise with a decreasing slope, or approach a plateau, rather than fall abruptly. The slope under conditions of comparable biomass gives information about the production of new active enzyme. Failure to distinguish the ordinate of a graph from its slope can lead to a mistaken reading of the classical evidence.

Jacob and Monod formulated their influential operon model in 1961. Its explanatory power came from separating the coding regions that produce cellular machinery, the local DNA region that controls their transcription, and a regulatory gene whose product interacts with that region. A single regulatory protein could therefore affect several adjacent coding sequences together. Moreover, a substance in the environment could change the effectiveness of that protein. This framework gave a molecular interpretation to inducibility and constitutivity, properties previously described largely through enzyme activities and mutant phenotypes.

The model predicted that the negative regulator was a molecule that could recognize both a specific chemical signal and a particular DNA site. Evidence for the protein itself came from the work of Walter Gilbert and Benno Müller-Hill, who reported isolation of the lac repressor in 1966. Binding to a labeled inducing compound provided a biochemical handle with which to distinguish a relevant protein fraction from many unrelated cellular proteins. The existence of an isolatable inducer-binding regulator supported a concrete molecular mechanism rather than an abstract genetic inhibitory property. Subsequent binding studies connected the repressor to operator DNA.

A final important refinement concerned the identity of the natural inducer. Although lactose in the growth medium causes induction under suitable conditions, that observation alone cannot establish that lactose is the direct regulatory ligand. In 1972, Jobe and Bourgeois identified allolactose as the natural inducer produced in lactose-grown cells. This is a particularly useful example of the difference between an environmental input and the molecular species that interacts directly with a regulatory protein. When a question asks for the physiological inducer of the lac system, the correct mechanistic answer is allolactose.

The historical progression is important because each kind of evidence answered a distinct question. Nutritional experiments established that enzyme production was responsive. Mutant analysis separated regulatory defects from defects in enzyme function. Transfer experiments supported a regulatory substance that acted within the common cell environment. Biochemical isolation supported a protein regulator, ligand binding established a chemical recognition event, and DNA binding studies identified the local regulatory target. A textbook diagram of a repressor on an operator summarizes this combined evidence; it is not simply an assumption invented to explain the presence or absence of an enzyme.

3. Reading the lactose control region

The conventional representation of the lac region of the E. coli chromosome includes three structural genes, lacZ, lacY and lacA, arranged in that order. The term structural gene in this context means a coding sequence whose product performs a function associated with the lactose utilization system, as distinct from the nearby regulatory gene. The three structural genes are normally expressed together from the lac promoter. The regulatory gene lacI has its own transcriptional control region and is not a fourth cistron of the usual lacZYA messenger RNA. This independent expression is important: a cell must have a supply of the regulatory protein even when production of the structural gene products is strongly repressed.

The lac promoter is a DNA region recognized by the bacterial transcription initiation machinery. It helps determine where RNA polymerase begins copying the genetic information. The operator is a specific DNA sequence recognized by the repressor. Although these regulatory elements occur close together and their functional regions can overlap, they perform different molecular roles. A promoter mutation can reduce transcription because polymerase can no longer initiate efficiently. An operator mutation can alter transcription by changing the ability of a regulator to bind. The two mutations therefore need not produce similar phenotypes even if both occur within a short stretch of DNA.

A useful convention in a genetic map is to mark the first nucleotide transcribed as position +1. Positions upstream are designated negative numbers; positions farther along the transcription unit are positive. This coordinate system does not mark the first translated codon of lacZ, which is a separate feature. Regulatory sequences can extend into transcribed regions. The primary lac operator, O1, is positioned close to the beginning of the transcription unit, and the wild-type repressor's interaction with this site is central to the negative control mechanism. A diagram showing boxes for promoter and operator in a linear order should not be interpreted as a precisely scaled map or as proof that their sequences cannot overlap.

Two auxiliary operators contribute to the strength of repression. O3 lies upstream of the primary operator and O2 lies downstream within lacZ. These additional sites help a tetrameric repressor form a DNA loop by binding two sites at once. The primary operator and its ability to interact with the repressor are sufficient for the simplest genetic predictions, but the auxiliary sites explain why physical repression in the intact chromosome can be much stronger than repression measured in a simplified system with only one operator. Later in this chapter we will distinguish the idealized operator-constitutive mutation used in examinations from a real change that weakens only one of several interacting sites.

The product of lacZ is beta-galactosidase. The enzyme catalyses hydrolysis of lactose to glucose and galactose. It can also catalyse transgalactosylation reactions, which are important because they produce allolactose. An enzyme can thus participate both in nutrient processing and in generation of the signal that increases the production of the relevant machinery. The product of lacY is lactose permease, a membrane protein that couples galactoside transport to the proton electrochemical gradient. Its contribution concerns transport across the cell membrane rather than chemical hydrolysis of the sugar. Confusing lacY with lacZ obscures the distinction between entry of a signal and processing of that signal.

The product of lacA is thiogalactoside transacetylase, which transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to certain galactosides. Its function is not equivalent to hydrolysis of lactose or transport of lactose. It is commonly associated with handling potentially problematic galactoside compounds, and it is not generally required for the core steps of lactose utilization in the same way as beta-galactosidase and permease. One must not infer that all three proteins are equally indispensable for growth on lactose simply because their genes are transcribed together. Coordination of gene expression does not imply equivalence of biochemical roles.

A single polycistronic RNA can contain the coding information for several different proteins. Ribosomes initiate translation at the appropriate coding regions, so the gene products are not normally synthesized as one large fused protein that must be cut into the three enzymes. Each cistron specifies a separate protein. Translation efficiency and protein lifetime may differ among these products. Consequently, a change in promoter activity can influence the supply of all three coding regions while leaving differences in the abundance of their corresponding proteins. This is why a study measuring beta-galactosidase alone provides a useful proxy for lac expression but is not an exact measurement of every product of the operon.

In genetic notation, lacI is often abbreviated I, the promoter P, the operator O, and the coding loci Z, Y and A. Superscript plus denotes the reference functional allele in a particular problem, whereas minus usually denotes a specified loss of function. The notation O superscript c indicates an operator mutation with constitutive behavior. The notation I superscript s denotes a superrepressor allele. These symbols describe different kinds of objects: I specifies a protein, O and P designate DNA control sites, and Z and Y specify metabolic proteins. Writing the symbols together on a line can hide these physical differences, so the first task in solving a genetic question is to translate each symbol into its molecular role.

One can think of the DNA molecule as the address at which the regulatory event occurs. The operator remains part of that particular molecule even in a cell containing a second copy of the lac region. The repressor protein, by contrast, is released from the site at which it was synthesized and can interact with compatible operators elsewhere in the same cytoplasm. The difference between a fixed regulatory address and a movable regulatory product is the basis of cis and trans reasoning. It explains why a second functional copy of lacI can restore repression to an operon that lacks its own functional lacI product, but cannot repair an operator whose DNA sequence no longer supports appropriate repressor binding.

4. LacI structure, recognition and allosteric control

The LacI protein has to recognize two very different molecular partners. One is a DNA sequence, whose bases and backbone form a negatively charged double helix. The other is a small galactoside molecule, whose chemical structure contains hydroxyl groups capable of forming specific interactions within a ligand-binding pocket. The two recognition processes occur in different functional regions of the protein and are coupled by changes in protein conformation. The regulatory consequence of ligand binding depends on the coupling between these regions, not merely on whether the protein can bind the ligand at all.

The N-terminal DNA-binding region of LacI contains a helix-turn-helix arrangement. Its helices participate in recognition of operator DNA. A flexible connecting region and hinge helices contribute to contacts and geometry when the protein binds its operator. The core of the protein contributes to inducer recognition, dimerization and allosteric communication. A C-terminal region participates in tetramerization. A useful structural model treats the native tetramer as a pair of dimers; a dimer supplies the cooperating DNA-binding domains needed to recognize one operator, while the second dimer can contribute to simultaneous recognition of another operator.

DNA sequence recognition involves more than attraction between a positive protein surface and a negatively charged nucleic acid. Such attraction could help association with DNA generally, but sequence specificity requires differences in the favorability of particular contacts and shapes. Amino acid side chains can recognize chemical groups presented in the grooves of DNA. DNA deformability and the orientation of protein domains also affect binding. Changing a base in an operator may therefore reduce repressor occupancy even though the overall negative charge of the DNA is essentially unchanged. It is the specific interaction pattern that is altered.

The term affinity describes the favorability of a binding interaction under specified conditions. A protein with greater affinity for a DNA site tends to occupy that site more frequently when other factors, including protein concentration, remain the same. The uninduced form of LacI has a high affinity for its operator. Binding of an appropriate inducer shifts the protein toward conformational states in which association with the operator is less favorable. This is the essence of allostery: an interaction at one location in the protein modifies the behavior of a physically distinct functional region.

Allolactose does not need to compete for the same physical site as DNA. Instead, it binds within the ligand-recognition region and changes the distribution of molecular conformations available to the repressor. The induced protein is not necessarily destroyed, nor does it have to leave the cell. It can remain present while spending less time associated with operator DNA. Removal of the inducer reverses the shift in conformational preference, allowing the repressor to recover effective operator binding. A reversible molecular switch of this type can respond more rapidly than a system that requires the complete degradation and subsequent resynthesis of a regulatory protein.

Structural studies have helped connect this interpretation with the actual organization of the protein. In 1996, Lewis and colleagues reported structures of the lac repressor and complexes involving operator DNA or the inducing analogue IPTG. Such structures show how the DNA-binding portions and the ligand-recognition core are arranged. They also help explain the potential for a tetramer to organize two DNA contacts. A static structure does not show every motion of a protein in a living cell, however. Structural comparisons should be interpreted together with binding and genetic evidence when explaining how ligand recognition changes operator occupancy.

An important implication of the separation between the functional regions is that mutations can disrupt one property without eliminating another. A mutation in a DNA-recognition region can produce a protein that recognizes inducer but cannot bind the operator effectively. A different mutation can preserve operator binding but prevent induction, for example by damaging inducer recognition or the communication of ligand binding to the DNA-binding domain. The latter kind of defect is a molecular basis for the superrepressor phenotype. Protein quantity alone cannot distinguish these variants because an abundant regulatory protein can be functionally defective.

It is also possible for a mutation to disrupt protein folding or oligomerization. Such an alteration may reduce several activities simultaneously rather than isolate one functional region. The classical genetic symbols represent experimentally defined behaviors, not a complete list of possible amino acid changes. The designation I minus indicates loss of effective negative regulation in the model being used, but the precise molecular cause may be absence of protein, incorrect folding, defective recognition or another alteration. A question specifying an inducer-binding-defective protein that still binds the operator gives more information than a question that merely states that a substitution occurred in lacI.

LacI-mediated control is a physical equilibrium coupled to ongoing transcription. Molecules bind and dissociate through thermal motion, and their probabilities of occupying the DNA depend on concentrations and binding free energies. The operator is not a permanent mechanical lid. The regulatory output arises because occupation reduces the opportunity for productive initiation by RNA polymerase. Accordingly, even a strongly repressed system can exhibit low but nonzero expression. This residual expression becomes biologically important when we consider how a cell begins responding to lactose in the first place.

5. The uninduced state: how a bound repressor reduces transcription

Imagine first a wild-type E. coli cell growing in conditions that supply adequate energy but contain no lactose. The lacI gene is expressed separately from the lac structural genes. Its product can therefore be available even when the lac promoter produces relatively little RNA. In the absence of a sufficient concentration of inducing ligand, the repressor is biased toward states that bind operator DNA effectively. A substantial proportion of the available time at the lac control region is then spent in a state unfavorable for productive transcription. This is the molecular starting point of an uninduced cell, rather than a state in which the genes are physically absent.

RNA polymerase and its associated initiation factor recognize promoter sequences and form a transcription initiation complex. Productive initiation requires appropriate contacts with promoter DNA, opening of the DNA near the start site and progression into the early RNA synthesis steps. The presence of a large protein bound near this region can interfere with the formation or functioning of the initiation complex. Because O1 is near the lac transcription start, LacI occupancy strongly suppresses initiation from this promoter. For elementary reasoning, it is sufficient to treat operator binding as blocking productive transcription; more detailed distinctions among polymerase recruitment, initiation and promoter escape do not change the basic genetic logic.

A repressor that occupies O1 does not directly inhibit the catalytic site of an existing beta-galactosidase molecule. If an enzyme isolated from an induced culture is mixed with an appropriate substrate in a test tube, a DNA-binding regulator added to that assay is not expected simply to turn the enzyme off. The substrate and enzyme can still react if the assay conditions support catalysis. In intact bacteria the repressor reduces the future supply of enzyme by reducing the transcription of the enzyme's gene. This explains why genetic repression and inhibition of enzyme activity must not be treated as interchangeable mechanisms. They can both reduce metabolic flux, but the causal steps and characteristic times differ.

The operator is a molecular target of finite occupancy. Consider a DNA site with repressor attached, followed by an interval during which the protein dissociates. A polymerase that accesses the relevant region during a favorable interval may initiate a transcript before the repressor occupies the site again. The probability of such a productive event depends on the rates of competing molecular interactions and the availability of polymerase. Over many copies of the cell or many intervals within one cell, these occasional events produce a small baseline of transcription. The qualitative expression category called off in a typical exam question means strongly repressed relative to an induced reference, not necessarily zero RNA molecules.

This distinction can be demonstrated experimentally by comparing an assay with limited sensitivity and a sensitive reporter assay. A coarse measurement may show no detectable beta-galactosidase in the uninduced culture. A more sensitive measurement may reveal low activity. There is no contradiction if the first measurement's detection threshold is above the true basal activity. The interpretation of a result reported as zero depends on the technique, the duration of measurement and the denominator used to report the result. It is especially important in gene-regulation studies to distinguish a true null phenotype from an amount below detection.

The wild-type system enhances repression through the spatial organization of the operators. A LacI tetramer can interact with O1 and a second site in the same DNA molecule. The intervening DNA bends into a loop, and the two DNA contacts help stabilize a repressive configuration. A freely diffusing repressor must encounter an operator in the three-dimensional cell environment, whereas a repressor already anchored at one nearby operator is positioned to make another local contact. The availability of a second binding interaction can increase the overall favorability of occupancy compared with a single isolated site. The actual amount of repression depends on the operator sequences, their relative locations and the physical cost of forming a loop.

DNA bending has an energetic cost because a double helix resists deformation. A loop is favored only when the combination of protein-DNA contacts and the geometry of the complex makes the resulting configuration sufficiently favorable. This principle explains why moving an auxiliary operator while leaving its sequence unchanged can alter repression. The rotational alignment of the DNA helix affects the orientation of two potential protein contacts, so distance is not the only geometrical variable. At examination level, one need not calculate the mechanics of individual DNA loops; the relevant inference is that operator position and protein oligomerization can affect the regulation even when the primary recognition sequence remains intact.

In 1990, Oehler and colleagues investigated the contribution of the three operators to repression of the chromosomal lac operon. Disrupting auxiliary sites reduced repression, with loss of both causing a particularly large decrease relative to the intact operator arrangement. The behavior of tetrameric and dimeric repressor forms helped link enhanced repression to the ability to form DNA loops. These findings refine the one-operator picture rather than contradict it. The simplified picture is adequate when the question concerns cis linkage and the effect of an idealized operator mutation; the multiple-operator picture is needed when the question specifies changes in auxiliary sites or the ability of a protein to bridge DNA regions.

A useful consequence of the basal-state mechanism is that changing the amount of LacI can influence the rate of residual transcription. If operator occupancy is already very high, modest extra repressor might cause little further reduction in output. If occupancy is incomplete, an increase in the concentration of DNA-binding repressor may have a measurable effect. This dependence also explains why copy number matters in engineered systems that contain many operators. A regulatory protein supplied in a fixed amount can be distributed among multiple binding sites. The elementary single-copy problem usually assumes that enough functional repressor is present for the expected wild-type behavior, but that assumption should not be silently extended to a high-copy experimental system.

6. Induction: from extracellular lactose to a change in operator occupancy

The uninduced cell contains small amounts of the proteins encoded by the lac operon as a consequence of residual expression and, depending on its history, proteins inherited from earlier growth. When extracellular lactose becomes available, some of the sugar can enter through existing lactose permease. LacY couples galactoside transport to the movement of protons down their electrochemical gradient. The gradient maintained by cellular metabolism can therefore supply the free energy required for transport. No ATP molecule is hydrolyzed directly by LacY during each transport cycle; its operation depends on the energetic state of the membrane.

After lactose reaches the cytoplasm, the catalytic activity of beta-galactosidase becomes relevant. The usual hydrolytic reaction yields glucose and galactose. The enzyme can also catalyse transfer of a galactosyl group to a sugar acceptor rather than water, allowing the formation of allolactose. In allolactose the galactosyl group is connected to glucose through a beta-1,6 linkage. The two sugars are therefore chemically distinguishable even though both contain the same constituent monosaccharides. The different position of the glycosidic linkage changes the three-dimensional arrangement of functional groups that a protein can recognize.

The distinction between lactose and allolactose clarifies why a mutation in a structural gene can alter regulation indirectly. A lacZ defect that eliminates all relevant catalytic activity can reduce production of the natural inducer from lactose, even if the cell contains a normal lacI gene and an unaltered operator. This does not turn lacZ into the gene encoding the repressor. Instead, it shows that an enzyme involved in the metabolism of an environmental substance can generate an intracellular signal for another protein. The genotype may affect the amount of inducer available without altering the intrinsic ability of LacI to respond to that inducer.

As allolactose accumulates to a sufficient concentration, it interacts with LacI and changes the relative favorability of its DNA-binding states. A greater fraction of the repressor population occupies conformations with reduced affinity for operator DNA. Consequently, the probability that a given operator is occupied by a strongly repressing configuration decreases. Productive transcription initiation becomes more frequent. The important mechanistic event is relief of inhibition, often called derepression. The inducer does not supply the information contained in the structural genes and does not need to bind the promoter as a transcription factor.

The increased frequency of initiation leads to a greater supply of lacZYA RNA. As the RNA is translated, more beta-galactosidase and permease become available. Under suitable conditions, the additional permease can increase lactose entry and the additional enzyme can increase allolactose formation. This creates a potential reinforcing relationship between the initial response and the generation of the inducer. Such feedback does not imply that induction must increase without bound. The availability of external lactose, the rates of hydrolysis and transport, the finite capacity of transcription and translation, and the removal or metabolism of the inducer limit the response.

It is useful to follow the direction of the causal arrows carefully. Lactose outside the cell affects lactose inside the cell; intracellular lactose can be converted into allolactose; allolactose affects the repressor; the repressor affects transcription; transcription affects the supply of the proteins that mediate transport and metabolism. A change in one element may alter more than one later outcome. For example, impairing the proton motive force could decrease induction by reducing transport even though the biochemical properties of purified LacI are unchanged. A failure to induce in an intact cell is therefore not sufficient proof of a mutation in lacI.

Conversely, a substance capable of entering the cell and binding the repressor without first requiring conversion by beta-galactosidase can bypass the natural signal-production step. This is one reason the synthetic analogue isopropyl beta-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, abbreviated IPTG, is widely used to study lac regulation. It acts as a gratuitous inducer: it can induce expression without serving as the ordinary metabolized lactose substrate. In an engineered experiment where sufficient IPTG reaches the cytoplasm, a cell can show induction of an intact lac-controlled reporter even if its native beta-galactosidase is inactive. The experimental ability to separate ligand sensing from lactose catabolism is a powerful tool for locating a defect.

IPTG entry can itself depend on transport and concentration, so the presence of IPTG outside a cell must not automatically be equated with a specified intracellular concentration. At appropriately chosen conditions, IPTG can enter sufficiently to induce without requiring normal LacY-mediated uptake, but an assay must establish that delivery is adequate when comparing permease mutants. An experiment with purified repressor avoids the membrane-transport issue entirely. A corresponding experiment using intact cells tests the combined consequences of transport, ligand availability and molecular response. These two experiments answer different questions.

The term induction is sometimes used informally to refer to the addition of lactose, but this wording can hide the chain of events that follows. An experimentally supplied nutrient is not necessarily the same as the regulatory ligand. Similarly, a ligand that induces expression is not necessarily a nutrient. Lactose and IPTG can both lead to increased lac transcription in suitable settings, yet they play different roles in cellular metabolism. Comparing them illustrates why a regulatory molecule is identified by its interaction with the regulatory system, rather than by its ability to support growth.

The induced state is also not a permanent change in the regulatory gene. When a sufficient concentration of ligand is maintained, the balance of repressor conformations favors weaker operator association. When the ligand concentration decreases, that balance changes again. A wild-type cell can move in either direction without genetic alteration. This reversibility distinguishes physiological induction from a constitutive mutant, whose behavior persists across environmental changes because a component of its regulatory mechanism is altered.

7. Turning the system down: signal removal and response times

The response to lactose removal provides as much information about the regulatory mechanism as the response to lactose addition. Suppose a culture has been induced and then transferred to an otherwise suitable medium without lactose. The internal pool of lactose is no longer replenished through uptake. Remaining lactose and allolactose may be consumed or removed over time. As the free concentration of the inducing ligand falls, a greater proportion of LacI can return to DNA-binding states. The primary operator again becomes more effectively occupied, and the rate of productive initiation decreases.

It would be incorrect to assume that RNA synthesis, translation and enzyme activity must all return to their original levels at exactly the same instant. Existing messenger RNA can still be translated while it remains intact. Beta-galactosidase and permease molecules already present can continue functioning. Depending on protein stability and cell division, their abundance can decline substantially more slowly than transcript abundance. A measurement of transcription initiation therefore tends to reveal the regulatory transition more directly than a measurement of the total amount of a stable protein. This is a general principle in analyzing any switch that controls production rather than destruction.

Consider a cell population in which the induced beta-galactosidase concentration remains approximately constant immediately after withdrawal of lactose. One explanation is that transcription remains high. Another is that transcription is already low but the previously produced enzyme is stable. A short time-course measurement of lac RNA can discriminate between these explanations, provided changes in RNA removal are also considered. If RNA declines rapidly while enzyme remains high, the data support a difference in the timescales of RNA and protein turnover. The observation would not, by itself, show exactly how quickly LacI occupied the operator, because RNA abundance also depends on RNA degradation.

The interpretation of an enzyme time course depends on whether activity is reported per culture volume, per cell, per unit of protein or per unit of biomass. An actively dividing culture can contain an increasing total quantity of an enzyme even while the amount per cell falls. For example, a stable enzyme distributed equally among daughter cells will be diluted on a per-cell basis if very little new enzyme is made. Total enzyme in the entire culture need not fall simply because individual cells contain less. Such accounting is central to interpreting induction and repression experiments on growing bacteria.

Regulation at the level of initiation can be represented by a simple balance equation for the average number of transcripts per cell. Let m denote transcript abundance and a the average synthesis rate, measured in transcripts per cell per minute. If removal and effective dilution are approximately proportional to the number of transcripts, their combined first-order constant can be represented by k in inverse minutes. The change in abundance per minute is then dm/dt = a - km. This is not a model of the detailed LacI binding mechanism; it translates an upstream change in transcription rate into an expected downstream RNA time course.

At a steady state the abundance is unchanged, giving a = km and hence m = a/k. When the transcription rate changes from a high value to a lower constant value at time zero, the new steady abundance is determined by the lower rate and the same removal constant if removal is unaffected. Subtracting that new steady value from m produces a quantity whose rate of change equals minus k times itself. Integration gives m(t) = mnew + (mold - mnew) exp(-kt). The exponential term shows why existing RNA is not immediately lost when a promoter is repressed.

As a hypothetical illustration, take a culture with 42 lac transcripts per cell on average before inducer withdrawal. Suppose the new steady abundance is two transcripts per cell and the effective transcript half-life is three minutes. After one half-life, half of the excess above the new steady level remains. The predicted abundance is 2 + 40/2 = 22 transcripts per cell. After six minutes it is 2 + 40/4 = 12, and after nine minutes it is 2 + 40/8 = 7. The average can be noninteger in a population even though an individual cell contains a whole number of molecules. The nonzero final level represents residual transcription, not a failure of repression.

The assumptions matter. If ligand is not cleared rapidly, the synthesis rate a may itself change gradually, and the simple equation with an instantaneous switch will not fit exactly. If removal changes when the carbon source changes, the same rate constant cannot be used for both states. If the cells cease dividing, their growth-related dilution term decreases. The simple model is most useful as a null expectation under controlled conditions; deviations invite tests of the assumptions rather than immediate claims of a new regulatory pathway.

A cell's previous history also affects its response to the next lactose exposure. A recently induced cell may retain permease and beta-galactosidase, allowing uptake and signal formation sooner than in a cell that has spent many generations without lactose. A difference in induction delay can therefore reflect inherited molecules, not necessarily a heritable DNA change. This type of physiological memory should not be confused with the specialized mechanisms of stable epigenetic regulation studied later in the curriculum. The immediate cause here can be persistence and partitioning of ordinary gene products.

Repeated exposure and withdrawal demonstrate that the control system operates in a cellular environment that is never static. Protein-DNA and protein-ligand interactions influence transcription, whereas transport, metabolism, RNA lifetime and growth influence how quickly the transcriptional change becomes visible as a functional phenotype. A well-designed examination problem specifies which variable is measured. A well-designed experiment measures enough variables to distinguish a change at the operator from a delayed change in the abundance or activity of the metabolic machinery.

8. A quantitative model of operator binding and derepression

A binding equilibrium offers a useful way to relate the molecular interaction of LacI with DNA to the likelihood that the operator is available for transcription. Begin with an idealized operator O and a free DNA-binding repressor species R. If R and O form a complex RO, the simplest reversible equilibrium is R + O ⇌ RO. The dissociation constant is Kd = [R][O]/[RO], where square brackets denote concentrations. All three concentrations must be expressed in compatible units. The resulting Kd has the units of concentration, and a smaller value corresponds to stronger binding under the stated conditions.

The total concentration of the operator is [O]total = [O] + [RO]. The occupied fraction, f, is defined as [RO]/[O]total. Rearranging the dissociation expression gives [RO] = [R][O]/Kd. Substitution into the fraction and cancellation of the common free-operator term yields f = [R]/(Kd + [R]). The free fraction is 1 - f = Kd/(Kd + [R]). These formulas concern the occupancy of a single independent site in equilibrium with a specified free concentration of the binding species. They do not assume that all cellular repressor molecules are free, nor that every form of the repressor has identical affinity.

If the unbound operator allows productive transcription at a rate vmax and an occupied operator permits none, the mean rate in this idealization is v = vmax(1 - f). A more realistic elementary expression can include a small rate vbound in the occupied state, giving v = vmax(1 - f) + vbound f. The first expression corresponds to the simplifying assumption that all residual transcription occurs during unoccupied intervals. The second allows some productive initiation even in configurations categorized as occupied. Both relate an average transcriptional rate to the probabilities of underlying states, rather than requiring that each cell exhibit the same rate at every instant.

Take a hypothetical free DNA-binding repressor concentration of 20 nM and a dissociation constant of 1 nM for the uninduced operator interaction. Substituting into the occupancy formula gives f = 20/(1 + 20) = 20/21, or about 0.952. The unoccupied fraction is about 0.0476. If a ligand shifts the effective dissociation constant to 100 nM while free repressor concentration is held fixed for the purpose of the example, f becomes 20/(100 + 20) = 1/6, or about 0.167. The unoccupied fraction is now 5/6, or approximately 0.833. Thus a change in binding affinity can strongly increase promoter availability without changing the amount of repressor in the calculation.

Under the model with zero transcription from an occupied site, the ratio of induced to uninduced initiation rates in this example is (5/6)/(1/21) = 17.5. This ratio is dimensionless. It does not mean the amount of beta-galactosidase necessarily increases exactly 17.5-fold immediately, since downstream RNA and protein production have their own dynamics. It would also be inappropriate to treat the illustrative dissociation constants as measured properties of the natural lac system. They are chosen to demonstrate how a change in affinity translates into an altered occupancy and a conditional prediction for expression.

The equation also reveals why increasing repressor concentration and increasing binding affinity can have similar effects on occupancy. If Kd is fixed at 10 nM, raising free repressor from 10 nM to 90 nM changes occupancy from one half to nine tenths. If free repressor instead remains at 10 nM while Kd decreases from 10 nM to approximately 1.11 nM, occupancy again approaches nine tenths. A single transcriptional measurement cannot identify whether the difference arose from abundance or affinity. An assay of protein concentration or an independent DNA-binding experiment is needed to distinguish the alternatives.

At half occupancy, [R] = Kd in this simple model. Students often mistake Kd for the total amount of protein added to an assay at half occupancy. That identification is valid only when the free concentration is approximately equal to the total or when the free concentration is measured directly. If the amount of DNA is high enough to bind a substantial fraction of the protein, the added protein concentration exceeds the free concentration. A calculation that ignores this difference can yield a misleading estimate of affinity. In genetic reasoning the same caution appears when extra operator copies can redistribute a finite pool of regulator.

The binding reaction can also be related to kinetics. If association is described by a second-order rate constant kon and dissociation by a first-order constant koff, equilibrium requires kon[R][O] = koff[RO]. Rearrangement gives Kd = koff/kon. In a concentration system using molar units, kon has units of inverse molarity per second and koff has units of inverse seconds; their ratio has units of molarity. Two interactions with the same Kd can therefore have different individual association and dissociation rates. They may give similar equilibrium occupancy yet differ in how rapidly they approach equilibrium after an environmental change.

For example, an illustrative binding interaction with kon = 2 × 10^7 M^-1 s^-1 and koff = 0.02 s^-1 has Kd = 10^-9 M, or 1 nM. An interaction with kon = 2 × 10^6 M^-1 s^-1 and koff = 0.002 s^-1 has the same Kd. The second complex has a lower dissociation rate; for a simple first-order dissociation process with no rebinding, its mean lifetime is 1/koff = 500 seconds rather than 50 seconds. The association rate constant is also lower. Equality of the equilibrium constant is therefore not evidence that the two interactions will have identical temporal responses.

A complete description of the native lac switch would include the binding of ligand to multiple subunits, the presence of several operators, interactions with other transcriptional regulators, variations in local DNA configuration and possible nonequilibrium steps in RNA synthesis. Those details are not needed to use the elementary model as a reasoning tool. When the question explicitly assumes one site, one DNA-binding repressor species and equilibrium, the occupancy equation is appropriate. When the question provides multiple operators and a looping-dependent protein, the equation cannot simply be applied independently and multiplied without considering the coupling between states. The value of a quantitative model lies in understanding its assumptions, not in forcing a complex living system to obey an oversimplified formula.

9. Single-copy mutants: locate the defect before predicting expression

The genotype of a bacterium specifies which alleles it carries. The phenotype describes a measurable property, such as its ability to produce active beta-galactosidase in the absence or presence of inducer. The same observed phenotype can be produced by mutations at different positions in the genetic system. A useful approach to the lac operon begins not by memorizing whether each genotype is on or off, but by asking which physical interaction or biochemical step the specified allele changes. The mutation may affect the availability of the regulator, its ability to recognize DNA, the response to ligand, the DNA recognition site, transcription initiation or the activity of a structural gene product.

Consider a wild-type cell with the genotype I+ P+ O+ Z+ Y+ A+. Without an adequate concentration of inducer, its operator can be occupied by a functional repressor, and expression is low. With sufficient inducer inside the cell and an otherwise permissive environment, repression is relieved and expression increases. Both regulatory states are compatible with the same structural gene sequences. The genotype is constant, whereas the distribution of regulatory molecular states changes with the environment. In a two-column examination table, the entries low and high summarize these conditional outcomes.

Now suppose the lacI allele is a simple loss of function, denoted I−. For the standard exam model, assume this allele produces no effective repressor and does not interfere with another functional repressor that might be supplied later. In a cell containing only this lac region, the operator remains a normal potential target, but the functional regulator needed to occupy it is missing. In the absence of inducer, negative control is lost. In the presence of inducer, there is still no effective LacI-mediated repression to relieve. The lac promoter is therefore available in both conditions, subject to the other influences on transcription. This phenotype is constitutive with respect to the lactose-specific negative control system.

The word constitutive must be interpreted relative to the regulatory input being tested. If glucose-dependent global regulation or an unrelated physiological limitation reduces transcription, an I− mutant is not guaranteed to synthesize maximal amounts of enzyme under every conceivable condition. In a comparison in which the carbon-regulatory background remains constant and permits expression, the mutant lacks the normal dependence on the inducing ligand. That is the property the notation is intended to capture. A constitutive mutant may still need a functional promoter, RNA polymerase, ribosomes, energy and intact coding information to produce an active protein.

A different mutant contains the allele Oc, where the superscript c indicates a constitutive operator. In the elementary model, this operator can no longer be recognized effectively by LacI. The cell may contain abundant normal repressor, and the repressor may still respond normally to allolactose, but neither fact restores the missing recognition interaction at that particular DNA molecule. The phenotype of a single-copy I+ Oc strain is therefore similar to the simple I− strain: expression is derepressed in both inducer conditions. An assay of beta-galactosidase under these two conditions cannot by itself decide which genotype the cell carries.

The two explanations nevertheless make distinct predictions when additional information is introduced. In the I− mutant, the recognition site is still intact, so a functional repressor supplied from another copy of lacI can restore normal regulation. In the Oc mutant, providing more wild-type LacI does not restore normal repression of the defective recognition site in the idealized model. This difference is not merely terminology about dominance. It follows from whether the missing function is supplied by a diffusible product or is a property of the target sequence itself. It provides the basis for experiments involving two copies of selected genetic regions in one cell.

Real operator mutations can weaken rather than abolish binding. Suppose a sequence change raises the dissociation constant tenfold while leaving measurable affinity for LacI. A sufficiently high free repressor concentration could still lead to appreciable occupancy. The resulting phenotype may be partial constitutivity rather than the completely unregulated state of the standard Oc problem. A question that gives experimental binding data requires attention to the stated affinity and concentration. One should not automatically replace the data with an absolute rule learned for an idealized mutant.

A third regulatory phenotype is produced by the superrepressor allele Is. In the usual model, the encoded protein can recognize and repress O+ DNA, but it does not lose effective operator binding in response to inducer. A lesion in the ligand-binding region or in allosteric coupling can produce such a functional separation. The bacterium is then unable to induce the normal lac promoter effectively even when the cytoplasm contains sufficient inducing ligand. In both conditions, operator occupancy remains unfavorable for transcription. The superrepressor phenotype does not mean that the regulator must bind DNA more strongly than the uninduced wild-type regulator. The crucial characteristic is its inability to be switched to an adequately nonrepressing state by inducer.

A promoter loss-of-function mutation, denoted P− in a simplified problem, gives a superficially similar low-output phenotype in both conditions. The molecular reason differs. In the superrepressor strain, the machinery is competent to transcribe the operon if the obstacle imposed by the repressor is removed. In the severe promoter mutant, releasing the repressor does not restore a promoter that cannot initiate productively. A strain carrying a nonfunctional promoter together with an operator-constitutive allele remains transcriptionally defective from that promoter. Removing an inhibitory step cannot replace the positive capability required to initiate transcription.

The coding genes create another layer of interpretation. A cell with a nonpolar Z− mutation may retain normal transcriptional control of the lac region, yet fail to produce active beta-galactosidase. An enzyme assay for beta-galactosidase would give low or absent activity with and without inducer, even if a transcript assay showed induction. In such a problem the term nonpolar is important: it states that the mutation does not reduce expression of downstream coding regions through a secondary effect on the polycistronic message. Without this specification, a premature stop or disruptive insertion can have consequences for later genes, and the independent coding-gene model may no longer be appropriate.

An examination question may ask whether the bacterium grows on lactose rather than whether a promoter is transcribed. The two phenotypes must be kept distinct. Growth on lactose requires that the cells obtain usable metabolic products and possess other essential metabolic functions. Derepressed expression of a defective Z gene does not restore its missing catalytic activity. A Y− mutant may produce functional beta-galactosidase while still having inadequate lactose uptake. A cell carrying an A− allele may retain the core functions needed for lactose catabolism. The regulatory classification must be made from the particular measured phenotype, not from an assumption that every lac-associated mutation changes all readouts in the same direction.

10. The logic of cis and trans: a common cytoplasm, two DNA templates

Bacteria ordinarily carry a single copy of many genes per completed chromosome, but bacterial genetic analysis can introduce an additional copy of a restricted chromosome region, for example on an F prime element. A cell with two copies of a particular genetic segment is called a partial diploid or merodiploid. It is not necessary for the whole bacterial genome to be present as two complete homologous sets. The usefulness of the arrangement is that the two copies of a control region reside in the same cellular environment. Their products may be shared even though their local DNA sequences remain distinct.

A genetic element acts in cis when the relevant effect depends on physical linkage to the target. An operator sequence provides a site at a particular location on a DNA molecule. It cannot detach itself from that molecule and move to another promoter simply because an additional copy of the operon is present. A functional O+ site on one DNA molecule does not substitute for an Oc site on the other. The two sites may both encounter proteins from the same cytoplasm, but their capacity to recognize those proteins is determined by their own sequences and local arrangements.

The gene encoding a repressor has a different relationship with its target. Translation produces a protein molecule that can interact with compatible operator sequences regardless of which DNA copy supplied the instructions for its synthesis. This is trans action. If the first lac region contains I− and the second I+, the functional protein supplied by the second can repress either O+ region in the absence of inducer. One functional lacI copy is usually sufficient for normal negative control in the standard partial-diploid model. The assumption is that the protein is made in a sufficient amount and that the mutant allele does not generate a product that interferes with the wild-type protein.

The phrase trans-acting gene is convenient but slightly imprecise if interpreted literally. The lacI DNA remains at its own location. It is the product encoded by lacI that can act in trans. Conversely, the operator is not a gene that encodes a small diffusible regulatory molecule in the standard lac model. It is a recognition site on DNA. This molecular distinction resolves many otherwise confusing statements about the dominance of different regulatory mutations. A DNA control site that changes the behavior of its linked genes and a diffusible protein that affects two separate DNA templates cannot be analyzed using exactly the same reasoning.

Consider the genotype I− P+ O+ Z+ / I+ P+ O+ Z−. Here the slash separates two lac regions in the same cell, and Y and A are omitted because the question concerns beta-galactosidase expression. The I+ copy supplies normal repressor despite carrying a defective Z allele. In the absence of inducer, both O+ sites can be occupied. The Z+ gene on the first copy is consequently repressed. When sufficient inducer reaches the common cytoplasm, repression at both normal operators is relieved. The first copy can then generate active beta-galactosidase, whereas the second cannot generate active enzyme because its Z allele is defective. The measurable phenotype is inducible beta-galactosidase production.

This example is a form of functional complementation. The missing regulatory activity associated with the first copy is supplied by the second copy. Complementation does not require exchange or repair of DNA sequences. If the second copy is subsequently lost, the remaining I− operon would return to its constitutive behavior, provided no other genetic change occurred. That distinction separates complementation from recombination, in which a new physical combination of alleles may be created. Genetic experiments can involve both processes, so a stable recombinant must not be confused with a cell that displays a phenotype only while it contains both original copies.

Now change the first region to I+ P+ Oc Z+ and retain I+ P+ O+ Z− as the second. In the absence of inducer, functional repressor can occupy the O+ site of the second copy but cannot repress the idealized Oc site of the first. The first copy produces functional beta-galactosidase without inducer. In the induced state, the first remains derepressed, while the second becomes transcriptionally available but still cannot produce active beta-galactosidase. The observable enzyme phenotype is constitutive. Supplying an additional O+ site has not corrected the altered recognition property of Oc.

The marker arrangement can reverse the observed phenotype without changing which control sequences are present in the cell. For I+ P+ Oc Z− / I+ P+ O+ Z+, the first region is constitutively transcribed but cannot supply functional beta-galactosidase. The second region can supply functional enzyme, but its O+ site is subject to the normal repressor. Without inducer, the active enzyme output is low even though one lac region is derepressed. With inducer, the second region contributes active enzyme. Thus the measured beta-galactosidase phenotype is inducible. It is insufficient to notice that Oc is present somewhere in the genotype; its linkage to the functional coding allele determines whether the chosen assay reveals constitutivity.

The same reasoning can be extended to two different structural markers. Let the first copy contain Oc Z+ Y−, and the second O+ Z− Y+. With normal repressor available, active beta-galactosidase is produced from the first copy in both conditions, whereas functional permease is produced primarily from the second copy after induction. An experiment measuring the two enzyme-related activities separately could classify Z output as constitutive and Y output as inducible. A single classification for the entire cell would miss this difference. The example demonstrates the value of associating each coding allele with its linked control region before considering the sum of gene products in the common cytoplasm.

The molecular functions of beta-galactosidase and permease are also shared at the cell level. Permease produced from one DNA region can facilitate lactose uptake for the same cell containing the other DNA region. Beta-galactosidase produced from either region can generate allolactose in that cell. Consequently, when natural lactose is used rather than an experimentally supplied intracellular inducer, expression on one copy can influence ligand availability for the other. Most classical genotype exercises avoid this extra feedback by specifying adequate inducer or by explicitly holding inducer availability constant. A more physiological question may require the student to account for the state of these structural genes before deciding whether an inducer is actually present inside the cell.

The general principle extends beyond the lac system. A diffusible transcription regulator may act on several genes sharing a compatible recognition site, whereas a local regulatory sequence usually modifies the expression of its linked region. In eukaryotic genetics an enhancer mutation and a mutation affecting a diffusible transcription factor are analogous distinctions, even though the mechanisms and chromosomal geometry may be different. The lac partial-diploid system provides a particularly transparent way to derive the distinction from experimental outcomes rather than to treat cis and trans as arbitrary labels.

11. Superrepressor, linked operator mutations and epistasis

A superrepressor can be understood by separating the two functions of LacI that are normally linked through allostery. A wild-type repressor recognizes O+ and reduces transcription when the inducer is absent. It also responds to sufficient inducing ligand by shifting toward states of reduced operator affinity. A typical Is allele retains the first function but lacks the second. Thus the protein can act as a negative regulator under both conditions. The defect is in the inducibility of repression, not necessarily in the production of the protein.

Consider the partial diploid Is P+ O+ Z− / I+ P+ O+ Z+. Both repressor variants can reach the O+ operator on the second copy. When no inducer is present, both kinds of functional DNA-binding repressor can contribute to repression. When inducer is supplied, the normal LacI population becomes less effective at occupying operators, but the superrepressor population remains capable of doing so. In the usual genetic model there is enough superrepressor to maintain repression of both O+ regions. The only functional Z allele is therefore not induced. This is why Is is normally described as dominant to I+ for inducibility and as acting in trans.

The dominance of Is is not equivalent to dominance of a DNA site such as Oc. A typical superrepressor influences both recognizable operator targets in the shared cytoplasm. Oc influences the transcription of the physically linked genes. Consider Is P+ O+ Z− / I+ P+ Oc Z+. The superrepressor can still repress O+ on the first copy, but the functional Z allele is linked to an operator that it cannot effectively recognize in the simplified model. Beta-galactosidase is produced in both conditions. The presence of Is does not guarantee that every lac structural gene in the cell is repressed; effective repression requires a compatible DNA target.

Switch the coding markers while preserving the regulatory arrangements: Is P+ O+ Z+ / I+ P+ Oc Z−. The only functional beta-galactosidase gene now lies downstream of the normal operator. The superrepressor prevents effective induction of that copy. The other copy may be constitutively transcribed, but it has no functional Z product to contribute to the assay. The cell therefore shows no substantial active beta-galactosidase induction. This comparison illustrates the need to follow local linkage even when a trans-dominant regulator is present.

Epistasis refers to a situation in which the effect of one genetic change masks or modifies the observable effect of another. A simple example is a severe P− mutation masking the derepression that would otherwise result from Oc. A functioning promoter is required for transcriptional output. If the promoter is effectively inactive, changing the operator from O+ to Oc cannot create a productive initiation site. The genotype P− Oc therefore has low transcription under both inducer conditions in a single-promoter model. One can recognize the ordering of dependencies without assuming that all epistatic interactions share the same molecular mechanism.

The same distinction arises if a reporter requires a functional structural gene. An O+ copy and an Oc copy may differ in transcriptional activity, yet a Z− lesion can mask that difference in an assay of active beta-galactosidase from the affected copy. Here the limiting step is not promoter function but coding function. If the student changes the readout from enzyme activity to messenger RNA abundance, the apparently masked effect may become visible. Epistasis is therefore interpreted relative to a phenotype. It is not necessarily a statement that one molecular defect physically reverses another.

A further complication arises from the oligomeric nature of LacI. The standard I− allele in an elementary merodiploid exercise is often treated as an absence of functional repressor or a noninterfering loss-of-function product. Some real mutant proteins can instead interact with wild-type subunits and interfere with formation or function of active oligomers. Such an allele may exhibit dominant-negative behavior, in which a defective product reduces the function supplied by a normal allele. This possibility should not be assumed for every I− mutation. The problem must specify the molecular behavior or provide evidence that distinguishes it from a simple null allele.

Likewise, different superrepressor mutations need not all retain identical binding affinities, ligand responses or oligomerization properties. The textbook Is notation represents an experimentally defined class with characteristic noninducible repression, and the idealized problem assumes a sufficiently active mutant protein. If a real data set shows partial induction in a cell carrying a superrepressor-like allele, the result might reflect incomplete dominance, a residual ligand response, differences in regulator dosage or another feature of the experimental conditions. The simplest model remains useful as a reference, but the actual observations determine whether further explanation is needed.

When two mutations appear together, a helpful method is to ask whether the effect of one requires the molecular component altered by the other. The ability of Is to prevent induction requires an operator that can be recognized. The ability of Oc to increase transcription requires a functional promoter. The ability of an induced promoter to generate active enzyme requires an intact enzyme-coding sequence and downstream expression machinery. Each relationship defines a dependency. Following the sequence of dependencies is a more reliable way to reason through a complex genotype than attempting to memorize every possible combination of superscripts.

12. A systematic method for solving partial-diploid questions

Start by identifying the requested output. A problem asking for beta-galactosidase activity is not identical to a problem asking for permease activity, lac messenger RNA abundance or growth on lactose. If the output is active Z product, only copies with a functional Z allele can contribute directly. If the output is active Y product, the Y alleles determine the contributions. If the problem asks for transcription, an inactive coding gene may still produce RNA. The requested phenotype determines which parts of the genotype need to be tracked through to the final answer.

Next read the regulator alleles across the entire cell. Treat I+ as a source of inducer-sensitive repressor, a simple I− as no effective repressor and Is as a source of inducer-insensitive repressor capable of acting on normal operators. In the standard model, the shared cytoplasm contains the combined functional regulatory products of both copies. Under uninduced conditions, I+ and Is can repress O+ targets; under induced conditions, I+ is relieved sufficiently for expression, whereas Is remains effective. This first step establishes the regulatory environment that each DNA template encounters.

Now inspect each promoter and operator separately. A promoter that cannot initiate remains inactive in the model regardless of whether repression is removed. A normal promoter linked to O+ responds to the repressor pool just identified. A normal promoter linked to Oc is not effectively subject to that negative control. This operation must be performed independently for both copies because the DNA sequences remain physically linked to their own coding regions. The presence of an O+ site elsewhere in the same cell is not a reason to assign normal regulation to an Oc-linked structural gene.

After determining transcriptional availability for each copy, examine the relevant coding marker. A functional allele downstream of an available promoter can contribute active product under the simplifying assumptions. A nonfunctional allele cannot contribute that product, even when highly transcribed. Finally, add the contributions to determine the cell-level phenotype. If one copy produces functional enzyme constitutively and another adds an inducible contribution, the overall phenotype is constitutive with a further inducible increase, not simply identical output in both conditions. A binary table may conceal the increase because it records only detectable presence or absence.

For a worked example, assume two copies produce identical amounts of active Z protein when fully derepressed and negligible amounts when repressed. The genotype is I+ P+ Oc Z+ / I− P+ O+ Z+. The I+ copy supplies wild-type repressor to the common cytoplasm. Without inducer, the first promoter is available because of Oc, but the second is repressed at O+. One expression unit is produced. With sufficient inducer, repression at O+ is relieved and both functional Z alleles contribute, giving two units. The cell produces beta-galactosidase in both conditions, but the total doubles on induction in this idealized calculation. A statement that constitutive expression necessarily precludes any further induction would be wrong for this mixed arrangement.

If each copy instead contributes a different amount of product, the same logic applies with different numerical weights. Suppose the Oc-linked Z+ copy contributes 30 activity units when available, while the O+-linked Z+ copy contributes 70. Let the latter retain five percent of its fully derepressed output when repressed. Without inducer, the sum is 30 + 0.05 × 70 = 33.5 units. With sufficient inducer, it is 30 + 70 = 100 units. The induced-to-uninduced ratio is approximately 2.99. These hypothetical values illustrate why regulatory classification, basal output and induction ratio describe different properties of an expression system.

The assumption that expression contributions add is appropriate only for a measured quantity that is additive under the assay conditions. Total production of a particular enzyme can often be treated this way in an elementary model, but growth rate or metabolic flux cannot generally be calculated by simply adding enzyme levels. If transport is limiting, doubling beta-galactosidase may produce little change in growth. If the enzyme substrate is limiting, the same conclusion can hold. In an activity assay using a fixed amount of extract and sufficient substrate, the measured initial rate may be proportional to the amount of active enzyme, but the relationship depends on the assay conditions.

A useful reverse problem begins with observed phenotypes and asks what genotype is consistent with them. A strain that produces beta-galactosidase constitutively could carry I−, Oc or another alteration that increases transcription in the absence of inducer. If introducing I+ restores inducibility under controlled conditions, a missing trans-acting function is supported. If it does not, an altered local operator is one explanation, but a dominant interfering repressor variant or an unrelated promoter alteration may also be possible. Distinguishing these alternatives may require reciprocal marker linkage, a repressor binding assay or direct sequence analysis. An observed pattern often narrows the hypotheses without uniquely identifying a molecular change.

In a reciprocal linkage experiment, the functional Z marker can be placed first downstream of the suspected operator on one copy and then downstream of a normal operator on the other. If constitutivity follows the physically linked marker, the observation supports a local cis effect. If a regulatory mutation affects expression from both normal operators regardless of which copy contains the marker, a diffusible regulatory activity is implicated. Care must be taken that changes in the marker do not alter the neighboring regulatory sequence or produce downstream polarity. Otherwise the two constructs may differ in more than the intended variable.

Before submitting an answer to a complex genetic question, check that the same repressor pool has been used for both DNA copies, that each local operator remains associated with the correct promoter and coding allele, and that the expression outcome corresponds to the stated assay. Check whether the question assumes a sufficient intracellular inducing ligand or instead asks how extracellular lactose generates the ligand. The same notation can lead to different conclusions if a natural-induction experiment lacks the proteins needed for uptake or signal production. This final check prevents a common error in which a correct solution to an idealized genotype problem is applied to a physiological experiment whose assumptions are different.

13. Experimental tests that distinguish the competing explanations

A useful experimental strategy begins with two rival explanations for a result rather than a technique chosen without a question. Suppose a bacterial strain shows little beta-galactosidase activity after exposure to lactose. The observation could arise because the structural gene is defective, because the operon fails to be induced, or because lactose does not reach an effective intracellular concentration. A direct activity assay alone cannot resolve these possibilities. Each explanation predicts a different pattern when independent measures of the pathway are obtained. The investigator therefore needs to choose measurements that isolate the molecular step most directly affected by each hypothesis.

One approach is to measure lac messenger RNA abundance in matched cultures with and without inducer. A reference strain known to possess a normal inducible system establishes that the environmental conditions are suitable for observing induction. A strain with a verified nonfunctional promoter provides a reference for low transcript production. A constitutive mutant provides a comparison for a strain whose expression should be relatively independent of the inducer in the chosen conditions. If the unknown strain produces abundant induced lac RNA but lacks beta-galactosidase activity, the failure cannot be explained solely by an inability to transcribe the measured region. A coding defect, translational problem or loss of catalytic function becomes more plausible.

Even this interpretation requires a careful choice of RNA measurement. An RNA probe directed at the beginning of lacZ might detect transcripts that do not extend through the full gene. A measurement of a downstream region could give a different result if premature termination occurs. In a question about polarity, measuring several regions of the polycistronic message is therefore preferable to measuring a single short region. The investigator can ask whether the change is already present near the transcription start or emerges progressively along the operon. A stable RNA fragment can also contribute to a measured signal, so abundant sequence-specific RNA is not invariably evidence of an intact translatable molecule.

A second approach uses a reporter gene placed under the control of a lac regulatory region. A reporter is a coding sequence whose product can be measured conveniently; its purpose is to reveal expression of the linked regulatory region. A reporter encoding a fluorescent protein, for example, could be useful when the unknown strain lacks functional beta-galactosidase. The reporter should be distinguished from the native lacZ locus, and the genetic context must be controlled. If the regulatory sequences are moved to a plasmid, copy number, the local arrangement of operators and the availability of repressor may differ from those in the chromosome. The engineered construct tests the control system in that context, not automatically the behavior of the unmodified chromosome.

A controlled comparison could pair an O+ reporter with an otherwise identical Oc reporter. If the O+ construct has low expression without inducer and higher expression with inducer, whereas the Oc construct is expressed in both conditions, the change is consistent with loss of a negative regulatory interaction. A control construct lacking a functional promoter should have little reporter output. Such a control distinguishes a genuine promoter-dependent signal from background fluorescence, enzyme activity or another source of measurement. An additional control that supplies a functional reporter independently of the lac promoter can test whether the strain is generally capable of producing the reporter product.

A biochemical DNA-binding assay can directly examine whether the repressor interacts with a particular DNA sequence. In an electrophoretic mobility shift assay, a DNA fragment bound by protein often migrates differently from the unbound fragment in a suitable native gel. When a labeled fragment containing O+ is mixed with purified functional LacI, a slower-moving species can indicate formation of a protein-DNA complex. Adding a sufficient inducing ligand can reduce the amount of shifted material under appropriate conditions. A fragment with a substantially altered operator can show less binding at the same protein concentration. The measured pattern supports a change in binding, provided the controls establish that the shift is associated with the intended interaction.

The appropriate controls include the DNA fragment alone, protein added at comparable concentration, and DNA fragments or competitors that test sequence specificity. If an unlabeled fragment containing a functional operator competes effectively for binding but an unrelated fragment does not compete to the same extent, specific recognition is supported. However, disappearance of a shift after adding a compound might also reflect altered buffer conditions or protein instability. The experiment should compare samples under matched conditions and, when possible, show that the protein remains functional in an independent measurement. The assay reveals binding behavior; it does not directly measure the rate of transcription in a living bacterium.

An informative hypothetical result might show strong O+ binding by both a wild-type repressor and a mutant in the absence of IPTG. Addition of IPTG strongly reduces binding by the wild-type protein but leaves the mutant's binding largely unchanged. This result supports an inducer-insensitive regulatory defect, consistent with a superrepressor-like phenotype if the protein also represses transcription in cells. It does not distinguish a mutation that prevents ligand binding from a mutation that permits ligand binding but prevents the corresponding conformational change. A separate ligand-binding assay is required to discriminate between those molecular explanations.

A purified transcription system offers another separation of variables. If a DNA template containing a functional promoter and operator is transcribed by suitable polymerase in the absence of LacI, the resulting RNA provides a baseline. Adding functional LacI can reduce transcription, and adding inducer can reverse much of this inhibition under suitable conditions. A parallel template with an altered operator can test the dependence of repression on the DNA recognition site. A promoter-defective template establishes that releasing repression cannot restore transcription from a defective initiation region. This experiment examines the direct influence of regulatory components without requiring sugar transport, metabolism or the synthesis of new cellular proteins.

No single assay establishes the entire causal pathway. Genetic complementation can reveal whether a missing function is supplied in trans, but it does not alone identify the physical nature of the product. Protein-DNA binding assays reveal molecular recognition but not necessarily the magnitude of expression changes in intact cells. Transcript measurements reveal abundance but do not automatically measure synthesis rate. Enzyme activity is a functional output but can be affected by protein stability and catalysis. The strength of the lac model comes from the consistency of predictions across these different methods, each designed to test a different link in the causal chain.

14. Biological constraints, comparison and useful applications

The lac system is closely connected with the general physiology of a bacterium. The inner membrane must maintain conditions favorable for the coupled transport of galactosides, and the cytoplasm must have the capacity to metabolize the products of lactose hydrolysis. A cell experiencing a major disruption of its energy metabolism might respond poorly to lactose even though its genetic regulatory region is normal. In that case, measuring the properties of the purified repressor could show an intact switch, while the whole-cell experiment exposes a problem upstream or downstream of the switch. This distinction is a reminder that a regulatory mechanism is embedded within the biological system whose behavior it helps determine.

The presence of glucose provides a second important constraint on the output of the lac promoter. Under the usual conditions considered for E. coli, expression is greatest when an appropriate inducer is available and glucose-dependent repression of the broader carbon utilization system is relieved. The lactose-specific repressor is not the only determinant of productive transcription. An O+ operator whose repressor has been released can still be associated with relatively weak promoter output when the activation conditions are unfavorable. The detailed cAMP–CRP regulatory mechanism, the combination of lactose and glucose conditions, and global carbon regulation will be developed in Node 3.D.04. For the present purpose, the important distinction is between removal of local negative control and attainment of the highest possible transcription rate.

Growth experiments in a medium with more than one carbon source can exhibit different phases as the cell's metabolic activities change. A population growth curve reflects nutrient use, physiological adaptation and cell division, rather than measuring occupancy of a specific operator directly. If a culture temporarily grows more slowly while adjusting to lactose utilization, the delay may reflect the time required to accumulate functional transporters and enzymes. The lag does not prove that lac genes were completely silent before the change. A different initial abundance of the relevant proteins can affect the size of the lag even when two strains have the same regulatory sequence.

Not every bacterium uses exactly the lac regulatory arrangement described for E. coli. Other organisms may organize carbohydrate-metabolizing genes differently, employ other regulators, transport carbohydrates through different mechanisms or generate distinct intracellular signals. The evolutionary principle is the ability to link the supply of useful gene products to the conditions in which they are beneficial. Conservation of that functional problem does not require conservation of every gene or operator sequence. When a question describes an unfamiliar bacterial carbohydrate system, the student should infer the sign of regulation from the described behavior rather than assume that all inducible operons are structurally identical to lac.

The contrast with a biosynthetic operon is especially instructive. The lac system increases expression of genes associated with obtaining and processing a nutrient when an appropriate signal is present. In a repressible biosynthetic system, the end product can instead provide a signal that reduces the need for further production. Both systems can use a repressor, but the regulatory ligand changes the repressor's effective DNA association in opposite directions in the simplest textbook models. In the next chapter, the tryptophan operon will illustrate this second logic and introduce an additional control step that depends on events in a leader region of the transcript. The contrast concerns what the ligand does to the regulator and what biological problem the cell needs to solve.

A common application of lac regulation is the controlled expression of a selected gene in a bacterial expression system. An engineered promoter-operator arrangement can connect the regulatory response to a coding sequence that is not naturally part of lacZYA. In the absence of inducer, expression may remain relatively low; after addition of an appropriate inducer, transcription can increase. This allows an investigator to separate a period of cell growth from a period of increased production of the desired protein. The use of IPTG is convenient because its role as an inducing ligand can be distinguished from the use of lactose as a metabolizable nutrient.

Such a system is not guaranteed to be completely silent before induction. Basal expression may be enough to affect growth if the product is burdensome or interferes with an essential cellular function. Increasing repressor availability or using an appropriate promoter arrangement can reduce background expression, but the outcome depends on the properties of the particular construct. Conversely, exceptionally strong transcription after induction can impose demands on cellular resources and does not necessarily maximize the amount of correctly folded functional protein. The regulatory switch influences the first stages of gene expression; later processes can limit the yield.

Another familiar application is the use of beta-galactosidase as a reporter. The enzyme can hydrolyze synthetic substrates that generate colored or otherwise measurable products. X-gal is an example of a chromogenic substrate whose cleavage can lead to a blue product. IPTG and X-gal have different functions in such a system: one supplies a regulatory signal, while the other provides a means of detecting enzyme activity. A colored colony indicates detectable enzymatic activity under the assay conditions. It does not directly demonstrate that a particular protein-DNA interaction occurred, and a lack of color can arise from several causes including loss of enzyme activity.

The principle of reporter analysis is valuable outside the bacterial example. A regulatory DNA sequence can be connected to a readily measured gene product, and changes in the output can provide evidence about how the regulatory region responds to a particular factor. The important controls remain the same: distinguish promoter function from regulatory recognition, compare equivalent genetic contexts, verify the integrity of the reporter, and specify the signal being measured. When the same reasoning is applied to eukaryotic regulation, additional factors such as chromatin and long-range interactions may become important. Those factors change the physical complexity but do not eliminate the distinction between local sequence, diffusible regulator and measured output.

The overall lac system illustrates a general route from a chemical signal to an altered biological capability. Its behavior can be understood by identifying which molecules are present, what each molecule recognizes, how binding alters the probability of a transcription event, and how the products of that event influence physiology. The predictive power of the model is greatest when its assumptions are made explicit. An examination question may deliberately isolate a single operator, supply adequate intracellular inducer and ignore basal expression. A whole-cell experiment may instead make uptake, growth, protein stability and alternative regulatory influences central to the result. Recognizing which kind of problem is being posed is part of mastering the biological mechanism.

15. Combining molecular, genetic and quantitative evidence

Imagine a hypothetical regulatory mutant M whose lac RNA level is close to the induced wild-type level both before and after IPTG treatment. A first observation of this kind suggests constitutive transcription under the chosen conditions. A researcher then introduces a functional lacI gene at a separate location and measures the original chromosomal transcript again. If the transcript level becomes low without IPTG and high with IPTG, the result supports a defect in a trans-complementable regulatory function. If the transcript remains high, a defect in the linked recognition site becomes a plausible explanation. Neither initial observation alone can establish which DNA sequence has changed; the second experiment tests a prediction that distinguishes two candidate mechanisms.

Suppose a different mutant N exhibits very low RNA with both inducer treatments. Functional lacI supplied in trans does not restore expression. This lack of restoration is compatible with a superrepressor but is also compatible with a defective promoter. To discriminate between them, a functional O+ promoter-reporter could be introduced into the same cell. If the reporter is also noninducible despite adequate intracellular IPTG, a diffusible inhibitory influence is supported. If the introduced reporter is inducible while the native locus remains inactive, a local defect in the native control region becomes more likely. Controls for reporter competence and ligand availability are needed in both arrangements.

An additional binding experiment can refine that conclusion. If the mutant's purified repressor binds O+ DNA and remains bound in the presence of IPTG, the result is consistent with a defect in inducer-dependent allostery. If the repressor binds normally in the absence of IPTG and responds normally to IPTG, a local promoter defect or an upstream problem in ligand delivery deserves greater consideration. In this way, the genetic experiment establishes whether the effect is shared across DNA copies, while the biochemical experiment identifies which physical interaction is altered. Neither experiment is interpreted as a substitute for the other.

Quantitative data can further reveal whether a mutation is partial rather than complete. Consider two hypothetical strains with 5 and 100 normalized transcription units in the uninduced and induced wild-type reference. Mutant M produces 50 and 100 units, whereas mutant N produces 5 and 10. M has a twofold induction ratio, with an elevated basal output; N also has a twofold induction ratio, but its induced output is greatly reduced. Their identical fold changes hide very different phenotypes. M may have weakened repression, whereas N may have a defect in maximal promoter function or another limitation. Absolute or appropriately normalized levels are needed alongside ratios.

A mechanistic conclusion should explain all observations without requiring untested assumptions to substitute for missing evidence. The lactose-sensitive control system is a case in which the environmental response, the genetics of the control region and the physical behavior of its protein product can be analyzed separately and then brought together. The ability to distinguish these layers prepares the student for the tryptophan operon, where an additional regulatory mechanism acts after transcription has begun, and for later examples in which gene expression is affected at still other stages. The goal is not to memorize a picture of LacI and its operator but to use that picture as a model from which testable predictions follow.

Part B — Conceptual practice

Original practice questions. Unless otherwise stated, promoters are functional, I− is noninterfering, Oc cannot be effectively repressed, carbon conditions are permissive, and adequate intracellular inducer is supplied. Low expression means repressed relative to the induced reference.

Question 1. Which molecule is the physiological ligand that relieves LacI repression in lactose-grown E. coli?

  1. Allolactose
  2. Glucose
  3. LacY
  4. Lactose permease
Answer and explanation

A. Allolactose. LacZ converts some lactose into allolactose, which binds LacI. Glucose is a metabolic product and LacY is the transporter; neither is the direct inducing ligand.

Question 2. Which lac element acts principally as a cis-regulatory DNA site?

  1. LacI protein
  2. β-galactosidase
  3. Allolactose
  4. O1 operator
Answer and explanation

D. O1 operator. O1 is a physically linked DNA recognition sequence. LacI protein diffuses within the cytoplasm; the enzyme and inducer are not cis DNA elements.

Question 3. What is the normal biochemical function of LacY?

  1. Hydrolysis of lactose
  2. DNA recognition at O1
  3. Coupled proton–galactoside transport
  4. ATP-dependent phosphorylation of lactose
Answer and explanation

C. Coupled proton–galactoside transport. LacY is a membrane symporter driven by an electrochemical proton gradient. LacZ hydrolyses lactose, while LacI recognizes DNA.

Question 4. Which gene encodes the repressor?

  1. lacZ
  2. lacI
  3. lacY
  4. lacA
Answer and explanation

B. lacI. lacI produces the regulatory protein independently of the lacZYA transcription unit; the other genes encode lactose-associated metabolic proteins.

Question 5. Which statement correctly distinguishes negative control from induction?

  1. Induction always requires a DNA-binding activator
  2. Induction relieves inhibition by a negative regulator
  3. Negative control means expression falls after adding inducer
  4. Induction requires destruction of the repressor
Answer and explanation

B. Induction relieves inhibition by a negative regulator. Negative refers to the effect of the bound regulator on transcription. An inducer can raise expression by reducing that inhibition without degrading the protein.

Question 6. What primarily distinguishes allolactose from lactose?

  1. The position of the β-glycosidic linkage
  2. Different monosaccharide composition
  3. A peptide bond in allolactose
  4. An ATP group on lactose
Answer and explanation

A. The position of the β-glycosidic linkage. Both contain galactose and glucose, but the linkages are β1→6 and β1→4, respectively. The difference alters the recognition geometry.

Question 7. The primary lac promoter is best described as which feature?

  1. A ligand-binding pocket on LacI
  2. A membrane transporter
  3. The complete polycistronic RNA
  4. A DNA region that supports transcription initiation
Answer and explanation

D. A DNA region that supports transcription initiation. The promoter provides a site for the transcription initiation machinery; the operator is recognized by repressor and the RNA is a product of transcription.

Question 8. What is the simplest molecular phenotype of an I− null mutation?

  1. Inability of LacZ to hydrolyze lactose
  2. Loss of a local operator only
  3. Absence of effective LacI repression
  4. A repressor that cannot respond to inducer but still binds O+
Answer and explanation

C. Absence of effective LacI repression. A simple I− null eliminates effective negative control; the last option instead describes the typical Is phenotype.

Question 9. Why can an Oc mutation remain constitutive even if LacI is normal?

  1. Oc synthesizes an inducer
  2. Oc destroys all LacI in the cell
  3. The linked operator no longer supports effective repressor recognition
  4. Oc creates an active β-galactosidase protein
Answer and explanation

C. The linked operator no longer supports effective repressor recognition. An operator alteration changes a DNA target, not the supply of repressor or catalytic function. Normal repressor can still regulate other recognizable sites.

Question 10. Which statement about the lacZYA transcript is correct?

  1. It is translated only into a single fused polypeptide
  2. It normally contains coding regions for three separate proteins
  3. It always contains lacI as a fourth cistron
  4. It is the molecule that transports lactose
Answer and explanation

B. It normally contains coding regions for three separate proteins. A polycistronic transcript contains distinct coding regions; independent translation can give different abundances. lacI has separate transcriptional control.

Question 11. What is the principal role of auxiliary operators O2 and O3?

  1. They can enhance repression through LacI-mediated DNA looping
  2. They both encode additional repressor proteins
  3. They hydrolyze lactose in the cytoplasm
  4. They replace the lac promoter in every induced cell
Answer and explanation

A. They can enhance repression through LacI-mediated DNA looping. Auxiliary operators provide additional DNA contacts for tetrameric LacI. They are not coding genes or metabolic enzymes.

Question 12. Which result best describes an ideal Is allele at an otherwise normal operon?

  1. High expression only without inducer
  2. Constitutive maximal expression in every medium
  3. Loss of permease activity with normal RNA induction
  4. Low expression both without and with adequate inducer
Answer and explanation

D. Low expression both without and with adequate inducer. A superrepressor retains operator repression but fails to respond effectively to the inducer. A Y coding defect is a different phenotype.

Question 13. Which process is directly reduced by LacI occupying O1?

  1. Hydrolysis by existing LacZ
  2. ATP hydrolysis by existing ribosomes
  3. Copying of the entire bacterial chromosome
  4. Productive transcription initiation
Answer and explanation

D. Productive transcription initiation. Operator occupancy interferes with expression from the lac promoter. Existing enzyme molecules need not lose catalytic activity when new transcription is repressed.

Question 14. Why does uninduced wild-type lac expression need not be exactly zero?

  1. The operator is absent from wild-type DNA
  2. All LacI is destroyed without lactose
  3. Transiently unoccupied configurations can permit occasional initiation
  4. RNA polymerase cannot recognize the promoter at any time
Answer and explanation

C. Transiently unoccupied configurations can permit occasional initiation. Protein-DNA binding is dynamic, so occasional productive events can occur. Basal proteins help make the initial response to lactose possible.

Question 15. Which is a gratuitous inducer used in lac-controlled expression systems?

  1. X-gal
  2. IPTG
  3. LacA
  4. RNA polymerase
Answer and explanation

B. IPTG. IPTG induces LacI-dependent derepression without serving as the normal lactose nutrient. X-gal is chiefly used as a chromogenic enzyme substrate.

Question 16. In the standard lac model, an I+ product can affect a separate O+ region because it is what?

  1. A diffusible protein
  2. A fixed cis element
  3. A glycosidic linkage
  4. A ribosomal RNA
Answer and explanation

A. A diffusible protein. LacI proteins encounter operators in the same cytoplasm. The lacI gene itself remains at its own DNA location.

Question 17. What is the correct interpretation of a lower Kd for the same binding reaction?

  1. Stronger equilibrium affinity under matched conditions
  2. Necessarily greater total DNA concentration
  3. Necessarily faster dissociation
  4. No ligand recognition
Answer and explanation

A. Stronger equilibrium affinity under matched conditions. Kd relates free reactants to complex at equilibrium. It is not by itself the association or dissociation rate.

Question 18. What is the classical interpretation of the delayed onset of repression in the PaJaMo experiment?

  1. The incoming lacZ must permanently mutate
  2. All recipient cells lose their RNA polymerase
  3. Lactose is incorporated directly into DNA
  4. The newly introduced regulatory information produces a diffusible inhibitory product
Answer and explanation

D. The newly introduced regulatory information produces a diffusible inhibitory product. The temporal separation between expression of the incoming gene and establishment of repression supports an accumulating regulatory product.

Question 19. Which is the best interpretation of a nonpolar Z− mutation?

  1. The operator becomes constitutive automatically
  2. All LacI is absent
  3. Active β-galactosidase is lost while downstream expression need not be lost
  4. All downstream coding sequences necessarily disappear
Answer and explanation

C. Active β-galactosidase is lost while downstream expression need not be lost. Nonpolarity means the downstream expression defect is not an obligatory consequence. The coding mutation need not change promoter control.

Question 20. What does derepression mean in this chapter?

  1. The inducer becomes a promoter sequence
  2. Removal or reduction of the inhibitory action of LacI
  3. The repressor phosphorylates RNA polymerase
  4. The lac region is deleted
Answer and explanation

B. Removal or reduction of the inhibitory action of LacI. Derepression increases access to productive transcription by weakening inhibition, distinct from direct positive activation.

Part C — Analytical practice

Original practice questions. Unless otherwise stated, promoters are functional, I− is noninterfering, Oc cannot be effectively repressed, carbon conditions are permissive, and adequate intracellular inducer is supplied. Low expression means repressed relative to the induced reference.

Question 21. I− O+ Z+ / I+ O+ Z−: promoters are functional; inducer reaches the cytoplasm. What active Z phenotype is expected?

  1. Constitutive
  2. Inducible
  3. Noninducible low
  4. Active only in the absence of inducer
Answer and explanation

B. Inducible. I+ on the second copy supplies shared repressor. The first O+ is repressed without inducer and derepressed with it. Only the first copy supplies active Z.

Question 22. I+ Oc Z+ / I+ O+ Z−: what happens to active Z without inducer?

  1. High relative to a repressed O+ copy
  2. Low because the second operator is normal
  3. Zero because both repressors act on Oc
  4. Zero because Z− is dominant
Answer and explanation

A. High relative to a repressed O+ copy. Oc is linked to the functional coding allele and cannot be effectively repressed in the idealized model. An O+ site on a separate copy does not repair it.

Question 23. I+ Oc Z− / I+ O+ Z+: what is the active Z phenotype?

  1. Constitutive because Oc is present
  2. Noninducible because the first Z is inactive
  3. Constitutive only at low IPTG
  4. Inducible
Answer and explanation

D. Inducible. The constitutively transcribed region supplies no active Z. The functional Z+ copy is linked to O+ and responds to the normal repressor.

Question 24. Is O+ Z− / I+ O+ Z+: which outcome is expected with abundant IPTG?

  1. High active Z because I+ is induced
  2. High active Z because Is is linked to Z−
  3. Low active Z because Is represses the functional copy in trans
  4. High activity due to repaired Z−
Answer and explanation

C. Low active Z because Is represses the functional copy in trans. A sufficient pool of superrepressor acts on both recognizable operators, independent of which coding marker is linked to its own gene.

Question 25. Is O+ Z− / I+ Oc Z+: which outcome follows in the idealized model?

  1. No Z activity because Is is always dominant at every DNA site
  2. Induction only when Is is degraded
  3. Active Z in both inducer conditions
  4. No Z activity because Oc lacks a promoter
Answer and explanation

C. Active Z in both inducer conditions. Is requires a recognizable operator. Oc is assumed not to support effective repression; the functional Z is linked to this copy.

Question 26. Is O+ Z+ / I+ Oc Z−: what does the active Z assay show with inducer?

  1. High because Oc is present
  2. Low because only Z+ remains linked to O+ under Is control
  3. High because I+ rescues Is
  4. High because Z− trans-complements itself
Answer and explanation

B. Low because only Z+ remains linked to O+ under Is control. The derepressed copy has no functional Z, while the only functional coding allele is under inducer-insensitive repression.

Question 27. A single-copy P− Oc Z+ strain carries normal I+. In a model with no alternative promoter, what happens after IPTG?

  1. Transcription remains low
  2. Transcription is restored by Oc
  3. Transcription doubles because P− binds more LacI
  4. Transcription is high because Z+ is functional
Answer and explanation

A. Transcription remains low. The promoter is the required initiation element. Removing operator repression cannot restore a disabled initiation mechanism.

Question 28. I+ Oc Z+ Y− / I+ O+ Z− Y+: assuming adequate intracellular inducer and nonpolar mutations, what are the phenotypes?

  1. Both constitutive
  2. Both noninducible
  3. Z inducible; Y constitutive
  4. Z constitutive; Y inducible
Answer and explanation

D. Z constitutive; Y inducible. The Z+ marker is linked to Oc; Y+ is linked to O+. The two measured products report separate local control arrangements.

Question 29. An Oc-linked Z+ copy yields 30 units, and an O+-linked Z+ yields 70 when derepressed and 3.5 when repressed. What is the fold increase on induction?

  1. About 1.10
  2. Exactly 7
  3. About 0.335
  4. About 2.99
Answer and explanation

D. About 2.99. Uninduced activity is 30 + 3.5 = 33.5; induced activity is 100. The ratio 100/33.5 is approximately 2.99.

Question 30. For a single operator with free R = 20 nM and Kd = 1 nM, what is the occupied fraction?

  1. 1/21
  2. 20/1
  3. 20/21, about 0.952
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

C. 20/21, about 0.952. f = R/(Kd + R) = 20/21. The fraction 1/21 is the unoccupied fraction, and an occupancy cannot exceed one.

Question 31. If Kd changes from 1 nM to 100 nM at free R = 20 nM, with transcription only from unoccupied sites, what is the predicted initiation-rate ratio?

  1. 5
  2. 17.5
  3. 100
  4. 1/17.5
Answer and explanation

B. 17.5. The free fractions are 1/21 and 5/6. Induced divided by uninduced is (5/6) × 21 = 17.5 under the specified model.

Question 32. A complex has kon = 2 × 10^7 M⁻¹ s⁻¹ and koff = 0.02 s⁻¹. What is its Kd?

  1. 1 nM
  2. 10 nM
  3. 0.02 nM
  4. 4 × 10^5 M
Answer and explanation

A. 1 nM. Kd = koff/kon = 0.02/(2 × 10^7) M = 10⁻⁹ M. Dividing the rate constants gives concentration units.

Question 33. A culture has 42 RNA molecules per cell on average and a new steady value of 2. Half-life of excess RNA is 3 min. What is predicted at 6 min?

  1. 12
  2. 10.5
  3. 21
  4. 2
Answer and explanation

A. 12. The initial excess is 40; after two half-lives it is 10. Add the continuing steady abundance of 2 to obtain 12.

Question 34. Mutants A and B have RNA output 50→100 and 5→10, respectively, upon induction. Wild type is 5→100. What is supported?

  1. Both necessarily have identical molecular defects
  2. A and B have normal induced abundance
  3. B has stronger constitutive transcription than A
  4. A has elevated basal output; B has reduced induced output
Answer and explanation

D. A has elevated basal output; B has reduced induced output. Both mutants have a twofold change, but different absolute levels relative to wild type. Ratios cannot establish the specific underlying lesion.

Question 35. An unknown mutant makes high lac RNA with or without IPTG. A separately supplied I+ allele restores inducibility. What is the best-supported explanation?

  1. A necessarily irreparable Oc mutation
  2. A severe P− mutation
  3. A complementable deficiency in trans-acting repression
  4. A mutation that only eliminates catalytic LacZ
Answer and explanation

C. A complementable deficiency in trans-acting repression. Restoration by a diffusible product supports a missing regulatory function. It does not by itself prove the exact DNA sequence change.

Question 36. A mutant is noninducible at its native locus. A newly introduced functional O+ reporter is also noninducible despite confirmed intracellular IPTG. What is suggested?

  1. A native promoter lesion alone
  2. A trans-acting inhibitory defect such as Is
  3. A nonpolar Z− mutation alone
  4. An Oc mutation on the native chromosome alone
Answer and explanation

B. A trans-acting inhibitory defect such as Is. A shared inhibitory influence can explain loss of induction at both independent templates. Native local defects alone should not disable a competent reporter.

Question 37. In a mobility-shift assay, WT and mutant LacI bind O+ without IPTG. Only WT releases DNA substantially after IPTG. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The mutant necessarily cannot bind IPTG
  2. Mutant DNA binding is relatively inducer-insensitive
  3. The mutant has no DNA-binding domain
  4. The operator has been proven deleted
Answer and explanation

B. Mutant DNA binding is relatively inducer-insensitive. The assay reports protein-DNA association. Ligand binding and allosteric coupling defects remain alternative molecular explanations.

Question 38. A mutant shows high lacZ RNA after induction but no active enzyme. Which experiment best separates a catalytic defect from a failure to make protein?

  1. Measure LacZ protein abundance independently of enzyme activity
  2. Measure only extracellular lactose concentration
  3. Repeat the same activity assay without any additional measurement
  4. Determine the color of the growth medium before induction
Answer and explanation

A. Measure LacZ protein abundance independently of enzyme activity. An independent protein measurement tests whether LacZ accumulates. If protein is present but inactive, impaired catalytic function or folding becomes plausible.

Question 39. An O2/O3-disrupted strain shows weaker repression despite an intact O1. Which model best explains the observation?

  1. O1 has been deleted
  2. O2 and O3 encode LacY
  3. LacI can bind only after translation of lacA
  4. Auxiliary operator contacts stabilize looped repression
Answer and explanation

D. Auxiliary operator contacts stabilize looped repression. The primary site can still bind, but loss of cooperative loop formation reduces overall repression. The observation agrees with the classical auxiliary-operator experiments.

Question 40. A lacY− strain responds weakly to lactose but normally to adequately delivered IPTG. Which explanation best fits?

  1. The repressor necessarily cannot bind DNA
  2. The promoter cannot initiate RNA
  3. Signal delivery via lactose transport is impaired; LacI can still respond
  4. The reporter protein is irreversibly inactive
Answer and explanation

C. Signal delivery via lactose transport is impaired; LacI can still respond. IPTG bypasses the normal lactose uptake/conversion route when its intracellular delivery is adequate. The functional response argues against a completely defective promoter or repressor response.

Question 41. A nonpolar lacZ− strain lacks the normal allolactose-generating enzyme. Its independent lac-controlled reporter is induced by intracellular IPTG. What is demonstrated?

  1. LacZ encodes LacI
  2. The operator is necessarily constitutive
  3. The regulatory switch can respond without normal LacZ-mediated ligand production
  4. IPTG must be hydrolyzed by LacZ first
Answer and explanation

C. The regulatory switch can respond without normal LacZ-mediated ligand production. Direct inducing ligand bypasses the defective signal-generation step. The reporter does not require active native β-galactosidase for detection.

Question 42. After lactose withdrawal, RNA falls but stable enzyme activity per cell declines slowly. Which explanation is most consistent?

  1. LacI must directly activate the enzyme
  2. Initiation has declined but existing protein persists
  3. RNA polymerase continues at the old rate by definition
  4. The enzyme must be absent before RNA falls
Answer and explanation

B. Initiation has declined but existing protein persists. The repressor regulates future transcription. RNA and protein have separate removal and dilution rates; delayed enzyme decline is compatible with rapid repression.

Question 43. Free R = 10 nM and Kd = 10 nM give half occupancy. If Kd falls to 10/9 nM, what occupancy is predicted?

  1. 0.90
  2. 0.10
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.99
Answer and explanation

A. 0.90. f = 10/(10 + 10/9) = 9/10. This is an affinity change, not a change in concentration.

Question 44. Two interactions have Kd = 1 nM. Their koff values are 0.02 and 0.002 s⁻¹. What follows for mean lifetime without rebinding?

  1. Both 1 ns
  2. 500 s versus 50 s
  3. Both 1 s
  4. 50 s versus 500 s
Answer and explanation

D. 50 s versus 500 s. The mean lifetime for simple first-order dissociation is 1/koff. Equal equilibrium affinity does not establish equal residence times.

Mixed-format and integrated practice

Original practice questions. Unless otherwise stated, promoters are functional, I− is noninterfering, Oc cannot be effectively repressed, carbon conditions are permissive, and adequate intracellular inducer is supplied. Low expression means repressed relative to the induced reference.

Question 45. Assertion: I+ can repress an O+ region on a separate F′ element. Reason: LacI is a diffusible protein that recognizes compatible operator DNA.

  1. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  2. Assertion true; reason false
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Both true; reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

D. Both true; reason explains assertion. The shared regulator pool is precisely the reason a local lacI gene can influence a separate DNA molecule.

Question 46. Assertion: a typical Oc operator is complemented by an extra O+ operator. Reason: operators are cis-regulatory DNA sites.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

C. Assertion false; reason true. The O+ sequence on a separate copy does not repair the altered linked recognition site; the cis nature of operator function explains the failure.

Question 47. Assertion: an Is protein may bind IPTG yet remain repressive. Reason: ligand recognition and allosteric coupling are distinct functions.

  1. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  2. Both true; reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and explanation

B. Both true; reason explains assertion. Binding a ligand need not transmit the appropriate conformational effect to DNA recognition. Some inducer-insensitive proteins can have defects in coupling.

Question 48. Which matching set is entirely correct?

  1. Z–hydrolase; Y–symporter; A–transacetylase; I–repressor
  2. Z–symporter; Y–hydrolase; A–repressor; I–transacetylase
  3. Z–repressor; Y–transacetylase; A–hydrolase; I–symporter
  4. Z–transacetylase; Y–repressor; A–symporter; I–hydrolase
Answer and explanation

A. Z–hydrolase; Y–symporter; A–transacetylase; I–repressor. The four genes encode distinct functional proteins. The matching must distinguish transport, catalysis, acetyl transfer and regulation.

Question 49. Evaluate statements: (1) O1 is DNA. (2) I+ acts through a protein. (3) lacZYA encodes a single fused protein. (4) Is is typically inducer-insensitive. Which are true?

  1. 1, 2 and 4
  2. 1 and 3 only
  3. 2 and 3 only
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

A. 1, 2 and 4. The polycistronic RNA supports translation of distinct proteins, so statement 3 is false. The other three describe the classical model.

Question 50. Evaluate statements: (1) Basal expression can assist induction. (2) IPTG is the natural metabolite of lactose. (3) Removing inducer need not immediately remove enzyme. (4) Oc repairs P−. Which are true?

  1. 2 and 4 only
  2. 1, 2 and 3
  3. All four
  4. 1 and 3 only
Answer and explanation

D. 1 and 3 only. IPTG is a synthetic inducer, not the natural metabolite allolactose. A defective promoter cannot be restored by loss of repression.

Question 51. Using the single-site occupancy figure, which curve gives greater occupancy at the same positive free R?

  1. Kd = 100 nM curve
  2. Both are always equal
  3. Kd = 1 nM curve
  4. Neither because Kd is unrelated to affinity
Answer and explanation

C. Kd = 1 nM curve. In f = R/(Kd + R), lowering Kd increases the fraction occupied for any positive fixed R. The graph illustrates this relationship.

Question 52. In the RNA withdrawal figure, what is the predicted abundance at 9 min?

  1. 5
  2. 7
  3. 10
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

B. 7. Three half-lives leave 40/8 = 5 molecules per cell above the new steady value. Adding 2 gives 7.

Question 53. A hypothetical merodiploid carries I+ Oc Z+ / I− O+ Z+. If copy outputs are 25 and 75 units when available, and repression is complete, which pair is correct?

  1. 0 without; 100 with
  2. 25 without inducer; 100 with inducer
  3. 100 without; 100 with
  4. 75 without; 25 with
Answer and explanation

B. 25 without inducer; 100 with inducer. The first copy is derepressed regardless of inducer; the second O+ copy responds to shared I+. Both contribute after induction.

Question 54. Which comparison most directly tests a local operator mutation rather than a change in total repressor concentration?

  1. Matched O+ and altered-operator templates tested with the same purified LacI concentration
  2. Two strains with unknown plasmid copy numbers
  3. Two cultures with different growth temperatures and different nutrients
  4. Two unrelated enzymes measured at unrelated substrate concentrations
Answer and explanation

A. Matched O+ and altered-operator templates tested with the same purified LacI concentration. Holding the regulator and conditions constant isolates the DNA recognition variable. Other comparisons confound the intended causal test.
