1. The biological problem: using lactose only when it is needed

A bacterium cannot carry out every possible biochemical reaction at the same rate simply because its genome contains the corresponding instructions. Making a protein requires amino acids, ribosomes, messenger RNA, chemical energy and time. These resources are also needed to reproduce the cell, maintain its membrane and repair damaged molecules. For a cell that encounters a changing food supply, the ability to alter the distribution of these resources is valuable. Escherichia coli provides a particularly clear example because it can exploit several different carbon compounds, including lactose. The lactose system demonstrates how a change outside the cell can produce a selective change in the amount of a particular group of proteins, without requiring any alteration in the inherited genome.

Lactose is a disaccharide containing galactose and glucose joined through a beta-1,4 glycosidic linkage. Its presence in the extracellular environment does not automatically make either monosaccharide available to the cytoplasm. The inner membrane presents a barrier to the free movement of many hydrophilic solutes, and a suitable enzyme must cleave the glycosidic bond before the products can enter the relevant metabolic pathways. The bacterium therefore faces two coordinated requirements: bring lactose into the cell and catalyse its utilization. It would be inefficient to manufacture a large amount of a lactose transporter when no lactose is available, and increasing transport alone would not supply the enzymes needed to metabolize the imported sugar.

A gene encoding a lactose-associated enzyme can remain present in a bacterium even when enzyme synthesis is strongly reduced. A change in enzyme abundance following the addition of lactose is thus a phenomenon of gene expression rather than a change in the genetic code. This distinction can be tested by withdrawing lactose. If the same cell lineage ceases to produce large amounts of the enzyme when lactose is removed and resumes production when lactose is restored, reversible regulation provides a plausible explanation. A mutation that permanently alters an enzyme's amino acid sequence cannot by itself account for repeated, reversible changes of this sort. Natural selection can change the composition of a culture over generations, however, so a population experiment must also consider whether the apparent response reflects changing cell types.

In a growing bacterium, the amount of an enzyme per cell depends on both the rate at which it is made and the rate at which it is lost or diluted by growth. If the production of a lactose enzyme suddenly decreases, the enzyme molecules that already exist do not necessarily disappear at once. A biochemical assay might therefore detect activity even after transcription of the associated gene has returned to a low level. Conversely, a newly activated promoter may produce detectable messenger RNA before enough functional enzyme has accumulated to alter the growth of the organism. Throughout this chapter it is important to keep gene transcription, protein abundance, catalytic activity and bacterial growth separate as measurable outcomes.

The lactose operon is an example of a negatively regulated inducible system. The word negative describes the effect of a regulatory protein when it occupies its relevant DNA target: transcription is reduced. The word inducible describes the overall response to an appropriate signal: expression increases. These terms describe different aspects of regulation and are not alternatives. An inducer can increase transcription by weakening an inhibitor rather than by directly stimulating RNA polymerase. This double relationship is the key to understanding why a protein called a repressor is central to a system that turns on in response to a nutrient.

A second regulatory influence arises from the general carbon status of the bacterium. The cell may prefer another available carbon source even when lactose is present, so release of the lactose-specific repressor does not always guarantee maximal expression. This chapter isolates the lactose-sensitive component of the switch. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, comparisons between induced and uninduced conditions assume that the background carbon conditions permit transcription and are held constant. The detailed regulation involving cyclic AMP and CRP, also called CAP, is reserved for the subsequent chapter on positive control. Separating these two inputs is essential for identifying what a mutation has changed.

The usefulness of the switch is not that the bacterium knows in advance whether a particular protein will be needed. A molecular arrangement that produces more transporter and enzyme under appropriate conditions and less under inappropriate conditions can confer a fitness advantage over many generations. The underlying process is the interaction of physical molecules: sugar, protein, DNA and RNA polymerase. No central decision-making organ directs the response. Understanding the behavior of these molecules, and how their interactions can be experimentally separated, explains the apparent coordination of the whole cell.

2. From enzyme adaptation to the genetic switch

When early studies of bacterial metabolism compared cultures grown on different carbon sources, the amount of a particular enzyme was found to depend on the previous nutritional conditions. The phenomenon was historically described in terms of enzyme adaptation. This description did not specify whether the environment modified an enzyme already present, selected cells with greater enzymatic capacity, or caused the synthesis of new enzyme molecules. Those possibilities predict different responses when the environmental signal changes. The study of lactose utilization became a way of addressing this broader question about the relation between genes and proteins.

The availability of mutants that could not efficiently metabolize lactose made it possible to examine the problem genetically. A mutant lacking functional beta-galactosidase could fail to use lactose even when its regulation was normal. Another mutant could contain normal enzyme-coding sequences but synthesize the enzyme in conditions in which a wild-type cell produced very little. The first kind of defect concerns the function of the metabolic machinery; the second suggests that the machinery is no longer subject to its usual control. The distinction between these classes led investigators to distinguish genes specifying metabolic proteins from genetic elements determining when those proteins were produced.

In 1959, Arthur Pardee, François Jacob and Jacques Monod published a study of the genetic control of beta-galactosidase inducibility. The experiment is often referred to as PaJaMo. Bacterial conjugation provided a way to introduce particular genetic information into a recipient with a different regulatory history. The value of the approach was that an incoming functional enzyme gene could encounter cytoplasm that did not yet contain the regulatory product expected from the newly introduced genotype. The experiment thus separated the immediate availability of genetic information from the later accumulation of its product.

In an important arrangement, the recipient lacked functional inducibility control and functional beta-galactosidase, whereas the donor could transfer functional genetic information for both. Following transfer, enzyme synthesis could initially occur without added inducer. Subsequently, synthesis declined as the functional regulatory system became established. The interpretation was that a wild-type regulatory gene gives rise to a product that can accumulate in the cell and inhibit expression when inducer is absent. The observation was particularly informative because the incoming enzyme gene could be expressed before the inhibitory influence of the incoming regulatory information was established. This explanation did not require the regulatory gene to be physically part of the enzyme molecule.

Consider an alternative hypothesis in which the regulatory region is merely a fixed piece of DNA that changes the coding sequence of beta-galactosidase. Under that hypothesis, the transfer of a normal enzyme gene would restore the ability to make the normal enzyme but would not by itself explain delayed establishment of a diffusible inhibitory effect. Another hypothesis could propose that an inducer is a required chemical substrate of RNA polymerase. Such a proposal has difficulty explaining enzyme synthesis in the absence of inducer when the relevant negative regulator is missing. Genetic and physiological observations together favored a model in which removal of inhibition permits synthesis.

This reasoning also helps explain why the rate of enzyme synthesis is more informative than total accumulated enzyme in such experiments. If existing enzyme is stable, its activity may remain high even after the formation of new enzyme slows. A curve of accumulated enzyme could continue to rise with a decreasing slope, or approach a plateau, rather than fall abruptly. The slope under conditions of comparable biomass gives information about the production of new active enzyme. Failure to distinguish the ordinate of a graph from its slope can lead to a mistaken reading of the classical evidence.

Jacob and Monod formulated their influential operon model in 1961. Its explanatory power came from separating the coding regions that produce cellular machinery, the local DNA region that controls their transcription, and a regulatory gene whose product interacts with that region. A single regulatory protein could therefore affect several adjacent coding sequences together. Moreover, a substance in the environment could change the effectiveness of that protein. This framework gave a molecular interpretation to inducibility and constitutivity, properties previously described largely through enzyme activities and mutant phenotypes.

The model predicted that the negative regulator was a molecule that could recognize both a specific chemical signal and a particular DNA site. Evidence for the protein itself came from the work of Walter Gilbert and Benno Müller-Hill, who reported isolation of the lac repressor in 1966. Binding to a labeled inducing compound provided a biochemical handle with which to distinguish a relevant protein fraction from many unrelated cellular proteins. The existence of an isolatable inducer-binding regulator supported a concrete molecular mechanism rather than an abstract genetic inhibitory property. Subsequent binding studies connected the repressor to operator DNA.

A final important refinement concerned the identity of the natural inducer. Although lactose in the growth medium causes induction under suitable conditions, that observation alone cannot establish that lactose is the direct regulatory ligand. In 1972, Jobe and Bourgeois identified allolactose as the natural inducer produced in lactose-grown cells. This is a particularly useful example of the difference between an environmental input and the molecular species that interacts directly with a regulatory protein. When a question asks for the physiological inducer of the lac system, the correct mechanistic answer is allolactose.

The historical progression is important because each kind of evidence answered a distinct question. Nutritional experiments established that enzyme production was responsive. Mutant analysis separated regulatory defects from defects in enzyme function. Transfer experiments supported a regulatory substance that acted within the common cell environment. Biochemical isolation supported a protein regulator, ligand binding established a chemical recognition event, and DNA binding studies identified the local regulatory target. A textbook diagram of a repressor on an operator summarizes this combined evidence; it is not simply an assumption invented to explain the presence or absence of an enzyme.

3. Reading the lactose control region

The conventional representation of the lac region of the E. coli chromosome includes three structural genes, lacZ, lacY and lacA, arranged in that order. The term structural gene in this context means a coding sequence whose product performs a function associated with the lactose utilization system, as distinct from the nearby regulatory gene. The three structural genes are normally expressed together from the lac promoter. The regulatory gene lacI has its own transcriptional control region and is not a fourth cistron of the usual lacZYA messenger RNA. This independent expression is important: a cell must have a supply of the regulatory protein even when production of the structural gene products is strongly repressed.

The lac promoter is a DNA region recognized by the bacterial transcription initiation machinery. It helps determine where RNA polymerase begins copying the genetic information. The operator is a specific DNA sequence recognized by the repressor. Although these regulatory elements occur close together and their functional regions can overlap, they perform different molecular roles. A promoter mutation can reduce transcription because polymerase can no longer initiate efficiently. An operator mutation can alter transcription by changing the ability of a regulator to bind. The two mutations therefore need not produce similar phenotypes even if both occur within a short stretch of DNA.

A useful convention in a genetic map is to mark the first nucleotide transcribed as position +1. Positions upstream are designated negative numbers; positions farther along the transcription unit are positive. This coordinate system does not mark the first translated codon of lacZ, which is a separate feature. Regulatory sequences can extend into transcribed regions. The primary lac operator, O1, is positioned close to the beginning of the transcription unit, and the wild-type repressor's interaction with this site is central to the negative control mechanism. A diagram showing boxes for promoter and operator in a linear order should not be interpreted as a precisely scaled map or as proof that their sequences cannot overlap.

Two auxiliary operators contribute to the strength of repression. O3 lies upstream of the primary operator and O2 lies downstream within lacZ. These additional sites help a tetrameric repressor form a DNA loop by binding two sites at once. The primary operator and its ability to interact with the repressor are sufficient for the simplest genetic predictions, but the auxiliary sites explain why physical repression in the intact chromosome can be much stronger than repression measured in a simplified system with only one operator. Later in this chapter we will distinguish the idealized operator-constitutive mutation used in examinations from a real change that weakens only one of several interacting sites.

The product of lacZ is beta-galactosidase. The enzyme catalyses hydrolysis of lactose to glucose and galactose. It can also catalyse transgalactosylation reactions, which are important because they produce allolactose. An enzyme can thus participate both in nutrient processing and in generation of the signal that increases the production of the relevant machinery. The product of lacY is lactose permease, a membrane protein that couples galactoside transport to the proton electrochemical gradient. Its contribution concerns transport across the cell membrane rather than chemical hydrolysis of the sugar. Confusing lacY with lacZ obscures the distinction between entry of a signal and processing of that signal.

The product of lacA is thiogalactoside transacetylase, which transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to certain galactosides. Its function is not equivalent to hydrolysis of lactose or transport of lactose. It is commonly associated with handling potentially problematic galactoside compounds, and it is not generally required for the core steps of lactose utilization in the same way as beta-galactosidase and permease. One must not infer that all three proteins are equally indispensable for growth on lactose simply because their genes are transcribed together. Coordination of gene expression does not imply equivalence of biochemical roles.

A single polycistronic RNA can contain the coding information for several different proteins. Ribosomes initiate translation at the appropriate coding regions, so the gene products are not normally synthesized as one large fused protein that must be cut into the three enzymes. Each cistron specifies a separate protein. Translation efficiency and protein lifetime may differ among these products. Consequently, a change in promoter activity can influence the supply of all three coding regions while leaving differences in the abundance of their corresponding proteins. This is why a study measuring beta-galactosidase alone provides a useful proxy for lac expression but is not an exact measurement of every product of the operon.

In genetic notation, lacI is often abbreviated I, the promoter P, the operator O, and the coding loci Z, Y and A. Superscript plus denotes the reference functional allele in a particular problem, whereas minus usually denotes a specified loss of function. The notation O superscript c indicates an operator mutation with constitutive behavior. The notation I superscript s denotes a superrepressor allele. These symbols describe different kinds of objects: I specifies a protein, O and P designate DNA control sites, and Z and Y specify metabolic proteins. Writing the symbols together on a line can hide these physical differences, so the first task in solving a genetic question is to translate each symbol into its molecular role.

One can think of the DNA molecule as the address at which the regulatory event occurs. The operator remains part of that particular molecule even in a cell containing a second copy of the lac region. The repressor protein, by contrast, is released from the site at which it was synthesized and can interact with compatible operators elsewhere in the same cytoplasm. The difference between a fixed regulatory address and a movable regulatory product is the basis of cis and trans reasoning. It explains why a second functional copy of lacI can restore repression to an operon that lacks its own functional lacI product, but cannot repair an operator whose DNA sequence no longer supports appropriate repressor binding.

4. LacI structure, recognition and allosteric control

The LacI protein has to recognize two very different molecular partners. One is a DNA sequence, whose bases and backbone form a negatively charged double helix. The other is a small galactoside molecule, whose chemical structure contains hydroxyl groups capable of forming specific interactions within a ligand-binding pocket. The two recognition processes occur in different functional regions of the protein and are coupled by changes in protein conformation. The regulatory consequence of ligand binding depends on the coupling between these regions, not merely on whether the protein can bind the ligand at all.

The N-terminal DNA-binding region of LacI contains a helix-turn-helix arrangement. Its helices participate in recognition of operator DNA. A flexible connecting region and hinge helices contribute to contacts and geometry when the protein binds its operator. The core of the protein contributes to inducer recognition, dimerization and allosteric communication. A C-terminal region participates in tetramerization. A useful structural model treats the native tetramer as a pair of dimers; a dimer supplies the cooperating DNA-binding domains needed to recognize one operator, while the second dimer can contribute to simultaneous recognition of another operator.

DNA sequence recognition involves more than attraction between a positive protein surface and a negatively charged nucleic acid. Such attraction could help association with DNA generally, but sequence specificity requires differences in the favorability of particular contacts and shapes. Amino acid side chains can recognize chemical groups presented in the grooves of DNA. DNA deformability and the orientation of protein domains also affect binding. Changing a base in an operator may therefore reduce repressor occupancy even though the overall negative charge of the DNA is essentially unchanged. It is the specific interaction pattern that is altered.

The term affinity describes the favorability of a binding interaction under specified conditions. A protein with greater affinity for a DNA site tends to occupy that site more frequently when other factors, including protein concentration, remain the same. The uninduced form of LacI has a high affinity for its operator. Binding of an appropriate inducer shifts the protein toward conformational states in which association with the operator is less favorable. This is the essence of allostery: an interaction at one location in the protein modifies the behavior of a physically distinct functional region.

Allolactose does not need to compete for the same physical site as DNA. Instead, it binds within the ligand-recognition region and changes the distribution of molecular conformations available to the repressor. The induced protein is not necessarily destroyed, nor does it have to leave the cell. It can remain present while spending less time associated with operator DNA. Removal of the inducer reverses the shift in conformational preference, allowing the repressor to recover effective operator binding. A reversible molecular switch of this type can respond more rapidly than a system that requires the complete degradation and subsequent resynthesis of a regulatory protein.

Structural studies have helped connect this interpretation with the actual organization of the protein. In 1996, Lewis and colleagues reported structures of the lac repressor and complexes involving operator DNA or the inducing analogue IPTG. Such structures show how the DNA-binding portions and the ligand-recognition core are arranged. They also help explain the potential for a tetramer to organize two DNA contacts. A static structure does not show every motion of a protein in a living cell, however. Structural comparisons should be interpreted together with binding and genetic evidence when explaining how ligand recognition changes operator occupancy.

An important implication of the separation between the functional regions is that mutations can disrupt one property without eliminating another. A mutation in a DNA-recognition region can produce a protein that recognizes inducer but cannot bind the operator effectively. A different mutation can preserve operator binding but prevent induction, for example by damaging inducer recognition or the communication of ligand binding to the DNA-binding domain. The latter kind of defect is a molecular basis for the superrepressor phenotype. Protein quantity alone cannot distinguish these variants because an abundant regulatory protein can be functionally defective.

It is also possible for a mutation to disrupt protein folding or oligomerization. Such an alteration may reduce several activities simultaneously rather than isolate one functional region. The classical genetic symbols represent experimentally defined behaviors, not a complete list of possible amino acid changes. The designation I minus indicates loss of effective negative regulation in the model being used, but the precise molecular cause may be absence of protein, incorrect folding, defective recognition or another alteration. A question specifying an inducer-binding-defective protein that still binds the operator gives more information than a question that merely states that a substitution occurred in lacI.

LacI-mediated control is a physical equilibrium coupled to ongoing transcription. Molecules bind and dissociate through thermal motion, and their probabilities of occupying the DNA depend on concentrations and binding free energies. The operator is not a permanent mechanical lid. The regulatory output arises because occupation reduces the opportunity for productive initiation by RNA polymerase. Accordingly, even a strongly repressed system can exhibit low but nonzero expression. This residual expression becomes biologically important when we consider how a cell begins responding to lactose in the first place.

5. The uninduced state: how a bound repressor reduces transcription

Imagine first a wild-type E. coli cell growing in conditions that supply adequate energy but contain no lactose. The lacI gene is expressed separately from the lac structural genes. Its product can therefore be available even when the lac promoter produces relatively little RNA. In the absence of a sufficient concentration of inducing ligand, the repressor is biased toward states that bind operator DNA effectively. A substantial proportion of the available time at the lac control region is then spent in a state unfavorable for productive transcription. This is the molecular starting point of an uninduced cell, rather than a state in which the genes are physically absent.

RNA polymerase and its associated initiation factor recognize promoter sequences and form a transcription initiation complex. Productive initiation requires appropriate contacts with promoter DNA, opening of the DNA near the start site and progression into the early RNA synthesis steps. The presence of a large protein bound near this region can interfere with the formation or functioning of the initiation complex. Because O1 is near the lac transcription start, LacI occupancy strongly suppresses initiation from this promoter. For elementary reasoning, it is sufficient to treat operator binding as blocking productive transcription; more detailed distinctions among polymerase recruitment, initiation and promoter escape do not change the basic genetic logic.

A repressor that occupies O1 does not directly inhibit the catalytic site of an existing beta-galactosidase molecule. If an enzyme isolated from an induced culture is mixed with an appropriate substrate in a test tube, a DNA-binding regulator added to that assay is not expected simply to turn the enzyme off. The substrate and enzyme can still react if the assay conditions support catalysis. In intact bacteria the repressor reduces the future supply of enzyme by reducing the transcription of the enzyme's gene. This explains why genetic repression and inhibition of enzyme activity must not be treated as interchangeable mechanisms. They can both reduce metabolic flux, but the causal steps and characteristic times differ.

The operator is a molecular target of finite occupancy. Consider a DNA site with repressor attached, followed by an interval during which the protein dissociates. A polymerase that accesses the relevant region during a favorable interval may initiate a transcript before the repressor occupies the site again. The probability of such a productive event depends on the rates of competing molecular interactions and the availability of polymerase. Over many copies of the cell or many intervals within one cell, these occasional events produce a small baseline of transcription. The qualitative expression category called off in a typical exam question means strongly repressed relative to an induced reference, not necessarily zero RNA molecules.

This distinction can be demonstrated experimentally by comparing an assay with limited sensitivity and a sensitive reporter assay. A coarse measurement may show no detectable beta-galactosidase in the uninduced culture. A more sensitive measurement may reveal low activity. There is no contradiction if the first measurement's detection threshold is above the true basal activity. The interpretation of a result reported as zero depends on the technique, the duration of measurement and the denominator used to report the result. It is especially important in gene-regulation studies to distinguish a true null phenotype from an amount below detection.

The wild-type system enhances repression through the spatial organization of the operators. A LacI tetramer can interact with O1 and a second site in the same DNA molecule. The intervening DNA bends into a loop, and the two DNA contacts help stabilize a repressive configuration. A freely diffusing repressor must encounter an operator in the three-dimensional cell environment, whereas a repressor already anchored at one nearby operator is positioned to make another local contact. The availability of a second binding interaction can increase the overall favorability of occupancy compared with a single isolated site. The actual amount of repression depends on the operator sequences, their relative locations and the physical cost of forming a loop.

DNA bending has an energetic cost because a double helix resists deformation. A loop is favored only when the combination of protein-DNA contacts and the geometry of the complex makes the resulting configuration sufficiently favorable. This principle explains why moving an auxiliary operator while leaving its sequence unchanged can alter repression. The rotational alignment of the DNA helix affects the orientation of two potential protein contacts, so distance is not the only geometrical variable. At examination level, one need not calculate the mechanics of individual DNA loops; the relevant inference is that operator position and protein oligomerization can affect the regulation even when the primary recognition sequence remains intact.

In 1990, Oehler and colleagues investigated the contribution of the three operators to repression of the chromosomal lac operon. Disrupting auxiliary sites reduced repression, with loss of both causing a particularly large decrease relative to the intact operator arrangement. The behavior of tetrameric and dimeric repressor forms helped link enhanced repression to the ability to form DNA loops. These findings refine the one-operator picture rather than contradict it. The simplified picture is adequate when the question concerns cis linkage and the effect of an idealized operator mutation; the multiple-operator picture is needed when the question specifies changes in auxiliary sites or the ability of a protein to bridge DNA regions.

A useful consequence of the basal-state mechanism is that changing the amount of LacI can influence the rate of residual transcription. If operator occupancy is already very high, modest extra repressor might cause little further reduction in output. If occupancy is incomplete, an increase in the concentration of DNA-binding repressor may have a measurable effect. This dependence also explains why copy number matters in engineered systems that contain many operators. A regulatory protein supplied in a fixed amount can be distributed among multiple binding sites. The elementary single-copy problem usually assumes that enough functional repressor is present for the expected wild-type behavior, but that assumption should not be silently extended to a high-copy experimental system.

6. Induction: from extracellular lactose to a change in operator occupancy

The uninduced cell contains small amounts of the proteins encoded by the lac operon as a consequence of residual expression and, depending on its history, proteins inherited from earlier growth. When extracellular lactose becomes available, some of the sugar can enter through existing lactose permease. LacY couples galactoside transport to the movement of protons down their electrochemical gradient. The gradient maintained by cellular metabolism can therefore supply the free energy required for transport. No ATP molecule is hydrolyzed directly by LacY during each transport cycle; its operation depends on the energetic state of the membrane.

After lactose reaches the cytoplasm, the catalytic activity of beta-galactosidase becomes relevant. The usual hydrolytic reaction yields glucose and galactose. The enzyme can also catalyse transfer of a galactosyl group to a sugar acceptor rather than water, allowing the formation of allolactose. In allolactose the galactosyl group is connected to glucose through a beta-1,6 linkage. The two sugars are therefore chemically distinguishable even though both contain the same constituent monosaccharides. The different position of the glycosidic linkage changes the three-dimensional arrangement of functional groups that a protein can recognize.

The distinction between lactose and allolactose clarifies why a mutation in a structural gene can alter regulation indirectly. A lacZ defect that eliminates all relevant catalytic activity can reduce production of the natural inducer from lactose, even if the cell contains a normal lacI gene and an unaltered operator. This does not turn lacZ into the gene encoding the repressor. Instead, it shows that an enzyme involved in the metabolism of an environmental substance can generate an intracellular signal for another protein. The genotype may affect the amount of inducer available without altering the intrinsic ability of LacI to respond to that inducer.

As allolactose accumulates to a sufficient concentration, it interacts with LacI and changes the relative favorability of its DNA-binding states. A greater fraction of the repressor population occupies conformations with reduced affinity for operator DNA. Consequently, the probability that a given operator is occupied by a strongly repressing configuration decreases. Productive transcription initiation becomes more frequent. The important mechanistic event is relief of inhibition, often called derepression. The inducer does not supply the information contained in the structural genes and does not need to bind the promoter as a transcription factor.

The increased frequency of initiation leads to a greater supply of lacZYA RNA. As the RNA is translated, more beta-galactosidase and permease become available. Under suitable conditions, the additional permease can increase lactose entry and the additional enzyme can increase allolactose formation. This creates a potential reinforcing relationship between the initial response and the generation of the inducer. Such feedback does not imply that induction must increase without bound. The availability of external lactose, the rates of hydrolysis and transport, the finite capacity of transcription and translation, and the removal or metabolism of the inducer limit the response.

It is useful to follow the direction of the causal arrows carefully. Lactose outside the cell affects lactose inside the cell; intracellular lactose can be converted into allolactose; allolactose affects the repressor; the repressor affects transcription; transcription affects the supply of the proteins that mediate transport and metabolism. A change in one element may alter more than one later outcome. For example, impairing the proton motive force could decrease induction by reducing transport even though the biochemical properties of purified LacI are unchanged. A failure to induce in an intact cell is therefore not sufficient proof of a mutation in lacI.

Conversely, a substance capable of entering the cell and binding the repressor without first requiring conversion by beta-galactosidase can bypass the natural signal-production step. This is one reason the synthetic analogue isopropyl beta-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, abbreviated IPTG, is widely used to study lac regulation. It acts as a gratuitous inducer: it can induce expression without serving as the ordinary metabolized lactose substrate. In an engineered experiment where sufficient IPTG reaches the cytoplasm, a cell can show induction of an intact lac-controlled reporter even if its native beta-galactosidase is inactive. The experimental ability to separate ligand sensing from lactose catabolism is a powerful tool for locating a defect.

IPTG entry can itself depend on transport and concentration, so the presence of IPTG outside a cell must not automatically be equated with a specified intracellular concentration. At appropriately chosen conditions, IPTG can enter sufficiently to induce without requiring normal LacY-mediated uptake, but an assay must establish that delivery is adequate when comparing permease mutants. An experiment with purified repressor avoids the membrane-transport issue entirely. A corresponding experiment using intact cells tests the combined consequences of transport, ligand availability and molecular response. These two experiments answer different questions.

The term induction is sometimes used informally to refer to the addition of lactose, but this wording can hide the chain of events that follows. An experimentally supplied nutrient is not necessarily the same as the regulatory ligand. Similarly, a ligand that induces expression is not necessarily a nutrient. Lactose and IPTG can both lead to increased lac transcription in suitable settings, yet they play different roles in cellular metabolism. Comparing them illustrates why a regulatory molecule is identified by its interaction with the regulatory system, rather than by its ability to support growth.

The induced state is also not a permanent change in the regulatory gene. When a sufficient concentration of ligand is maintained, the balance of repressor conformations favors weaker operator association. When the ligand concentration decreases, that balance changes again. A wild-type cell can move in either direction without genetic alteration. This reversibility distinguishes physiological induction from a constitutive mutant, whose behavior persists across environmental changes because a component of its regulatory mechanism is altered.

7. Turning the system down: signal removal and response times

The response to lactose removal provides as much information about the regulatory mechanism as the response to lactose addition. Suppose a culture has been induced and then transferred to an otherwise suitable medium without lactose. The internal pool of lactose is no longer replenished through uptake. Remaining lactose and allolactose may be consumed or removed over time. As the free concentration of the inducing ligand falls, a greater proportion of LacI can return to DNA-binding states. The primary operator again becomes more effectively occupied, and the rate of productive initiation decreases.

It would be incorrect to assume that RNA synthesis, translation and enzyme activity must all return to their original levels at exactly the same instant. Existing messenger RNA can still be translated while it remains intact. Beta-galactosidase and permease molecules already present can continue functioning. Depending on protein stability and cell division, their abundance can decline substantially more slowly than transcript abundance. A measurement of transcription initiation therefore tends to reveal the regulatory transition more directly than a measurement of the total amount of a stable protein. This is a general principle in analyzing any switch that controls production rather than destruction.

Consider a cell population in which the induced beta-galactosidase concentration remains approximately constant immediately after withdrawal of lactose. One explanation is that transcription remains high. Another is that transcription is already low but the previously produced enzyme is stable. A short time-course measurement of lac RNA can discriminate between these explanations, provided changes in RNA removal are also considered. If RNA declines rapidly while enzyme remains high, the data support a difference in the timescales of RNA and protein turnover. The observation would not, by itself, show exactly how quickly LacI occupied the operator, because RNA abundance also depends on RNA degradation.

The interpretation of an enzyme time course depends on whether activity is reported per culture volume, per cell, per unit of protein or per unit of biomass. An actively dividing culture can contain an increasing total quantity of an enzyme even while the amount per cell falls. For example, a stable enzyme distributed equally among daughter cells will be diluted on a per-cell basis if very little new enzyme is made. Total enzyme in the entire culture need not fall simply because individual cells contain less. Such accounting is central to interpreting induction and repression experiments on growing bacteria.

Regulation at the level of initiation can be represented by a simple balance equation for the average number of transcripts per cell. Let m denote transcript abundance and a the average synthesis rate, measured in transcripts per cell per minute. If removal and effective dilution are approximately proportional to the number of transcripts, their combined first-order constant can be represented by k in inverse minutes. The change in abundance per minute is then dm/dt = a - km. This is not a model of the detailed LacI binding mechanism; it translates an upstream change in transcription rate into an expected downstream RNA time course.

At a steady state the abundance is unchanged, giving a = km and hence m = a/k. When the transcription rate changes from a high value to a lower constant value at time zero, the new steady abundance is determined by the lower rate and the same removal constant if removal is unaffected. Subtracting that new steady value from m produces a quantity whose rate of change equals minus k times itself. Integration gives m(t) = mnew + (mold - mnew) exp(-kt). The exponential term shows why existing RNA is not immediately lost when a promoter is repressed.

As a hypothetical illustration, take a culture with 42 lac transcripts per cell on average before inducer withdrawal. Suppose the new steady abundance is two transcripts per cell and the effective transcript half-life is three minutes. After one half-life, half of the excess above the new steady level remains. The predicted abundance is 2 + 40/2 = 22 transcripts per cell. After six minutes it is 2 + 40/4 = 12, and after nine minutes it is 2 + 40/8 = 7. The average can be noninteger in a population even though an individual cell contains a whole number of molecules. The nonzero final level represents residual transcription, not a failure of repression.

The assumptions matter. If ligand is not cleared rapidly, the synthesis rate a may itself change gradually, and the simple equation with an instantaneous switch will not fit exactly. If removal changes when the carbon source changes, the same rate constant cannot be used for both states. If the cells cease dividing, their growth-related dilution term decreases. The simple model is most useful as a null expectation under controlled conditions; deviations invite tests of the assumptions rather than immediate claims of a new regulatory pathway.

A cell's previous history also affects its response to the next lactose exposure. A recently induced cell may retain permease and beta-galactosidase, allowing uptake and signal formation sooner than in a cell that has spent many generations without lactose. A difference in induction delay can therefore reflect inherited molecules, not necessarily a heritable DNA change. This type of physiological memory should not be confused with the specialized mechanisms of stable epigenetic regulation studied later in the curriculum. The immediate cause here can be persistence and partitioning of ordinary gene products.

Repeated exposure and withdrawal demonstrate that the control system operates in a cellular environment that is never static. Protein-DNA and protein-ligand interactions influence transcription, whereas transport, metabolism, RNA lifetime and growth influence how quickly the transcriptional change becomes visible as a functional phenotype. A well-designed examination problem specifies which variable is measured. A well-designed experiment measures enough variables to distinguish a change at the operator from a delayed change in the abundance or activity of the metabolic machinery.

8. A quantitative model of operator binding and derepression

A binding equilibrium offers a useful way to relate the molecular interaction of LacI with DNA to the likelihood that the operator is available for transcription. Begin with an idealized operator O and a free DNA-binding repressor species R. If R and O form a complex RO, the simplest reversible equilibrium is R + O ⇌ RO. The dissociation constant is Kd = [R][O]/[RO], where square brackets denote concentrations. All three concentrations must be expressed in compatible units. The resulting Kd has the units of concentration, and a smaller value corresponds to stronger binding under the stated conditions.

The total concentration of the operator is [O]total = [O] + [RO]. The occupied fraction, f, is defined as [RO]/[O]total. Rearranging the dissociation expression gives [RO] = [R][O]/Kd. Substitution into the fraction and cancellation of the common free-operator term yields f = [R]/(Kd + [R]). The free fraction is 1 - f = Kd/(Kd + [R]). These formulas concern the occupancy of a single independent site in equilibrium with a specified free concentration of the binding species. They do not assume that all cellular repressor molecules are free, nor that every form of the repressor has identical affinity.

If the unbound operator allows productive transcription at a rate vmax and an occupied operator permits none, the mean rate in this idealization is v = vmax(1 - f). A more realistic elementary expression can include a small rate vbound in the occupied state, giving v = vmax(1 - f) + vbound f. The first expression corresponds to the simplifying assumption that all residual transcription occurs during unoccupied intervals. The second allows some productive initiation even in configurations categorized as occupied. Both relate an average transcriptional rate to the probabilities of underlying states, rather than requiring that each cell exhibit the same rate at every instant.

Take a hypothetical free DNA-binding repressor concentration of 20 nM and a dissociation constant of 1 nM for the uninduced operator interaction. Substituting into the occupancy formula gives f = 20/(1 + 20) = 20/21, or about 0.952. The unoccupied fraction is about 0.0476. If a ligand shifts the effective dissociation constant to 100 nM while free repressor concentration is held fixed for the purpose of the example, f becomes 20/(100 + 20) = 1/6, or about 0.167. The unoccupied fraction is now 5/6, or approximately 0.833. Thus a change in binding affinity can strongly increase promoter availability without changing the amount of repressor in the calculation.

Under the model with zero transcription from an occupied site, the ratio of induced to uninduced initiation rates in this example is (5/6)/(1/21) = 17.5. This ratio is dimensionless. It does not mean the amount of beta-galactosidase necessarily increases exactly 17.5-fold immediately, since downstream RNA and protein production have their own dynamics. It would also be inappropriate to treat the illustrative dissociation constants as measured properties of the natural lac system. They are chosen to demonstrate how a change in affinity translates into an altered occupancy and a conditional prediction for expression.

The equation also reveals why increasing repressor concentration and increasing binding affinity can have similar effects on occupancy. If Kd is fixed at 10 nM, raising free repressor from 10 nM to 90 nM changes occupancy from one half to nine tenths. If free repressor instead remains at 10 nM while Kd decreases from 10 nM to approximately 1.11 nM, occupancy again approaches nine tenths. A single transcriptional measurement cannot identify whether the difference arose from abundance or affinity. An assay of protein concentration or an independent DNA-binding experiment is needed to distinguish the alternatives.

At half occupancy, [R] = Kd in this simple model. Students often mistake Kd for the total amount of protein added to an assay at half occupancy. That identification is valid only when the free concentration is approximately equal to the total or when the free concentration is measured directly. If the amount of DNA is high enough to bind a substantial fraction of the protein, the added protein concentration exceeds the free concentration. A calculation that ignores this difference can yield a misleading estimate of affinity. In genetic reasoning the same caution appears when extra operator copies can redistribute a finite pool of regulator.

The binding reaction can also be related to kinetics. If association is described by a second-order rate constant kon and dissociation by a first-order constant koff, equilibrium requires kon[R][O] = koff[RO]. Rearrangement gives Kd = koff/kon. In a concentration system using molar units, kon has units of inverse molarity per second and koff has units of inverse seconds; their ratio has units of molarity. Two interactions with the same Kd can therefore have different individual association and dissociation rates. They may give similar equilibrium occupancy yet differ in how rapidly they approach equilibrium after an environmental change.

For example, an illustrative binding interaction with kon = 2 × 10^7 M^-1 s^-1 and koff = 0.02 s^-1 has Kd = 10^-9 M, or 1 nM. An interaction with kon = 2 × 10^6 M^-1 s^-1 and koff = 0.002 s^-1 has the same Kd. The second complex has a lower dissociation rate; for a simple first-order dissociation process with no rebinding, its mean lifetime is 1/koff = 500 seconds rather than 50 seconds. The association rate constant is also lower. Equality of the equilibrium constant is therefore not evidence that the two interactions will have identical temporal responses.

A complete description of the native lac switch would include the binding of ligand to multiple subunits, the presence of several operators, interactions with other transcriptional regulators, variations in local DNA configuration and possible nonequilibrium steps in RNA synthesis. Those details are not needed to use the elementary model as a reasoning tool. When the question explicitly assumes one site, one DNA-binding repressor species and equilibrium, the occupancy equation is appropriate. When the question provides multiple operators and a looping-dependent protein, the equation cannot simply be applied independently and multiplied without considering the coupling between states. The value of a quantitative model lies in understanding its assumptions, not in forcing a complex living system to obey an oversimplified formula.
