1. Protein production is a flux, not a transcript count

A gene can be transcribed without producing a corresponding increase in its protein. This distinction follows from the fact that a messenger RNA is a reusable template, not a protein precursor consumed in a one-to-one chemical reaction. Each accessible mRNA can be translated repeatedly by different ribosomes. A cell can therefore change the number of new protein molecules produced per minute without making a new transcript. Conversely, a large increase in transcript abundance need not yield much new protein if ribosomes cannot initiate on those transcripts or cannot complete their coding sequences. Translational regulation encompasses mechanisms by which a cell changes this rate of productive decoding. It is especially important whenever the time required to synthesize new RNA or remove old proteins is inappropriate for the biological response.

Consider a bacterial cell suddenly exposed to a higher temperature. The physical conditions under which proteins fold change immediately, and unfolded proteins may accumulate before transcriptional feedback has produced enough protective machinery. A pre-existing messenger carrying the coding sequence for a protective chaperone offers an alternative. If that RNA is already present but its ribosome-binding region is inaccessible, a temperature-induced structural rearrangement can permit ribosomes to engage the same transcript. The cell has changed the use of information rather than the information itself. This example illustrates the key distinction between the quantity of a messenger and its translational competence. Both may be regulated during a natural response, and a realistic explanation should not assume that transcription is irrelevant simply because a translational mechanism exists.

The same distinction is apparent in an animal cell exposed to amino acid shortage. Translation requires a continuous supply of aminoacylated transfer RNAs and a large expenditure of metabolic energy. Continuing to recruit new ribosomes onto thousands of mRNAs when a required amino acid is becoming scarce would not necessarily improve protein production. It might increase the number of stalled complexes and compete for the limited resources needed for adaptive proteins. By suppressing initiation, the cell can reduce demand while preserving some of its existing transcripts for renewed translation after recovery. Specially organized messages can even gain a relative advantage during this general reduction. This selective response is not inconsistent with inhibition of translation, because the same change in a shared factor need not affect every mRNA identically.

To reason accurately, separate the processes of transcript production, transcript destruction, ribosome recruitment, start-site recognition, elongation, termination, protein maturation and protein loss. A change in the amount of a protein measured by an antibody is not by itself evidence that any particular one of these processes changed. For example, if the protein is destroyed more slowly, its amount can increase although fewer new molecules are made. If a fluorescent reporter has not yet matured, the measured fluorescence may underestimate recently synthesized protein. If a culture grows more rapidly, dilution can reduce protein abundance per cell even in the absence of increased proteolysis. The central skill in the study of translational control is therefore to connect an observation to a molecular step using measurements that can discriminate between alternative causes.

A useful conceptual organization distinguishes cis-acting features from trans-acting regulators. A cis feature is carried by the particular transcript whose translation it affects. Examples include the nucleotide sequence around a bacterial start site, an RNA hairpin in the leader and an upstream open reading frame in a eukaryotic message. A trans factor is a separately produced molecule that can interact with one or more target messages or components of the translation apparatus. A bacterial small regulatory RNA and the eukaryotic initiation factor eIF2 are examples. The categories describe molecular relationships rather than whether the regulator is protein or RNA. A hairpin within a messenger is cis acting, whereas a complementary RNA produced from a distinct locus is trans acting in the usual example. A cis element can respond to a trans factor, and their joint action often determines the specificity of regulation.

It is also useful to distinguish control over translational activity from control over capacity. A cell that possesses many ribosomes but temporarily recruits only a small fraction of them has low activity relative to its capacity. A cell that has made few ribosomes because nutrient availability has been poor for a prolonged period has limited capacity even if all of them are working. Acute changes in phosphorylation or binding equilibria can regulate activity without waiting for new ribosome synthesis. Chronic changes in ribosome biogenesis can alter capacity over a longer interval. During recovery the timing of these two responses matters: available ribosomes may resume working rapidly, while rebuilding the ribosome pool takes longer. Neither the amount of ribosomal RNA nor the amount of an initiation factor alone establishes how many functional translation cycles occur per unit time.

In the conventional bacterial examples of this chapter, 30S subunits recognize an initiation region and recruit an initiator tRNA before joining the 50S subunit. In eukaryotic cytosolic translation, a 40S-containing complex is commonly recruited near the capped end of an mRNA and searches for a suitable initiation codon. These different recognition processes provide different entry points for regulation. Both systems nevertheless face the same general allocation problem: they must select messages, provide charged tRNAs, use energy and prevent unproductive demand. The detailed molecular examples differ between bacteria and eukaryotes, and archaea should not be treated as bacteria merely because they lack a nucleus. Archaeal translation shares several initiation-factor features with eukaryotic systems, while archaeal mRNA organization and initiation strategies vary. The standard bacterial and mammalian examples are therefore models rather than universal rules for all prokaryotic or eukaryotic organisms.

2. Quantitative separation of RNA abundance, translation and protein loss

A minimal mathematical model makes the interpretation of expression measurements more precise. Let M denote the number of usable mRNA molecules per cell and P the number of molecules of a protein per cell. Define a as the mean rate at which transcripts are added to the pool, in molecules per cell per minute. Let dM be an effective first-order transcript loss constant with units of inverse minutes. The loss term dM multiplied by M then has the same units as a. If all transcripts in this pool have the same average properties and the constants are independent of time, the RNA balance is dM/dt = a − dM M, where the d preceding M/dt indicates a derivative, not the loss constant. To avoid ambiguity we can call the constant kM. At steady state the time derivative vanishes, giving Mss = a/kM. Increasing transcription or decreasing transcript loss can therefore produce the same steady-state RNA abundance by different mechanisms.

Define kT as the average number of completed protein molecules produced per usable transcript per minute. This is an effective translation output coefficient and not the speed of a ribosome in codons per second. The instantaneous production flux is J = kT M, in protein molecules per cell per minute. Let kP be the effective first-order loss constant of the protein. In a growing population measured per cell, this constant can include both degradation and dilution under an appropriate growth model. Then dP/dt = kT M − kP P. At steady state Pss = kT Mss/kP = kT a/(kM kP). The formula is derived by setting the two balances to zero; it is not a general identity valid for every time point. In a transient experiment one must consider the rates and starting amounts separately. The model deliberately treats a complex cellular system as homogeneous to reveal which parameters are distinguishable from which measurements.

Suppose a cell contains 200 messenger molecules and each produces 0.5 completed protein molecules per minute on average. The synthesis flux is 100 molecules per cell per minute. If a regulatory change lowers kT to 0.2 without altering M, the new flux becomes 40 molecules per minute, a reduction of 60 percent. A protein immunoblot obtained immediately after the change may still show nearly the original amount, because previously made protein has not disappeared. If kP is 0.05 per minute and the cell was initially at steady state, the original protein count was 2000, while the new steady-state prediction is 800. The difference between these two numbers is not an immediate drop; it is the difference between eventual equilibria under constant conditions. A pulse-label experiment that measures the synthesis flux shortly after the regulatory change is more sensitive to acute translation than a measurement of the total protein pool.

The time dependence can be derived for a simple step change in translation with M held fixed. Write Pss,new = Jnew/kP and subtract this constant from P on both sides of the protein differential equation. If Q = P − Pss,new, then dQ/dt = −kP Q, whose solution is Q(t) = Q(0) exp(−kP t). Thus P(t) = Pss,new + [P(0) − Pss,new] exp(−kP t). The exponential describes the disappearance of the excess above the new equilibrium, not necessarily the disappearance of the entire protein pool. The half-time of this excess is ln(2)/kP. For the example with kP = 0.05 per minute, the half-time is about 13.86 minutes, and after that interval P is 1400 molecules per cell. A student who instead divides the entire original pool by two obtains 1000, missing the contribution of ongoing synthesis. The distinction is essential in examination problems involving partial inhibition rather than complete inhibition.

There is a second route to a reduced translation flux even if kT itself does not change. A decrease in M can lower J proportionally. If the transcript pool declines from 200 to 80 at a fixed kT of 0.5, the production flux is again 40 molecules per minute. An assay of newly synthesized protein alone cannot distinguish this situation from the first example. A simultaneous assay of mRNA abundance allows a useful operational estimate J/M. Provided the measurements refer to the same cell population and comparable time interval, the first example has a J/M ratio of 0.2, whereas the second has a ratio of 0.5. This ratio describes effective protein production per transcript, not a particular biochemical factor. A reduced ratio can result from slow initiation, incomplete elongation or increased premature release. It establishes a location for further investigation, not a complete molecular explanation.

The number of ribosomes associated with an mRNA also differs from the completion flux. To see why, let L be the coding length in codons and v be the mean elongation speed in codons per second. If pauses and termination delays are negligible, the transit time is approximately τ = L/v seconds. Let i be the average initiation frequency per transcript per second. For a steady stream of successful translations with negligible ribosome interactions and abortive events, the mean occupancy N is approximately iτ. This relation follows from counting how many initiated ribosomes remain on the transcript during their residence time. The units cancel: initiations per second multiplied by seconds gives ribosomes per transcript. An increase in initiation rate at fixed transit time increases both occupancy and output. An increase in transit time at fixed initiation rate increases occupancy without necessarily increasing output.

For a hypothetical mRNA with a coding region of 300 codons and an average elongation speed of 5 codons per second, τ is 60 seconds. If a ribosome initiates once every 15 seconds, i is about 0.0667 per second, and the predicted average occupancy is four ribosomes. If elongation slows to 2.5 codons per second with the same initiation rate, the transit time doubles to 120 seconds and the predicted occupancy becomes eight. At the new steady state, if every initiated ribosome still finishes and crowding is negligible, the completion rate remains one protein per 15 seconds per transcript. During the transient adjustment after slowing, the completion flux may change. If traffic becomes dense enough to impede recruitment or cause abortive events, the assumptions of the model break down and productive flux may fall. Therefore more ribosomes on an mRNA do not prove faster translation.

Measurements of RNA and ribosomes often use relative normalization rather than absolute counting. If an experiment reports a twofold increase in ribosome footprints for a gene and a twofold increase in its transcript abundance, the simple normalized footprint-per-mRNA index is unchanged. A fourfold increase in footprints with a twofold increase in RNA produces a twofold increase in this index. But the index measures occupancy per RNA rather than a direct rate of completed polypeptides. Global translation changes can also complicate the normalization of sequencing libraries. Equal total reads do not imply equal biological amounts of RNA or translating ribosomes per cell. Absolute standards, measurements of total synthesis, and explicit normalization assumptions improve the inference. These distinctions will recur when interpreting experimental data from stress-treated cells.

3. Bacterial initiation-site accessibility and RNA structure

In many bacterial mRNAs, a purine-rich Shine–Dalgarno sequence upstream of a start codon helps to recruit the small ribosomal subunit by complementary pairing with the anti-Shine–Dalgarno region near the 3′ end of 16S rRNA. The position of this interaction relative to the start codon helps place the initiator tRNA in the correct reading frame. Successful recognition also depends on the initiation factors, the availability of initiator fMet-tRNA in standard bacterial systems, and the organization of surrounding RNA. The DNA promoter and this RNA initiation region are different physical objects: a promoter determines where transcription begins, while the ribosome-binding region influences translation. A mutation in the untranslated leader may change translation even when the promoter and protein-coding sequence are unchanged.

Why does a good complementary sequence not guarantee a strong initiation site? RNA is flexible and can form intramolecular base pairs. The bases of a potential Shine–Dalgarno sequence may already be paired with a complementary tract of the same messenger, forming part of a stem. Before those bases can pair with rRNA, the competing interaction must be disrupted or circumvented. A strong predicted rRNA complement can therefore be a poor practical ribosome-binding site if it is buried in a stable secondary structure. The accessibility of the start codon itself and the footprint occupied by the initiating ribosome also matter. It is insufficient to examine a short motif without considering the surrounding leader and the beginning of the coding sequence.

A useful mechanistic distinction is between the thermodynamic favorability of forming a productive initiation complex and the kinetic accessibility of that complex. In a simplified equilibrium picture, an RNA population exchanges between closed and open conformations. If the free energy difference is ΔG = Gopen − Gclosed, the ratio of open to closed populations is exp(−ΔG/RT), where R is the gas constant and T is the absolute temperature. The open fraction in the two-state model is 1/[1 + exp(ΔG/RT)]. If ΔG is positive, the closed state is more favorable and the open fraction is less than one half. A change in sequence, temperature or ligand binding may shift this equilibrium. For illustration, at 310 K, with R = 0.001987 kcal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, RT is about 0.616 kcal/mol. A free energy difference of +1.23 kcal/mol gives an open fraction of about 0.12; a difference of −1.23 kcal/mol gives about 0.88. These are hypothetical energies chosen to illustrate the model, not measured values for a named biological RNA.

Even this calculation cannot by itself determine protein output. The ribosome may bind to an open state and stabilize it, depleting the unbound open population. RNA folding may occur while transcription is still in progress. An alternative conformation might persist because the barrier to rearrangement is high, even if another structure is thermodynamically favored. RNA-binding proteins, helicases and molecular crowding can alter the effective rates. Multiple stem arrangements can compete, invalidating a two-state assumption. The mathematical model is therefore a way of predicting the direction of a simple accessibility change, not a universal quantitative predictor of translation in a living cell. In an exam, use the provided assumptions rather than silently replacing an experimentally observed rate with an equilibrium fraction.

A strong experimental demonstration of an inhibitory stem compares sequence variants that perturb and restore base pairing. Imagine a reporter containing a leader with a six-base stem that masks its initiation region. Mutating three bases on one side may destabilize the stem and increase reporter synthesis. Mutating the corresponding bases on the other side to restore complementary pairing may reduce synthesis toward the original level. If the reporter protein sequence is held constant, mRNA levels are measured and the mutations do not directly alter rRNA complementarity or create a new start codon, the result supports a structural rather than a purely sequence-specific explanation. However, some compensatory changes may inadvertently create another structure or alter RNA stability; a structural probing experiment and several independent mutations strengthen the interpretation.

The natural range of bacterial initiation architectures is broader than the conventional Shine–Dalgarno mechanism. Some messages lack a recognizable Shine–Dalgarno motif, and leaderless messages can initiate at or very near the transcript's 5′ end. Species differ in the frequency and mechanism of such initiation. A bacterial polycistronic transcript may contain several separately recognized initiation sites, but not every coding region is necessarily translated independently of its neighbor. Local RNA structure, upstream translation and ribosome movement can connect the output of neighboring genes. Thus the correct general conclusion is that access to productive initiation is regulatable; the exact sequence feature or factor through which this occurs depends on the organism and the individual transcript.

4. Regulatory RNAs: inhibition, activation and coupled changes in stability

A separately transcribed small RNA can alter the accessibility of a bacterial message by forming intermolecular base pairs. The simplest negative mechanism is direct competition between the small RNA and the ribosome for overlapping regions of the target messenger. When the small RNA occupies the initiation region, a small ribosomal subunit cannot readily establish the interactions required for initiation. This mechanism changes the probability that a free target messenger enters translation. Since the regulator is not incorporated into the polypeptide, a target can retain its original coding sequence while its translation changes. Many bacterial regulatory small RNAs are only partially complementary to their targets and can interact with more than one mRNA through a recognition region.

The RNA chaperone Hfq facilitates many such interactions in well-studied Gram-negative bacteria. Its contributions can include protection of the small RNA against degradation, binding to target RNA and promotion of productive pairing. It is not correct to infer that Hfq itself always blocks the ribosome, or that every bacterial regulatory RNA needs Hfq. Different RNAs and species have different requirements. In a conventional negative regulation model, the small RNA–target duplex overlaps the translation initiation site; Hfq makes formation or persistence of the duplex more likely. Other arrangements can involve a bound protein helping to occlude an initiation region even when the small RNA pairs elsewhere. The defining result in either case is impaired productive initiation, not necessarily direct binding of a regulator to the AUG triplet.

Positive regulation follows from a different use of the same pairing principle. Suppose a target messenger forms an inhibitory stem between its ribosome-binding sequence and a more upstream region. A small RNA complementary to that upstream region can bind it and compete with the inhibitory intramolecular pairing. The ribosome-binding sequence is then more accessible. Here the regulator does not recruit the ribosome by encoding another translation factor; it changes the structure of the target. The sign of regulation is determined by which interaction is displaced. The same class of molecule, a small regulatory RNA, can consequently increase translation of one target and decrease translation of another.

The Escherichia coli rpoS message is a canonical illustration of activation by restructuring. RpoS is a sigma factor important for the general stress response. Under conditions in which its translation leader is inhibitory, several small RNAs, including DsrA and RprA, can promote access by pairing with the leader. This provides a route from environmental signals to increased production of a regulatory protein using control over an existing message. Different regulators are themselves produced under distinct conditions; their shared effect on the target does not imply that their expression patterns are identical. It is also important that rpoS expression has other levels of regulation, including protein turnover. A measured change in RpoS protein alone cannot be uniquely assigned to an RNA-remodeling event.

A small RNA that inhibits initiation may also alter the lifetime of the target messenger. In bacteria, translating ribosomes can protect regions of RNA against some nucleolytic attacks. A message no longer efficiently recruited to ribosomes may become more susceptible to decay. In other cases a small RNA–messenger interaction and associated proteins can promote degradation more directly. Consequently, a single regulatory interaction may decrease both protein synthesis per transcript and the number of available transcripts. This does not invalidate its classification as translational regulation when initiation is directly affected; it demonstrates that translation and RNA stability can be causally connected. Conversely, a small RNA that shields a cleavage site can increase protein output through stabilizing the message without necessarily increasing initiation per molecule.

Consider a compensatory base-pairing test between two separate RNAs. In a wild-type strain, induction of an inhibitory small RNA reduces reporter output. A mutation in the target pairing site eliminates repression, and a mutation in the small RNA pairing region alone also eliminates it. Introducing both mutations together restores repression if they re-establish complementarity. The most informative design uses the same reporter coding sequence, verifies that the mutated small RNAs accumulate comparably, and tests target RNA abundance. This four-genotype pattern supports a specific pairing interaction more strongly than overexpressing a small RNA and observing reduced fluorescence. It also helps distinguish direct sequence recognition from an indirect response mediated by another gene product. If pairing is restored but repression is not, the investigator must consider secondary structure, auxiliary protein binding or other sequence-dependent steps.

The temporal order of the effects can be informative. If reporter synthesis falls rapidly while mRNA abundance remains unchanged, and the message decays later, direct initiation repression followed by destabilization is a plausible mechanism. If mRNA falls before a measurable decrease in protein synthesis per transcript, altered RNA lifetime may instead be primary. Nevertheless, measurement resolution and feedback can obscure the order. A cell-free translation assay with equal quantities of purified target RNA, a regulator and any required chaperone can isolate a direct effect on translation, while an independent RNA decay assay examines stability in cells. No single type of measurement is universally sufficient. Convergence of sequence, structure, protein synthesis and transcript abundance evidence makes a causal explanation more convincing.

5. RNA thermometers and metabolite-sensitive initiation

A regulatory RNA need not depend on a separately produced small RNA to sense a change in the environment. The structure of a transcript can itself be sensitive to a physical variable. Bacterial RNA thermometers illustrate this possibility particularly clearly. At a relatively low temperature, base pairing and stacking interactions in a messenger leader can favor a conformation that masks its initiation region. A temperature rise changes the distribution of conformations and the rates of their interconversion. If the inhibitory structure becomes sufficiently unstable, the ribosome gains better access to the message. It is the change in structural accessibility, rather than temperature directly instructing a ribosome which gene to translate, that accounts for the specificity of this control.

RNA thermometers are often described as melting hairpins, but a useful molecular interpretation is more nuanced. A hairpin can contain internal mismatches, loops or weakly paired segments. Changes in temperature may first increase flexibility at a small set of strategically placed base pairs, exposing the recognition site without unfolding the entire leader. The RNA need not switch from a perfectly closed state to a fully unstructured molecule. The probability of a productive encounter with a ribosome can rise gradually as local access improves. This behavior is advantageous when a cell requires a graded response rather than a sharp on–off threshold. Conversely, some RNA architectures can generate a relatively steep response over a useful temperature interval.

The FourU element associated with the Salmonella enterica agsA message is a well-known bacterial example. A sequence containing four uridines participates in pairing with a region that includes the Shine–Dalgarno sequence. Temperature-sensitive accessibility allows increased translation of a heat-associated protective protein. Another extensively studied family is the ROSE class of bacterial RNA thermometers, whose name refers to repression of heat shock gene expression at lower temperatures. The examples are biologically informative because they connect a physical property of RNA to the immediate physiological problem of protein folding under heat stress. Not every heat shock gene is controlled by an RNA thermometer, and some transcripts are subject to additional transcriptional and protein-level controls.

The evidence for a thermometer is stronger when its behavior can be transferred to an unrelated reporter using the candidate leader sequence. A suitable comparison tests two temperatures, maintains identical reporter coding sequences and uses a promoter whose activity is independently assessed. A destabilizing leader mutation is predicted to reduce low-temperature repression; a stabilizing mutation can reduce induction at the higher temperature. Compensatory mutations can test whether base pairing is the relevant feature. If the RNA is probed in the absence of ribosomes, temperature-dependent accessibility near the initiation region provides evidence that structural changes can occur independently of translation. A purified translation system can further test whether the temperature dependence is retained without other transcription factors. Because temperature influences many cellular reactions, a control reporter lacking the thermometer is particularly important.

A related but distinct regulatory mechanism is a translational riboswitch. Its sensory portion binds a small molecule, and ligand binding changes an RNA conformation that communicates with an expression platform. In a translationally repressive switch, ligand binding may favor a conformation that masks an initiation site. In an activating switch, binding can instead favor a conformation that exposes it. This direction is specified by the architecture of the particular RNA, not by a general rule that metabolites always repress protein synthesis. If the metabolite is the product of a biosynthetic pathway and the inhibited message encodes a pathway enzyme, repression can implement negative feedback. If the regulated product is useful specifically in the presence of the ligand, activation may be advantageous.

The distinction between a translational riboswitch and a transcriptional riboswitch depends on which downstream process is directly altered. Some switches promote formation of a transcription terminator, reducing production of the full-length transcript; others leave the transcript intact but alter ribosome access. In an experiment that measures only reporter activity, either mechanism could appear to be repression. Measuring full-length RNA and using equal amounts of preformed RNA in a translation assay helps separate them. The general ligand-binding and structural properties of riboswitches were assigned to the preceding RNA subunit; here the important point is how the structure determines use of a mature or nascent message by a ribosome. A riboswitch and an RNA thermometer both exploit conformational changes, but their principal inputs differ: occupancy by a particular ligand in one case and physical temperature in the other.

6. Feedback through RNA-binding proteins and translational coupling

Regulatory proteins in bacteria can bind mRNA instead of DNA. This is particularly useful when the protein being synthesized is itself part of a molecular assembly. Ribosomes, for example, require many ribosomal proteins together with rRNA. If synthesis of one assembly component continued independently while other components were scarce, large excesses of unassembled protein would be produced. A mechanism that couples production of a protein to the availability of its binding partner can limit this waste. In several well-studied bacterial ribosomal protein operons, excess unassembled ribosomal proteins act as repressors of translation of their own operon messages.

The feedback can be understood by considering competing binding partners. A regulatory ribosomal protein can bind an appropriate site during assembly with rRNA. When sufficient suitable rRNA is available, assembly consumes much of the newly synthesized protein, reducing the concentration of the free regulatory form. When rRNA supply falls relative to ribosomal protein synthesis, a larger free pool becomes available. A protein that also recognizes a regulatory structure in the operon mRNA can then occupy that structure and reduce production of the encoded proteins. This introduces an automatic link between assembly demand and synthesis. The regulatory mRNA site may mimic aspects of the protein's rRNA recognition site, but the precise structural similarity and binding arrangements differ among examples.

This feedback is autogenous in the sense that a product of the regulated genetic system contributes directly to its regulation. It should not be confused with every form of negative regulation. A separate regulator made at another locus may repress translation but need not be an autogenous feedback regulator. Nor is all autogenous feedback translational: a protein that binds its own promoter can regulate transcription. The location and function of the binding site establish which level is directly controlled. Work on E. coli ribosomal protein operons supports translational feedback for many but not all operons. The presence of a ribosomal protein gene in a cluster is not sufficient evidence that its product has this regulatory role.

A testable prediction of the competition model is that an increase in suitable rRNA availability can reduce the free regulatory protein pool, relieving repression of the mRNA. However, this prediction assumes that the added rRNA can engage the relevant binding partner and that assembly conditions allow the interaction. Adding a defective RNA lacking the necessary binding site would be a poor rescue. A mutation in the regulatory recognition region of the mRNA might abolish negative feedback without removing the assembly function of the protein. In contrast, a mutation within the protein that disrupts both RNA recognition and ribosomal assembly can cause multiple defects. Comparing regulatory-site mutants with protein mutants, and measuring both synthesis and assembled ribosomes, helps distinguish these possibilities.

The simplest binding model offers a quantitative intuition. Suppose a fixed concentration of free regulatory protein R binds a single mRNA site with dissociation constant Kd. If depletion of the protein by target binding is negligible, the equilibrium bound fraction is R/(Kd + R). If only unbound messages initiate at an unchanged rate, the normalized initiation rate is Kd/(Kd + R). At R = Kd, half the sites are bound and the predicted initiation rate is half of the unrepressed rate. Increasing R from Kd to 9Kd raises occupancy from 0.5 to 0.9 and reduces the predicted rate from 0.5 to 0.1. The model illustrates how the free regulator concentration, not necessarily its total amount, determines occupancy. It neglects cooperative interactions, ligand depletion, synthesis and decay, and possible initiation from partially occupied structures.

Bacterial polycistronic messages introduce a further level of dependence known as translational coupling. The downstream gene can have its own initiation region, yet its utilization may depend on translation of an upstream gene. One way this occurs is through RNA structure: the start region of the downstream coding sequence is buried in a stem that overlaps the upstream coding region. Passage of an upstream ribosome disrupts the structure, providing a period during which another initiation complex can recognize the downstream site. In other cases a ribosomal particle finishing one reading frame can contribute to reinitiation near a neighboring start site. These mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive, and their relative contribution is sequence dependent.

Consider two proteins A and B encoded in consecutive reading frames of the same transcript. If the first reading frame is prematurely terminated before a segment that contributes to an inhibitory stem at the second start site, production of B may decline even though the sequence of B is unchanged. The explanation is not that a ribosome translates the first gene and then chemically transfers its finished product into the second. Rather, translation of the upstream region helps determine downstream initiation competence. A premature stop can also reduce downstream transcript abundance through transcriptional polarity in certain bacterial contexts, so the experiment should measure the full-length RNA. Mutation of the downstream stem that restores accessibility without repairing the upstream reading frame can support the structural coupling explanation.

In bacteria, transcription and translation can overlap in time because the two processes are not separated by a nuclear envelope. A ribosome may engage a nascent transcript before RNA polymerase reaches the end of the transcription unit. This fact has consequences for RNA folding, transcript stability and some termination processes. It is distinct from translational coupling between adjacent reading frames, despite the shared word coupling. When a ribosome protects nascent RNA or alters its folding, it can influence subsequent transcription or translation events. These interactions illustrate why measurements of one expression layer cannot always be interpreted as isolated from the others. The detailed transcriptional attenuation and integrated operon programs are treated in the gene-expression subunit rather than repeated here.

7. Bacterial nutrient limitation, ribosomal activity and recovery

Protein synthesis consumes amino acids, ATP and GTP. A shortage of one amino acid may reduce the charging fraction of its cognate tRNA, even though the total number of those tRNA molecules is unchanged. An elongating ribosome encountering a codon for that amino acid requires a correctly charged tRNA; uncharged tRNA cannot donate the missing amino acid. If the supply of charged tRNA becomes low relative to demand, waiting times at susceptible codons can increase. This constitutes a direct biochemical constraint on translation and provides a signal that the cell's pattern of resource allocation needs adjustment.

In E. coli, the stringent response provides an important connection between this problem at the ribosome and broader resource management. RelA can respond to ribosomes associated with deacylated tRNA in the A site during amino acid starvation. Activated RelA synthesizes guanosine alarmones, collectively called (p)ppGpp, using ATP together with GDP or GTP as substrates. The notation refers to ppGpp and pppGpp, not to a single ambiguous type of tRNA. The alarmone response helps coordinate metabolism and macromolecular synthesis with nutrient availability. In the classical E. coli framework, synthesis of stable RNA including rRNA is reduced under stringent conditions, limiting investment in new ribosomes when resources are scarce. Other gene-expression changes support adaptation.

It is important to identify the causal step in a question about this response. RelA is not a tRNA synthetase; increasing its activity does not directly attach an amino acid to tRNA. An uncharged tRNA at a stalled ribosome is not a normal substrate for peptide-bond formation. The biochemical shortage can still cause slow elongation in a cell that lacks RelA, because deleting the signaling protein does not provide the missing charged tRNA. What is disrupted is an important adaptive signaling route. A relA mutant may retain other alarmone-related responses depending on its genetic background, and bacteria differ in the proteins used to synthesize or degrade these nucleotides. Results from E. coli should not be turned into claims about every bacterium.

A hypothetical comparison illustrates the distinction between limitation and signaling. Both wild-type cells and cells with impaired RelA activity receive an amino acid supply insufficient for their original growth rate. Direct pulse labeling shows a rapid decrease in protein synthesis in both cultures. In the wild type, the alarmone pool subsequently rises and rRNA production falls strongly. In the mutant the alarmone response to this particular input is smaller. This dataset is compatible with a direct reduction in elongation from low charged tRNA in both cultures, and a difference in the adaptive allocation response. It does not establish that all of the reduction in protein synthesis is caused by RelA. Measuring aminoacylation, ribosome occupancy and RNA synthesis at several time points makes the mechanistic distinction clearer.

Bacteria can also control the fraction of ribosomes available for productive translation. During starvation or stationary phase, some ribosome-associated factors promote inactive states. In E. coli, ribosome modulation factor (RMF) and hibernation-promoting factor (HPF) participate in formation and stabilization of inactive ribosome assemblies often described as 100S particles. These involve dimerization of 70S ribosomes. In other bacteria, different hibernation factor arrangements can promote 100S formation; some factors stabilize inactive 70S particles instead. Sedimentation terminology describes physical behavior rather than simple arithmetic addition of sedimentation coefficients.

Hibernation is not the same as ribosome degradation. A cell may benefit from conserving existing ribosomes in a protected or inactive form during a period when their immediate use is restricted. When favorable conditions return, inactive particles can be converted back into active ribosomes, allowing quicker recovery than synthesis of an entirely new pool. Nevertheless, the effect of a particular hibernation factor on long-term survival and ribosome stability depends on the species and conditions. The key exam-level point is that ribosome abundance and the fraction engaged in productive translation can vary independently. A sample with abundant rRNA can exhibit poor protein synthesis if much of its ribosome pool is unavailable.

A sucrose-gradient profile might show an increase in a 100S peak during starvation and a decline in actively translating polysomes. Following nutrient restoration, the 100S peak might diminish while polysomes increase. This pattern is consistent with reversible redistribution of the ribosome pool. However, the sedimentation peak alone is not proof that the particles were inactive in living cells; sample preparation and ionic conditions influence sedimentation. A suitable interpretation combines gradients with independent synthesis measurements and, where feasible, mutants lacking specific hibernation factors. The physiological advantage of such a response cannot be inferred solely from the size of an inactive-particle peak. Growth recovery, survival or ribosome integrity provides additional evidence.

These examples show that bacterial translation can be regulated at multiple temporal scales. Local initiation control can alter a selected message rapidly. Decreased aminoacyl-tRNA supply limits ongoing elongation and may trigger a signaling response. Changes in rRNA synthesis modify future ribosomal capacity. Redistribution into inactive complexes changes the fraction of existing capacity being used. A recovery response must reverse appropriate inhibitory events while re-establishing a balance between demand and resources. It is misleading to describe bacterial translational control as exclusively a Shine–Dalgarno phenomenon; that motif offers one important target, but the whole apparatus remains subject to physiological constraints.

8. Eukaryotic initiation: two separable requirements

Most cellular mRNAs translated by mammalian cytosolic ribosomes possess a 5′ cap, an untranslated leader, a coding region and a 3′ end whose organization often includes a poly(A) tail. The cap is a chemical modification of the RNA, not the start codon. It can serve as a molecular recognition site for the cap-binding factor eIF4E. The initiation machinery uses this interaction to help recruit a small-subunit-containing preinitiation complex. The complex must also carry the initiator methionyl tRNA in an appropriate factor-bound state. Cap recognition and initiator-tRNA recruitment are therefore distinct requirements. Their distinction explains why a cell can regulate the same initiation process through at least two separate sets of factors.

The eIF4F complex provides a conceptual bridge between an mRNA and the ribosomal recruitment machinery. It contains eIF4E, which recognizes the cap; eIF4G, which serves as a scaffold interacting with other factors; and eIF4A, an ATP-dependent RNA helicase involved in remodeling RNA structures during recruitment and scanning. The scaffold helps connect the cap-associated complex to the 40S-containing machinery, including interactions with eIF3. Poly(A)-binding protein can interact with eIF4G and thereby help coordinate the two ends of the transcript. Such interactions can promote initiation and influence the stability or functional organization of an mRNA. A schematic circularized message is a model for these contacts, not evidence that every translated mRNA is permanently arranged as a rigid closed circle.

After recruitment, a scanning complex normally searches the leader for an initiation codon in an appropriate context. Recognition of AUG is influenced by its neighboring nucleotides and by initiation factors that help determine the fidelity of start-site selection. Strong inhibitory RNA structures can slow recruitment or impede scanning. Their effect can depend on position, stability and availability of remodeling activities. A stem near the cap can interfere with initial engagement, whereas another further downstream can alter the scanning journey. A change in eIF4A activity can therefore affect different messages unequally. It is not sufficient to assume that every messenger has identical factor requirements because all use the same ribosome type.

A second component, the eIF2 ternary complex, contains eIF2 bound to GTP and the initiator Met-tRNAi. The initiator tRNA has a specialized role in selecting and occupying the initiation site. Elongator methionine tRNA, although charged with the same amino acid, is not an interchangeable replacement in the normal initiation pathway. The amount of available ternary complex influences the rate at which new productive preinitiation complexes can form. When a start codon is accepted, GTP hydrolysis and phosphate release are coordinated with initiation transitions, and eIF2 leaves in a GDP-bound form. Before it can efficiently deliver another initiator tRNA, its bound nucleotide must be exchanged.

In many textbook diagrams the initiation process is drawn as a linear row of factors, making it appear that regulation at any one factor has exactly the same consequences for every transcript. Actual messages differ in their leaders, binding interactions and start-site arrangements. A decrease in eIF4F assembly can especially affect recruitment of messages that depend strongly on the cap-associated apparatus. A decrease in available eIF2 ternary complex changes the availability of initiator-tRNA-bearing complexes and can modify reinitiation behavior after an upstream reading frame. Both changes may reduce bulk protein production, yet their effects on individual mRNAs can differ. The central mechanistic task is to identify which required resource has been restricted and how the target transcript uses that resource.

Some eukaryotic messages can recruit translation machinery in ways that do not require conventional cap recognition, and some initiation events have nonstandard factor requirements. These exceptions are useful for interpreting carefully designed reporter experiments. They should not be used to claim that all cap-independent translation is independent of every initiation factor, or that all stress-responsive messages use an internal ribosome entry site. The well-established canonical ATF4 response discussed later is primarily understood through the arrangement of upstream reading frames and reinitiation under altered ternary-complex availability. The detailed study of viral translation strategies lies outside the present node. The transferable concept is that a factor can be limiting for one recruitment or initiation route without being equally limiting for all routes.

9. The eIF2 nucleotide cycle and its regulation by phosphorylation

The nucleotide carried by eIF2 changes its functional state during initiation. The GTP-bound factor associates with initiator Met-tRNAi, forming a ternary complex that can participate in assembly of a preinitiation complex. During start-site selection, eIF2-associated GTP is hydrolyzed in the regulated initiation cycle. The factor ultimately leaves the ribosome in a GDP-bound form. GDP is not converted to GTP while it remains attached simply by adding an inorganic phosphate. Instead the bound GDP is released and another nucleotide, GTP, can bind. A guanine nucleotide exchange factor, eIF2B, promotes the exchange process.

It is worth distinguishing the energy-consuming reaction from the exchange reaction. The hydrolysis of GTP is associated with directional progress through initiation. eIF2B does not synthesize GTP from GDP as a metabolic kinase. Its catalytic contribution is to facilitate nucleotide exchange so that eIF2 can be reused. The cell's nucleotide metabolism maintains an environment in which GTP can become available for rebinding. If eIF2B activity is inhibited, GDP-bound eIF2 accumulates relative to the normal cycling state, and the supply of newly assembled ternary complexes can decline. A cell may therefore contain nearly normal total amounts of eIF2 while exhibiting a substantial loss of its initiation-supporting activity.

In mammalian cells, phosphorylation of serine 51 of the eIF2 alpha subunit is a major mechanism for controlling this exchange cycle. The alpha subunit is not itself the principal GTP-binding subunit; eIF2 is a heterotrimer, with the gamma subunit carrying the nucleotide-binding function. Phosphorylation changes the way the factor interacts with eIF2B. Phosphorylated eIF2 is inhibitory to the exchange machinery, rather than simply becoming an efficient substrate that releases GDP more rapidly. As a consequence, recycling of the available eIF2 pool is impaired and the concentration of active ternary complex decreases. A relatively limited amount of phosphorylated factor can have a substantial influence when exchange capacity is limiting.

The inhibitory interaction is an example of why phosphorylation cannot be interpreted by a universal rule of activation. Adding a phosphate changes molecular charge, interaction surfaces and sometimes conformational preferences. In this case phosphorylation of one component inhibits the productive activity of another. In the mTOR pathway discussed later, phosphorylation of a different inhibitory protein relieves repression. Both involve phosphorylation, but the signs of their effects on translation are opposite. An examination question that merely states that a translation-associated protein becomes phosphorylated is incomplete unless it specifies the target and the functional consequence of that modification.

One can reason about the exchange step with a simple illustrative model in which the effective active exchange machinery is reduced from a value of one to a fraction f. Suppose production of usable ternary complex is proportional to f and its consumption is first order in ternary-complex concentration. If all other parameters remain fixed, the steady-state ternary-complex pool is proportional to f. An inhibition that reduces effective exchange capacity to 25 percent of its initial value would then reduce that pool to 25 percent of its original value. This is a pedagogical linear model, not a measured description of the actual multimeric eIF2B system. Real exchange activity and initiation can show saturation, competition, feedback and nonlinear sensitivity. It clarifies the causal direction without assigning an unjustified quantitative law to the biological machinery.

The expected effects of mutations follow from their positions in this cycle. A nonphosphorylatable eIF2 alpha S51A mutant cannot acquire the canonical serine-51 phosphate at that residue. Under a stress whose acute initiation response depends on that phosphorylation, this mutant should diminish that specific mode of repression, assuming the mutant replaces sufficient endogenous factor and retains normal basal activity. Other inhibitory mechanisms may still reduce translation. A mutation that severely impairs eIF2B exchange activity can reduce translation even without increased eIF2 alpha phosphorylation. A variant of eIF2B that retains exchange function but is less sensitive to phosphorylated eIF2 may partially uncouple stress sensing from the normal decrease in ternary-complex availability. The outcome depends on residual activity and cellular context; these are mechanistic predictions, not blanket guarantees of viability.

Removal of the phosphate allows the system to recover its productive cycling behavior. In mammalian cells, protein phosphatase 1 complexes with regulatory partners contribute to dephosphorylation of eIF2 alpha. Constitutive and stress-induced regulatory components help balance kinase action. The induction of a stress-responsive phosphatase regulatory component such as GADD34 can form part of feedback that restores translation. This creates a temporal structure: an acute stress signal inhibits general initiation, selected adaptive products are made, and appropriate recovery mechanisms can reverse the inhibition. Prolonged inhibition may be maladaptive, and recovery before the original stress is resolved can also be costly. Translational regulation thus functions as a reversible allocation system rather than a permanent erasure of genetic information.

10. Four stress-sensitive kinases converge on a common initiation factor

The mammalian integrated stress response illustrates a general design principle: different physiological problems can converge on a shared molecular event while retaining distinct mechanisms of sensing. Four well-known protein kinases phosphorylate the same regulatory residue of eIF2 alpha. They are usually designated GCN2, PERK, PKR and HRI. Their names refer to different physiological contexts or historical discoveries. In each case, the downstream effect on eIF2B and ternary-complex availability can reduce the initiation of many messages. The common downstream reaction does not imply that the upstream kinases recognize the same stimulus.

GCN2 is classically associated with amino acid limitation. As the concentration of a required amino acid falls, the charging of cognate tRNAs may become inadequate. Uncharged tRNAs and features of the stressed translation machinery contribute to activation of GCN2. The resulting eIF2 alpha phosphorylation lowers general initiation, thereby reducing the demand for aminoacyl-tRNAs. A selective increase in the synthesis of stress-responsive proteins can help the cell adjust amino acid uptake, biosynthesis and metabolism. This mechanism is conceptually related to the bacterial stringent response in that both respond to difficulties arising during protein synthesis, but the signal-transduction molecules are different. GCN2 does not produce ppGpp, and RelA is not a eukaryotic eIF2 kinase.

PERK illustrates the connection between protein synthesis and the folding capacity of the endoplasmic reticulum. Newly synthesized secretory and membrane proteins enter or associate with the ER through mechanisms treated in later chapters. When the ER folding environment is disturbed, the rate at which newly arriving proteins can be processed may become smaller than the rate at which they are produced. In this situation, PERK activation contributes to an acute reduction in general translation through eIF2 alpha phosphorylation. Lowering new protein influx can provide an opportunity for folding and quality-control systems to recover. This branch of the unfolded protein response is particularly useful as an illustration of negative feedback at the level of protein demand. The complete ER stress signaling program includes other branches and should not be identified solely with PERK.

PKR is best known for activation by double-stranded RNA in appropriate cellular contexts. Activation of this kinase during certain viral infections can restrict translation through phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha. This creates a physiological tradeoff, because a virus commonly uses host components to produce its proteins, yet host synthesis also depends on the same apparatus. The change is not restricted automatically to viral mRNAs. A cell's capacity to balance antiviral responses, adaptive protein synthesis and other functions determines the eventual outcome. Viral mechanisms that evade this response are diverse and are assigned to the subsequent gene-expression chapter; the important translation principle is the convergence of a nucleic acid-related signal on a shared initiation factor.

HRI was classically characterized in the context of heme availability and hemoglobin production in erythroid cells. Hemoglobin requires both globin protein chains and heme groups. If heme supply becomes inadequate while globin continues to be made at the original rate, the imbalance can lead to accumulation of unpaired or poorly handled globin chains. HRI-dependent phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha can reduce general initiation and thereby help adjust globin production to the availability of its nonprotein partner. HRI can respond to additional forms of stress in some cellular contexts, so the classical name does not restrict it exclusively to heme. The example nevertheless offers a memorable causal link between the synthesis of a protein and the availability of a required prosthetic group.

A common experimental strategy tests whether the downstream phosphorylation is necessary for a particular translational response. Cells with a normal eIF2 alpha allele can be compared with cells in which the relevant serine cannot be phosphorylated. After an appropriate stress, an immunoblot with a phosphorylation-specific antibody establishes whether the modification occurred, and a direct measure of protein synthesis tests the functional response. A kinase-deficient cell line can help identify the upstream branch. A negative result needs careful interpretation: incomplete deletion, compensating kinases, alternative stress pathways, and differences in the initial state can obscure the predicted response. Restoration of the wild-type kinase or an appropriate genetic rescue increases causal confidence. Measuring the total abundance of eIF2 alpha as well as its phosphorylated form distinguishes a modification change from altered protein expression.

Convergence also creates a limitation for diagnosis. Detecting elevated eIF2 alpha phosphorylation in a cell does not uniquely identify which kinase was responsible, just as observing a low synthesis rate does not uniquely identify the inhibited step. To distinguish the upstream input, one needs information about the stress, activity of candidate kinases, genetic perturbations or appropriate pathway markers. Similarly, a normal phosphorylation signal does not exclude translation inhibition through mTOR, RNA-binding proteins, aminoacyl-tRNA scarcity or other routes. A good examination answer therefore proceeds from the stimulus to the sensing branch, from the kinase to its substrate, and from the modified substrate to the exchange and initiation mechanism, without treating any single measurement as proof of an entire pathway.

11. Upstream reading frames and selective protein synthesis during stress

The reduction of general initiation following eIF2 alpha phosphorylation might suggest that every protein must be produced more slowly. A particularly important exception arises when the leader of a messenger contains short upstream open reading frames, often abbreviated uORFs. An open reading frame starts at a suitable initiation codon and continues in the same reading frame until a termination codon. An upstream reading frame is recognized before the principal protein-coding start site in the conventional scanning model. Translating it can change the probability that a ribosome subsequently initiates at the main coding sequence. It is therefore a structural feature of the message that can convert a global change in initiation-factor availability into a transcript-specific effect.

To understand how this occurs, distinguish leaky scanning from reinitiation. In leaky scanning, a scanning complex does not initiate at an upstream start codon and continues toward a later start site. Its ability to bypass that codon can depend on the initiation context and factor state. In reinitiation, the ribosome actually translates an upstream reading frame and terminates. Under some conditions a small-subunit-containing complex remains associated with the message, resumes movement along the leader and later acquires the factors necessary for another initiation event. Reinitiation is not a continuation of the same polypeptide. The upstream polypeptide has been released, and the new start site establishes a new protein product and reading frame.

Whether the ribosome can reinitiate depends on multiple properties. Short reading frames may permit retention of factors or interactions needed for resumption of scanning more readily than some long reading frames. The distance from the upstream termination site to a downstream start determines how much time is available to reacquire an initiator-tRNA-bearing complex. The upstream start context affects how many scanning particles translate the upstream region in the first place. The downstream context determines whether a competent scanning complex accepts that codon. The sequence of a uORF peptide can sometimes influence elongation or termination as well. Thus an upstream reading frame is not a universal inhibitory switch; its consequence depends on the entire arrangement.

The mammalian ATF4 message provides the classic stress-responsive example. Its leader contains a first upstream reading frame that facilitates the regulated reinitiation mechanism, followed by a second upstream reading frame that is inhibitory for production of the main ATF4 protein. The second reading frame begins upstream of the ATF4 start and extends into the main coding region in a different reading frame. If a ribosome initiates at this second uORF, it passes the principal start site while translating another sequence. Under the usual forward-scanning mechanism, termination downstream of that start prevents the ribosome from simply returning to initiate at the ATF4 start that it already passed.

In favorable growth conditions, ternary complexes are relatively available. After translation and termination at uORF1, a scanning small-subunit-containing complex can reacquire initiation competence in time to recognize the start of the inhibitory uORF2. Translation of uORF2 then reduces the fraction that reaches the main ATF4 coding region as a competent initiating complex. This means that efficient availability of initiation components can actually support a route that suppresses ATF4 synthesis. It is a local consequence of the arrangement of start and stop sites, not a direct inhibitory action of eIF2 on the ATF4 protein.

During the canonical integrated stress response, phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha decreases eIF2B activity and ternary-complex supply. After the first uORF has been translated, reacquisition of initiation competence can take longer on average. Some scanning complexes therefore pass the start of uORF2 before becoming able to initiate again. If they acquire the required ternary complex before reaching the main ATF4 start, they can initiate at that start instead of the inhibitory one. The overall probability of productive initiation on many other messages falls, while the routing of a subset of ribosomes along the ATF4 leader changes in a way that favors ATF4 synthesis. The resulting ATF4 protein is a transcription factor that can influence expression of adaptive genes, but its own acute increase in synthesis is an example of translational regulation.

The time window is crucial. If ternary-complex availability became extremely low, initial recruitment and subsequent reinitiation at the main start could both fail. The model does not predict an unlimited rise in ATF4 production as initiation capacity approaches zero. Rather, it predicts that an intermediate reduction can increase the relative probability of bypassing an inhibitory site while retaining sufficient initiation competence for the desired downstream site. Stress strength, message abundance and alternative modes of regulation can modify the observed absolute output. Thus a relative increase in the use of the main coding frame does not guarantee an absolute increase in protein synthesis under every degree of stress.

A simplified probability model illustrates the delay. Assume a ribosome has just completed uORF1 and resumes scanning at a constant speed. Assume acquisition of ternary complex follows a first-order waiting-time distribution with rate constant λ per second. If the inhibitory start is reached after time t2 and the main start after time t3, with t3 greater than t2, the probability of remaining unprepared at t2 but becoming prepared by t3 is exp(−λt2) − exp(−λt3). This follows from the probability of waiting longer than a given time, exp(−λt), and subtracting the subset that is still unprepared at the later site. We assume every complex prepared before the inhibitory start initiates there and every one acquiring competence in the interval initiates at the main start. Neither assumption is exactly true for real ATF4, but both make the causal prediction explicit.

Take hypothetical values t2 = 2 seconds and t3 = 6 seconds. At λ = 1 per second, the calculated probability for the desired interval is exp(−2) − exp(−6), approximately 0.1329. At λ = 0.3 per second it is exp(−0.6) − exp(−1.8), approximately 0.3835. At λ = 0.02 per second it is exp(−0.04) − exp(−0.12), approximately 0.0739. An intermediate reduction in the acquisition rate favors the main frame in this example; a very strong reduction does not. The probabilities are conditional on retention of a scanning particle following uORF1 and do not incorporate the fall in initial recruitment. They are not experimentally measured percentages of ATF4 translation. Multiplying them by the number of particles entering the pathway would be necessary to model absolute protein output.

The model also suggests a useful mutation experiment. Removing the inhibitory uORF2 start codon while preserving the main coding sequence can relieve basal repression and reduce the contrast between unstressed and stressed cells. Disrupting the first uORF may alter the regulated reinitiation route and diminish normal stress-dependent activation, although the exact residual output depends on how scanning complexes engage the remaining starts. Changing the spacing between the uORFs can alter the probability of reacquiring ternary complex before the inhibitory start. A change that adds time before uORF2 may increase initiation at the inhibitory site in conditions that would otherwise favor bypass. To attribute a result to spacing rather than a newly introduced RNA structure, the inserted sequence should be tested with appropriate alternatives.

Classic reporter experiments by Vattem and Wek in 2004 tested the function of the two ATF4 upstream reading frames and the requirement for scanning-dependent reinitiation. Independent work by Lu and colleagues in the same period also supported regulated reinitiation at alternative reading frames. The strength of such experiments is that the leader sequence can be altered separately from the principal reporter protein. Genetic control of eIF2 phosphorylation and comparisons of leader variants connect factor state to the RNA architecture. The studies establish the canonical mechanism without requiring one to assume that every stress condition or every uORF-containing message behaves identically. The distinction between a demonstrated mechanism for a specific transcript and a prediction for a new transcript is an important scientific boundary.

12. Yeast GCN4 as a comparative genetic model

The GCN4 system in budding yeast provides a historically important model for understanding translational regulation by upstream reading frames. Gcn4 is a transcriptional regulator that contributes to the response to amino acid limitation, including the induction of genes involved in amino acid biosynthesis. The message contains four well-studied upstream reading frames. Under nutrient-replete conditions, the availability of initiation components favors reinitiation at downstream inhibitory upstream frames rather than efficient synthesis of the main Gcn4 protein. Under amino acid starvation, signaling through Gcn2 reduces availability of the ternary complex and changes the probability of reinitiation at the different sites.

The first upstream reading frame is important because translation of it can be followed by retention of scanning competence. In the standard simplified model, a scanning particle emerging from uORF1 can subsequently reinitiate at one of the downstream upstream frames if it acquires ternary complex sufficiently early. Initiation at the more distal inhibitory frames, especially the fourth in the classical simplified comparison, generally reduces main-frame expression. During amino acid starvation, delayed reacquisition can allow particles to bypass inhibitory starts and become competent nearer the GCN4 coding region. The logic resembles the ATF4 example, but the actual numbers, spacing and properties of the upstream frames differ. One should not draw the yeast leader as the two-frame mammalian ATF4 leader.

Classical genetic work by Hinnebusch and colleagues used deletion and alteration of upstream frames and reporter constructs to distinguish the roles of upstream regions. If all upstream frames were merely equivalent barriers, each deletion should have a similar directional effect. The finding that particular upstream frames are required for efficient regulated derepression while others have inhibitory functions instead supports a model in which ribosomal history and timing matter. Further tests of the sequences around the first frame helped explain why some terminating complexes can retain the ability to resume scanning. This progression from genetic observation to a mechanistic model is a useful illustration of how translational regulation was investigated before genome-wide ribosome occupancy methods became common.

From the standpoint of comparative biology, both yeast and mammalian examples exploit changes in a shared initiation resource to adjust the synthesis of proteins that promote adaptation. However, the full mammalian set of four eIF2 alpha kinases should not be assigned to budding yeast as though all eukaryotes possess identical stress signaling. The classic yeast nutrient-response model involves Gcn2. Likewise, the downstream proteins are not identical in every organism even though the general logic is similar. Comparing conserved biochemical steps and differing RNA architectures is more informative than memorizing a list of factor names without their biological relationships.

An examination may describe a hypothetical eukaryotic reporter with an upstream positive frame followed by an inhibitory frame. If lowering ternary-complex abundance increases expression of the main reporter but reduces general protein synthesis, the most reasonable first hypothesis is altered reinitiation timing. To distinguish it from enhanced transcription, one should measure reporter mRNA. To distinguish reinitiation from direct downstream recruitment, one can change the first upstream start or insert an appropriate scanning barrier, while controlling RNA stability. To test the role of eIF2 phosphorylation, one can compare cells capable and incapable of the relevant phosphorylation response. These experimental predictions follow from the causal structure of the model and do not require memorizing the exact nucleotide sequence of GCN4 or ATF4.

13. Nutrient-sensitive mTORC1 and cap-associated initiation

Cells growing in favorable nutrient conditions face a different allocation problem from cells experiencing acute stress. They must decide whether the available resources justify investment in protein synthesis, cell growth and production of additional ribosomes. In mammalian cells, mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1, abbreviated mTORC1, integrates information associated with nutrient supply, growth signaling and energetic state. Its translation-related targets provide a direct route from those conditions to initiation machinery. The detailed network that activates mTORC1 belongs to cell signaling. For the present purpose, its relevant function is that of a protein kinase acting on regulators of translation.

The major mechanistic example involves eIF4E-binding proteins, commonly called 4E-BPs. In a relatively hypophosphorylated state, an inhibitory 4E-BP can bind eIF4E and compete with eIF4G for an interaction needed to assemble a productive eIF4F complex. This distinction is important: eIF4E may still bind the cap while a 4E-BP blocks the interaction with eIF4G. The repressor need not physically cover the cap itself to prevent productive recruitment. A decrease in the availability of eIF4E for eIF4G association can reduce cap-dependent initiation even when the amount of capped mRNA and total eIF4E are unchanged.

When mTORC1 activity is sufficient, phosphorylation of 4E-BPs reduces their inhibitory association with eIF4E in the conventional regulatory model. eIF4G can associate more readily, promoting assembly of the cap-associated machinery and recruitment of preinitiation complexes. Thus phosphorylation of the inhibitor removes a brake. When nutrient limitation or other appropriate conditions reduce mTORC1 activity, 4E-BPs can remain or become less phosphorylated and exert stronger repression. The result is a reduction in synthesis of many proteins, but the magnitude of the response depends on the transcript and the cellular context. The principal substrate in this regulatory example is 4E-BP; it is incorrect to say that mTORC1 stimulates translation simply by phosphorylating eIF2 alpha.

The other important branch involves S6 kinases. mTORC1 can phosphorylate and activate S6K proteins, which act on additional substrates involved in translational processes. Some of these downstream effects enhance the activity or availability of factors participating in initiation and RNA remodeling. The ribosomal protein S6 is a well-known substrate in this signaling network, but the presence of phosphorylated S6 is a marker of pathway activity rather than a direct universal measurement of protein production. Changes in phospho-S6 and actual synthesis can diverge because other limiting steps remain. A complete inference should distinguish a pathway readout, the molecular target responsible for a given effect and the measured protein synthesis flux.

Messages carrying a 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine motif, usually termed TOP mRNAs, are especially useful in this context. Many encode ribosomal proteins or other components of the protein synthesis machinery. The motif occurs at the extreme 5′ end rather than being defined simply by a random pyrimidine-rich segment anywhere in the coding sequence. Under conditions favoring growth, enhanced translation of such messages can support an increase in future translational capacity. When growth conditions are poor, restricting their translation can limit investment in additional machinery. Classical ribosome-profiling studies, including the 2012 study by Thoreen and colleagues, found strong sensitivity of TOP and related messages to mTOR inhibition and demonstrated an important contribution from 4E-BPs in the examined system. This finding should not be generalized to a claim that every mTOR-responsive message contains a TOP motif or that every cell uses exactly the same regulatory components.

The pathway is not adequately represented by a single label such as mTOR on equals all translation on. For instance, abundant cap-associated complexes cannot compensate indefinitely for a lack of charged tRNA or very low ternary-complex availability. An actively growing cell still needs amino acids and energy for elongation. Conversely, reducing mTORC1 signaling need not eliminate all protein production, because some messages have distinct recruitment requirements and the degree of inhibition can vary. A growth response is produced by a coordinated change in multiple processes, not by the assumption that one kinase is sufficient for every step of synthesis.

Genetic comparisons can connect changes in pathway activity to changes in initiation. If mTOR inhibition reduces production of a reporter strongly in cells expressing the relevant 4E-BPs but less strongly in cells lacking them, the difference supports mediation through those inhibitory proteins. It does not show that every mTOR-dependent effect has been removed. A nonphosphorylatable 4E-BP variant that retains eIF4E binding is predicted to continue competing with eIF4G even under conditions that activate the kinase. A variant unable to bind eIF4E would be expected to lack that particular direct repression mechanism. The appropriate controls include total factor amounts, reporter RNA abundance, basal synthesis and confirmation that the kinase perturbation reached its target.

Consider an illustrative equilibrium competition for eIF4E. Let G denote free eIF4G, B free 4E-BP and K_G and K_B the corresponding dissociation constants for mutually exclusive binding. Assume that eIF4E does not substantially deplete either free partner. The fraction of eIF4E associated with eIF4G in this simplified one-site model is (G/K_G)/(1 + G/K_G + B/K_B). This is obtained by summing the statistical weights of unoccupied, G-bound and B-bound states. If G/K_G = 4 and B/K_B = 5, the fraction in the G-bound state is 4/10 = 0.4. If phosphorylation lowers effective inhibitor binding so that B/K_B = 1, the fraction becomes 4/6, or approximately 0.667. This model illustrates competition; it ignores the cap, additional factors, multisite phosphorylation and changing protein concentrations. It does not justify converting those fractions directly into biological protein synthesis rates unless a problem supplies the necessary additional assumptions.

14. Specific RNA-binding control: ferritin and iron availability

The cell must balance iron acquisition with iron storage because iron is indispensable for many enzymes but excess reactive iron can contribute to cellular damage. Ferritin is a protein complex that stores iron in a relatively protected form. When iron availability is low, large investments in new ferritin are less useful than making available iron accessible to essential functions. When intracellular iron becomes abundant, increased storage capacity is advantageous. A post-transcriptional system can adjust the production of ferritin and proteins involved in iron uptake without requiring each individual change to be initiated at a promoter.

The ferritin mRNAs contain a structured sequence in the 5′ untranslated region known as an iron-responsive element, or IRE. Iron regulatory proteins, IRP1 and IRP2, can recognize such structures. Under low-iron conditions their RNA-binding activity promotes occupancy of the ferritin IRE, which impairs productive translation initiation. The relatively cap-proximal location of the element is important to the classical mechanism of interference with recruitment. When iron becomes sufficiently available, the RNA-binding activity or abundance of the appropriate regulatory proteins changes, permitting more efficient ferritin synthesis. IRP1 can switch between an RNA-binding form and a form with an iron-sulfur cluster associated with cytosolic aconitase activity, whereas the abundance of IRP2 is regulated differently. Neither protein should be described simply as iron itself binding the mRNA.

The response becomes especially instructive when compared with the transferrin receptor message. Transferrin receptor 1 participates in iron acquisition and its mRNA contains iron-responsive elements in its 3′ untranslated region. Under low-iron conditions, binding by IRPs contributes to stabilization of this message. Thus low iron can reduce ferritin translation while maintaining more of a message that supports iron uptake. The apparent opposition is not a contradiction in the molecular specificity of IRPs. The outcomes differ because the relevant RNA elements occupy different regulatory contexts. The same kind of binding event can interfere with initiation in a cap-proximal leader or influence transcript survival in a 3′ region. When iron becomes more available, decreased IRP-mediated binding can favor ferritin synthesis and allow increased turnover of the receptor transcript.

This comparison illustrates a crucial examination trap. It is sometimes suggested that an RNA-binding protein always inhibits translation, or that a regulatory motif has the same functional effect wherever it is inserted. Neither generalization is reliable. The location of an element relative to a molecular event is often essential. A ferritin reporter with a disrupted IRE that cannot bind IRP should show reduced sensitivity to the classical low-iron repression mechanism, assuming other properties remain intact. Transferring the IRE to a different region of a transcript need not recreate the same repression, because the geometry relative to ribosome recruitment is changed. A test of these predictions should compare reporter transcript abundance and newly synthesized reporter protein. Changes in cellular iron can also influence metabolism and other processes, so a reporter lacking the element is an important control.

In a hypothetical dataset, cells with low iron produce 20 units of ferritin reporter per minute from 100 units of reporter mRNA, while iron-replete cells produce 80 units per minute from the same RNA quantity. The effective synthesis-per-RNA index increases fourfold. In a separate transferrin receptor experiment the RNA level itself falls from 200 units to 50 after iron replenishment, while production per RNA unit remains constant. The first observation fits translational control and the second fits regulation of available RNA, although more evidence would be required to establish the involvement of specific IRPs. Comparing these two mechanisms teaches more than memorizing the names of the elements: it demonstrates that an identical environmental input can coordinate protein outputs through different expression layers.

15. Elongation, ribosomal loading and competition for resources

Initiation is a powerful control point because preventing entry of a ribosome can avoid a large downstream expenditure of amino acids and nucleotides. But a cell cannot regulate its protein output exclusively through entry. A ribosome that has initiated needs to receive the appropriate charged tRNA for each new codon, transfer the growing peptide and move to the next codon. Problems at any of these steps can reduce the probability or rate of successful completion. Translational regulation at the level of elongation and the availability of active ribosomes therefore complements initiation control. This distinction is especially important when a biochemical experiment measures ribosomes rather than finished products.

Eukaryotic elongation factor eEF2 promotes translocation of the ribosome along the mRNA. In mammalian systems, phosphorylation of eEF2 by eEF2 kinase can reduce its translocation-promoting activity. This offers another example in which phosphorylation inhibits a component of the translation apparatus rather than activating it. An increase in inhibitory eEF2 phosphorylation can slow elongation without first preventing recruitment of the 40S subunit at the cap. Nutrient and stress signaling can influence this regulatory branch, although the exact signaling inputs and their relative importance vary by context. A student should distinguish eEF2, an elongation factor, from eIF2, an initiation factor involved in delivery of initiator tRNA. Despite the similar names and use of GTP-dependent cycles, the factors act at different stages.

Consider the energy required for producing a new polypeptide. Aminoacylation of each tRNA ordinarily consumes ATP to AMP and pyrophosphate, conventionally counted as two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents when pyrophosphate hydrolysis is included. For each typical elongation addition after the first residue, one GTP is used for factor-mediated aminoacyl-tRNA delivery and another for translocation in the standard simplified accounting. Initiation, termination and recycling can require additional nucleotide use depending on the system and assumptions. For a chain of 100 residues, charging 100 tRNAs accounts for 200 equivalents, and the 99 typical elongation cycles account for 198 GTP equivalents, yielding 398 before those other costs. This convention does not imply that four ATP molecules are chemically hydrolyzed at each peptide bond, nor does it include the metabolic cost of synthesizing amino acids or maintaining cell physiology.

If a cell initiates fewer new chains, it avoids much of the future expenditure associated with those chains. If instead elongation arrests after many residues have already been incorporated, the resources spent up to that point may not yield a usable full-length product. There can also be an opportunity cost: a stalled ribosome cannot immediately initiate another productive translation. If quality control removes an incomplete polypeptide, additional resources may be required for recycling or degradation. These considerations help explain why coordinated suppression of initiation can be useful during resource shortage even when the initial biochemical problem is a reduced elongation substrate supply. The detailed molecular systems that rescue stalled complexes are outside the core scope, but the distinction between a paused, terminated and recycled ribosome is essential.

The cell has a limited pool of initiation factors, ribosomes and charged tRNAs. Distinct transcripts therefore compete, directly or indirectly, for shared machinery. Even if the affinity of one message for a recruiting factor remains unchanged, reducing the number or activity of competing messages can alter its relative access to the factor. A change in transcript-specific output need not require the physical binding of a unique regulatory protein to that message. On the other hand, this competition alone is not sufficient to explain every selective response. The specific leader architectures of ATF4, GCN4 or iron-regulated messages provide mechanisms that can be tested by mutating those features. A clear explanation distinguishes global competition effects from experimentally demonstrated cis-dependent regulation.

16. Measuring synthesis, occupancy and transcript abundance

To distinguish the mechanisms discussed in this chapter, measurements should be chosen according to the physical quantity of interest. An RNA blot or quantitative reverse-transcription PCR measures a property of the RNA pool. The assay can show that a particular transcript becomes more or less abundant, although its result depends on which sequence is measured, the integrity of the RNA and the normalization. A western blot measures accumulated protein recognized by the antibody. Both measurements are valuable, but their ratio need not be the instantaneous translation rate because protein maturation and loss occur at their own rates. A short pulse of labeled amino acid incorporated into nascent chains can provide information about new protein synthesis during the labeling window.

Radiolabeled amino acid incorporation is a classical approach to observing newly synthesized material. A comparison of incorporation over equal short time intervals can reveal differences in synthesis if precursor specific activity, uptake and available amino acid pools are appropriately controlled. A compound such as puromycin can also be used in suitable assays of nascent protein synthesis because its aminoacyl-like structure allows it to become associated with a growing chain through the ribosomal catalytic reaction, resulting in premature termination. An assay using an antibody against incorporated puromycin can provide a comparative snapshot of synthesis under defined conditions. The measurement is not simply a count of completed mature proteins, and the labeling reagent can itself perturb translation. The duration and interpretation of such assays must therefore be controlled rather than treating the signal as an exact universal rate.

Sucrose-gradient sedimentation separates ribosomal assemblies according to physical behavior. An RNA associated with several ribosomes sediments differently from free messenger or smaller complexes. When new initiation is suppressed while elongation and termination continue, pre-existing translating ribosomes can finish and leave the message. Over time the distribution may shift toward lighter fractions and away from heavily loaded polysomes. If elongation is slowed while initiation continues, ribosomes may remain associated for longer and the distribution may instead shift toward more heavily loaded states. These are predictions under the relevant assumptions, not universally diagnostic signatures. Global stress can change both steps simultaneously, and a heavily loaded fraction may include messages whose ribosomes are not progressing normally.

Ribosome profiling measures short RNA fragments protected from digestion by ribosomes and maps those fragments to transcript sequences. The approach introduced in a widely cited 2009 study by Ingolia and colleagues made it possible to examine translation-associated occupancy across many genes and positions. Reading-frame periodicity and the distribution of footprints relative to coding boundaries can provide evidence about where translation is occurring. A change in occupancy near a particular codon can indicate altered dwell time, but an increased local signal is not automatically evidence that more protein has been produced. A local pileup can result from slowed progress; conversely, fewer ribosomes per message can reflect more rapid transit if initiation is unchanged. The assay is particularly useful when interpreted alongside RNA abundance and an independent measurement of protein synthesis.

A reporter experiment can isolate a particular regulatory feature by placing a candidate leader before a common coding sequence. The comparison should keep the promoter, coding sequence and other relevant features constant where feasible. A control with a mutated regulatory element tests necessity; transferring a candidate element to a suitable otherwise unresponsive reporter can test whether that element confers regulation in the new context. Necessity and sufficiency are different claims. A sequence may be necessary for a response in its natural messenger but insufficient to reproduce it in a different leader. Likewise a transferred element can confer a response in one artificial arrangement without establishing that the native regulatory context is identical.

An important qualification applies to assays of translation initiation based on reporter luminescence or fluorescence. The readout may depend on the folding, maturation and stability of the reporter protein and on metabolic substrates required for its detection. A stress treatment could reduce luminescence through reduced ATP availability even when the amount of the enzyme is unchanged, depending on the assay. Using an appropriate control reporter, measuring reporter protein independently and assessing RNA abundance helps prevent a purely technical change from being mistaken for translational repression. Time courses also distinguish rapid translational effects from delayed changes in expression or cell number.

The most persuasive design uses orthogonal evidence. For instance, a stress-dependent reduction in cap-associated eIF4G interaction, decreased nascent protein synthesis, unchanged abundance of a selected reporter RNA and weaker inhibition in 4E-BP-deficient cells jointly support a cap-recruitment mechanism. No individual readout establishes the entire chain. If a factor knockout rescues reporter output but also changes the RNA pool substantially, it becomes necessary to determine how much of the rescue occurs per transcript. This approach to evidence is the foundation for Part C questions: identify what was actually measured, state what the result implies, and distinguish the favored mechanism from alternatives that remain possible.

17. Integrating experimental results and comparing regulatory systems

Suppose a cultured mammalian cell population is examined before and shortly after nutrient withdrawal. Measurements show that total eIF2 alpha is unchanged, phosphorylation of eIF2 alpha rises, phosphorylation of 4E-BP falls, bulk nascent protein synthesis decreases and ATF4 synthesis increases. This hypothetical set of observations is compatible with activation of an integrated stress response together with reduced mTORC1-associated signaling. Both pathways can decrease general initiation, but the increase in ATF4 can be understood by regulated reinitiation. The data do not establish that the two pathways act independently, nor do they quantify their individual contributions. To separate them one might compare a phosphorylation-resistant eIF2 alpha background, cells lacking relevant 4E-BPs and appropriately controlled combinations of perturbations.

Imagine that bulk synthesis is normalized to 100 in untreated cells. In the stressed wild type it is 30. In stressed cells with a phosphorylation-resistant eIF2 alpha variant it is 55, and in stressed cells lacking 4E-BPs it is 60. In a double-perturbed background it is 85. These illustrative measurements support contributions from both regulatory branches, assuming the unstressed baselines and experimental conditions are comparable. They do not establish a unique additive biochemical law. The cell can respond differently to chronic mutations, and nonlinear changes in common limiting resources may affect the measured outcome. Residual inhibition of 15 units in the double perturbation could reflect other stress effects or incomplete functional rescue; the data alone cannot specify which. A careful conclusion states the evidence for contributions and the limitations of the intervention.

A second hypothetical case concerns two bacterial reporters expressed from the same promoter. Their protein coding regions are identical, but one possesses a leader containing an inhibitory stem. At a lower temperature both reporters have similar mRNA amounts, while production of the stem-containing reporter is markedly lower. At higher temperature its output rises relative to the control. The result is consistent with an RNA thermometer, but the control is essential because temperature can alter general translation kinetics. If disrupting the stem leads to high production at both temperatures and restoring pairing restores the temperature response, the structural hypothesis gains support. If the mutated sequences also strongly alter RNA abundance, however, the contribution of altered translation per transcript needs to be reassessed. Structure probing of the leader can provide independent evidence for the change in accessibility.

The comparative logic of these examples is that environmental information changes the ability of a translation apparatus to use particular RNAs or shared factors. Bacterial initiation frequently provides a direct opportunity to control access to a localized ribosome-binding region. Eukaryotic cap-associated recruitment creates a different opportunity to restrict productive engagement with the message. The eIF2 system regulates delivery and recycling of the initiator-tRNA-bearing factor, while specialized leaders can exploit the timing of reinitiation. All of these mechanisms change a molecular probability or resource that influences output. The specific bacterial factors and eukaryotic factors are not interchangeable, and the absence of a nuclear envelope does not imply a universal bacterial model in every prokaryote.

Translation regulation also connects to development and physiology. Cells can retain messages whose translation is delayed until a suitable stage or cellular location, allowing production to be coordinated with biological requirements. In growing cells, regulation of ribosome production helps match the future capacity for protein synthesis to metabolic supply. In cells handling secretory proteins, regulation of new synthesis can help maintain balance with organelle folding capacity. In bacteria experiencing a changing environment, RNA structures and regulatory molecules can rapidly adjust protein output. These examples share a useful causal theme, but the detailed developmental programs, cellular signaling networks and protein-targeting pathways have their own chapters. The aim here is to understand the control points in the synthesis machinery, not to duplicate every biological pathway in which those control points are used.

In practice, a student encountering a novel mechanism can ask four connected questions. First, which molecular quantity has changed: transcript availability, recruitment, initiator-tRNA supply, elongation, active ribosome number or protein lifetime? Second, which component or RNA feature is directly responsible for the change? Third, which experiment would separate that explanation from alternatives? Fourth, does the proposed mechanism explain both the behavior of the individual transcript and the general response of the cell? The four questions provide a framework for reasoning about unfamiliar figures, genetic interactions and quantitative data. The strength of the chapter's conceptual models lies not in the ability to memorize every named example, but in their ability to generate testable predictions under clearly defined assumptions.

Part C: Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Original analytical problems. Numerical datasets are hypothetical educational examples.

31. A cell has 400 usable mRNAs, each producing 0.25 proteins/min. A stress reduces the rate per RNA to 0.10 without changing RNA abundance. Find both synthesis fluxes and percentage reduction.

numerical

Answer: 100 and 40 proteins/min; 60%

J=kT M, giving 400×0.25=100 and 400×0.10=40. The loss is (100−40)/100×100=60%. The unchanged RNA excludes an abundance-only explanation in this model.

32. At steady state transcription adds 60 RNAs/min and kM=0.2/min. If kT=0.4 proteins per RNA/min and kP=0.03/min, calculate M and P.

numerical

Answer: 300 RNAs; 4000 proteins

M=60/0.2=300, J=0.4×300=120 proteins/min, P=120/0.03=4000. This requires time-independent rates at steady state.

33. An initial steady protein pool of 1000 receives a new synthesis flux of 20/min; kP=0.04/min. What is the pool after one loss half-time?

numerical

Answer: 750 proteins

New equilibrium=20/0.04=500. Excess above equilibrium halves from 500 to 250, so P=750. The total pool does not simply halve.

34. A 450-codon ORF is translated at 7.5 codons/s. Initiation occurs every 12 s. Assuming no interference, find transit time and mean occupancy.

numerical

Answer: 60 s and 5 ribosomes

τ=450/7.5=60 s. N=τ/12=5. Occupancy is not output; flux is one completion per 12 s at steady state if all finish.

35. A gene has RNA counts 100 and 250 and footprint counts 200 and 750 in two conditions. Calculate the fold change in footprints per RNA.

numerical

Answer: 1.5-fold

Before: 200/100=2; after: 750/250=3. Ratio=3/2=1.5. Footprints per RNA represent occupancy, not necessarily completed protein flux.

36. In a two-state leader at 310 K, ΔG(open−closed)=0. Calculate the open fraction. If ΔG=RT ln 3, recalculate.

numerical

Answer: 0.5 and 0.25

f=1/(1+exp(ΔG/RT)); for zero it is 1/2, for RT ln 3 it is 1/4. Positive ΔG favors the closed state.

37. A repressor binds an RNA with Kd=10 nM. Free repressor rises from 10 to 40 nM. If only free RNA initiates, find normalized initiation before and after.

numerical

Answer: 0.50 and 0.20

Unbound fraction=Kd/(Kd+R). Values are 10/20 and 10/50. Output decreases by 60% relative to the first state, not 30% of baseline in relative units.

38. Assume ternary-complex reacquisition is exponential with λ=0.5/s and inhibitory/main sites are reached at 2 and 4 s. Calculate the conditional probability of bypass followed by successful reacquisition.

numerical

Answer: About 0.2325

P=e^(−0.5×2)−e^(−0.5×4)=e^−1−e^−2=0.3679−0.1353=0.2325. It is conditional on scanning after uORF1.

39. For mutually exclusive binding of eIF4G and 4E-BP, G/KG=3 and B/KB=6. After phosphorylation B/KB becomes 2. Find G-bound eIF4E fractions.

numerical

Answer: 0.30 and 0.50

fG=(G/KG)/(1+G/KG+B/KB). Before=3/10, after=3/6. This does not by itself give the protein synthesis rate.

40. A 250-residue polypeptide uses the simplified charging and elongation accounting, excluding initiation, termination and recycling. Find high-energy equivalents.

numerical

Answer: 998 equivalents

Charging=2×250=500. Elongation=2×249=498. Total=998; this is phosphate-bond equivalents, not 998 literal ATP molecules.

41. A bacterial leader mutant produces 5× more reporter but mRNA abundance is 5× greater as well. What conclusion is justified?

analytical

Answer: No demonstrated increase in output per transcript

The reporter/RNA ratio is unchanged if the numerator measures actual synthesis. Increased RNA stability or transcription could explain the output. A direct initiation assay is required before assigning a mechanism.

42. Four reporter/sRNA genotypes show repression only for wild-type/wild-type and a complementary double mutant. Why is this stronger than a single knockout?

analytical

Answer: It supports sequence-specific RNA pairing

Single mismatches disrupt the predicted duplex and compensatory changes restore it. Comparable RNA abundance and structure controls remain necessary to exclude indirect effects.

43. An RNA thermometer reporter rises from 10 to 60 at high temperature; a control lacking the leader rises from 50 to 100. RNA is unchanged. Is the increase explained entirely by general temperature effects?

analytical

Answer: The data favor a leader-specific component

The test rises sixfold while control rises twofold. General temperature effects can contribute, but do not readily account for the extra relative response; structure mutations and probing test causality.

44. An operon reporter retains downstream RNA after an upstream premature stop but downstream protein output decreases. A mutation opening the downstream stem rescues output. Explain.

analytical

Answer: Translation-dependent exposure of the downstream start

The rescue supports structural coupling between upstream ribosome passage and downstream initiation. Full-length RNA measurement reduces the likelihood of transcriptional polarity as the only cause.

45. A starvation mutant cannot activate RelA but loses charged tRNA and protein synthesis nonetheless. Does this refute the stringent response?

analytical

Answer: No; direct substrate limitation is independent of the signaling defect

Elongation still lacks charged tRNA; RelA mediates a separate adaptive alarmone branch. Measure (p)ppGpp and rRNA synthesis to assess the response.

46. After stress, bulk RNA remains constant, polysomes become heavier and nascent labeling falls. Which mechanism should be investigated first?

analytical

Answer: Slower elongation or stalling

Longer residence time increases occupancy while synthesis declines. An initiation increase alone would not readily explain the decreased flux, although combined regulation remains possible.

47. A mutant eIF2α S51A prevents stress-dependent loss of initiation but does not restore cap-associated eIF4G recruitment. Interpret.

analytical

Answer: Distinct initiation checkpoints remain

The mutant disrupts canonical phosphorylation-dependent control, not necessarily 4E-BP-mediated repression. Measuring both factor states distinguishes the two branches.

48. Total eIF2 is unchanged, phosphorylation is low, but eIF2B activity is severely impaired. Predict ternary-complex supply.

analytical

Answer: Reduced

Exchange is necessary to recycle GDP-bound eIF2 independently of the phosphorylation signal. Normal total eIF2 does not guarantee active GTP-loaded factor.

49. A reporter carrying the ATF4 leader loses most stress inducibility when uORF2 is removed, and basal synthesis increases. Explain.

analytical

Answer: Removing the inhibitory frame relieves basal repression

Normally early reinitiation at uORF2 diverts ribosomes from the main frame. Removing that start reduces the need for delayed reinitiation under stress; residual regulation is possible.

50. A synthetic ATF4 reporter has a longer interval from uORF1 termination to uORF2 start. Assuming scanning speed and other features unchanged, predict effect at moderate stress.

analytical

Answer: More opportunity for early ternary-complex reacquisition

The increased time may favor initiation at inhibitory uORF2 and reduce main-frame activation. Sequence insertions can also affect structure, so use matched controls.

51. mTORC1 is active but a nonphosphorylatable 4E-BP retains eIF4E binding. Predict cap-dependent initiation.

analytical

Answer: It may remain restricted

The inhibitor can still compete with eIF4G despite upstream kinase activity. Other mTORC1 targets may retain effects, so suppression is not necessarily complete.

52. mTOR inhibition represses a TOP reporter in wild type but has a smaller effect in a 4E-BP-null cell. What is supported?

analytical

Answer: Contribution of 4E-BPs to the response

Genetic reduction of sensitivity supports mediation through 4E-BPs. It does not prove all mTOR targets act through them or that the reporter responds identically in every cell.

53. Low iron reduces ferritin reporter synthesis without changing reporter RNA, while transferrin receptor RNA increases. Why are the responses opposite?

analytical

Answer: Element position determines different post-transcriptional consequences

IRP binding at a ferritin 5′ IRE inhibits recruitment, whereas 3′ IRE binding can stabilize receptor RNA. The common input coordinates storage and uptake.

54. A luciferase reporter loses light output after ATP depletion, but an immunoblot shows the same amount of reporter protein. Can the result alone establish translational repression?

analytical

Answer: No

Detection chemistry or protein activity can change independently of synthesis. Measure nascent protein production, RNA abundance and an appropriate activity control.

55. A stress elevates phospho-eIF2α and lowers phospho-4E-BP. Double genetic perturbation restores synthesis only partially. What can the experiment establish?

analytical

Answer: Both branches may contribute; residual inhibition is unresolved

Rescue comparisons support contribution if basal conditions are controlled. Residual inhibition could reflect substrate shortage, incomplete rescue or other mechanisms; simple additivity is not guaranteed.

Revision and active recall

High-yield relationships

RNA steady state: M = a/kM. Protein synthesis flux: J = kT M. Protein steady state: P = J/kP. Protein adjustment: P(t) = Pss,new + [P(0) − Pss,new] exp(−kP t). Mean ribosome occupancy in the uncrowded steady-state model: N ≈ iL/v. Two-state RNA accessibility: fopen = 1/[1 + exp(ΔG/RT)]. Conditional reinitiation timing: P = exp(−λt2) − exp(−λt3).

Mechanism map

Bacterial RNA access → small-subunit recruitment. Amino acid scarcity → charged tRNA limitation and stringent signaling. Mammalian stress → eIF2α phosphorylation → inhibited eIF2B → reduced ternary complex. Growth conditions → mTORC1 → 4E-BP phosphorylation → eIF4F assembly. Selected uORF architectures → altered reinitiation → selective translation.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Transcript abundance is not synthesis flux
A transcript count cannot establish output per molecule.
Total protein is not new protein
Protein loss and maturation affect accumulation.
Shine–Dalgarno strength is not accessibility
An inhibitory RNA stem can mask a complementary motif.
Not every bacterial message uses a canonical RBS
Leaderless and noncanonical messages exist.
Small RNAs are not always repressors
They can expose masked initiation sites.
Hfq is not universal
Different species and RNAs use different auxiliary factors.
RelA is not a tRNA synthetase
It generates alarmones rather than charging tRNA.
Ribosome hibernation is not necessarily destruction
Inactive particles may be reactivated.
eIF2 and eEF2 are different
Initiation and translocation are distinct steps.
eIF2B exchanges nucleotides
It does not synthesize GTP from bound GDP.
Phosphorylation need not activate a target
eIF2α phosphorylation inhibits recycling; 4E-BP phosphorylation relieves repression.
ATF4 regulation does not mean unlimited synthesis
Extremely low ternary complex can impair all initiation.
4E-BP need not block cap binding itself
Its key competition is with eIF4G.
Heavy polysomes do not prove rapid synthesis
Slower transit can increase ribosome residence.
Ferritin and receptor IREs have different outcomes
Their location and regulatory context distinguish initiation from stability.

Active recall

1. How can a protein increase despite lower synthesis?

Slower degradation or dilution can raise the accumulated pool.

2. Why does a compensatory RNA mutation matter?

Restoring pairing while changing sequence tests a structural or complementarity hypothesis.

3. What does a temperature-sensitive leader regulate?

Accessibility of a ribosome-binding region in the classical bacterial thermometer.

4. How does excess free ribosomal protein control its own operon?

It can bind an mRNA regulatory region and reduce initiation in relevant feedback systems.

5. Why can a RelA mutant still have low translation during starvation?

Loss of charged tRNA directly constrains elongation.

6. What are the three constituents of the eIF2 ternary complex?

eIF2, GTP and initiator Met-tRNAi.

7. What are the four classical mammalian eIF2α kinases?

GCN2, PERK, PKR and HRI.

8. Why is ATF4 uORF2 inhibitory?

Its reading frame overlaps the main start region and diverts initiating ribosomes.

9. What changes when mTORC1 phosphorylates a 4E-BP?

Its inhibitory association with eIF4E weakens, promoting eIF4F assembly.

10. How does low iron affect ferritin initiation?

IRP binding to the cap-proximal IRE inhibits recruitment.

11. Which observation distinguishes initiation inhibition from slowed elongation?

Combine run-off or occupancy patterns with independent synthesis flux; either measurement alone is ambiguous.

12. How should a student interpret ribosome footprints per RNA?

As an occupancy proxy whose relationship to completed protein depends on transit and completion assumptions.
