1. Acid production, buffering and the problem of steady state

Living cells maintain reaction conditions that are remarkably different from the outside environment. Enzymes depend on the protonation of amino acid side chains for catalysis and binding, and membrane proteins rely on electric and chemical gradients for transport. A change in hydrogen ion activity alters these molecular conditions even when the total amount of water, sodium or protein in an organism has not changed. Mammalian extracellular fluid is normally maintained close to pH 7.4. This value is not a consequence of an absence of acid production. It is the result of continuous regulation in an open system. To understand renal acid handling, distinguish the immediate chemical response to an acid load from the eventual removal of that load from the body.

An acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. In dilute physiological solutions the activity of hydrogen ions can be approximated by their concentration for introductory calculations. By definition, pH is the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity relative to its standard state. A change from pH 7.4 to 7.1 approximately doubles the hydrogen ion activity because 10 raised to 0.3 is approximately 2. At pH 7.4 the nominal concentration is about 40 nanomoles per litre. Although sodium and bicarbonate are present in millimolar amounts, free protons are present in nanomolar amounts. The resulting difference is essential to understanding why urine pH cannot reveal the quantity of acid eliminated by the kidney. Most acid is transported or excreted in buffered chemical forms, not as an equivalent concentration of freely dissolved hydrogen ions.

Carbon dioxide production accompanies oxidative metabolism. Carbon dioxide reacts reversibly with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into bicarbonate and a proton. Carbonic anhydrase accelerates the interconversion of carbon dioxide and water with the components of the carbonic acid system, without shifting the equilibrium. The lungs remove carbon dioxide, so they can regulate the respiratory contribution to systemic acid balance. Sulfur-containing amino acids, in contrast, can yield nonvolatile acid equivalents during metabolism. These cannot simply be exhaled as the original acid. Metabolic acid production and gastrointestinal acid or base losses must be balanced by appropriate renal excretion if extracellular bicarbonate stores are to remain stable.

A buffer decreases the magnitude of a pH change by reversibly accepting or donating protons. Proteins, phosphate and bicarbonate all contribute to physiological buffering. A buffer is not an inexhaustible acid sink: protonating bicarbonate consumes an equivalent of bicarbonate, and protonating phosphate changes the ratio of its acid and base forms. Unless metabolic reactions consume the acid, or an organ eliminates acid equivalents, the original buffer distribution cannot be restored indefinitely. A useful distinction is that buffers redistribute an acid load, whereas acid excretion changes the net balance of acid equivalents between the organism and its surroundings.

Consider a simplified extracellular compartment with 14 litres of water and bicarbonate at 24 mmol/L. Its extracellular bicarbonate pool is approximately 336 mmol. A hypothetical net fixed acid load of 50 mmol would consume a substantial fraction of this pool if no additional buffering, metabolic reactions or renal correction occurred. Actual extracellular bicarbonate changes cannot be calculated simply by subtracting this load because intracellular buffering, distribution, carbon dioxide exchange and physiological responses also matter. Nevertheless, this accounting explains why maintaining pH requires replenishment of base rather than solely maintaining a low free proton concentration. The kidney performs two distinct tasks: conserving filtered bicarbonate and adding replacement bicarbonate to the body when net acid is excreted.

2. The bicarbonate system and quantitative pH reasoning

For the extracellular bicarbonate buffer, the relevant acid–base relationship involves bicarbonate concentration and dissolved carbon dioxide. At approximately 37 degrees Celsius, the familiar physiological Henderson–Hasselbalch expression is pH = 6.1 + log10([HCO3−]/(0.03 × PCO2)). The bicarbonate concentration is expressed in mmol/L, PCO2 in mmHg, and 0.03 is the approximate carbon dioxide solubility coefficient in mmol/L/mmHg. The apparent pKa of 6.1 incorporates the equilibria involving dissolved carbon dioxide and carbonic acid. Substituting dissolved carbon dioxide directly rather than multiplying by solubility a second time avoids a dimensional error.

This equation follows from the mass action relationship for a weak acid and its conjugate base. For HA ⇌ H+ + A−, the dissociation constant is proportional to the product of hydrogen ion activity and conjugate base activity divided by acid activity. Taking logarithms and rearranging gives pH = pKa + log10(aA−/aHA). Physiological bicarbonate calculations replace the activity ratio with a concentration-based approximation using dissolved carbon dioxide as the operational acid component. This works well for standard arterial blood calculations at body temperature but is not a universal constant for every temperature, species or fluid.

At bicarbonate 24 mmol/L and arterial PCO2 40 mmHg, dissolved carbon dioxide is 1.2 mmol/L. The ratio is 20, log10(20) is approximately 1.301, and pH is approximately 7.401. Suppose bicarbonate falls to 12 mmol/L while PCO2 initially remains 40 mmHg. The ratio is now 10 and the calculated pH is 7.1. If effective ventilation subsequently lowers PCO2 to 25 mmHg, dissolved carbon dioxide is 0.75 mmol/L, giving a ratio of 16 and pH approximately 7.304. The pH improvement does not demonstrate that bicarbonate has been restored. It demonstrates a respiratory response to a metabolic disturbance.

The opposite perturbation illustrates why the ratio must be considered rather than one measured variable. If bicarbonate is 36 mmol/L and PCO2 is 60 mmHg, the ratio again equals 20, giving pH close to 7.4. The two values are nevertheless different from their reference values. The equation describes the chemical state, not the sequence of causes that produced it. An isolated near-normal pH therefore cannot establish that there is no disturbance. The history of the perturbation, measurements of carbon dioxide and bicarbonate, and the direction and adequacy of compensation must be considered together.

An important advantage of bicarbonate as a physiological buffer is its openness. Respiratory control can change the denominator and renal control can change the numerator over different timescales. The two organs cannot be considered chemically independent because their actions change the same equilibrium. A decrease in PCO2 can alter bicarbonate even before renal adaptation, through immediate chemical buffering. Renal bicarbonate changes can alter pH and hence respiratory drive. A useful examination strategy is to identify the primary disturbance first, then ask which regulated response changes the other variable in the direction that opposes the initial pH change.

3. Filtered bicarbonate and proximal tubular reclamation

The first renal problem is the large quantity of bicarbonate that passes the glomerular filtration barrier. A normal illustrative adult GFR of 125 mL/min corresponds to 180 L/day. Multiplying this filtration volume by plasma bicarbonate of 24 mmol/L gives a filtered load of approximately 4320 mmol/day. Bicarbonate is a small ion that is readily filtered; the calculation neglects small differences between plasma and ultrafiltrate composition. The resulting filtered load is far greater than ordinary daily net fixed acid production. Thus even a small persistent fraction of filtered bicarbonate loss could have substantial consequences for systemic acid balance.

Most filtered bicarbonate is reclaimed in the proximal tubule. A crucial feature of this process is the polarity of the epithelial cell. The apical membrane faces tubular fluid; the basolateral membrane faces interstitial fluid and the peritubular circulation. The sodium–potassium ATPase in the basolateral membrane maintains a low intracellular sodium concentration. Apical sodium–hydrogen exchanger NHE3 can then use the inward sodium electrochemical gradient to move sodium into the cell while moving a proton into tubular fluid. This is secondary active transport: the exchanger does not itself hydrolyse ATP for each transport cycle, but it depends on an ion gradient supported by ATP consumption elsewhere.

Secreted hydrogen ions combine with filtered bicarbonate in the lumen. Luminal carbonic anhydrase, particularly membrane-associated CA IV in appropriate proximal segments, accelerates conversion of the carbonic acid intermediate to carbon dioxide and water. Carbon dioxide readily enters the epithelial cell. Within the cell, cytosolic CA II accelerates hydration and dissociation, regenerating bicarbonate and hydrogen ions. The hydrogen ion can be reused for apical secretion; bicarbonate leaves across the basolateral membrane mainly through the electrogenic sodium–bicarbonate cotransporter NBCe1. Bicarbonate subsequently returns to the circulation. This pathway accomplishes net bicarbonate reclamation without requiring that each filtered bicarbonate ion physically cross the apical membrane in its original ionic form.

Follow the reaction stoichiometry carefully. One filtered bicarbonate reacts with one secreted proton. Carbon dioxide formed in tubular fluid enters the cell, and the intracellular reaction produces one bicarbonate and one proton. The bicarbonate returned to the body replaces the bicarbonate removed from the filtrate. The proton was recycled rather than excreted as a net acid equivalent. Consequently this mechanism prevents loss of existing bicarbonate, but it does not automatically generate a new equivalent of base for neutralising an unrelated metabolic acid load.

Inhibition of carbonic anhydrase slows the reactions needed for rapid proximal bicarbonate reclamation. More bicarbonate can reach distal nephron segments and initially escape in urine. Because the filtered bicarbonate concentration subsequently decreases as systemic bicarbonate falls, the size of the filtered load also changes. A single early measurement and a later steady-state measurement therefore need not show the same urine composition. The transport phenotype depends both on the transporter's capacity and on the quantity of solute delivered to it. This distinction is central to experimental questions that compare an immediate intervention with adaptation over several days.

Bicarbonate reabsorption continues beyond the proximal tubule, including in the thick ascending limb and distal acid-secreting segments. Approximate fractional contributions differ among experimental preparations, physiological states and definitions of nephron segments. It is more informative to understand the proximal tubule as a high-capacity reclamation site and the collecting system as a regulated final adjustment site than to treat a particular percentage as an immutable property. The downstream acid-secreting machinery operates on the fluid left after upstream reabsorption and secretion.

4. Intercalated cells and distal acid or base secretion

The collecting duct contains several epithelial cell populations. Principal cells have prominent roles in sodium handling and regulated water permeability. Intercalated cells specialize in acid–base transport and possess abundant mitochondria and transport proteins suited to their functions. In a simplified type A intercalated cell, cytosolic carbonic anhydrase generates hydrogen ions and bicarbonate from carbon dioxide and water. Apical vacuolar H+-ATPase uses energy from ATP hydrolysis to secrete protons into tubular fluid, while apical H+/K+-ATPase can couple hydrogen secretion to potassium uptake. On the blood-facing side, AE1 exchanges intracellular bicarbonate for extracellular chloride. These coordinated processes can transfer bicarbonate to the extracellular fluid and acid to the urine.

What determines whether this transfer represents conservation or generation of bicarbonate? If the secreted proton reacts with bicarbonate remaining in tubular fluid, the resulting net effect is reclamation of that filtered bicarbonate. If the proton instead becomes part of a phosphate or ammonium species that leaves in urine, the associated bicarbonate transferred to blood is a net addition of base relative to the body's previous bicarbonate stores. Identifying the luminal proton acceptor is therefore as important as identifying the proton pump.

Type B intercalated cells have a different distribution of acid–base transport proteins. The apical chloride–bicarbonate exchanger pendrin can secrete bicarbonate into tubular fluid in exchange for chloride. The basolateral proton pump transfers protons toward the interstitial compartment. This orientation promotes bicarbonate elimination when the physiological context favours base excretion. The type A and type B patterns are useful teaching models; actual collecting duct populations include cells with other patterns of transporter localization. It would be misleading to conclude that each intercalated cell is permanently restricted to a perfectly symmetric pair of transport proteins.

Distal acid secretion is constrained by the electrochemical gradient opposing proton movement. Urinary pH may reach approximately 4.5 in strongly acidifying conditions, corresponding to free hydrogen ion concentration around 0.032 mmol/L. Compared with an arterial pH of 7.4, this represents a free proton concentration ratio near 800. If two litres of urine were excreted per day at that minimum pH, the quantity of free protons would still be only about 0.063 mmol per day. The great majority of acid must therefore be eliminated through buffering rather than by increasing free proton concentration without limit.

Electrogenic sodium reabsorption through ENaC in principal cells can contribute to a lumen-negative potential that favours some forms of distal proton secretion, although collecting duct regulation cannot be reduced to voltage alone. Aldosterone, potassium balance, distal delivery, acid–base status and epithelial transporter abundance all interact. Changes in extracellular potassium also affect proximal ammonium production. A mutation or inhibition of a distal acidifying transporter may therefore alter more than one observable variable, and the measured urine pH depends on buffering capacity as well as transport activity.

5. Ammoniagenesis, ammonium recycling and urinary buffering

The kidney has an adjustable mechanism for producing a urinary proton acceptor rather than relying entirely on filtered solutes. Proximal tubular cells metabolise glutamine. Phosphate-dependent glutaminase converts glutamine to glutamate with release of ammonium; subsequent metabolism of glutamate yields a second nitrogen equivalent as ammonium and a carbon skeleton that can be metabolised to generate bicarbonate equivalents. In the standard net accounting, metabolism of one glutamine can yield two ammonium ions and two bicarbonate ions. The acid–base benefit depends on the fate of the ammonium: ammonium that reaches the urine supports net acid excretion, whereas ammonium returned to systemic circulation and metabolised through hepatic pathways does not necessarily have the same net effect on body bicarbonate.

Ammonia exists as NH3 and NH4+ according to the equilibrium NH4+ ⇌ NH3 + H+. The pKa is approximately 9.2 in the relevant teaching approximation, so most total ammonia at physiological pH is present as ammonium. NH3 is uncharged and can cross some biological membranes more readily than NH4+, but membrane permeation is not unrestricted. Ammonia transport proteins, including Rh glycoproteins in the distal nephron, contribute importantly to effective transport. NH4+ can substitute for other cations in several transport pathways. For example, it can occupy a potassium site on the NKCC2 cotransporter in the thick ascending limb, permitting movement of ammonia equivalents into the medullary interstitium.

The resulting medullary ammonia pool helps supply the collecting duct. Transport of NH3 into collecting duct fluid, in coordination with proton secretion, permits formation of NH4+ in the lumen. Because ammonium and ammonia have different transport properties, lowering the luminal pH favours retention of total ammonia in the protonated form and enhances its urinary elimination. The older idea of diffusion trapping captures the chemical direction of this effect but should not be mistaken for a complete molecular description. Regulated NH3 pathways and ion transporters matter, and the medullary pool is shaped by tubular transport and blood flow.

During sustained metabolic acid loading, the ability of the kidney to increase ammonium excretion is generally much greater than its ability to increase phosphate excretion as titratable acid. Glutamine uptake and ammonium-producing metabolic pathways adapt to the disturbance. This delayed increase provides a reserve of acid excretion beyond the limited amount of pre-existing phosphate buffer delivered into tubular fluid. Potassium balance modifies this response: hyperkalaemia can suppress renal ammoniagenesis and impair net acid excretion, whereas hypokalaemia tends to stimulate ammoniagenesis. These effects cannot be inferred solely by examining collecting duct proton secretion.

A different urinary buffer is phosphate. At physiological pH, phosphate exists in protonated and less protonated forms. As tubular pH falls, HPO4²− accepts a proton to form H2PO4−. This reaction permits additional acid to leave the body without requiring an equivalent increase in freely dissolved protons. The amount of phosphate available depends on filtered load, reabsorption and tubular delivery, making this pathway less adaptable than ammonium generation during prolonged acid loading. Other urinary buffers contribute to the quantity measured as titratable acid. The term describes a laboratory measurement rather than a single chemical species.

In an operational titration, a urine sample is titrated with base back toward the reference plasma pH, usually about 7.4. The quantity of base required reflects the protonated titratable buffers relative to that reference. Ammonium is accounted for separately because it remains predominantly protonated at this endpoint. The measured titratable acid is not identical to the total protons ever secreted along the nephron, since proton secretion used for bicarbonate reclamation does not necessarily increase titratable acid in final urine. The distinction between measured acid excretion and local transporter flux is fundamental for interpreting renal physiology experiments.

6. Net acid excretion and worked mass balances

For a simplified physiological accounting, net acid excretion (NAE) equals urinary ammonium excretion plus titratable acid excretion minus urinary bicarbonate excretion. Each term must represent the same time interval and be expressed in equivalent units. The minus sign matters because loss of bicarbonate is loss of base, which has the same direction of effect on body acid balance as addition of acid. Since NH4+ and HCO3− are monovalent, their mmol and mEq values coincide numerically in this calculation. Titratable acid is expressed as the number of acid equivalents determined by titration.

Consider an illustrative 24-hour urine sample containing 45 mmol of ammonium, 25 mEq of titratable acid and 2 mmol of bicarbonate. NAE equals 45 + 25 − 2 = 68 mEq/day. If net fixed acid production is 68 mEq/day and relevant nonrenal acid or base fluxes are negligible, this value is consistent with steady-state acid balance. If fixed acid production rises to 95 mEq/day but net acid excretion remains 68 mEq/day, approximately 27 mEq/day of acid equivalents are initially retained. Body buffering, changing ventilation and subsequent renal adaptation may change pH and measured bicarbonate over time. The amount of retained acid is not identical to the concentration of hydrogen ions in plasma.

A second illustrative sample has a higher urinary pH than the first but contains 80 mmol/day of ammonium, 15 mEq/day of titratable acid and 5 mmol/day of bicarbonate. NAE is 90 mEq/day. Even though free proton concentration might be lower in this urine, the total net acid elimination is higher because the buffered terms dominate the accounting. In the reverse case, urine may be quite acidic but contain little ammonium and phosphate, leading to relatively poor elimination of acid equivalents. Thus urine pH and acid excretion are complementary observations, not interchangeable measurements.

If ammonium is measured as 30 mmol/L and urine flow is 1.5 L/day, the excretion rate is 45 mmol/day. Using a concentration as if it were a daily amount would introduce a 50 percent difference in this example. If the titratable acid value is measured as 18 mEq/L, its daily amount is 27 mEq/day, and if bicarbonate is 2 mmol/L its daily amount is 3 mmol/day. The resulting NAE is 69 mEq/day. When flow changes, concentrations and excretion rates can change in different directions. This is analogous to clearance problems in the filtration chapter and is especially useful in interpreting experimental diuresis.

The simplified NAE relationship neglects certain additional acid–base equivalents such as urinary organic anions that may be relevant to more comprehensive metabolic accounting. At examination level, its strength is to separate the principal measured renal components. It is essential to state whether the comparison involves urinary net acid excretion, whole-body acid balance, or transport along an individual segment. An increase in net acid excretion after an acid load is an adaptive response, not evidence that renal secretion caused the original acid load.

7. Integrated metabolic and respiratory disturbances

An acidifying process is called acidosis; an alkalinising process is called alkalosis. Acidaemia and alkalaemia describe the measured blood pH being below or above the normal reference interval. These words are not synonymous because opposing primary processes can coexist. In metabolic acidosis the primary change reduces bicarbonate relative to dissolved carbon dioxide. In metabolic alkalosis the primary change raises bicarbonate relative to dissolved carbon dioxide. In respiratory acidosis the primary change is an increase in arterial PCO2; in respiratory alkalosis it is a decrease in arterial PCO2. The classification is causal, rather than simply based on which measured concentration is abnormal.

In an otherwise intact organism, metabolic acidosis stimulates a ventilatory response that reduces arterial PCO2. The lower denominator of the bicarbonate equation increases the ratio toward normal. The kidneys also conserve bicarbonate and increase net acid excretion when their function and the available substrates permit. If the primary disturbance is an inability of the kidney to excrete sufficient acid, the renal compensatory response is limited by the very defect that caused the condition. A metabolic acidifying process caused by loss of gastrointestinal bicarbonate, by contrast, can evoke an intact renal response. Different origins can therefore give similar blood chemistry but different urine findings.

Metabolic alkalosis evokes a tendency toward hypoventilation, increasing PCO2 and reducing the ratio. Hypoventilation cannot increase without limit because gas exchange and oxygen homeostasis must also be maintained. Renal bicarbonate excretion may restore the base balance if extracellular volume, chloride availability and hormonal state permit. Volume depletion and chloride depletion can maintain bicarbonate retention even when systemic pH is high. The kidney simultaneously regulates extracellular volume, electrolytes and acid balance, so the transport response must be understood in its physiological context.

When PCO2 rises acutely, nonbicarbonate buffers accept some hydrogen ions formed from hydrated carbon dioxide, and bicarbonate concentration increases to a limited extent even before substantial renal compensation. With sustained respiratory acidosis, renal adjustments increase bicarbonate conservation and net acid excretion, gradually increasing plasma bicarbonate beyond the initial chemical response. With sustained respiratory alkalosis, renal adjustments favour a lower plasma bicarbonate concentration. These adaptations generally require hours to days to develop fully, whereas chemical buffering is immediate and changes in ventilation may occur within minutes. The timescales overlap rather than forming perfectly separate stages.

The anion gap is a useful supporting calculation based on electroneutrality. In its commonly used potassium-excluding form, the serum anion gap is measured sodium concentration minus the sum of measured chloride and bicarbonate concentrations. With sodium 140, chloride 104 and bicarbonate 24 mmol/L, the gap is 12 mmol/L. It represents the net contribution of ions not included in that expression, not a literal deficit of negative charge. If sodium remains 140 and chloride rises to 116 while bicarbonate falls to 12, the calculated gap remains 12. If chloride instead remains 104 while bicarbonate falls to 12, the gap is 24. These contrasting patterns show how replacement by measured chloride differs from accumulation of unmeasured anions. Albumin concentration and the analytical method influence the reference value, so a single numerical cutoff is not universal. The calculation is a tool for understanding charge balance, not a substitute for a full physiological explanation.

8. Experimental reasoning and interpretation

Renal acid–base regulation can be studied at multiple scales. Whole-body balance studies measure acid input or generation and urinary output over a defined time interval. These studies establish whether acid equivalents are retained or eliminated, but they do not by themselves locate the responsible transporter. Micropuncture sampling of tubular fluid along accessible nephron segments can identify changes in solute delivery. Interpretation requires attention to water reabsorption: a bicarbonate concentration can change because bicarbonate was removed, because water was removed, or both. Comparing the fraction of filtered bicarbonate remaining with the fraction of filtered water remaining provides stronger mechanistic information than comparing concentrations alone.

Isolated perfused tubular preparations allow investigators to examine luminal and basolateral conditions separately. For example, changing luminal sodium availability while maintaining the other experimental conditions can test the dependence of proximal proton secretion on sodium-linked exchange. A decrease in bicarbonate reclamation under sodium removal is consistent with a sodium-dependent mechanism, but sodium removal affects multiple transport processes and cell physiology. Additional evidence from selective inhibitors, transporter expression or genetic models can strengthen identification of the particular exchanger. It is scientifically important not to attribute an entire change in transport to a single protein on the basis of a nonspecific intervention.

Immunolocalisation of transporter proteins reveals epithelial polarity. An apical proton pump in a type A intercalated cell and basolateral AE1 provide structural evidence for the proposed acid-secreting pathway. A corresponding functional study can measure changes in luminal pH or bicarbonate flux. Protein localization alone does not establish transport rate, and measured flux alone does not uniquely identify the molecular protein. Converging structural and functional observations make the mechanistic interpretation stronger.

A hypothetical acid-loading experiment provides an opportunity to combine these ideas. Suppose urine ammonium excretion increases from 35 to 95 mmol/day after several days, titratable acid increases from 20 to 29 mEq/day, and bicarbonate excretion changes from 3 to 1 mmol/day. Baseline net acid excretion is 52 mEq/day and the later value is 123 mEq/day. Ammonium increased by 60 equivalents, phosphate and other titratable buffers contributed 9 more equivalents, and reduced bicarbonate loss contributed 2 more. The net increase is 71 mEq/day. These invented teaching measurements are not results from a published experiment; they illustrate why the adjustable ammonium pathway can dominate adaptation to a sustained acid load.

An experiment comparing two organisms after an identical acid load should also assess filtration, urine volume, potassium balance and the duration of the stimulus. A difference in urinary ammonium could reflect proximal ammonium synthesis, medullary recycling, distal transfer, systemic substrate delivery or a change in the number of functioning nephrons. The student should distinguish the observation from the candidate mechanisms and identify what additional measurement would separate them. This discipline connects renal acid physiology to the general scientific reasoning required in CSIR-NET Part C.

9. From acid balance to buffer capacity and compartmental exchange

The concentration of free hydrogen ions is the variable used to express pH, but the quantity of acid that must be added to produce a specified pH change depends on buffering. Consider two vessels initially adjusted to the same pH. One contains a dilute phosphate buffer and the other contains ten times as much phosphate at the same ratio of conjugate base to acid. The initial pH values can be almost identical. Addition of an equal small amount of strong acid, however, produces a smaller pH change in the more concentrated buffer. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation predicts the ratio that sets pH, whereas buffer capacity describes how resistant that ratio is to alteration. Confusing these two properties leads to a false conclusion that a normal pH necessarily indicates normal capacity to resist the next disturbance.

If the total concentration of a simple monoprotic buffer is C and the dissociation constant is Ka, the ratio of conjugate base to acid determines the fractions of the two chemical forms. The fraction present as base is r/(1+r), where r = 10^(pH − pKa). A student need not memorize a more elaborate buffer-capacity expression to understand this relationship. When the pH is near the pKa, both acid and base forms are available in substantial amounts. When the pH is many units below the pKa, the base form becomes scarce, limiting the capacity to accept further protons. This principle explains why the chemical form of phosphate delivered into a tubule affects the amount of additional acid it can accept.

The bicarbonate system is unusual because carbon dioxide can be continually exchanged with the atmosphere. In a closed bottle, addition of acid to bicarbonate increases dissolved carbon dioxide and the ratio changes as both terms move. In a ventilated organism, some of the carbon dioxide formed in the buffering reaction is removed through the lungs. The respiratory system therefore helps sustain a favourable bicarbonate-to-carbon-dioxide ratio without itself manufacturing new bicarbonate. Nonetheless, the base consumed in buffering fixed acid remains a real loss of extracellular base, and restoring the balance requires the kidney or an appropriate metabolic process.

Different compartments possess different buffering constituents. Haemoglobin contributes strongly to red-cell buffering because its amino acid residues can accept and release protons, and its oxygenation state affects proton binding. Intracellular proteins provide substantial capacity within tissues. Bone mineral can participate in buffering prolonged acid loads, although its involvement is not a substitute for normal renal control. The extracellular bicarbonate concentration is thus a convenient systemic variable, not a direct measure of every buffer present in the body. A blood sample reflects exchange with other compartments whose responses need not occur at identical speeds.

The distinction between a concentration and an amount becomes especially important when extracellular volume changes. Imagine a compartment that contains 240 mmol bicarbonate in 10 L. Its concentration is 24 mmol/L. Addition of bicarbonate-free isotonic fluid sufficient to increase its volume to 12 L would, in a deliberately simplified mixing model, reduce the concentration to 20 mmol/L before redistribution and physiological correction. No bicarbonate was removed in the mixing step. In an organism, the composition of infused or ingested fluid, chloride distribution, ventilation and renal regulation influence the subsequent result. This calculation is not a complete clinical model; it illustrates why acid–base concentrations must be interpreted together with volume and mass balance.

The pH of intracellular fluid can also diverge temporarily from arterial pH. Carbon dioxide passes through biological membranes relatively readily, and carbon dioxide retention can alter intracellular acid–base conditions even before charged solutes have redistributed. A cell can then regulate intracellular pH using membrane exchangers and cotransporters. The renal epithelium is distinctive because intracellular pH regulation also enables directional movement of acid–base equivalents between two extracellular spaces. The goal of a tubular cell is not merely to protect its own cytosol. Its transport polarity contributes to the acid balance of the entire animal.

10. The origin and fate of fixed acid equivalents

The phrase fixed acid does not mean that a proton is chemically immobile. It distinguishes acid equivalents whose ultimate elimination cannot be achieved simply by pulmonary exchange of carbon dioxide. During the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids, sulfur may be oxidised to sulfate. Sulfate represents an anionic product of metabolism, and the associated acid equivalents require appropriate balancing. The detailed biochemical pathways belong to metabolism; the physiological consequence here is that an animal can have a continuous nonvolatile acid burden even when oxygenation and ventilation are entirely normal.

Organic acids need more careful interpretation. Lactate is the conjugate base of lactic acid; its formation, transport and eventual metabolism cannot be interpreted by counting every lactate molecule as a permanently retained proton. A transient imbalance between production and utilisation may accompany acidification, but subsequent metabolism of the organic anion can change the net acid–base balance. Similarly, ketone body production during particular metabolic states can contribute to acid loading when production and disposal are imbalanced. The distinction between transient production and net retention prevents an oversimplified idea that every organic acid formed in a biochemical pathway creates an identical daily requirement for renal proton excretion.

Diet changes the supply of substrates whose metabolism yields acid or base equivalents. Protein sources rich in sulfur-containing amino acids can increase the nonvolatile acid load, while metabolism of certain organic anions supplied by plant foods can contribute base equivalents. This is a statement about metabolism and balance rather than about the measured pH of the food itself. A substance may taste acidic because of its protonated form but have metabolic products with a different effect on net acid–base accounting. Conversely, a solution that is not strongly acidic can contain precursors that generate acid equivalents after absorption and metabolism.

The digestive tract creates an additional route for loss or gain of acid–base equivalents. Gastric parietal cells secrete acid into the stomach. Bicarbonate entering the blood during this process is balanced in the overall digestive cycle by the secretion of alkaline fluid and subsequent processes in the intestine. The transient redistribution of acid and base between compartments does not mean that every meal permanently adds bicarbonate to the body. What matters for the whole-body balance is the final fate of the secretions. Loss of acid-rich gastric contents can remove acid equivalents from the body; loss of bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluid can remove base equivalents. The renal response depends on which material was lost and on associated changes in volume and electrolytes.

A useful accounting approach is to define a body acid-equivalent pool relative to a baseline state. Over an interval, its change depends on net metabolic acid generation, external acid or base entry, gastrointestinal loss and renal net acid excretion. If the terms balance over the interval, the pool does not progressively change. This is a statement of conservation, not a detailed prediction of blood pH. The same retained acid load can yield different pH changes depending on its distribution and the buffering state at the start of the interval. The equation therefore complements the equilibrium relationship rather than replacing it.

In steady state, excretion need not remain numerically constant from one day to the next. An acid load may be larger after one diet and smaller after another; renal adaptation can lag the change. The relevant question is whether cumulative acid excretion eventually balances cumulative net acid production and other losses. An isolated urine collection represents the interval collected, and an organism can remain near its regulated pH during a transient mismatch because buffers absorb part of the discrepancy. The direction of the longer-term trend is more informative than demanding instantaneous equality at every moment.

These distinctions lead to a recurring principle in examination problems. A perturbation in metabolism, a perturbation in pulmonary gas exchange, and a perturbation in kidney function can all change arterial pH. Their effects are not mechanistically interchangeable. A respiratory problem primarily changes carbon dioxide exchange; a fixed acid load changes base requirements; a kidney defect changes the ability to conserve base or excrete acid. Once the original perturbation is identified, the predicted adaptive responses follow from the conservation relationships and the direction of the bicarbonate ratio.

11. Segmental bicarbonate delivery and epithelial transport energetics

Bicarbonate transport is best understood by following a defined quantity through a series of connected segments. Suppose a model nephron receives 100 arbitrary units of filtered bicarbonate per minute. If the proximal tubule reclaims 80 units, 20 units enter the loop and later segments. If the remainder of the nephron reclaims 19 units, one unit appears in final urine. The proximal tubule is responsible for most absolute reclamation, yet a loss of the last unit of distal transport capacity could double urinary bicarbonate excretion from one unit to two. Fractional contributions to total reabsorption and contributions to fine control are therefore different properties.

Because bicarbonate is filtered at a rate dependent on both filtration and plasma concentration, an increased plasma bicarbonate concentration increases delivery at constant GFR. A fall in GFR reduces delivery at constant concentration. In a real kidney, reabsorptive rates may change when filtration changes, a phenomenon related to the coordination of filtration and proximal transport. It would be unjustified to assume that reabsorption is a fixed numerical quantity independent of every physiological condition. The simple arithmetic is useful precisely when assumptions are specified. It identifies the direction of a change if the other quantities are held constant, and it indicates which additional variables must be considered when they are not constant.

The NHE3 exchanger illustrates how an apparently energetically unfavourable flux can be coupled to a favourable one. The free energy change for transferring a charged solute between two compartments depends on the chemical concentration ratio and the electrical potential difference. For an ideal dilute solute of charge z, the electrochemical contribution can be expressed as RT ln(Cfinal/Cinitial) + zF(ψfinal − ψinitial), where R is the gas constant, T absolute temperature, F the Faraday constant and ψ electrical potential. Sodium entry into a proximal cell is favoured by the inward sodium concentration gradient. Coupling that movement to outward hydrogen ion movement allows the combined process to proceed when the total free energy change is favourable.

For one-for-one exchange of sodium and hydrogen ions in opposite directions, the direct electrical contributions to the exchange cycle cancel because both ions have charge +1. This does not imply that membrane potential is irrelevant to the rest of tubular transport. It influences many other fluxes, including the basolateral electrogenic cotransporter, and the concentrations maintained within the cell depend on the integrated activity of transport proteins. The crucial point is that the thermodynamic analysis must be applied to the complete transport cycle rather than to one ion in isolation.

Consider a very simplified condition in which luminal sodium is 140 mmol/L and intracellular sodium is 14 mmol/L. The sodium concentration ratio provides a tenfold favourable chemical gradient for sodium entry. If intracellular pH is 7.2 and luminal pH is 6.2, hydrogen ion concentration in the lumen is ten times that in the cell. Under ideal one-for-one electroneutral exchange assumptions, the chemical gradients of the two ions oppose one another with equal logarithmic magnitude. This calculation identifies a condition close to equilibrium for this simplified exchange. An actual proximal tubule has variable local concentrations, transporter regulation, nonideal activities and continuous reactions that influence the achievable flux. The example demonstrates why a sodium-linked exchanger cannot indefinitely pump protons against an arbitrarily large gradient.

The proton ATPase in the distal nephron uses a different source of free energy. ATP hydrolysis can drive proton translocation even when the sodium-linked chemical gradient alone is insufficient. Acid-secreting cells have machinery adapted to operating when the luminal hydrogen ion activity is high. Yet the energy supplied by ATP is finite; membrane leak, electrical effects and transporter kinetics constrain acidification. A low urine pH therefore reflects the balance of active secretion, backflux, luminal buffering and tubular conditions, not an unlimited pumping capacity.

Carbonic anhydrase changes the rate at which a transport sequence can run, not the free energy available from the initial and final chemical states. Without rapid reaction in the lumen, protons delivered by NHE3 may not be converted efficiently into diffusible carbon dioxide during the time that filtrate passes the transport surface. With rapid catalysis, removal of a reaction intermediate can support continued transport under nonequilibrium conditions. Catalysis and active transport thus have different functions that cooperate: one accelerates chemical equilibration and the other maintains directional differences between compartments.

This cooperation becomes clearer when considering an inhibition experiment. Blocking luminal CA activity could reduce net bicarbonate reclamation without directly blocking the basolateral transporter. Blocking basolateral bicarbonate exit could alter intracellular bicarbonate and pH, which in turn would influence upstream reactions. In a coupled epithelial system, inhibition at one point can change flux at other points. Therefore the observation that a transporter-dependent flux has decreased does not establish that the inhibited compound directly bound that transporter. An experimental claim requires appropriate controls and, ideally, independent evidence for the molecular target.

12. The collecting duct as a final adjustable acid–base epithelium

A high-capacity proximal reclamation system alone cannot explain the full flexibility of urine acidification. The fluid arriving in the collecting system may contain little bicarbonate, yet an organism exposed to a nonvolatile acid load must eliminate additional acid equivalents. Distal proton secretion is particularly suited to coupling with urinary buffers at this point. The final fluid composition is determined by the combined actions of multiple cells and transporters. A functional model of the collecting duct therefore places acid-secreting and base-secreting cell types alongside principal cells rather than treating the entire epithelium as a single homogeneous membrane.

In a type A intercalated cell, the apical H+-ATPase transfers hydrogen ions from cytosol to lumen. The basolateral anion exchanger AE1 supports bicarbonate exit by exchanging it with chloride. Because the exchanged anions have the same charge, the exchanger does not directly carry net electrical charge in a simple one-for-one cycle. Direction of exchange depends on the ion gradients established by other processes. Carbon dioxide entry and cytosolic carbonic anhydrase can replenish the intracellular reactants used for both proton secretion and bicarbonate exit. The continuous removal of products through the two membranes supports sustained reaction and transport.

It is tempting to imagine that secreting a proton automatically increases blood bicarbonate by one unit. The missing consideration is the identity of the proton acceptor. If the lumen contains bicarbonate, the secreted proton may neutralise bicarbonate that would otherwise be lost. If phosphate accepts the proton and leaves in urine, the operation creates a net base equivalent for the body. If ammonium formation traps the secreted proton in final urine, the complete accounting must include ammonia production and transport to determine the net effect. Thus the same cellular machinery can serve different whole-organ acid–base functions depending on delivery of substrates from upstream.

The H+/K+-ATPase illustrates how one regulatory function can intersect another. Coupling hydrogen ion secretion to potassium absorption provides a mechanism by which distal acid handling may change in potassium-depleted states. The net effect on whole-body potassium cannot be predicted from this transporter alone because principal-cell potassium secretion and other nephron processes operate simultaneously. In a simplified experimental preparation, one may isolate the effect of changing this pump. In an intact organism, it is necessary to distinguish that direct transport effect from additional adaptive responses that alter potassium distribution and excretion.

Type B intercalated cells allow the collecting system to excrete bicarbonate, an important ability when net base accumulation occurs. Pendrin exchanges luminal chloride for intracellular bicarbonate at the apical surface. If luminal chloride delivery is low, the operation of chloride-dependent bicarbonate secretion may be constrained. This illustrates why an animal can display an acid–base disturbance and a volume or chloride disturbance simultaneously, and why correction of one regulated variable may require attention to the others. Pendrin also contributes to renal chloride handling; the protein is not an isolated switch dedicated only to pH.

Adaptation to acid or alkali loading involves changes in the activity, localization and expression of transport machinery. A physiological response can begin with redistribution of existing proteins to or from membrane surfaces, followed by slower changes in protein abundance. This principle is familiar from aquaporin regulation in water balance, but the particular transport proteins and regulatory cues differ. A membrane-rich intracellular compartment containing a proton pump is not equivalent to an active pump in the apical membrane. An observation of total cellular protein abundance must be interpreted with information about membrane localization and transport activity.

A useful comparison is between a secretory defect and a shortage of luminal buffer. If proton secretion is impaired, distal fluid may fail to become as acidic as expected after acid loading. If proton secretion is intact but buffer availability is inadequate, free proton activity can become high while the total excretion of acid equivalents remains low. Measuring urine pH alone cannot discriminate these situations reliably across all physiological states. Measurement of ammonium and titratable acid, combined with information about systemic acid–base balance, gives a more complete account of the kidney's response.

13. Quantitative chemistry of phosphate and ammonia in the tubular lumen

Consider the phosphate buffer pair H2PO4− and HPO4²−. At a given pH, the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship determines the ratio of the less protonated to the more protonated form. For an illustrative pKa of 6.8, the ratio at pH 7.4 is 10^0.6, approximately 3.98. About 3.98/(1+3.98), or 80 percent, of this idealized buffer is in the HPO4²− form. At pH 5.8, the ratio is 0.1, and the HPO4²− fraction is about 9 percent. The buffer has accepted protons as its distribution shifted toward H2PO4−.

If 20 mmol of total phosphate were delivered and retained in a hypothetical compartment that changed from pH 7.4 to pH 5.8, the difference between the base fractions would be about 0.80 − 0.09 = 0.71. Multiplying by 20 mmol gives approximately 14.2 mmol of proton equivalents accepted. This assumes that total phosphate remains constant, that the selected apparent pKa is appropriate, and that the relevant equilibrium is the dominant one in the stated pH interval. Actual urinary titration includes additional buffers and depends on ionic conditions. The worked example shows how titratable acid can be far larger than the free proton content.

It also explains the saturation-like limit of a given phosphate pool. Once nearly all available HPO4²− has been protonated, further lowering pH adds little more acid to that buffer pair. More acid excretion would require delivery of additional buffer or use of another chemical acceptor. The kidney can alter phosphate handling, but phosphate supply is restricted by filtration, systemic phosphate homeostasis and the need to conserve phosphate for other physiological purposes. Increasing proton secretion without increasing buffer delivery therefore cannot raise titratable acid indefinitely.

The ammonium system has a much higher pKa, near 9.2 in a useful approximation. At pH 7.4, the ratio NH3/NH4+ is 10^−1.8, or about 0.016. At pH 6.0, it is 10^−3.2, about 0.00063. The fraction of total ammonia present as NH3 is therefore much smaller in the acidified lumen. If total ammonia concentration were 10 mmol/L at pH 7.4, the NH3 concentration would be approximately 0.16 mmol/L. If it were 10 mmol/L at pH 6.0, NH3 would be approximately 0.0063 mmol/L. These values illustrate equilibrium speciation, not measured nephron concentrations.

The speciation calculation provides a chemical explanation for why ongoing proton secretion can sustain delivery of NH3 into the lumen. Conversion of luminal NH3 into NH4+ lowers the fraction remaining in the NH3 form. If NH3 transport is available and a suitable gradient exists, continued transfer can occur. However, a calculation assuming equal NH3 concentrations on both sides of a membrane cannot predict total ammonia excretion without information about membrane transport properties, flow, the medullary pool and proton supply. Chemical equilibrium is a component of the model, not its entirety.

The difference between these two buffer systems is important. Phosphate arriving from filtration already contains a mixture of acid and base forms. The titratable proton increment is determined relative to its reference state. Renal glutamine metabolism, in contrast, generates ammonium and associated bicarbonate equivalents. Urinary excretion of that ammonium contributes to net acid balance even though ammonium itself is a weak acid and is predominantly protonated across the usual urine pH range. The two quantities are included separately in the net acid excretion formula, preventing double counting.

Suppose two hypothetical urine samples have identical pH, flow and phosphate concentrations. One has a total ammonia concentration ten times greater than the other. Because their pH values are identical, the fractions in the NH3 and NH4+ forms are similar, but the absolute quantities differ tenfold. The sample with greater ammonia can represent much more net acid excretion despite an indistinguishable pH. This is the same mathematical distinction between a ratio and a total pool that appeared earlier in bicarbonate buffering. It is one of the most transferable concepts in renal physiology.

14. Why renal ammonia production is an integrated metabolic response

Glutamine provides the kidney with both nitrogen and a carbon skeleton. Its uptake into proximal epithelial cells increases the available substrate for ammonium production when conditions favour this pathway. The first nitrogen is removed from the amide group by glutaminase, leaving glutamate. Further metabolism can remove the amino nitrogen, yielding an intermediate related to the citric acid cycle. The carbon skeleton can be metabolised through pathways whose net effect under the usual physiological accounting is generation of bicarbonate equivalents. The significance of this metabolism is not merely waste nitrogen disposal: it couples a metabolic process inside the tubular cell to the acid–base needs of the organism.

After formation, ammonium has several possible fates. Some can enter tubular fluid directly, including through transport processes in which ammonium substitutes for a proton. Some ammonia equivalents can reach renal venous blood. In the loop of Henle, reabsorption of ammonium and its movement within the medulla contribute to a regional ammonia reservoir. In the collecting duct, transport of ammonia and active secretion of hydrogen ions cooperate to enhance urinary ammonium excretion. The fraction of ammonia generated that ultimately leaves in urine is therefore not determined by glutamine metabolism alone. Production, recycling, transfer and excretion form a connected sequence.

The liver is important in this accounting because systemic nitrogen metabolism can consume or generate acid–base equivalents depending on the pathway. In particular, disposal of ammonium through urea synthesis has a bicarbonate requirement. A simplified kidney-only description that counts all renal ammonium production as net new body bicarbonate can therefore overstate the net benefit if much of the ammonium returns to the circulation. The physiologically useful quantity for ordinary renal acid accounting is the ammonia equivalent actually excreted in urine. This is why the NAE formula uses urinary ammonium rather than renal tissue glutaminase activity.

A prolonged metabolic acid load can increase renal ammonium production, substrate uptake and the transport processes involved in urinary ammonia delivery. Different steps adapt with different kinetics. An immediate acid load may initially be buffered by extracellular and intracellular systems while renal ammonium excretion has not yet reached its later value. Over subsequent hours and days, increased renal ammonium disposal may become a major component of compensation. The delayed response explains why a brief experiment may detect only a portion of the organism's capacity to adapt.

Potassium homeostasis provides an instructive cross-link. Changes in extracellular potassium can influence intracellular conditions and renal metabolism. Hyperkalaemia can reduce ammonium production and excretion, so that a kidney may eliminate fewer acid equivalents even if distal proton secretion is not completely absent. Conversely, hypokalaemia often favours ammoniagenesis. These effects demonstrate why the kidney's regulation of acid–base balance cannot be fully separated from its regulation of electrolytes. The direction of the acid–base phenotype depends on several processes operating together, including potassium distribution, aldosterone-dependent transport and the condition of the renal epithelium.

Experimental evidence for this integrated pathway can come from measurements of renal arterial and venous ammonia concentrations, renal blood flow and urine excretion. A whole-organ balance estimates ammonia production as the sum of net release into venous blood and urinary output, after accounting for arterial delivery and any other relevant fluxes. For example, if a hypothetical kidney releases a net 20 micromoles per minute to venous blood and excretes 30 micromoles per minute in urine, the inferred net production is 50 micromoles per minute under steady conditions. An intervention that changes the urinary value without altering total inferred production might redistribute ammonia between urine and blood rather than changing synthesis. This provides an experimental way to distinguish production from disposal.

The lesson extends to other physiological variables: the amount formed inside an organ is not necessarily equal to the amount removed from the body. Urea can be recycled in the medulla, filtered bicarbonate can be reclaimed, and ammonium can be reabsorbed and secreted again before excretion. The final urine represents the net result after all these exchanges. Tracing an individual molecule can clarify the transport pathway, but mass balance across the organ is required to quantify the effect on the organism.
