1. A chromosome must be copied without reversing its chemistry

A cell that is preparing to divide faces two distinct tasks. It must reproduce the sequence of its genetic information, and it must produce physical DNA molecules that can be inherited independently. Complementary base pairing supplies the principle by which sequence information can be reproduced. Yet complementarity alone does not provide the molecular machinery that separates a stable double helix, selects nucleotides, forms covalent bonds, manages mechanical strain and completes uninterrupted daughter strands. Replication is therefore best understood as a moving assembly of interacting molecular machines rather than as a single chemical reaction.

Imagine examining a small segment of a chromosome just before it is replicated. The two strands are connected by base pairing and stabilized by interactions between adjacent bases. They cannot both be copied efficiently while their bases remain paired to each other. The parental strands must become locally accessible to enzymes. If the entire chromosome were unwound at once, it would expose an enormous quantity of single-stranded DNA to folding, inappropriate interactions and damage. A much more manageable strategy is to expose a limited region of template and convert it rapidly into daughter duplexes. This is what the advancing replication fork accomplishes. The DNA ahead of the fork is parental duplex; the DNA behind it normally consists of two duplex products, each containing a parental strand and its newly made partner.

The fork is a geometric description of the region where a parental duplex separates into two template paths. A replisome, by contrast, is the molecular assembly associated with this region. It includes the activities needed to unwind the duplex and extend complementary strands. Although individual proteins can associate and dissociate, the functional assembly can remain near the junction for a prolonged period. Some accessory reactions occur behind the junction rather than at exactly the same point. A diagram that places all proteins at one coordinate is therefore an instructional simplification, not a claim about their physical positions.

A replication origin is a locus or region from which replication starts, whereas a fork is a moving boundary. A bubble has two boundaries, even though particular replication systems may use only one actively progressing fork. An ordinary bidirectional origin produces two active forks moving in opposite directions. At each fork two complementary daughter strands must be synthesized. The origin may have been activated minutes earlier and may no longer be physically close to the proteins at the junction. This separation between an initiation site and an elongating structure is important when interpreting experiments that distinguish initiation defects from elongation defects.

The daughter DNA is not made by copying one parental strand and then discarding the other. Both original strands are valuable templates. Suppose the parental duplex contains one strand with sequence 5′-AGCT-3′ and its partner written below it as 3′-TCGA-5′. Copying the first makes a complementary strand equivalent in sequence to the second, whereas copying the second makes a strand equivalent to the first. In the absence of errors, both daughter duplexes therefore have the same base-paired sequence as the parental molecule. The inherited material in each duplex is different, however. One daughter contains the first old strand and the other contains the second old strand. This is the semiconservative logic established in the preceding chapter.

To understand why a fork is asymmetric, we must inspect the directional structure of DNA. Numbering a sugar carbon with a prime mark distinguishes it from numbered atoms in a nitrogenous base. In deoxyribose the base is linked to carbon 1′. Carbon 2′ lacks the hydroxyl group present in ribose. Carbon 3′ carries the hydroxyl that can participate in chain extension, and carbon 5′ lies outside the sugar ring and is commonly connected to phosphate. A phosphodiester linkage joins the 3′ oxygen of one sugar to a phosphate attached to the 5′ carbon of the next sugar. Repetition of this arrangement gives a backbone that has distinguishable ends. Calling a strand 5′ to 3′ specifies chemical orientation, not simply the direction in which a drawing is read.

In a normal antiparallel duplex the two backbones have opposite polarity. If one parental strand is 3′ to 5′ from left to right, its partner is 5′ to 3′ in the same spatial direction. Both descriptions are compatible with Watson–Crick pairing. The orientations are not arbitrary labels that can be exchanged to simplify a problem: reversing the labels without reversing the strand would change the chemical identity of its ends. A sequence reported by convention in the 5′ to 3′ direction must be reverse-complemented, rather than merely complemented, when reporting its partner in that same convention. For example, the partner of 5′-ATGCCA-3′ is 3′-TACGGT-5′ when placed below the first strand. Written in the conventional direction it is 5′-TGGCAT-3′. The two answers represent the same physical strand.

Consider a fork moving to the right, with the still paired parental DNA on its right. The upper parental template runs 3′ to 5′ from left to right. An enzyme that reads that template toward its 5′ end can extend a complementary daughter toward the right, in the same direction as fork progression. The lower parental template instead runs 5′ to 3′ from left to right. Its new complement must extend toward the left if it follows the normal polymerization chemistry. This geometrical conflict exists independently of the names or structures of the proteins. Changing the drawing to a leftward moving fork reverses the spatial positions that can support continuous extension; it does not reverse the chemistry of either DNA polymerase.

This is why examination questions that ask whether a particular parental strand is always a leading template are misleading unless the direction of an individual fork is specified. For a bidirectional bubble, a single old strand can be the leading template at one boundary and the lagging template at the other. To solve a problem, first find the boundary between unreplicated and replicated DNA, then mark its direction of movement, then inspect the parental polarity. Only after these steps should one label the new strands. A diagram of a duplex without a fork arrow or another indication of the unreplicated region may be insufficient to assign the labels.

2. The chemical reaction that determines polymerization direction

DNA synthesis requires more than free nitrogenous bases. The polymerase uses deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, abbreviated dNTPs. In ordinary DNA replication these are dATP, dGTP, dCTP and dTTP. Each contains the relevant base, deoxyribose and a chain of three phosphate groups on the 5′ position. From the sugar outward the phosphates are called alpha, beta and gamma. The phosphate closest to the sugar is the one retained in the DNA backbone during incorporation. The other two leave together as pyrophosphate. This distinction explains why the labeled phosphate position matters in biochemical experiments: a label in the alpha phosphate can become part of the backbone, while a gamma phosphate label is not retained in that position in ordinary extension.

The other substrate is an existing primer with an available terminal 3′ hydroxyl. The primer is paired with its template, which extends beyond the primer's growing end. DNA polymerase positions the next incoming nucleotide opposite the next exposed template base. The oxygen of the primer's 3′ hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphorus of the incoming dNTP. A new covalent linkage results, and pyrophosphate is released. The incoming nucleotide becomes the new terminal residue, with its own 3′ hydroxyl available for the next round. Thus extension proceeds by repeatedly renewing the same chemical functional group at the growing end.

The reaction may be written schematically as DNA(n) + dNTP → DNA(n+1) + PPi. Here DNA(n) denotes the extendable chain of length n residues; PPi is inorganic pyrophosphate. Hydrolysis of PPi yields two orthophosphate molecules. Under appropriate cellular conditions, consumption of the released pyrophosphate favors the forward overall reaction. The polymerase does not generally need to hydrolyze one separate ATP molecule for each phosphodiester linkage; the activated nucleotide already supplies the chemical potential needed for its incorporation. The additional ATP use by helicases, clamp loaders and some other enzymes supports different tasks.

The active site has to solve a problem of charge. Phosphate groups are negatively charged, and the attacking oxygen must be appropriately oriented and made reactive. Conserved acidic residues and divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium, help coordinate the substrates and stabilize unfavorable charge distributions during the reaction. The standard two-metal-ion model assigns complementary catalytic roles to the metal ions: promoting productive alignment and activation of the primer hydroxyl, and stabilizing the nucleotide triphosphate and leaving group. It is a useful molecular model of phosphodiester formation, not a statement that every polymerase has identical kinetic intermediates or exactly two metal ions in every observable structural state. A mutation that removes a key metal-coordinating carboxylate can leave the protein capable of binding nucleic acids yet severely compromise chemistry.

The polymerase active site is often described using a hand analogy. The palm region contains the catalytic center; other regions help position the primer-template and incoming nucleotide. A fingers region often undergoes conformational changes upon nucleotide binding, whereas the thumb contributes to contacts with duplex DNA. This description captures a functional division of labor without implying that all polymerases are structurally identical. The enzyme can discriminate among candidate nucleotides by recognizing shape and interactions in the active site. Correct complementary pairing favors a geometry suitable for chemistry. Wrong pairing can prevent efficient closure, substrate alignment or reaction. Fidelity therefore depends on more than the number of hydrogen bonds between isolated bases.

After incorporation, the enzyme must shift its relationship to the template so that the next template position reaches the active site. A polymerase that binds and incorporates but cannot translocate efficiently would produce very short extension products. A different mutant could translocate normally yet fail to select the appropriate base. These mechanistic distinctions matter because decreased DNA synthesis is a common final readout for very different molecular failures. Measuring the total incorporated label does not directly reveal which stage failed.

The terminal hydroxyl provides a simple way to understand chain termination. If the last residue lacks a usable 3′-OH, the next incoming triphosphate has no ordinary acceptor oxygen for the phosphodiester reaction. A dideoxynucleotide can therefore serve as an illustrative chain terminator once incorporated. It is important to distinguish absence of a 3′ hydroxyl from the ordinary absence of a 2′ hydroxyl in deoxyribose. The latter is a defining feature of the usual DNA substrate and does not prevent extension. An incoming nucleotide bearing a normal 3′ hydroxyl is not enough to rescue a previously blocked primer end, because its own hydroxyl will only become available for another round after it has been attached.

The same logic explains why a DNA polymerase needs a primer rather than just an exposed template. A template base identifies an appropriate incoming base but does not provide an acceptor 3′ oxygen on the nascent strand. Polymerase binding to unprimed single-stranded DNA is therefore not equivalent to productive synthesis. An experiment comparing otherwise identical single-stranded templates with and without annealed primers tests this requirement. The primer must be oriented so its available 3′ end faces an uncopied portion of template. A short complementary oligonucleotide annealed at the wrong extreme may have no downstream template for extension, even though it is chemically intact and binds strongly.

A primer may be DNA in an artificial biochemical assay, or RNA in the normal initiation of many chromosomal replication events. Its indispensable property for the replicative polymerase is a suitable end and junction. This explains why an externally supplied DNA primer can bypass the need for primase on a simple preprimed substrate while failing to rescue a complete moving fork that needs a succession of new primers. Some biological replication systems use alternative initiation strategies, including protein priming. Such examples should not be interpreted as evidence that the ordinary chromosomal replicative polymerase can simply start DNA chains from freely diffusing dNTPs without an acceptor.

An additional argument connects synthesis direction with error correction. In the normal reaction each new nucleotide arrives already activated at its 5′ end. If a mispaired terminal residue is removed by a proofreading exonuclease, the newly exposed chain end can provide a 3′ hydroxyl. Another incoming activated nucleotide can then participate in the usual extension chemistry. In an imagined reversed system in which the growing end had to carry the activated group, removing the last residue could remove the activation needed for continuing synthesis, unless a separate mechanism restored it. This comparison explains a functional advantage of the established architecture without claiming that hypothetical alternative chemical systems are physically impossible. The experimentally established direction of conventional chromosomal DNA polymerization is 5′ to 3′.

The direction of proofreading must not be confused with the direction of synthesis. Removal of an incorrect terminal nucleotide by a 3′ to 5′ exonuclease is a cleavage reaction rather than backward DNA copying. The activity examines the end that has most recently been extended and removes nucleotides from that end. A 5′ to 3′ nuclease that removes material ahead of a polymerase during primer processing performs another distinct task. The consequences of losing proofreading will be explored in Node 3.A.07; here it is sufficient to recognize that different reactions at different ends can coexist within one protein or multiprotein assembly without violating the direction of polymerization.

3. How biochemical evidence separated synthesis from complete replication

A historical difficulty was that visual knowledge of DNA structure did not reveal the proteins responsible for copying the molecule. In the 1950s, Arthur Kornberg and coworkers investigated the formation of DNA in cell-free systems from activated nucleotide substrates. Their work showed that DNA-directed synthesis could be attributed to a purified enzymatic activity and examined through chemical measurements. The enzyme initially characterized from Escherichia coli later became known as DNA polymerase I. The discovery supplied experimental access to the chemistry of DNA synthesis, but it did not establish that this particular enzyme was the main polymerase that copies the bacterial chromosome in vivo.

The distinction between activity in a test tube and function in a cell is fundamental. A purified protein can catalyze synthesis on a convenient artificial template without possessing the speed, processivity or partners necessary to duplicate a complete chromosome. If the substrate is a short, already primed strand, a polymerase with modest processivity may perform extremely well. The same enzyme might fail to sustain rapid, coordinated synthesis across a large double-stranded genome. The bacterial chromosome introduces constraints of DNA opening, torsional strain, repeated priming and assembly of multiple enzyme activities. Thus identification of polymerase activity addressed one essential question while opening several others.

A conceptual reconstruction of such an assay would compare reactions containing template, extendable primer, all necessary nucleotide substrates, appropriate metal ions and enzyme against reactions missing one of these components. Incorporation of a detectable nucleotide into a DNA product provides a readout. A reaction containing inactive enzyme helps establish the background from nonspecific retention of label. Separation of unincorporated nucleotide from macromolecular product is necessary: a detector that measures all isotope in the tube would give a signal whether or not a bond had formed. A time series is more informative than a single final point because it can distinguish the onset and rate of accumulation from the final capacity of the reaction.

If one dNTP is omitted, a heterogeneous template generally permits extension only until the enzyme encounters a template position requiring that missing nucleotide. The effect is therefore sequence dependent. A template consisting of one base repeated over a region may be copied for that region with only its complementary dNTP. This observation is a useful control for a sequence-defined assay and cautions against interpreting the absence of one nucleotide as inevitably producing no extension at all. In real cells, the four substrate pools must support the variety of bases encountered across the genome.

Suppose an extract produces substantial labeled DNA from a primed single-stranded template but little from a closed duplex. This result need not imply that it contains a defective polymerase. It may lack the machinery needed to open the duplex or provide a primer-template junction on it. A second assay using a partially open, fork-like substrate could test the ability to extend once an accessible junction exists. If the missing activity is supplied and synthesis on the duplex increases without changing synthesis on the preprimed single strand, the results suggest an accessory role rather than a direct effect on nucleotide chemistry. Even this conclusion requires assurance that the added preparation does not carry extra polymerase or alter other reaction conditions.

This reasoning illustrates how researchers decompose a complicated biological process into simpler biochemical functions. No single substrate is adequate for testing every replication protein. A strand-separation assay tests whether a duplex can be opened, a primer synthesis assay tests the formation of short initial chains, and a nick-sealing assay tests completion of the phosphodiester backbone. The most informative interpretation compares these specific activities with the behavior of a reconstructed fork. A protein might have normal activity in isolation but fail to interact productively with its partners. Conversely, an accessory factor may appear inactive when offered an artificial substrate that bypasses the task it normally performs.

Genetics added another layer of evidence. Mutations that cause replication to stop under restrictive conditions can help identify proteins needed for replication. A temperature-sensitive variant, for example, allows a protein to function at a permissive temperature and lose function at a restrictive one. Such a change must be interpreted with appropriate wild-type temperature controls because temperature also influences nucleotide metabolism, protein stability and general physiology. The strongest assignment comes when a genetic defect is complemented by a functional gene or protein, and purified versions of the relevant factor demonstrate a particular biochemical role. This combination of genetic and biochemical evidence allows students to infer causality rather than simply associate a protein with DNA.

The historical story also warns against equating the numeral in a polymerase name with its order of action. The enzyme called polymerase I was the first identified in the bacterial system, not necessarily the enzyme that initiates chromosome replication or the major enzyme at every growing fork. The functional assignments of bacterial and eukaryotic polymerases require separate treatment, which will follow in the next nodes. The central lesson at this point is that the synthesis of a complementary chain is only one operation in the production of complete daughter molecules.

4. Leading and lagging synthesis are consequences of geometry

Once the direction of chemical extension has been established, the leading-strand solution follows naturally. At a rightward-moving fork, the template oriented 3′ to 5′ toward the junction can be read toward the moving boundary. After an initial primer has supplied an extendable end, newly exposed bases can be incorporated in succession as the parental duplex opens. The growing 3′ end remains close to the point where new template is becoming accessible. Under ideal conditions a long complementary tract can therefore be made without repeatedly creating a new primer. This is the meaning of continuous leading-strand synthesis.

Continuous is a description of the predominant geometry of strand formation, not an assertion that the enzyme never pauses, changes partners, encounters damage or dissociates. A leading polymerase can stop transiently, and special situations may require a new start. A molecular trace containing pauses is compatible with the leading-strand model. What distinguishes the leading strand from the lagging strand is not uninterrupted enzyme attachment at all times; it is that fork movement ordinarily reveals additional template in a direction that the existing nascent end can follow.

The opposite parental template cannot be read toward the same moving junction by an ordinary DNA polymerase. Extension can only proceed away from that junction along the appropriate template polarity. One solution would be to wait until the entire DNA molecule had opened and then copy the second parental strand from the far end, but such a solution would create large stretches of single-stranded DNA and would be unsuitable for many replication geometries. Instead, the fork exposes a finite interval of template. A new primer is made near the recently exposed region, and a polymerase extends that primer away from the junction toward DNA that was synthesized earlier. As more template becomes exposed, another primer is placed closer to the moving fork.

The order of events is sometimes counterintuitive. At a rightward fork, the newest lagging-strand fragment begins farther to the right than its predecessor. Its own synthesis travels left. Consequently, the chronological sequence of fragment initiation moves rightward with the fork even though nucleotide addition on each fragment moves leftward. The 5′ end of a fragment marks where its primer began, and its growing 3′ end moves away from the fork toward the preceding fragment. Adjacent fragments are later processed and joined into one long complementary strand. They are not normally transferred to another DNA molecule.

A convenient diagram can make both new strands appear to move in the same direction if it draws the lagging template bent back into a loop. This represents a spatial rearrangement of the flexible DNA, not a switch in the chemical polarity of polymerase action. A molecular machine can advance overall in one direction even if its subunits act along differently oriented sections of folded substrates. This point will become especially important when discussing the trombone model of lagging-strand synthesis.

In many bacterial systems, textbook Okazaki fragment lengths are roughly one to two kilobases. In eukaryotic nuclear replication they are often approximately one to two hundred nucleotides. These are representative scales, not fixed numerical constants. The intervals vary with the organism, the replisome and the conditions under which products are measured. A question providing explicit lengths should be solved using those values rather than substituted memorized approximations. The smaller fragments of eukaryotic replication imply more frequent priming events per unit length of DNA, all else being equal, but they do not alone tell us how fast a whole chromosome is copied. Fork speed, origin density and the total number of active forks also matter.

To visualize the geometry of a pair of diverging forks, draw a horizontal old top strand oriented 5′ to 3′ from left to right and the old bottom strand oriented 3′ to 5′. At the left boundary the upper template is read toward the left, and its complement can extend leftward continuously. At the right boundary the lower template is read toward the right, so its complement is the continuous product there. The remaining products in each case require fragments. Thus two leading strands and two lagging strands are being synthesized around an ordinary bidirectional bubble. The two leading products are on opposite parental templates. A student who assigns both leading products to the same original strand has ignored the opposing directions of fork travel.

The products eventually form continuous duplexes even though their mode of production differs. A mature double-stranded molecule does not necessarily preserve visible evidence of every primer junction, because the RNA and any unwanted DNA from initiation are removed, the required DNA is filled in, and the remaining nicks are sealed. To discover the discontinuous intermediates one must study DNA during the period of synthesis, not merely after replication has completed.

5. Helicase turns chemical energy into directional DNA opening

The simplest way to separate a DNA duplex in a laboratory is to change its physical environment, for example by heating it sufficiently that base pairing and stacking no longer maintain the double helix. A living cell cannot use bulk heating each time it replicates a chromosome. Such a change would compromise numerous other molecules and would not direct the separation to the precise region needed by the replication machinery. The cell instead uses a helicase to couple local DNA opening to a controlled molecular movement. The relevant chromosomal replicative helicases are molecular motors that draw on nucleoside triphosphate hydrolysis to promote directional movement along a nucleic acid strand.

A duplex experiences transient local opening and reclosing as a consequence of thermal fluctuations. The helicase does not have to create every fluctuation from nothing. An important physical question is whether its movement chiefly captures spontaneously exposed nucleotides or actively destabilizes the base pairs ahead of it. Real helicases combine interactions with the substrate and energy-coupled conformational transitions to varying degrees. For examination purposes, it is more useful to distinguish the direction of movement, the strand along which the motor tracks, and the consequence of inhibition than to assign all helicases to an overly rigid mechanical category.

Many replicative helicases have ring-shaped arrangements of subunits. In a simplified steric exclusion model, one parental strand passes through the central channel of the motor while its partner is excluded from that channel. The arrangement promotes separation because the two strands cannot both follow the same path through the motor. Binding and hydrolysis of ATP at motor sites, coupled to changing contacts with the tracking strand, allow directional progression. The energy is not used to cleave the covalent phosphodiester backbone. Instead the motor reorganizes noncovalent interactions at the duplex junction and moves with respect to the DNA. The old strands must remain intact to preserve their information.

The directional designation of a helicase refers to its movement along the strand that it actually tracks. A helicase that moves 5′ to 3′ on the lagging template at a rightward-moving fork and a helicase that moves 3′ to 5′ on the leading template can both move rightward in space. The bacterial DnaB helicase of Escherichia coli illustrates the former arrangement; the eukaryotic CMG complex illustrates the latter. These examples demonstrate why one should not memorize that every helicase moves in a particular chemical direction. The two motors occupy different template paths in an antiparallel duplex while accomplishing the same general task of opening DNA ahead of synthesis. Their distinct loading and activation mechanisms belong to the organism-specific chapters.

The orientation and position of the helicase create a junction that the polymerases cannot produce efficiently on their own in a normal chromosomal fork. On a simple artificial substrate, some DNA polymerases can displace a downstream strand to varying extents. It would nevertheless be incorrect to conclude that helicase is dispensable for chromosomal replication in general. Strand displacement during a particular biochemical reaction is not equivalent to the processive, regulated opening of a large parental duplex while two strands are copied. A motor that separates the templates also serves as an important site of interaction with other fork proteins, creating a functional link between opening DNA and using the exposed strands.

Helicase directionality can be investigated with a partial duplex substrate carrying an available single-stranded tail. Imagine two related DNA substrates in which the double-stranded region is the same but the unpaired extension of the longer strand is placed at its 5′ end in one substrate and its 3′ end in the other. A directional helicase that loads onto the appropriate extension and travels toward the duplex should preferentially displace the complementary short strand from the substrate that presents the correctly oriented tail. The position of a fluorescent or radioactive label permits the intact partial duplex to be distinguished from the released strand by a separation method. A no-enzyme control helps quantify spontaneous dissociation, while an ATP omission or an ATPase-defective protein tests the energy requirement. Equal duplex stability and comparable substrate binding are essential before interpreting the difference as a difference in directional translocation.

This assay would provide evidence for the biochemical behavior of a particular protein under the chosen conditions. It would not automatically establish which strand the protein occupies in a native replisome because assembly partners may influence loading and substrate recognition. A helicase with weak activity on both test substrates might require additional proteins to form its functional complex. Another motor might bind a junction directly rather than enter from a simple exposed end. The experiment is most powerful when substrate polarity, protein binding and motor activity are studied together.

An enzyme that binds ATP but cannot hydrolyze it might bind at a fork without showing sustained progression. By contrast, a helicase defective in loading might not reach the appropriate DNA substrate even though the purified motor has a normal ATPase activity when experimentally positioned. The two defects could produce similar reductions in chromosome synthesis, yet they are different mechanistic failures. A preassembled fork experiment can distinguish a requirement for loading from a requirement for motor activity if the experimental design really bypasses the loading step. This is the same distinction between initiation and elongation introduced when comparing origins and forks.

When helicase movement stops, polymerases can sometimes extend across a limited amount of template that has already been exposed. Consequently a short-term signal of DNA synthesis does not prove that new duplex unwinding is continuing. If a polymerase is inhibited instead, the helicase may sometimes continue opening some DNA before other interactions restrain the fork. The exposed single-stranded region can then increase. The degree and duration of such uncoupling depend on the organism and conditions, and the resulting fork can become vulnerable to damage. Comparing unwound DNA with newly synthesized DNA is thus more informative than measuring either alone.

A practical conceptual link to energetics is that motor work and nucleotide incorporation consume different activated substrates. ATP hydrolysis can sustain movement even in an assay without deoxynucleotides, provided the helicase is capable of unwinding the offered substrate independently. Conversely, a preprimed single-stranded substrate can be extended without a helicase and without supplying a separate motor ATP pool, provided the polymerase has its required dNTPs and cofactors. An ATP-free complete replisome reaction cannot be interpreted as a selective helicase test, because other proteins such as clamp loaders and ATP-dependent ligases may also require ATP. Distinguishing these dependencies makes the logic of a reconstruction experiment much stronger.

6. Single-stranded DNA is a transient but demanding substrate

The exposed parental strands are chemically intact, but they are not equivalent to stable duplex DNA. A single strand can fold back on itself wherever parts of its sequence are complementary. For instance, an inverted repeat can form a stem-loop structure in an exposed region. The bases paired within such a stem are not readily accessible to a polymerase reading the template. Secondary structures can therefore slow copying or create a need for additional unwinding. Even without a stable hairpin, an exposed single strand can interact with other complementary nucleic acids, bind inappropriate proteins or become susceptible to nuclease action. The lagging template frequently experiences repeated intervals of exposure while waiting to be primed and copied, making protection particularly important.

Single-stranded DNA-binding proteins address these difficulties through preferential interaction with exposed nucleic acid. Bacterial SSB and eukaryotic replication protein A, usually abbreviated RPA, are functionally analogous examples. The names should not obscure their structural differences. The familiar E. coli SSB protein is a homotetramer, whereas the conventional eukaryotic RPA complex contains three different protein subunits. Both employ DNA-binding domains to interact with single-stranded regions, but the modes, affinities and interactions are adapted to their own replication systems. A diagram may use small circles to represent the proteins on DNA; this shorthand is not a statement that they are structurally interchangeable.

Binding of SSB or RPA changes the equilibrium among possible conformations of the exposed strand. A region that would otherwise form a hairpin can become more available when protein binding favors its extended or protein-bound state. The proteins also reduce the tendency of complementary parental strands to reanneal prematurely. These effects do not require SSB itself to behave as a directional ATP-driven helicase. Instead, preferential association with single-stranded DNA stabilizes an exposed substrate that another motor has helped generate. This distinction between generating a state and maintaining it is relevant to many biological assemblies.

Protection creates a second challenge: the protein-bound strand must remain accessible to the enzymes that copy it. A permanent, irreversible coat that prevented primase and polymerase from reaching the bases would be counterproductive. In functioning forks, binding is dynamic, and the replication machinery can rearrange or displace DNA-binding proteins as required. Protein-protein interactions can facilitate this handoff. The result is a protected but usable template rather than an inert nucleic acid trapped beneath an impermeable coating. Such balance also explains why maximum affinity for single-stranded DNA is not necessarily optimal for fork progression.

A simple binding model helps separate affinity from abundance. If one DNA binding site and one freely diffusing protein interact reversibly as P + S ⇌ PS, the dissociation constant is Kd = [P][S]/[PS]. For independent equivalent sites and a free protein concentration p that is effectively constant, the occupied fraction is p/(Kd + p). To obtain this expression, note that the total site concentration is [S] + [PS], substitute [PS] = p[S]/Kd into the ratio [PS]/([S] + [PS]), and cancel the common free-site term. If Kd is 10 nM and the free protein concentration is 30 nM, the model predicts an occupied fraction of 30/(10+30) = 0.75. Raising free protein to 90 nM predicts 90/(10+90) = 0.90.

This elementary calculation is not a quantitative model of a real replication fork. Actual single-stranded binding proteins can cover several nucleotides, interact cooperatively, adopt different binding modes, and be present at concentrations where binding substantially depletes the free protein pool. Nevertheless the model reveals the distinction between a change in protein amount and a change in binding affinity. A mutant with Kd of 90 nM at 30 nM free protein would produce a predicted occupancy of only 0.25. Overexpression might partly compensate for the weaker binding under this idealized equilibrium model, but it would not necessarily restore defective interactions with primase or polymerase. A rescue by protein abundance is evidence for a concentration-sensitive defect, not proof that all functions are intact.

To study the protection function, an experiment could compare the fate of the same exposed DNA in the presence and absence of the binding protein. A challenge involving a nuclease can reveal whether the bound substrate is less susceptible to cleavage, provided the protein does not simply inhibit the nuclease in solution. Including a separate control substrate that the protective protein does not bind efficiently helps distinguish substrate protection from nonspecific nuclease inhibition. A structural assay comparing hairpin formation can test the effect on template conformation. Finally, a primed DNA extension assay with a structured template can determine whether binding promotes polymerase progression. A protein that protects DNA but impedes its use by a polymerase would behave differently in the latter assay.

RPA also participates in signaling from single-stranded DNA during eukaryotic replication stress. The accumulation of RPA-coated DNA can be an indicator of a mismatch between unwinding and synthesis, and it provides a platform for interactions with stress-response machinery. This is not the same as saying that every normal RPA-coated region is a sign of damage. Exposed template is a normal intermediate during replication. Excessive persistence or accumulation, combined with other evidence of fork dysfunction, is what suggests a stress state. The detailed checkpoint pathways are treated in the cell-cycle material, and the present chapter concentrates on the immediate consequences for template accessibility.

An examination scenario may describe a mutant that displays abundant short stretches of exposed DNA, secondary structures and frequent polymerase pausing while the purified polymerase and helicase retain their catalytic activities. Defective template-binding proteins would be plausible candidates. However, the phenotype is not unique, because a defect in polymerase recruitment could also create prolonged exposure. To select among explanations, one would test direct binding to single-stranded DNA and compare the effects of adding functional SSB or RPA to the appropriate reconstructed system. The conclusion should follow the discriminatory experiment rather than the mere association of exposed DNA with one protein name.

7. Primase creates the starting material for polymerase

Having exposed and stabilized a template, the replisome must establish a chemical starting point. The standard replicative DNA polymerases require an existing chain end, so simply supplying them with the four dNTPs and a long unprimed strand cannot establish ordinary chromosomal synthesis. Primase solves this initiation problem through an RNA synthesis reaction that can begin without a pre-existing oligonucleotide primer. It uses the DNA sequence to determine a complementary RNA sequence and builds a short chain whose terminal hydroxyl can be used by downstream replication activities.

Primase must select and align the initial nucleotides so that the first phosphodiester linkage can form. Once a short RNA has been created, subsequent additions extend its 3′ end. The initial product therefore has the same general chemical directionality as other template-directed nucleic acid synthesis. The difference is the ability to start a new chain. RNA synthesis is not inherently a reversed reaction, and RNA priming is not a workaround that changes the polarity of DNA replication. Primase makes an appropriately oriented starting segment, and DNA polymerase subsequently lengthens the chain in the same chemical direction.

The primer is short relative to the DNA tract it initiates. Its length must be sufficient for a productive interaction with the next enzyme but need not extend across the entire exposed region. Making long RNA segments would increase the amount of material that must later be removed from the daughter strand. The task of primase is to establish a usable end; the task of the replicative polymerase is to copy the much larger tract efficiently. This functional specialization is analogous to an initiation reaction that creates a substrate for an enzyme that is optimized for elongation.

The bacterial DnaG primase and the eukaryotic primase associated with DNA polymerase alpha are representative solutions to this problem. In a bacterial system the primase can make a short RNA that is subsequently extended by the relevant DNA polymerase. In the eukaryotic nuclear system the primase first synthesizes RNA and polymerase alpha extends it with a short tract of DNA, forming a composite primer before the main elongation polymerases undertake extensive synthesis. The details of the handoffs, named subunits and polymerase assignments are covered in subsequent chapters. The essential distinction here is between de novo initiation, short primer extension and processive elongation; these need not be conducted by the same protein.

The leading strand usually requires a primer when its synthesis is established, and additional primers can become necessary if synthesis is restarted. The lagging strand requires repeated priming during ordinary fork progression because each new fragment begins at a different location. This creates a selective vulnerability. If primase function is lost after both strands have already been initiated, existing elongating chains may continue temporarily. The leading strand can potentially extend farther without requiring a new primer than the lagging strand. The latter will stop initiating additional fragments after its current primers and intermediates are used. At the level of a real replisome, coordination and checkpoint responses may soon slow or stop both strands, so prolonged leading synthesis is not guaranteed.

The frequency of productive priming affects fragment length. If a fork travels at speed v base pairs per second and successful new primers are placed at average intervals of tau seconds, the mean separation in template coordinates is approximately v × tau base pairs under a steady fork model. A fork progressing at 500 bp/s with one successful priming event every three seconds would place primers about 1,500 bases apart. At unchanged fork speed, reducing the average interval to two seconds would lower the predicted separation to 1,000 bases. This relationship explains why fragment length cannot be treated as a property of primase alone: fork progression speed also contributes to the amount of template exposed between successive starts.

The approximation assumes that the sites used for fragment initiation are consistently placed close to the advancing fork and that priming intervals are representative of the observed products. Real primase can sample sites with different efficiencies, and not every short RNA molecule becomes a successful fragment. A defective handoff may produce apparently normal RNA primers yet yield fewer productive DNA chains. The useful variable in the approximation is therefore the frequency of successful fragment starts, not simply the total rate at which all RNA products are made in isolation.

This distinction can be tested by comparing short RNA formation with the production of DNA containing the primer-derived starting segment. If a mutant primase yields ordinary amounts of RNA but the corresponding elongation system produces very little DNA, the deficiency could lie in primer length, product accessibility, handoff or downstream polymerase recruitment. Conversely, if the amount of RNA decreases and adding an appropriately positioned synthetic primer restores polymerization on an artificial substrate, the polymerase may be intact and the initiating step impaired. A rescue assay must provide a chemically suitable end at the correct location. An excess of unrelated RNA in solution will not generally substitute for a template-bound primer.

The temporary presence of RNA in a molecule that will ultimately become DNA is not an inconsistency. It is a solution to the start-up chemistry of DNA synthesis. The RNA segment is a transient part of a nascent product, not a permanent alteration of the genetic message. A normal maturation system removes that temporary material and replaces the required positions with DNA. The next sections will show why efficient extension and subsequent primer removal are both necessary: even a perfectly copied sequence may be embedded in a structurally unfinished molecule.

8. The polymerase needs sustained contact: processivity and speed

A DNA polymerase can be described by several properties that should not be collapsed into a single measure of efficiency. Catalytic speed refers to the rate at which nucleotide additions occur under particular conditions. Processivity refers to the number of nucleotides added during an association with the substrate before dissociation. Fidelity describes the accuracy of the resulting sequence. An enzyme can be fast but frequently detach, or slow but stay attached for long tracts. High fidelity does not automatically mean high processivity, and accessory proteins can change processivity without directly changing nucleotide discrimination chemistry.

The distinction becomes obvious when comparing two hypothetical enzymes. Polymerase A adds 100 nucleotides per second while engaged but typically detaches after 50 additions. Polymerase B adds only 50 nucleotides per second while engaged but averages 1,000 additions before detaching. The first has greater instantaneous extension speed, and the second has greater processivity. If each dissociation requires one second before productive binding resumes, the first enzyme needs about 0.5 seconds for extension and one second for rebinding per average 50-nucleotide cycle. Its effective long-run rate is about 33 nucleotides per second. The second requires about 20 seconds for a 1,000-nucleotide tract and one second for the interruption, giving about 48 nucleotides per second. Under these assumptions, the slower catalytic enzyme achieves the greater average throughput.

These values are educational examples rather than measured properties of named polymerases. The calculation assumes simple independent cycles, negligible initiation overhead other than the stated rebinding time, and enough substrate and dNTPs. The model explains why processivity matters for a chromosome: every unsuccessful or delayed encounter with the template can consume time without adding genetic material. It also shows why an endpoint incorporation assay may conceal the mechanistic basis of a difference. Increasing total nucleotide incorporation might reflect fewer interruptions rather than faster chemistry.

A sliding clamp allows DNA polymerase to maintain a productive relationship with duplex DNA without requiring unusually strong, immobilizing contacts at every backbone position. The clamp forms a ring around the DNA, making its association partly topological. It can move along the duplex while remaining linked to it, rather like a loose ring that slides along a cord but cannot simply leave through its side while the ring remains closed. Polymerase binds the clamp through specific protein-protein contacts. It can therefore travel with the clamp and repeatedly interact with the template without relying entirely on its own DNA-binding affinity.

The analogy has limits. DNA is a structured, charged polymer, and clamps make molecular contacts with it. The ring is not infinitely mobile or indestructible, and the associated polymerase can still dissociate from its binding surface. A clamp does not catalyze nucleotide addition or read the sequence. Its function is to support productive association, and it can serve as a docking site for other proteins that act on the nascent DNA. Because the clamp must encircle DNA, however, an intact closed ring cannot simply be placed around an internal primer-template junction by ordinary lateral binding. A clamp-loading activity solves that geometrical problem.

9. Clamp loaders assemble a topological attachment at the correct junction

A useful distinction between a sliding clamp and its loader is that the first is retained around DNA during productive elongation, whereas the second catalyzes the assembly of the clamp around the substrate. A clamp is not simply a stronger DNA-binding domain of the polymerase. It can be a separate oligomeric protein that remains on the duplex after the original polymerase has departed. Its association with the chromosome consequently depends on both physical enclosure and transient interactions with several proteins. The fact that an elongation polymerase can interact with such a ring makes repeated DNA synthesis more efficient, but the ring has to be placed at a position where a new DNA chain can be extended.

The appropriate loading substrate is generally a primer-template junction, with a paired region and a single-stranded template extension next to the primer's available end. That junction contains both an indication of where synthesis can begin and a duplex tract that can support the sliding clamp. The loader recognizes features of this DNA structure in combination with its protein partners. This is a different specificity from complementary base selection. A loader is not expected to identify the nucleotide sequence of every origin or every lagging-strand primer by reading bases as a sequence-specific initiator would. Its central responsibility is to assemble an accessory structure at a usable junction.

A simplified loading cycle begins when ATP binding changes the conformation of the loader and promotes interactions with the clamp. The loader engages and opens a clamp interface, allowing DNA to enter the ring. Recognition of the primer-template junction promotes progression through the loading reaction. Subsequent conformational changes associated with ATP hydrolysis allow the loader to disengage and leave a closed clamp encircling DNA. The polymerase can then engage the clamp at an appropriate interaction surface. The exact order of ring opening, DNA entry, clamp closure, ATP hydrolysis and release varies in detail among systems; the conserved principle is the coupling of nucleotide-dependent conformational changes to the placement of a ring around a DNA substrate.

The bacterial beta clamp of E. coli is assembled from two subunits, whereas the familiar eukaryotic PCNA ring contains three subunits. Each nevertheless presents a central channel and interaction surfaces that allow it to support DNA synthesis. The eukaryotic ATP-dependent loader is replication factor C, abbreviated RFC; the bacterial clamp loader is a component of the polymerase III assembly. These examples help students identify the same functional category in different organisms without assuming that the named proteins or the number of subunits are identical. The general physical task of placing a ring around a primer-template is shared.

A lagging-strand polymerase needs a new attachment at successive fragment starts. If it had to wait for the previous clamp to travel back along the entire older fragment and somehow cross a gap to the next primer, the process would be unnecessarily restrictive. A loading system can assemble a fresh clamp at a newly initiated junction. After the polymerase reaches the preceding fragment, it can relinquish its current association and be recruited to another primed site. The old clamp may remain associated with the replicated duplex for a period, where it can participate in the recruitment or coordination of other DNA-processing activities. Clamps are eventually unloaded or otherwise removed as required by the system.

A defect in clamp loading and a defect in polymerase-clamp interaction can both shorten newly synthesized DNA. Their immediate molecular causes differ. An ATPase-defective loader could be unable to complete a loading cycle despite an otherwise functional clamp. A mutation in a polymerase interaction motif could leave normal numbers of loaded clamps but prevent the polymerase from benefiting from their presence. To distinguish these possibilities, one should measure clamp association with the substrate in addition to DNA extension. On a suitably designed DNA molecule that already carries a functional clamp, a polymerase with a defective interaction surface should remain deficient even if the loader is bypassed. A functional polymerase suffering only from absent loading may be rescued by such a preassembled substrate.

A separate conceptual complication arises in assays using linear DNA. Even a closed ring can slide off a free end if there is no obstacle. Therefore loss of clamp association from a short linear substrate need not mean that the ring spontaneously opened. A circular DNA substrate or a properly designed end-blocked substrate can provide a more appropriate test of topological retention. One can then compare the behavior of the intact clamp with an interface mutant or test whether removal of the end block allows loss of the association. These controls clarify why the geometry of a biochemical substrate matters as much as the identities of the proteins.

Consider a processivity model in which each successful nucleotide addition is followed by an independent probability q of polymerase dissociation. The probability of completing at least n additions, for positive integer n, is (1−q) raised to the power n−1 if the first addition is assumed to occur. The number of additions before detachment has a geometric distribution with mean 1/q. This mean follows by summing the survival probabilities over n from one to infinity, which gives the geometric series 1 + (1−q) + (1−q)^2 + ... = 1/q. If q equals 0.01, the mean is 100 additions. If a clamp reduces q to 0.0002 without changing the instantaneous addition rate, the predicted mean becomes 5,000 additions, a fiftyfold improvement. The model assumes constant risk and independence, ignores pauses and template-specific obstacles, and is not a measurement of a particular polymerase. It demonstrates quantitatively why a modest probability of dissociation per step can have a dramatic effect when many steps must occur consecutively.

An appropriate experimental comparison therefore measures product lengths after single encounters rather than simply allowing unlimited cycles of rebinding. If repeated binding is allowed, an enzyme with low processivity may eventually produce long DNA, obscuring the number of additions per association. In a conceptual single-hit assay, productive reattachment of released enzymes is suppressed while already engaged enzymes are permitted to continue. The product length distribution then reports how far those initial complexes progress. Controls must establish that suppression of rebinding does not strip already bound polymerase from DNA. A shift toward longer products in the presence of a loaded clamp supports its processivity function; a faster initial chemical rate alone is not sufficient evidence.

10. Short nascent DNA molecules provided evidence for discontinuous growth

A model that predicts transient products should be tested at a time when those products exist. Mature DNA contains phosphodiester bonds throughout its backbone, and examination of a completed chromosome does not tell us whether all the bonds were made in one uninterrupted extension event. The discovery associated with Reiji and Tuneko Okazaki and coworkers was important because it addressed the temporal behavior of newly made chains. Their experiments in the 1960s examined the size of DNA synthesized during very brief labeling periods and provided evidence for discontinuous chain growth. The 1968 paper in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences explicitly investigated newly synthesized short DNA chains.

The logic of pulse labeling is straightforward. During a pulse, a detectable precursor is available for incorporation into macromolecules. Only those portions synthesized during that interval acquire the new label at high abundance. During a chase, the supply is replaced or diluted with unlabeled precursor, allowing synthesis to continue while reducing the appearance of new label. If an initially short labeled chain is subsequently joined into a much larger molecule, label that was originally associated with the small chain can later be recovered as part of a longer product. The assay does not need to assign a separate identity to every old and new nucleotide; it tracks the physical state of the population of molecules that received label during a defined interval.

The distinction between the length of a labeled region and the length of the covalently continuous molecule that contains it is vital. A short pulse may label only a small section of a long continuously growing strand. Under conditions that separate DNA molecules according to their overall strand length, however, that labeled section remains part of the long covalently linked molecule. Therefore the appearance of label in a small single-stranded DNA species cannot be explained merely by the short duration of exposure to the precursor. It suggests that the labeled material itself belongs to a short covalent chain, provided that the isolation procedure has not artificially broken the DNA.

A denaturing separation is especially informative because complementary strands do not have to be covalently joined to behave as a large duplex under native conditions. Newly made short fragments could remain attached to a long parental template by base pairing and appear associated with a large complex. Separating the strands without creating extensive new breaks exposes the actual lengths of the daughter chains. Classical sedimentation measurements could distinguish shorter and longer nascent DNA under appropriate conditions. Modern educational diagrams often substitute denaturing gels to illustrate the same logical principle, although these diagrams should not be presented as exact reconstructions of the historical equipment or data.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which newly incorporated radioactivity is distributed between a short-chain fraction and a long-chain fraction. Immediately after a brief pulse, suppose 70 percent of recovered label occurs in the short fraction and 30 percent in the long fraction. Following a chase, imagine that the short fraction contains only 10 percent and the long fraction contains 90 percent, with total recovered label approximately unchanged. A parsimonious interpretation is that many short labeled intermediates were incorporated into larger covalent chains. It is not enough to observe that unlabeled DNA became larger, because unlabeled synthesis cannot explain the redistribution of the pre-existing radioactive label. Conservation of recovered label helps rule out simple preferential disappearance of the short products.

These invented values are not the historical results and should never be cited as such. They illustrate how an experimental conclusion follows from temporal transfer of signal rather than merely from observing short DNA. If the total recovered label fell dramatically during the chase, the short molecules might have been degraded instead of joined. If label were continually supplied throughout the nominal chase, newly synthesized long products could contain new label rather than receive it from existing fragments. If the extraction procedure fragmented DNA differently between samples, an apparent shift in molecular size could reflect the method rather than the replication mechanism. Matched sample handling, stable recovery and an effective chase are necessary for a strong inference.

Additional evidence can be obtained by preventing the completion of fragments. In a condition where the relevant DNA ligase activity is impaired, the predicted accumulation of unjoined nascent intermediates supports the idea that normally these molecules undergo joining. Yet the interpretation depends on knowing which molecules are measured. A ligase defect can produce DNA breaks or influence other repair pathways as well as prevent normal lagging-strand completion. It is therefore helpful to determine whether the accumulated species contain newly synthesized DNA, whether they occur in the expected replication-associated context and whether restoration of ligase activity leads to the expected change.

Early observations of short nascent chains should also be interpreted in the light of the history of the field. Some early discussions considered the possibility of discontinuous synthesis on both strands; the size of nascent products alone does not establish which parental template produced them. Subsequent biochemical, genetic and structural evidence supports the standard semidiscontinuous model for ordinary chromosomal forks, with predominantly continuous leading-strand extension and repeated lagging-strand starts. Recognizing the distinction between evidence for short intermediates and evidence for their strand assignment prevents the modern diagram from being read backward into every early observation. It also helps the student appreciate that experimental models can become more precise as new methods distinguish processes that were previously combined.

One useful extension of the reasoning is to examine strand polarity in nascent products. An experiment may distinguish the recently incorporated ends of short chains by exploiting enzymes that remove nucleotides from defined termini. If a newly added label is preferentially released by an activity that attacks the 3′ end, that finding is consistent with extension at that end, assuming the label has not moved through secondary metabolism or other reactions. The Okazaki group investigated such directional evidence in subsequent work on short DNA chains. Together with purified polymerase chemistry, these observations strengthen the conclusion that discontinuous fragments are not produced by a hidden reverse-direction polymerase.

The pulse-chase approach recurs throughout molecular and cell biology. It can reveal the conversion of a precursor into a mature product, movement through cellular compartments or the lifetime of a labeled population. Here it teaches a particular lesson about DNA replication: a temporarily discontinuous covalent intermediate can yield a continuous final product. A student who understands that difference can make predictions about ligase inhibition and primer-processing failure without memorizing a list of unrelated mutant phenotypes.

11. A fragment is not finished when polymerase reaches its neighbor

The appearance of complementary DNA behind the fork does not guarantee that the daughter strand has reached its final chemical composition. Every normal RNA-primed Okazaki fragment initially contains a primer-derived region at its 5′ end. Even if the rest of the sequence has been copied accurately, this RNA is temporary and must be replaced as part of daughter-strand maturation. A second issue is the discontinuity between neighboring pieces. Two adjacent chains can both pair perfectly with a common template and still lack a phosphodiester bond at their boundary. In order to produce a continuous DNA strand, the machinery must remove unwanted primer material, synthesize replacement DNA and seal the remaining backbone discontinuity.

Three molecular structures should be distinguished precisely. A nick is a break in one covalent backbone at a position where no nucleotide is missing. The upstream nucleotide provides a 3′ end and its adjacent downstream nucleotide a 5′ end; both may remain paired to the intact template. A gap is a missing stretch of one or more nucleotides, so the neighboring chain ends are separated by positions on the template that have no corresponding daughter nucleotides. A flap is a single-stranded extension displaced away from its complementary template, often at the 5′ end of a downstream fragment during maturation. Each structure presents a different enzymatic problem. A ligase cannot supply the missing bases of a gap, and a polymerase cannot necessarily seal a simple nick without additional processing.

When a lagging polymerase extends a new fragment toward the previously synthesized fragment, the growing 3′ end encounters a region that is already paired with a primer-containing chain. Completion can require removal or displacement of that older primer. The exact reactions differ in bacteria and eukaryotes. In the E. coli textbook model, DNA polymerase I has a 5′ to 3′ nuclease function and a DNA synthesis function that permit removal of downstream material while replacement DNA is added from the upstream available 3′ end. Such coordinated replacement can move the nick along the DNA, a process called nick translation. The important conceptual feature is that cleavage at the 5′ side and polymerization at the 3′ side can occur in a coordinated manner while the overall synthesis direction remains unchanged.

In eukaryotic nuclear replication, extension can displace material into a flap, and specialized nucleases including FEN1 process suitable flap structures. RNase H activities can participate in the removal of RNA from RNA-DNA hybrid intermediates. No single description should imply that RNase H always removes every final ribonucleotide in every context. The maturation pathway depends on the architecture of the primer, the structure of the intermediate and the particular enzyme system. In a standard eukaryotic model, polymerase delta-mediated extension and flap processing are coordinated so that the final DNA junction is suitable for ligase I. The separate organism-specific chapters will give the more detailed reaction sequences.

The relative directions can be checked by imagining the nascent strand laid out from left to right in its own 5′ to 3′ orientation. An upstream fragment ends in a 3′ hydroxyl on the left side of a junction. The downstream fragment begins with its 5′ region on the right side of that junction. To replace the initial material of the downstream fragment, a polymerase extends the upstream 3′ end toward the right while appropriate nucleases remove material ahead. Once the necessary sequence has been made and the two ends become immediately adjacent, the remaining discontinuity is a nick. This drawing follows the daughter strand rather than the direction in which the fork moves in space. For the lagging strand, those two reference directions are generally opposite.

A mutation in a nuclease involved in primer processing could cause RNA-containing junctions or abnormal flap structures to persist. A mutation that selectively impairs polymerization during gap filling could leave unpaired template positions, even if RNA removal is efficient. A mutation affecting ligase could produce relatively complete DNA segments that remain separated by nicks. These predictions are simplified outcomes of the primary defects and may be modified by alternative enzymes or secondary consequences in cells. The underlying examination skill is to ask which structural intermediate is the direct substrate of the missing function and which product would ordinarily be made from it.

Alkaline sensitivity can help test for ribonucleotides within DNA-associated products because RNA has a 2′ hydroxyl that can participate in backbone cleavage under suitable conditions. A molecule containing residual RNA may therefore respond differently to an alkaline treatment from a purely DNA product of similar sequence and length. The interpretation requires comparison with matched control substrates, because harsh treatment or pre-existing damage can also influence apparent DNA integrity. RNase H treatment can provide complementary information if the suspected RNA is paired to DNA. Neither test alone specifies precisely where the RNA lies or which protein failed to remove it.

A completed DNA fragment must be distinguished from a complete daughter chromosome. A gel that detects total synthesis may indicate that most bases are copied while revealing little about the fraction of junctions that have been sealed. Even a small proportion of unresolved discontinuities distributed over a large genome can be biologically serious. Those sites may interfere with subsequent DNA transactions or become substrates for break formation under certain circumstances. This is why enzymes that act after most nucleotide incorporation has occurred can still be indispensable for cell survival. They solve a structural problem that the primary polymerase reaction does not solve by itself.

12. DNA ligase completes the covalent backbone without copying sequence

A DNA ligase catalyzes the formation of a phosphodiester bond at a suitable junction between adjacent DNA ends. At an ordinary replication-associated nick, those ends include an available 3′ hydroxyl and a neighboring 5′ phosphate. The template and its paired daughter bases position the ends near one another, but this positioning does not automatically cause the bond to form at a biologically useful rate. The reaction requires chemical activation and an enzyme that can organize the substrates. Ligase supplies that activity.

The reaction can be understood as a sequence of adenylation and transfer events. In an ATP-dependent ligase reaction, ATP supports formation of a covalent enzyme-AMP intermediate, with pyrophosphate released in the activation step. The activated adenylyl group is then transferred to the 5′ phosphate at the nick, creating an activated DNA end. The neighboring 3′ hydroxyl attacks that activated phosphate, forming the phosphodiester linkage while AMP is released. In this way, the bond that joins the two DNA segments is produced without adding a new nucleotide to the daughter sequence. The enzyme catalyzes chemical continuity rather than sequence copying.

Some bacterial DNA ligases use NAD+ instead of ATP as their immediate adenylation donor, releasing nicotinamide mononucleotide during formation of the enzyme-AMP intermediate. Many other ligases, including the eukaryotic nuclear replicative ligase, use ATP. These cofactor differences demonstrate why DNA ligase should not be defined solely as an ATP-hydrolyzing protein. The general function is activation of a DNA end and sealing of a nick through an adenylyl intermediate in the usual ligase mechanism. In a mixed assay, removal of ATP might selectively impair an ATP-dependent ligase but not a NAD+-dependent one, while simultaneously influencing other ATP-dependent replication factors. Knowledge of the particular enzymes present is essential.

A ligase has no template-directed polymerization activity comparable to DNA polymerase. If the substrate contains a gap of three nucleotides, bringing the two ends together would not restore the correct three missing bases. A polymerase must first use the intact template to fill those positions, or the substrate must be repaired by another suitable route. Similarly, the chemical requirements of the ends matter: an unphosphorylated 5′ hydroxyl is not the ordinary 5′ phosphate substrate of a nick-sealing reaction. Restoration of an appropriate end by another enzyme may be necessary before ligase can act. This helps explain why a simple observation of unsealed DNA does not uniquely prove loss of ligase.

A conceptual nick-sealing assay can compare a labeled short strand paired to a longer template next to an unlabeled adjacent fragment. Under denaturing separation, the labeled strand will initially be recovered at its original length. Successful covalent joining will yield a labeled molecule whose length equals the sum of the adjacent pieces. If the fragments are merely associated by complementary pairing, denaturation separates them and the labeled strand remains short. A control containing a gap, or one lacking the appropriate 5′ phosphate, tests the substrate requirements. A no-ligase condition reveals whether spontaneous joining or another enzyme contributes to the readout.

Consider a hypothetical mixture containing 1,000 nicked molecules. An assay reports 900 covalently joined products after incubation with an active ligase and 30 joined products in a matched no-ligase condition. Under an additive background assumption, 870 joined products above background can be attributed to the presence of the ligase. This corresponds to 87 percent of the original input molecules. That is distinct from the observed total joining fraction of 90 percent. If the no-ligase signal arises from a systematic measurement artifact rather than true product, the background correction might need a different treatment. The calculation is a reminder that appropriate controls affect quantitative inference, not merely the appearance of a convincing graph.

Ligase function also connects replication to DNA repair and biotechnology. The same general nick-sealing chemistry is needed after repair synthesis, and ligases are used to join DNA ends in molecular cloning when substrates and end chemistry are appropriate. The contexts differ in how compatible ends are brought together and which cellular factors participate. A ligase that efficiently seals an ordinary nick in a paired duplex should not automatically be assumed to join every possible combination of free double-stranded DNA ends with equal efficiency. The immediate replication objective is the production of a covalently continuous daughter backbone after primer processing, not arbitrary joining of different chromosomes.

The completed lagging strand therefore emerges from a series of specialized reactions. Primase creates a suitable starting end, polymerase extends it, nucleases remove temporary material, polymerase activity supplies the correct replacement DNA, and ligase makes the final junction continuous. In a molecular diagram these activities may be placed close together, yet they need not be synchronized at a single instant. A transient population of products at various stages is a normal consequence of a continuously progressing fork. The question is whether the pathway processes these intermediates efficiently enough that unresolved structures do not accumulate to damaging levels.

13. DNA opening creates a topological problem

Until now we have considered the two parental strands mainly in terms of their polarity and accessibility. There is an additional constraint because they wind around one another. A long double helix that is free to rotate at its ends can dissipate some rotational strain as a helicase separates the strands. Chromosomes, however, are often constrained by circularity, attachment to cellular structures, protein organization or the geometry of neighboring DNA. Local opening in such a system cannot simply eliminate the pre-existing mutual winding of the two strands. Unless winding is redistributed or removed, further separation generates mechanical resistance.

A useful physical analogy involves two cords twisted together and held at their ends. Separating several turns in the middle while preventing the ends from rotating forces the remaining cord segments to accommodate the displaced winding. The resulting shapes and tension oppose additional separation. Real DNA is much smaller, is subject to thermal motion and can bend, twist and change its axis in several ways. Nevertheless the analogy explains why helicase activity can require another enzyme even when the motor itself is chemically functional. The obstacle may be stored mechanical strain rather than an inaccessible base pair or exhausted ATP.

The topology of covalently closed circular duplex DNA is described by its linking number, Lk. Imagine the two strands as closed curves. Their linking number counts their mutual linkage in a signed manner and is an integer invariant under continuous deformation that does not allow one strand to pass through another. Stretching, bending or twisting the molecule without breaking the appropriate backbone cannot by itself change the linking number. For a closed circular DNA duplex, this number can be expressed as the sum of twist and writhe: Lk = Tw + Wr. Twist describes the local winding of the strands around the duplex axis; writhe describes the coiling of that axis in three-dimensional space. Twist and writhe individually need not be integers.

This distinction separates the local double-helical structure from supercoiling. A molecule can redistribute its existing linking number between twist and writhe. A change in writhe may reduce the amount of torsional distortion stored as twist. A relaxed circular B-form DNA molecule has an approximate reference linking number Lk0 = N/h, where N is its length in base pairs and h is the number of base pairs per helical turn under the specified reference conditions, often taken to be approximately 10.5. Actual linking numbers of intact closed circles are integers; the theoretical reference is not required to be an integer because it describes an ideal relaxed helix. If a problem assumes N is a multiple of h, this distinction becomes numerically simple.

The linking difference is ΔLk = Lk − Lk0. A useful dimensionless measure of supercoiling is the superhelical density σ = ΔLk/Lk0. As an example, consider a circular 4,200-bp duplex with h = 10.5 bp per turn and an actual linking number of 376. The relaxed reference is 4,200/10.5 = 400. The linking difference is −24, and the superhelical density is −24/400 = −0.060. Under these assumptions, the molecule is negatively supercoiled relative to the relaxed reference. If an enzyme increases Lk by 16 without changing length or the reference helical repeat, the final Lk becomes 392, and σ becomes −8/400 = −0.020. The magnitude of the negative linking difference has decreased, so the molecule has become less negatively supercoiled.

Negative supercoiling can facilitate local opening in some circumstances because strand separation can help accommodate the deficit in linking relative to relaxed DNA. Positive supercoiling can oppose opening by increasing the energetic cost of separating additional turns. However, the sign of supercoiling does not tell us that all regions of a molecule behave identically. DNA sequence, protein binding, ionic conditions and the partition of linking difference into twist and writhe can affect the local response. The simple calculation describes global topology relative to a stated reference, not a complete molecular map of the chromosome.

Suppose a fork progresses through 1,050 bp of DNA with the reference repeat of 10.5 bp per turn. The parental duplex in that interval contains approximately 100 helical turns. If the fork cannot rotate, if the DNA ahead is constrained and if there are no strand-passage reactions or other compensating processes, the winding removed from that interval must be accommodated elsewhere. Under this idealized bookkeeping model, the region ahead receives approximately 100 extra turns relative to its relaxed state. It would be misleading to describe that number as 100 physical visible loops: the excess linking can be distributed as changes in twist and writhe, and the actual partition depends on the mechanical properties of the DNA and proteins. The calculation is an estimate of winding burden rather than a prediction of the exact shape.

Real replication forks can rotate, and some of the parental linking can be transferred into interwinding of daughter duplexes behind the junction. Such interwinding is often described in terms of precatenanes during replication. At the completion of replication of constrained circular DNA, two daughter circles may remain topologically linked, producing catenanes. The problem then becomes one of separating intact daughter molecules, not merely easing unwinding ahead of the fork. A DNA strand-passage enzyme that can alter the relationships between duplexes is especially important for this task. Linear chromosomes have free physical ends at the level of whole molecules, but topological domains and cellular organization can still restrict rotational relaxation, so their replication also requires topoisomerase activities.

A student should distinguish three related observations. Positive torsional stress ahead of a fork indicates difficulty in accommodating local parental duplex opening. Interwinding behind the fork reflects the geometry of newly forming daughter molecules. Catenation of completed circular products means that separate covalently closed DNA rings remain linked. The same broad class of enzymes participates in solving these problems, but the required strand-passage operation is not identical in every case. This explains why different topoisomerase mutants can show different defects even though they all affect DNA topology.

14. Topoisomerases alter topology by controlled breakage and resealing

Topoisomerases are unusual because their normal catalytic action involves transient covalent breakage of DNA. At first this may sound incompatible with faithful replication. The crucial point is that cleavage is controlled and normally coupled to resealing. An active-site tyrosine commonly participates in the formation of a transient covalent linkage between the enzyme and a DNA phosphate. The enzyme thereby retains a chemically activated connection to the broken backbone and can subsequently promote restoration of the phosphodiester linkage. Unlike an uncontrolled nuclease that leaves persistent fragments, a functional topoisomerase uses temporary strand breakage as part of a reversible topological operation.

Type I enzymes transiently cleave one strand of a duplex. Their mechanisms are not all identical. In a common textbook distinction, type IA enzymes pass a strand through a single-strand break, changing linking number in steps of one for suitable duplex substrates. Type IB enzymes permit controlled rotation around the intact strand at a nick, and more than one turn may be relaxed during a cleavage event. Many type I enzymes do not need ATP for the ordinary relaxation reaction because they use the energy of the phosphodiester linkage retained in the covalent intermediate and the tendency of strained DNA to relax. There are exceptions to broad ATP-independence rules, including reverse gyrase, so a question about a particular enzyme must specify the system. The essential contrast is the one-strand cleavage mechanism versus double-strand passage.

Type II topoisomerases transiently cleave both strands of one DNA duplex, hold that segment as a gate and allow another duplex segment to pass through before resealing the gate. ATP-dependent conformational changes promote directional completion of the strand-passage cycle. For a closed duplex, an ordinary intramolecular double-strand passage changes the linking number by two. This class can also disentangle interlinked DNA duplexes, a capacity directly relevant when circular daughter chromosomes must be separated. The linking number used to describe strand winding within a single duplex should not be confused with the catenation number between two duplex rings; they refer to different curves and different geometrical relationships.

DNA gyrase, a bacterial type II topoisomerase, is a familiar example of an enzyme that can use ATP to introduce negative supercoils into DNA. This property distinguishes it from an enzyme that simply lets supercoiled DNA approach a relaxed state. Bacterial topoisomerase IV is particularly important in decatenating replicated molecules in several well-studied species. Eukaryotic topoisomerases I and II contribute to management of torsion and entanglement during chromosomal replication. These general functional contrasts are sufficient here; the exact bacterial termination and eukaryotic chromosome-context mechanisms will be examined in Nodes 3.A.04 and 3.A.05.

The arithmetic of linking-number changes can be used to reason about a hypothetical type II reaction. Suppose a closed circular substrate has Lk = 420 and a relaxed reference of 400. An intramolecular reaction that lowers linking number by exactly two per productive cycle would require ten net cycles to reach Lk = 400. If two cycles occurred in the reverse direction along the way, the total number of cycles needed would be greater than ten. This distinction between net change and total turnover is important in interpreting energy consumption: topology reports the net outcome of strand-passage events, not necessarily every catalytic event that took place. The calculation assumes that each cycle affects the same molecule in the specified way and that the relaxed reference does not change.

An important distinction in pharmacological reasoning is the difference between suppressing the catalytic activity of an enzyme and trapping a normally transient reaction intermediate. A catalytic inhibitor that prevents DNA cleavage or productive strand passage can increase torsional stress by preventing normal relaxation. An agent that stabilizes a topoisomerase-DNA cleavage complex may additionally increase the persistence of protein-linked DNA breaks. Certain antibacterial quinolones illustrate the latter principle for bacterial type II topoisomerases. The consequences of such treatment are not equivalent to those of simply deleting the enzyme. The trapped intermediate can itself obstruct a moving fork or create a source of DNA damage. This concept connects with antimicrobial action and subsequent DNA damage chapters without requiring clinical treatment detail.

One can investigate topology by comparing populations of closed DNA circles with different linking numbers. Under suitable electrophoretic conditions, their different conformations may cause them to migrate differently. The pattern can include multiple discrete topoisomers. A nicked circular DNA molecule does not retain the same fixed mutual winding constraint because its broken backbone allows rotational relaxation, whereas a covalently closed circle remains topologically constrained. A linear DNA control provides a further distinction in shape and topology. The direction of migration alone does not uniquely identify the sign of supercoiling under every electrophoretic condition, because migration depends on the gel, intercalating compounds and molecular conformation. Appropriate standards are needed before assigning a particular band to a precise linking difference.

An idealized comparison between a functional topoisomerase and a cleavage-defective mutant could begin with the same supercoiled circular substrate. If the functional enzyme produces a changed topoisomer distribution while the mutant leaves the starting distribution largely unchanged, the result supports a requirement for its catalytic activity. A separate test for DNA breakage helps distinguish a productive topology change from conversion of closed circles into open or linear molecules. A band corresponding to relaxed or open DNA should not automatically be interpreted as a correctly resealed topoisomer. The experiment must verify covalent integrity as well as mobility.

In the context of the moving fork, the failure to relieve positive torsion can slow DNA opening even when the helicase retains normal ATP hydrolysis on a simple substrate. This illustrates the value of studying a protein both alone and as part of its natural task. A helicase that performs well on a short unconstrained partial duplex may be unable to sustain chromosome replication without topological assistance. By contrast, a topoisomerase cannot substitute for the helicase simply because it changes winding. The helicase opens parental base pairs at the advancing boundary; the topoisomerase allows otherwise constrained DNA to redistribute or alter its topology. These complementary functions explain why both are needed.

15. Replisome coordination integrates different molecular clocks

All of the preceding reactions can now be assembled into a causal model of fork progression. Helicase exposes the parental templates and DNA-binding proteins stabilize accessible regions. Primase provides a starting chain whenever one is required. Clamp loaders establish productive clamp associations at suitable junctions, and polymerases extend the new DNA. Behind the active synthesis zone, primer processing and ligation transform discontinuous intermediates into continuous daughter backbones. Topoisomerases manage the mechanical consequences of opening and the topological relationships among DNA molecules. None of these activities alone represents the complete fork.

In many descriptions of the bacterial replisome, leading and lagging synthesis are physically coordinated through interactions among the helicase, clamp loader and polymerase components. The lagging template can bend into a loop so that a polymerase attached to the moving complex can extend an Okazaki fragment while remaining near the other components. During extension, the path of DNA through the complex changes, and the loop can increase in size. When extension terminates at a suitable point and the polymerase relinquishes its attachment to the completed fragment, the loop can be released and a new cycle can begin at another primer. The repeated growth and release of the loop gives rise to the trombone analogy.

The loop must not be confused with a replication bubble. A replication bubble describes a region of parental duplex undergoing copying and is bounded by forks. The lagging-strand loop is a local spatial configuration of a template and its nascent partner within or near one fork assembly. Nor is the loop a mechanism for extending DNA in reverse. Chemical polarity remains unchanged along the curved path. A correct drawing can trace the growing 3′ end along the folded strand even when the physical displacement of the polymerase assembly has the same general orientation as the leading polymerase.

The trombone model is useful for understanding how proteins can remain associated with the fork while their substrates change. It should not be treated as a universal photograph of every replisome or a proof that leading and lagging polymerases have identical instantaneous speeds. Single-molecule investigations of the E. coli replication system, including work reported by Graham and colleagues in 2017, have supported a more dynamic picture in which the two polymerase activities can vary independently on short timescales. Coordination of final genome duplication need not require perfect synchronization of every nucleotide addition. Intermittent synthesis, transient pauses and rearrangements can coexist with an overall functional fork.

A simple quantitative framework helps describe coordination without assuming exact synchrony. Let the fork expose lagging template at an average speed v, measured in nucleotides of that template per second. Let the lagging synthesis system convert exposed template to complementary DNA at an average rate u over the same period. In a simplified interval without other sources or sinks of accessible template, the net increase in uncopied template is approximately (v−u)t when v exceeds u. If v = 400 nt/s and u = 300 nt/s over five seconds, the net additional uncopied region is approximately 500 nucleotides. This result concerns the imbalance of template exposure and copying, not the total number of single-stranded nucleotides bound by proteins at any instant. The latter also depends on initial conditions, primer coverage, DNA folding and processing intermediates.

If the lagging polymerase becomes temporarily faster than the rate at which template is exposed, it can reduce an accumulated backlog until available substrate is exhausted. Its activity cannot indefinitely exceed template availability on a steadily advancing fork. Similarly, the leading polymerase can pause without the entire fork instantly becoming inactive, but continued helicase movement can increase the amount of exposed template. The biological consequence depends on the duration of imbalance and the cell's capacity to restrain unwinding, protect the substrate or resume copying. These considerations explain why an average fork speed may mask substantial molecular dynamics.

Replication speed must also be distinguished from the rate of total nucleotide incorporation. A fork moving across a parental duplex at 200 bp/s makes approximately 200 new nucleotides per second on each daughter strand when synthesis is balanced, or 400 new DNA nucleotides per second in total. Two such forks operating independently would collectively incorporate approximately 800 new nucleotides per second. If a single bidirectional origin duplicates an ideal circular chromosome of 4.8 million base pairs with two forks traveling at a constant 800 bp/s, the forks together traverse 1,600 bp/s of original genome length. The ideal completion time is 4,800,000/1,600 = 3,000 seconds, or 50 minutes. The total new DNA is 9.6 million nucleotides, ignoring temporary primer replacement and any repair. Dividing the genome length by the nucleotide incorporation rate of all four daughter strands would give the wrong time because it mixes base pairs of parental genome with individual new nucleotides.

Fragment counting uses a similarly explicit convention. A lagging strand covering 30,000 template nucleotides with a mean fragment spacing of 1,500 nucleotides would require roughly 20 productive fragment starts. With a mean spacing of 150 nucleotides, the same distance would require roughly 200. If all internal fragment junctions must be joined, a linear run of 20 initially separate adjacent fragments contains 19 boundaries between those fragments. Additional junctions can be required where the run meets other nascent segments, and the leading-strand initiation primer may need processing as well. Stating whether the problem counts internal joins only or completion of the entire molecule prevents an off-by-one error.

These models establish useful limiting cases, not exact cellular timing laws. Multiple origins, variable velocities, fork convergence, sequence obstacles, changes in substrate supply and coordination among enzymes all affect actual completion. The equations are most reliable when the question explicitly states the assumptions and defines the measured quantities. A strong examination answer identifies which quantity is being held constant before predicting what will happen if another quantity changes.

16. Inferring the missing activity from independent measurements

A general framework for interpreting replication phenotypes begins by asking whether the observed defect concerns substrate availability, chemical synthesis, sustained association, maturation or topology. A mutation that prevents helicase-mediated opening reduces the supply of new exposed template. A mutation that impairs SSB or RPA may leave accessible DNA vulnerable to secondary structures or inappropriate interactions. Primase defects reduce the number of productive starting chains. A catalytic polymerase defect impairs extension from even a suitable primer. Clamp or loader defects can reduce the average length made in a productive encounter. Primer-processing and ligation defects affect the conversion of synthesized segments into mature covalent strands. Topoisomerase defects can impose mechanical limitations on otherwise functional enzymes.

The categories overlap at the level of the intact cell because a fork is an interacting system. For example, a severe ligase defect can eventually halt synthesis through secondary damage, and a clamp interaction defect can impair recruitment of maturation proteins as well as polymerase retention. A reduced incorporation signal is consequently an outcome, not a diagnosis. The task in an analytical question is to compare readouts that respond to different primary operations and determine which defect most directly explains their pattern.

Imagine a reconstructed replication system in which four independent measurements are made: duplex opening, short primer production, extension of a preprimed unstructured template, and the fraction of nascent DNA that has become covalently continuous. Suppose an altered factor leaves opening, primer formation and polymerase activity near their controls but markedly reduces the last measurement. A failure in maturation is more plausible than a primary helicase deficiency. A further assay of nick sealing would distinguish a ligase defect from impaired primer processing. If isolated nick sealing is normal but RNA-containing junctions accumulate, primer removal is a stronger candidate. If RNA is removed and gaps are filled yet pre-existing nicks remain, ligase becomes the stronger candidate.

The reasoning changes if the defective complex shows normal polymerization on a short preprimed template but reduced product lengths during a single productive association. A defect in clamp loading or polymerase-clamp interaction becomes plausible, particularly if chemical addition rates are unchanged. Measuring clamp association on a suitable substrate would distinguish these alternatives. This analysis does not require knowledge of every subunit in a specific organism. It requires understanding the operation that each functional component contributes to the fork and constructing an experiment that bypasses or isolates that operation.

A final comparison concerns information and physical completion. A proofreading-defective polymerase might synthesize long DNA rapidly and produce mature uninterrupted strands while introducing more sequence errors. A ligase-defective system might copy most of the correct sequence yet leave covalent interruptions. An origin-loading defect might preserve the accuracy and integrity of individual products while reducing the number of active forks. Each defect reduces the suitability of the genome for inheritance, but the appropriate measurement is different. Students who distinguish sequence accuracy, chromosome completion and initiation frequency can transfer their reasoning to later topics in DNA repair, cell-cycle regulation and inheritance.

The common architecture examined here provides the conceptual foundation for the next two chapters. Bacterial and eukaryotic replication employ different named proteins and distinct regulatory arrangements, but they must address the same chemical and geometrical problems. Once a student understands the polarity of the two templates, the requirement for an extendable end, the difference between processivity and velocity, the purpose of temporary primers, the maturation of fragment junctions and the management of topology, the organism-specific details become solutions to recognizable problems rather than isolated lists of protein names.

18. Part B — 20 original conceptual MCQs

Choose one best answer. These are original practice questions, not previous-year questions.

  1. What specifies the 5′ and 3′ ends of a DNA chain?
    1. Position of the sugar carbons
    2. Number of bases
    3. Number of hydrogen bonds
    4. Direction of chromosome movement
    Answer and reasoning

    A. Prime numbers refer to sugar carbons and determine backbone polarity; fork motion and base composition do not define the ends.

  2. A rightward fork has an upper template 3′→5′ left to right. Which new strand is predominantly continuous?
    1. Lower complement
    2. Upper complement
    3. Both
    4. Neither
    Answer and reasoning

    B. The upper template is read 3′→5′ toward the fork. Both new products extend 5′→3′, so the lower complement requires repeated starts.

  3. Which atom directly attacks the incoming dNTP alpha phosphorus?
    1. Template 5′ oxygen
    2. Incoming base nitrogen
    3. Primer 3′ oxygen
    4. Incoming gamma phosphorus
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The terminal primer 3′ hydroxyl is the nucleophile; the template base determines selection but is not the reacting acceptor.

  4. Which dNTP phosphate is retained during normal incorporation?
    1. Alpha
    2. Beta
    3. Gamma
    4. All three
    Answer and reasoning

    A. The alpha phosphate becomes part of the phosphodiester backbone; beta and gamma leave as pyrophosphate.

  5. What is the principal contribution of primase?
    1. Seals nicks
    2. Creates an extendable primer de novo
    3. Relaxes supercoils
    4. Removes mismatches
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Primase can initiate RNA synthesis without a pre-existing chain; ligase, topoisomerase and proofreading perform the other tasks.

  6. What does SSB primarily accomplish?
    1. Creates covalent strand breaks
    2. Stabilizes exposed single-stranded DNA
    3. Synthesizes RNA
    4. Introduces negative linking
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Binding reduces problematic folding and premature reannealing. It is not the directional motor that opens the fork.

  7. Why is a clamp loader required?
    1. To select complementary dNTPs
    2. To remove RNA primers
    3. To open and position a sliding clamp around DNA
    4. To make ATP
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The ring needs to encircle a suitable junction; the loader couples ATP-dependent changes to assembly.

  8. What is processivity?
    1. Nucleotides incorporated per productive binding encounter
    2. Nucleotides per second
    3. Fraction of correct bases
    4. Number of forks per origin
    Answer and reasoning

    A. The other options describe speed, fidelity and replication architecture, respectively.

  9. Which intermediate is the direct ordinary substrate for a replication ligase?
    1. A long unprimed gap
    2. An intact duplex
    3. A nick with adjacent 3′-OH and 5′ phosphate
    4. A free dNTP
    Answer and reasoning

    C. An ordinary nick has chemically adjacent ends. Missing nucleotide positions require other activities before ligation.

  10. Which cofactor statement about DNA ligase is accurate?
    1. Every ligase requires ATP
    2. Some bacterial ligases use NAD+
    3. All ligases use dGTP
    4. Ligases never require activated donors
    Answer and reasoning

    B. ATP-dependent and NAD+-dependent ligases both form adenylated intermediates; donor choice varies.

  11. In a bidirectional bubble, one old strand can be what?
    1. Leading template at both forks
    2. Lagging template at both forks
    3. Leading at one fork and lagging at the other
    4. Never a template
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Opposing fork directions reverse which old strand has suitable polarity for continuous extension.

  12. Which enzyme directly removes torsional constraints by transient DNA cleavage and resealing?
    1. Primase
    2. Polymerase
    3. Topoisomerase
    4. SSB
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Topoisomerases alter topology; helicase separates bases, not the covalent backbone.

  13. Which statement best describes the trombone model?
    1. A loop changes polymerase chemistry to 3′→5′ synthesis
    2. A lagging-template loop permits spatial coordination without changing chemical polarity
    3. A bubble always contains one fork
    4. DNA ends become permanently linked
    Answer and reasoning

    B. The loop changes the path of the DNA, not its chemical orientation or extension direction.

  14. What is the expected effect of a primer ending in a dideoxynucleotide?
    1. Normal extension
    2. Faster ligation
    3. Ordinary polymerization stops at that end
    4. Helicase cannot bind anywhere
    Answer and reasoning

    C. The 3′-OH required for the next phosphodiester bond is absent.

  15. Which protein is the eukaryotic functional counterpart of the bacterial beta clamp?
    1. RPA
    2. PCNA
    3. ORC
    4. FEN1
    Answer and reasoning

    B. PCNA forms the sliding clamp; RPA protects ssDNA, ORC participates in initiation and FEN1 processes flaps.

  16. Which statement about helicase polarity is correct?
    1. All helicases track 5′→3′
    2. All track 3′→5′
    3. Tracking polarity depends on the motor and strand
    4. Tracking polarity is the same as synthesis polarity
    Answer and reasoning

    C. DnaB and CMG exemplify different tracking strand polarities; both can advance with their respective forks.

  17. Which operation does not add new nucleotide positions to DNA?
    1. Gap filling
    2. Leading extension
    3. Ligation of a nick
    4. Lagging extension
    Answer and reasoning

    C. Ligase makes a covalent bond between existing adjacent residues, rather than selecting and incorporating dNTPs.

  18. What does a 3′→5′ proofreading exonuclease do?
    1. Copies the opposite strand backwards
    2. Removes residues from the growing end
    3. Removes all 5′ primers
    4. Generates RNA primers
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Proofreading cleaves the nascent terminus; it is chemically distinct from extension and downstream primer removal.

  19. Which historical observation supports discontinuous DNA synthesis?
    1. All old DNA is degraded
    2. Short nascent chains become part of long covalent chains
    3. Both parental strands disappear
    4. DNA is synthesized without enzymes
    Answer and reasoning

    B. Pulse-chase conversion of short labeled chains into long products supports precursor-product joining, with appropriate controls.

  20. What is the approximate relationship between Lk, Tw and Wr in closed circular duplex DNA?
    1. Lk = Tw − Wr
    2. Lk = Tw + Wr
    3. Lk = Tw × Wr
    4. Lk is always zero
    Answer and reasoning

19. Part C — 26 original analytical questions

The datasets and scenarios are hypothetical educational examples.

  1. A fork advances rightward at 300 bp/s. The upper old strand is 5′→3′ left to right. Identify the leading template and the spatial direction of new lagging extension.
    Answer and reasoning

    The lower old strand is the leading template. The upper new strand extends leftward from each primer, because its template is read right to left. The fork moves right regardless.

  2. A primed 100-base template is extended by an enzyme lacking dCTP. The first template G is the ninth uncopied base. What is the longest ordinary error-free extension?
    Answer and reasoning

    Eight nucleotides. Template G requires dCTP at position nine; ordinary correct extension stops before it. The absence of one nucleotide does not abolish earlier additions.

  3. A polymerase has normal incorporation on preprimed ssDNA, but an extract fails to make RNA primers and cannot start on unprimed DNA. Which defect is most direct, and what rescue is informative?
    Answer and reasoning

    Primase activity is the direct candidate. A correctly oriented synthetic primer should restore extension on the simple substrate; it cannot replace repeated priming at a whole moving fork.

  4. Two helicase substrates have identical duplexes. One presents a 5′ tail on the long strand; the other a 3′ tail. A motor releases the short strand only with the 5′ tail and ATP. Interpret.
    Answer and reasoning

    The result supports 5′→3′ movement on the long tracking strand if loading occurs on the tail. Controls must exclude unequal stability, binding or spontaneous separation. Native replisome orientation is not proven by this assay alone.

  5. A 4.2-Mb circular chromosome has two forks moving at 700 bp/s each. Calculate ideal time and new DNA residues.
    Answer and reasoning

    Time = 4,200,000/(2 × 700) = 3,000 s = 50 min. Each parental base pair requires two new residues, so 8.4 million nucleotides are incorporated into final DNA.

  6. One fork moves at 450 bp/s, with successful lagging initiation every 2.4 s. Estimate spacing. What if the interval doubles at fixed speed?
    Answer and reasoning

    450 × 2.4 = 1,080 bases; doubling the interval gives 2,160 bases. This is ideal primer spacing, not an invariant fragment length.

  7. A polymerase adds 120 nt/s while bound, averages 60 nt per encounter and waits 1.5 s to rebind. Compute effective speed.
    Answer and reasoning

    Extension takes 60/120 = 0.5 s; total cycle = 2 s. Effective throughput = 60/2 = 30 nt/s, assuming identical repeated cycles.

  8. A clamp reduces dissociation probability per addition from 0.02 to 0.001. Calculate mean processivity and fold increase using the geometric model.
    Answer and reasoning

    Means are 1/0.02 = 50 and 1/0.001 = 1,000 nucleotides; increase = 20-fold. Instantaneous chemistry need not change.

  9. In a single-hit assay, clamp A increases median DNA length but not the initial nucleotide incorporation rate per bound enzyme. What property changed?
    Answer and reasoning

    The length shift supports improved processivity. Rate per engaged enzyme is unchanged, so it does not demonstrate faster chemical catalysis.

  10. A mutant has normal loaded-clamp abundance, normal short-template catalysis, but unusually short single-hit products. Which interaction is worth testing?
    Answer and reasoning

    Polymerase-clamp binding or retention. A loader defect is less likely given normal loaded-clamp abundance. A direct interaction assay and a functional wild-type polymerase rescue discriminate.

  11. An intact closed clamp disappears from a 50-bp linear DNA molecule but persists on a circular molecule. Does that prove ring opening?
    Answer and reasoning

    No. A closed ring can slide off a free linear end. End-blocked linear DNA and ring-interface mutants would help distinguish loss by sliding from opening.

  12. A DNA-binding protein has Kd = 20 nM. At free protein 60 nM, compute occupied fraction. What is occupancy if Kd increases to 60 nM?
    Answer and reasoning

    60/(20+60)=0.75; mutant 60/(60+60)=0.50. Assumes independent equivalent sites and known free, not total, protein concentration.

  13. The fraction of label in small chains changes from 80% immediately after pulse to 15% after chase. Total recovered label is constant. What does this support and what is not established?
    Answer and reasoning

    It supports conversion of many small labeled chains into larger covalent products. It does not on its own assign them to leading or lagging templates or prove which joining enzyme acted.

  14. Pulse-chase small-chain radioactivity falls from 600 to 60 units, but long-chain radioactivity stays at 400 units. Is joining established?
    Answer and reasoning

    No. Total recovery fell from 1,000 to 460 units; loss or degradation could explain disappearance. A transfer of signal to long chains and matched recovery controls are needed.

  15. An extract synthesizes DNA normally but retains RNA-containing fragment junctions and seals a preformed DNA nick efficiently. Identify the likely operation impaired.
    Answer and reasoning

    Primer removal or associated processing is more plausible than ligase failure. RNA sensitivity and gap/flap assays could further localize the defect.

  16. A labeled 20-nt oligo and a 35-nt oligo form a nick on a common template. After ligase treatment and denaturation a labeled 55-nt product appears. What is demonstrated?
    Answer and reasoning

    Covalent joining of the two chains, because base pairing alone would not retain the 55-nt product under denaturing conditions. No new DNA nucleotide need be incorporated.

  17. A 6,300-bp circular DNA has h=10.5 and Lk=570. Calculate relaxed linking number, ΔLk, and σ.
    Answer and reasoning

    Lk0=6,300/10.5=600; ΔLk=−30; σ=−30/600=−0.05. The molecule is negatively supercoiled relative to that reference.

  18. The same DNA is acted on by an enzyme that increases Lk by 2 per cycle. How many net cycles achieve Lk=600?
    Answer and reasoning

    (600−570)/2=15 net cycles. Total cycles may exceed this if reverse events occur.

  19. A constrained nonrotating fork opens 2,100 bp with h=10.5 and no topoisomerase. Estimate the winding that must be accommodated.
    Answer and reasoning

    2,100/10.5=200 turns. This is a topological bookkeeping estimate; it does not mean exactly 200 visible loops.

  20. A mutant replisome exposes template at 280 nt/s and copies the lagging template at 220 nt/s for 8 seconds. Calculate additional uncopied template.
    Answer and reasoning

    (280−220) × 8 = 480 nt under the stated simple model. Initial exposure and primer/maturation contributions are not included.

  21. A single linear stretch consists of 36 separate adjacent lagging fragments, all processed to pure DNA. How many internal nicks remain before joining?
    Answer and reasoning

    35 internal boundaries. Ends connecting to other stretches are excluded; if the question counts all chromosomal joins, additional information is needed.

  22. Two equal-speed forks travel 15,000 bp each; mean lagging fragment spacing is 150 nt. Estimate total productive lagging starts.
    Answer and reasoning

    Each fork makes approximately 15,000/150=100 starts; two forks give 200. This excludes leading initiation and assumes exact average spacing.

  23. A helicase mutant unwinds short unconstrained substrates normally but replication of closed DNA stops. Addition of topoisomerase restores copying. Interpret.
    Answer and reasoning

    The rescue supports a limitation caused by topology rather than intrinsic helicase motor failure under these conditions. Confirm that added protein restores topology and does not supply another missing activity.

  24. A catalytic topoisomerase inhibitor and a cleavage-complex stabilizer both reduce DNA synthesis. Which additional readout separates the mechanisms?
    Answer and reasoning

    Measure persistent protein-linked DNA breaks alongside topology and synthesis. A poison can stabilize cleavage complexes, whereas catalytic blockade may inhibit strand passage without generating the same trapped intermediate burden.

  25. A proofreading-defective system makes long DNA and has normal nick sealing but has a tenfold increase in mismatches. A ligase-defective system retains nicks with near-normal initial base selection. Why can both be lethal?
    Answer and reasoning

    Sequence fidelity and covalent chromosome integrity are distinct requirements. Long products or high incorporation alone are not measures of viable completed chromosomes.

  26. If a primase is inhibited just after initiation, which strand-specific signal should decrease first in an ideal uncoupled fork? What caveat applies?
    Answer and reasoning

    New lagging-fragment starts should drop first because repeated priming is necessary. Leading extension can temporarily continue from its existing end, but real coupling and stress responses may inhibit both.

20. Assertion–reason, matching and multiple-statement practice

Assertion–reason

Determine whether each statement is true and whether the reason explains the assertion.

  1. Assertion: A leading strand is normally made continuously. Reason: Its parental template is read toward the advancing fork in the 3′→5′ direction.
    Answer and reasoning

    Both true; reason explains assertion. The existing 3′ end can follow newly exposed template.

  2. Assertion: DNA ligase fills a five-nucleotide gap. Reason: Ligase forms phosphodiester bonds at adjacent suitable ends.
    Answer and reasoning

    Assertion false, reason true. Missing nucleotide positions require synthesis before ordinary nick sealing.

  3. Assertion: A primase defect can preferentially reduce new lagging starts after initiation. Reason: Each ordinary Okazaki fragment needs a fresh starting chain.
    Answer and reasoning

    Both true; reason explains assertion. Secondary fork effects may subsequently alter both strands.

  4. Assertion: Every type I topoisomerase changes Lk by exactly one per cleavage event. Reason: Type I enzymes cleave a single DNA strand.
    Answer and reasoning

    Assertion false, reason true. Type IB controlled rotation can relax multiple turns in one cleavage event.

  5. Assertion: A closed circular duplex has invariant Lk in the absence of strand passage or breakage. Reason: Tw and Wr can redistribute without changing their sum.
    Answer and reasoning

    Both true; the reason expresses the topological constraint.

Integrative questions

  1. Evaluate statements: I. RPA binds ssDNA. II. PCNA directly catalyzes DNA polymerization. III. RFC loads PCNA. IV. Ligase normally incorporates dNTPs. Which are correct?
    Answer and reasoning

    I and III. PCNA is an accessory clamp, while ligase seals adjacent ends without adding nucleotide positions.

  2. Match each function with primase, ligase, helicase, topoisomerase: (a) temporary starter, (b) nick sealing, (c) directional unwinding, (d) strand passage/relaxation.
    Answer and reasoning

    a primase; b ligase; c helicase; d topoisomerase. These are distinct tasks even though their loss can reduce replication.

  3. Evaluate statements: I. A gap differs from a nick. II. A flap can arise by strand displacement. III. A closed sliding clamp cannot slide off a free linear end. IV. Nascent fragment RNA is normally removed.
    Answer and reasoning

    I, II and IV. A ring can leave a linear duplex through its free end without opening.

  4. Match the assay to the inferred operation: tail-dependent duplex separation, preprimed single-hit length distribution, covalent joining after denaturation, change in closed-circle topoisomers.
    Answer and reasoning

    Helicase; processivity; ligase; topoisomerase, respectively. Include controls for energy, rebinding, end chemistry and backbone integrity.

  5. A polymerase can extend DNA at 5′→3′ and remove residues at 3′→5′. A student claims this violates antiparallel copying. Correct the claim.
    Answer and reasoning

    The directions refer to different chemical operations. Addition extends the available 3′ end; proofreading hydrolyzes residues from that same end. Neither entails 3′→5′ synthesis.
