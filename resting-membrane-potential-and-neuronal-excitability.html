1. The biological problem: a neuron poised to respond

A neuron must solve a particular problem of communication. Its dendrites and soma receive signals that vary in size and timing, yet the cell can convert an appropriate combination of those signals into an electrical impulse. The conversion requires a stable starting condition. Before a sensory stimulus opens a channel, before a transmitter binds a receptor, and before a voltage-sensitive sodium channel enters its rapidly activating state, the neuron already possesses a potential difference across its surface. That difference is the resting membrane potential. It is not the consequence of the next signal; it is part of the physical preparation that makes the signal possible.

A voltmeter does not measure an absolute voltage at one point in space. It compares the electric potential at two points. Neurophysiologists ordinarily define the membrane voltage as the intracellular electric potential minus the extracellular electric potential, with the external reference taken as zero. A reading of −70 mV therefore means that the interior has an electric potential seventy thousandths of a volt below the local extracellular solution. If a second neuron rests at −55 mV, its interior remains negative relative to its exterior, but its membrane is more depolarized than the first. The word depolarization describes a change toward a less negative or more positive voltage, not an obligation to reach zero. A change from −70 to −60 mV is depolarization; a change from −70 to −80 mV is hyperpolarization.

The resting value is an operating condition, not an immutable electrical identity of all neurons. Different neuronal classes express different mixtures of channels, transporters and receptors. A cell's recent activity, extracellular environment and metabolic state also affect its baseline. Some neurons fluctuate continually because of spontaneous channel openings and incoming synaptic signals. Others undergo spontaneous oscillations or rhythmic discharge, so a particular stable resting potential may be difficult to identify under physiological conditions. In a controlled preparation, an investigator usually defines the baseline during an interval without deliberately applied current or a strong imposed stimulus. This definition makes it possible to quantify responses relative to the starting voltage.

An excitable cell can change its membrane potential rapidly in response to a stimulus, often using voltage-dependent positive feedback to generate a regenerative event. However, a membrane potential itself does not establish that a cell produces an action potential. Many non-neuronal cells maintain a potential difference and use electrochemical gradients for transport without generating an ordinary fast neuronal impulse. Likewise, a neuron can respond electrically below its firing threshold. The amplitude of a small response may encode stimulus strength, while the subsequent regenerative signal may encode strength through firing frequency or timing. The distinction separates the present chapter, which concerns the baseline and passive responses, from the next chapter, which concerns the dynamics of impulse generation.

A helpful first experiment in thought is to imagine a membrane that encloses a potassium-rich solution and sits in a sodium-rich bath. If the membrane were completely impermeable to ions, the concentration differences would not automatically produce a predictable negative voltage. Concentration gradients provide potential free energy, but an electrical separation requires some route for charges to redistribute. Now introduce a channel that allows potassium to cross. Potassium can leave the interior, and impermeant negatively charged material cannot follow through that channel. The resulting charge separation creates an electrical force opposing further outward movement. The final voltage depends on how these forces balance. If another pathway admits sodium, the balance changes again. This sequence, rather than a memorized claim that potassium is negative or that sodium is positive, explains why the composition and properties of a membrane must be considered together.

The rest of this chapter develops three layers of explanation. The first is the thermodynamic prediction of the voltage at which one ion has no net passive movement. The second is the steady voltage produced when several passive ionic pathways operate simultaneously and active transport maintains the concentration differences. The third is the way membrane capacitance, resistance and cell geometry determine how quickly and how far a local signal travels. A student who can move between these layers can analyze a problem whether it is expressed as a table of concentrations, a current–voltage graph, a drug experiment or a voltage recording. In each case the key is to identify which quantities are fixed and which are permitted to change.

2. Ionic asymmetry and the electrically insulating membrane

The aqueous environment of a mammalian neuron is divided by a lipid bilayer into cytosol and extracellular fluid. Both compartments contain water, dissolved salts, proteins and other molecules. A dissolved salt such as potassium chloride dissociates into oppositely charged ions. Because the central portion of a lipid bilayer is hydrophobic, a hydrated ion cannot ordinarily pass through it at a substantial rate unless a protein supplies a suitable pathway. Ion channels provide such pathways. Some have a narrow selectivity filter that favors particular ionic species; some allow several cations through; others preferentially conduct chloride. Their presence converts a highly resistive lipid surface into a selectively permeable membrane.

A useful representative set of concentrations for calculations in a mature mammalian neuron is 140 mM potassium inside and 4 mM outside, 12 mM sodium inside and 145 mM outside, and 10 mM chloride inside and 120 mM outside. These are illustrative values, not measurements from a universal neuron. The actual intracellular chloride concentration, in particular, can differ markedly among cell types and developmental stages. Free cytosolic calcium is usually around a tenth of a micromolar in a resting cell, whereas extracellular free calcium is commonly around one to two millimolar. Calcium is an especially instructive case because a large concentration difference can exist despite the small contribution of its closed channels to resting voltage. A concentration gradient becomes an electrical influence only when a corresponding transport pathway permits charge movement.

Concentration describes an amount per volume rather than a total amount. A small extracellular compartment can contain fewer potassium ions than a large cytoplasm even if both have the same concentration. The distinction matters when neurons fire repeatedly into a confined extracellular space. A modest absolute release of potassium may noticeably raise its local external concentration, altering the gradient for subsequent potassium movement. In contrast, an experimental bath of large volume, continuously perfused with fresh solution, can hold the external concentration close to a chosen value. A model that assumes fixed concentrations can be excellent for one preparation and misleading for another. The assumptions must be stated before numerical predictions are interpreted.

The major ionic asymmetries have biochemical causes. The sodium–potassium ATPase transports sodium out and potassium in, using ATP to perform movement against the prevailing electrochemical gradients. Other transporters use gradients established by this pump to move solutes. In mature neurons, chloride may be kept relatively low by potassium–chloride cotransport; in developing neurons or particular neuronal compartments, other transport processes can support a higher chloride concentration. Calcium pumps, sodium–calcium exchangers and intracellular stores maintain low free cytosolic calcium. Negatively charged intracellular proteins and metabolites also influence ionic composition and osmotic balance, but they cannot by themselves explain the exact value of a neuronal resting potential.

The interior and exterior of a resting neuron are each approximately electroneutral in bulk. The statement that a cell is negative inside refers to the potential difference and the tiny excess of charge near the inner membrane surface, not to a cytoplasm containing large amounts of uncompensated negative charge. Nearly every intracellular positive charge is balanced by a negative charge elsewhere in the intracellular fluid. The electrical separation required to charge a membrane is minuscule compared with the total number of dissolved ions. This distinction is fundamental: membrane voltage can change by tens of millivolts while the bulk sodium and potassium concentrations barely change over a single brief event.

The amount of charge needed for a voltage change can be estimated by treating the membrane as a capacitor. Consider a spherical soma of radius ten micrometres. The area is four times pi times the radius squared, approximately 1,257 square micrometres or 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ square centimetres. A specific capacitance of one microfarad per square centimetre gives a total capacitance near 12.6 picofarads. Changing its voltage by 70 mV corresponds to approximately 8.8 × 10⁻¹³ coulombs of separated charge. Dividing by the elementary charge, 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, gives roughly 5.5 million elementary charges. Yet the same sphere contains about 3.5 × 10¹¹ potassium ions if its potassium concentration is 140 mM. The charge change is equivalent to a minute fraction of that inventory. This is an idealized geometric estimate; actual dendrites increase surface area, and multiple ionic species contribute to the charge displacement. Its importance is conceptual rather than anatomical: a substantial electrical change does not require wholesale replacement of the cell's ions.

An ion channel is not the same as a pump. A channel permits passive flux in the direction favored by electrochemical free energy, regardless of whether the channel was opened by voltage, a ligand or mechanical force. Energy may be required to regulate a channel, but ions traversing its passive pore are not actively transported uphill. A pump couples a transport cycle to an energy source such as ATP hydrolysis and can sustain transport against a gradient. Cotransporters occupy a related but distinct category, coupling one solute's downhill movement to another solute's uphill movement. When an examination question describes a pathway opening, it is essential to identify the electrochemical force before choosing the direction of ionic flow. When it describes a pump or coupled transporter, the stoichiometry and source of energy must also be considered.

Permeability must be distinguished from concentration and conductance. A membrane can contain a very high extracellular sodium concentration while exhibiting low resting sodium permeability. A permeability coefficient describes the ease with which a species crosses a specified barrier under a concentration and electrical gradient. Conductance relates current to voltage and reflects channel availability as well as the properties of the permeant ions. For a simple linear channel, its conductance is the reciprocal of its resistance. The numerical relationship between permeability and conductance is not generally a fixed unit conversion; conductance can depend on concentrations, membrane voltage and channel gating. This distinction will matter when comparing the Goldman equation, which uses permeability, with a circuit approximation, which uses conductance.

The selective nature of the resting membrane is especially important in an environment that continually changes. Neural activity transfers some potassium into extracellular spaces. Astrocytes and other cellular processes help manage this potassium, and the blood and extracellular fluid provide additional buffering and exchange. A neuron embedded in living tissue therefore does not behave like a sealed container with a permanent ion recipe. Its baseline is the outcome of cell-autonomous transport, membrane pathways and tissue-level homeostasis. We can begin with fixed concentrations for tractable calculations, then relax that assumption when predicting slower changes during sustained activity or metabolic stress.

3. Electrochemical gradients: from diffusion to electrical equilibrium

A concentration gradient gives rise to diffusion because random molecular movement tends, in the absence of other influences, to reduce differences in concentration. If a selective channel connects a region containing more potassium with a region containing less potassium, potassium ions cross in both directions, but initially more ions cross from the high concentration side. The term net flux refers to the difference between the two opposing fluxes. It is not a claim that every particle travels in one direction. Even at equilibrium, microscopic motion continues; the time-averaged difference between the opposing movements becomes zero.

Charged particles experience an additional influence from the electric field. Positive ions tend to move toward lower electric potential; negative ions tend to move toward higher electric potential. For a neuron that is negative inside, the electrical influence on potassium is inward while its concentration influence is ordinarily outward. Both influences on sodium are usually inward at rest because sodium is abundant outside and positive. For chloride, the concentration influence is often inward but the electrical influence is outward, because the negative interior repels chloride. Calcium typically has both a large inward concentration influence and an inward electrical influence. These qualitative predictions are the first step, but opposing forces cannot be ranked reliably from concentrations alone.

Thermodynamics expresses these influences using electrochemical potential. For an ionic species with activity a, charge number z and electric potential φ, the electrochemical potential per mole is the reference chemical potential plus RT ln a plus zFφ. The gas constant R is measured in joules per mole per kelvin, T is absolute temperature in kelvin, and F is the Faraday constant, approximately 96,485 coulombs per mole of elementary charge. Multiplying F by electric potential gives joules per mole because one volt is one joule per coulomb. The charge number is +1 for sodium and potassium, −1 for chloride and +2 for calcium. The logarithm contains a dimensionless activity relative to a standard state. In elementary calculations, the activity ratio is approximated by the ratio of concentrations in matching units. This is an approximation because interactions between ions can modify their effective activities.

For transfer of one mole of an ion from outside to inside, the electrochemical free-energy change is RT ln(a_in/a_out) + zF(V_in − V_out). This expression is negative when inward movement is thermodynamically favored and positive when outward movement is favored. It is zero when neither direction is favored overall. Notice that the expression includes the sign of z. A negative membrane voltage lowers the electrical part of the free energy for inward movement of a cation but raises it for inward movement of an anion. Treating all ions as if they were positively charged reverses the prediction for chloride and is a frequent source of mistakes.

Imagine potassium with a concentration ratio of thirty-five inside to one outside. At zero membrane voltage, its chemical free-energy contribution for entering the cell is positive, so net passive movement is outward if a potassium-selective pore is available. As the inside becomes more negative, inward movement becomes increasingly favorable electrically. At a sufficiently negative voltage, the inward electrical tendency exactly counteracts the outward chemical tendency. That voltage is the potassium equilibrium potential. If the interior becomes still more negative, potassium will enter the cell through open potassium channels even though the concentration inside is higher. This result is not a violation of diffusion; it is movement down the total electrochemical potential.

The same reasoning explains how two ions with the same charge number can favor opposite directions across one resting membrane. If potassium has an equilibrium potential near −95 mV and sodium has one near +67 mV, a membrane at −70 mV lies above the potassium equilibrium potential but below the sodium equilibrium potential. Passive potassium tends to leave and passive sodium tends to enter. The membrane has only one actual voltage at a given point, but it has multiple ion-specific equilibrium potentials. Each of these is a hypothetical voltage at which the corresponding ion would be in electrochemical equilibrium with the present concentration gradient. It is perfectly possible, indeed typical, for the actual voltage to be different from all of them.

For equilibrium to matter experimentally, the ion must have an available pathway. If all sodium channels are closed and sodium leakage is negligible, sodium may remain far from equilibrium without producing a measurable passive sodium current. A large driving force is analogous to a large pressure difference across a closed valve: it predicts a potential movement, not an actual flow rate. The analogy is limited because electrical currents depend on charge, and channels may alter their gating in response to voltage. Nevertheless, the distinction between driving force and pathway availability is an essential guide to experimental prediction.

An equilibrium potential is thermodynamic; a reversal potential is operationally measured as the voltage at which current through a specified channel or population of channels reverses direction. For a truly selective passive channel conducting only one ionic species, the reversal potential matches that ion's equilibrium potential under the given conditions. For a channel that conducts both sodium and potassium, the reversal potential is instead a combined balance. At that voltage the total channel current is zero, while individual ions may still move in opposing charge-current directions. A zero-current point should therefore never be interpreted as proof that every permeant ion has reached electrochemical equilibrium. This distinction becomes particularly important for postsynaptic channels, whose full treatment is reserved for the synaptic transmission chapter.

One final distinction is between flux of particles and electric current. Conventional positive current represents movement of positive charge in the stated direction or negative charge in the opposite direction. Neurophysiology commonly defines outward transmembrane current as positive. Potassium leaving the cell contributes a positive outward current. Sodium entering contributes a negative inward current. Chloride entering is physically inward movement of negatively charged particles, but its conventional electrical current is positive outward. The free-energy method predicts ion movement directly; a current equation predicts movement of electrical charge. To pass from one description to the other, remember the valence of the transported species.

4. Deriving and applying the Nernst equation

At equilibrium for one permeant species, the free-energy change for passive transfer from outside to inside vanishes. Setting RT ln(a_in/a_out) + zFE_ion equal to zero and rearranging gives E_ion = (RT/zF) ln(a_out/a_in). This is the Nernst equation using the convention of intracellular voltage relative to extracellular voltage. The minus sign created in rearrangement is absorbed by reversing the activity ratio inside the logarithm. The result predicts a voltage, not a current or a rate of approach to equilibrium. It requires a nonzero valence and a specified concentration ratio, and its physical derivation assumes thermodynamic equilibrium for that species.

Many examination calculations use base-ten logarithms because common ratios are easier to evaluate mentally. Since the natural logarithm of x equals approximately 2.303 times log₁₀ x, the equation becomes E_ion = (2.303RT/zF) log₁₀([ion]_out/[ion]_in) in the ideal concentration approximation. At 37 °C, T is approximately 310 K. Substituting R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹ yields 0.0615 V for 2.303RT/F, or 61.5 mV. Therefore the convenient mammalian-temperature form is E_ion = (61.5 mV/z) log₁₀(out/in). At 25 °C the coefficient is approximately 59.2 mV, and at 20 °C it is about 58.2 mV. Use the specified temperature rather than automatically inserting 61.5 mV for every animal or isolated preparation.

A tenfold gradient is a useful reference. For a monovalent cation at 37 °C, an outside-to-inside concentration ratio of ten corresponds to +61.5 mV; a ratio of one tenth corresponds to −61.5 mV. For chloride the signs reverse because z is negative. A divalent cation gives half the voltage for the same ratio because the electrical work per mole is doubled. This does not mean calcium necessarily has a smaller equilibrium potential than sodium. Its physiological concentration ratio may be thousands to tens of thousands, and the logarithm of that ratio can outweigh the factor of one half.

Using the representative concentrations in the previous section, the potassium equilibrium potential is 61.5 log₁₀(4/140) mV. The ratio is approximately 0.02857, and its common logarithm is −1.544. Multiplication gives approximately −95.0 mV. Sodium gives 61.5 log₁₀(145/12) mV, or approximately +66.6 mV. Chloride gives −61.5 log₁₀(120/10) mV, approximately −66.4 mV. For calcium, choose outside free calcium of 1.2 mM and inside free calcium of 0.0001 mM; the ratio is 12,000. Its logarithm is 4.079, and multiplication by 30.75 mV yields approximately +125.4 mV. Concentrations must first be converted into the same units. Confusing nanomolar calcium with millimolar calcium can change the calculated result by tens of millivolts.

Suppose extracellular potassium rises from 4 to 8 mM, with intracellular potassium initially fixed at 140 mM and temperature unchanged. The new potassium equilibrium potential is 61.5 log₁₀(8/140), approximately −76.4 mV. The change is +18.5 mV, which is exactly the coefficient multiplied by log₁₀ 2 within rounding. A doubling of the external concentration produces the same change in equilibrium potential regardless of the starting external concentration if the other quantities are fixed. This is a logarithmic relationship. Adding 4 mM to a solution initially containing 4 mM is a doubling, while adding 4 mM to a solution containing 40 mM is only a ten percent increase. The two additions do not produce equal changes in equilibrium voltage. Exam questions often test this distinction using graphs of membrane potential against either potassium concentration or its logarithm.

A reversible calculation can infer a concentration ratio from an ion equilibrium potential. If a monovalent potassium-selective pathway reverses at −90 mV at 37 °C, then log₁₀(out/in) is approximately −90/61.5 = −1.463. The outside-to-inside ratio is 10 raised to that power, approximately 0.0344. If intracellular potassium is known to be 140 mM, the external concentration inferred under the assumptions is about 4.8 mM. However, if the measured current comes from a mixed-selectivity channel, this inference is not valid. The investigator would need independent selectivity information before treating the zero-current point as the potassium equilibrium potential. A mathematical calculation cannot compensate for a wrongly identified biological pathway.

Another way to use the Nernst equation is to hold a concentration ratio fixed and vary temperature. Increasing absolute temperature increases the equilibrium voltage magnitude because the chemical contribution of the concentration difference is proportional to RT. The sign does not change unless the concentration ratio or valence changes. The thermal dependence of a living neuron's resting potential is more complex because permeability, transporter activity and concentrations may themselves change with temperature. A question that specifies fixed concentrations and a purely selective membrane is isolating the Nernst prediction. A question describing an intact animal undergoing a temperature change may require additional physiological reasoning.

Consider the limit of equal activities inside and outside. The logarithm is zero and the equilibrium potential is zero regardless of valence. If the actual membrane voltage is −70 mV, however, an ion with equal concentrations is not necessarily at equilibrium; the electrical gradient remains. For a cation, opening the channel can generate an inward current despite the absence of a concentration difference. Conversely, unequal concentrations can coexist with zero net passive flux if an opposing voltage is established. Equilibrium is not a synonym for equal concentration. This is a particularly important idea for students who have learned diffusion using uncharged solutes.

The Nernst equation should not be used to conclude that resting voltage equals the equilibrium potential of the most abundant ion. The equation contains a single concentration ratio and no membrane permeability or conductance terms. When the membrane is permeable almost exclusively to potassium, the resting potential may approximate the potassium equilibrium voltage. When sodium and chloride pathways are appreciable, their currents modify the actual voltage. When the pump exports net charge, it can make an additional contribution. The equation remains useful in the more complex case because it supplies the reversal potential for each individual selective pathway, but a further current-balance model is needed to obtain the steady membrane voltage.

5. From equilibrium voltages to a balance of ionic currents

The Nernst equation gives a reference point for a single ionic species. A resting neuron, however, is open to several ionic pathways, and each of them tends to move the voltage toward a different value. To understand the combined effect, begin by asking what would happen if the actual voltage were briefly displaced from its resting value while the concentrations and channel populations remained fixed. The displacement would change the free energy for ion movement, hence the current through available channels. If the sum of these currents tends to return the voltage toward the starting value, that value is a stable operating point. If the currents instead amplify the displacement, a different form of electrical behavior becomes possible.

A convenient approximation for an open channel population is I_ion = g_ion(V_m − E_ion), where I_ion is the conventional outward current, g_ion is its conductance, V_m is the membrane voltage and E_ion is its reversal potential. Voltage is measured in volts, conductance in siemens and current in amperes. A practical neuronal unit combination is nanosiemens multiplied by millivolts, which yields picoamperes. The voltage difference V_m − E_ion is commonly called the driving force for the current. The expression assumes a linear current–voltage relation over the range of interest and a fixed conductance. Channels can rectify or change their gating, so the approximation is not a universal description of microscopic channel behavior. Its strength is to expose the sign and approximate size of currents and to make the logic of current balance transparent.

Consider a neuron at −70 mV with E_K = −95 mV and a potassium conductance of 4 nS. The potassium driving force is +25 mV, and the resulting current is +100 pA, which corresponds to net outward movement of positive charge. For sodium with E_Na = +67 mV and a conductance of 0.5 nS, the driving force is −137 mV and the current is −68.5 pA. The smaller sodium conductance still generates a substantial current because sodium lies very far from equilibrium at rest. A third conductance, for chloride, with E_Cl = −66 mV and g_Cl = 1 nS would generate −4 pA at this voltage. Because chloride carries negative charge, a negative conventional outward current here corresponds to net outward movement of chloride particles, not inward movement. The total of these currents is +27.5 pA. If these were the only pathways, −70 mV would not be the zero-current resting voltage, and the positive total outward current would make the membrane more negative.

The membrane does not maintain an arbitrary voltage simply because individual ions have concentration gradients. In a single electrically uniform compartment with no imposed current, a stable resting voltage requires the sum of transmembrane currents, including electrogenic transport if important, to be zero. If a neuron is spatially extended and local axial current enters a region, that local region can have a nonzero net membrane current even when its voltage is steady; axial current supplies the balance. In an isopotential model, however, steady voltage and zero net current are directly linked. This is a conservation-of-charge statement rather than an assertion that every ion has stopped moving. Sodium can enter continuously through a leak pathway while potassium leaves continuously through another, with opposing electrical currents. At the same time the sodium–potassium pump can move ions uphill to maintain long-term balance.

For a simple parallel conductance model with three ions and no pump current, setting the sum of the individual currents to zero gives g_K(V_m − E_K) + g_Na(V_m − E_Na) + g_Cl(V_m − E_Cl) = 0. Collecting terms containing V_m gives the steady voltage as (g_K E_K + g_Na E_Na + g_Cl E_Cl)/(g_K + g_Na + g_Cl). It is a conductance-weighted average of reversal potentials. The equation is especially useful for reasoning about the opening of a channel because increasing its conductance increases its relative weight in the average. The final voltage lies between the smallest and largest reversal potentials if all conductances are positive and no independent current source is present. A result outside that interval signals an algebraic error or a missing current source.

Use the conductances from the preceding example. The numerator is 4(−95) + 0.5(67) + 1(−66), giving −412.5 nS·mV. The denominator is 5.5 nS, yielding −75 mV. Check the answer by computing the currents at −75 mV: potassium gives +80 pA, sodium gives −71 pA and chloride gives −9 pA, which sum to zero. This verification is more instructive than merely trusting the arithmetic. It confirms that a physically interpretable steady state has been found, and it demonstrates that the voltage is far from the sodium equilibrium potential despite the substantial sodium current.

Now increase sodium conductance from 0.5 to 1 nS while retaining the other conductances. The numerator becomes −379 nS·mV and the denominator becomes 6 nS, yielding about −63.2 mV. Increasing the contribution of sodium brings the steady voltage closer to +67 mV. It also increases the total conductance, reducing membrane resistance. A conductance change therefore has two logically separate consequences: it can shift the baseline voltage and it can alter the response to an additional imposed current. A small neuronal response is not determined solely by how close the baseline lies to threshold. The conductance of the membrane determines how much further voltage change a particular input can produce.

At the reversal potential of a channel, increasing that channel's conductance need not change the baseline immediately. Suppose a cell rests at −70 mV and a ligand opens chloride channels whose reversal potential is also −70 mV. At the moment of opening, the chloride current is approximately zero. But the new conductance lowers input resistance and provides a route that counteracts subsequent voltage excursions. A depolarizing input now causes a larger outward chloride current than it did before the channels opened. The resulting reduction in the response is called shunting. This elementary mechanism explains why a change in excitability may occur without a visible hyperpolarization. A full synaptic account belongs to the later chapter; for now the circuit principle is enough.

The relative positions of the reversal potentials also allow rapid qualitative predictions. If a membrane at −70 mV opens a conductance reversing at −40 mV, that conductance tends to depolarize the cell. If it opens a conductance reversing at −90 mV, the immediate effect tends to be hyperpolarization. If it opens a conductance reversing exactly at −70 mV, its immediate effect on baseline may be negligible. These statements refer to the direction of the direct ionic current before changes in other conductances occur. In a living neuron, voltage-sensitive channels may react to the resulting change and amplify or oppose the initial movement. The examination habit to cultivate is to predict the first effect from the given reversal potential and then consider secondary feedback separately.

6. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz voltage equation

Although the conductance model is useful, it does not derive the relationship between membrane permeability, the concentration profile within a membrane and current. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation supplies a different approximation that explicitly includes ionic concentrations and relative permeabilities. Its historical importance lies in helping researchers move beyond a simple view in which one ionic gradient alone fixed the resting voltage. Goldman developed a constant-field description of ionic transport through membranes in 1943. Hodgkin and Katz applied related reasoning to the squid giant axon in 1949, where changing the ionic composition of the external medium helped distinguish resting electrical behavior from the active voltage excursion. The resulting voltage equation provides a standard bridge from thermodynamics to experimentally accessible ionic predictions.

For a membrane permeable to potassium, sodium and chloride, and using inside-minus-outside voltage, the usual monovalent form is V_m = (RT/F) ln[(P_K[K]_out + P_Na[Na]_out + P_Cl[Cl]_in)/(P_K[K]_in + P_Na[Na]_in + P_Cl[Cl]_out)]. P denotes the permeability coefficient for the indicated ion. Concentrations in each sum must use matching units. If permeability is measured in centimetres per second and concentration in millimoles per litre, each term in numerator and denominator has the same combined dimensions. Their ratio is dimensionless, as required for a logarithm. Since only relative permeabilities matter for this equation, all the coefficients may be divided by the potassium permeability when that coefficient is nonzero.

The chloride terms appear in the opposite orientation from the cation terms. That is not a convention chosen to make the answer negative. It arises because chloride has a charge number of minus one and experiences the electric field in the opposite direction. In the chloride-only limit, the Goldman equation reduces to (RT/F) ln([Cl]_in/[Cl]_out), which is exactly the Nernst expression for chloride. The potassium-only and sodium-only limits similarly reproduce their Nernst equations. These limiting cases are excellent safeguards when reconstructing the equation under examination pressure. An expression that fails one of these tests cannot be the correct form for the chosen voltage convention.

The constant-field derivation assumes that the electric field is uniform across the membrane in the model, that ionic movement can be described by diffusion and electrical drift, and that each ionic species contributes independently under the relevant conditions. It treats the specified concentrations or activities at the boundaries as fixed. The usual voltage equation is obtained by requiring zero total passive ionic current, not by requiring zero flux of every individual ion. Its familiar algebraic form assumes monovalent ions. The model abstracts away many molecular details of ion channels, such as saturable binding, ion–ion interactions within a pore and channel-specific rectification. These simplifications are reasons to use the expression thoughtfully, not reasons to dismiss it. The model is valuable precisely because it predicts many physiological trends with a small number of measurable or inferable parameters.

The relation to the Nernst equation becomes clear in a short conceptual derivation. Under the constant-field approximation the passive current of a monovalent cation is proportional to P times V_m multiplied by a ratio containing the inside concentration minus the outside concentration multiplied by exp(−FV_m/RT), divided by 1 − exp(−FV_m/RT). For a monovalent anion the exponential changes sign because the valence is reversed. Rewriting the anion expression with the same denominator reverses the inside and outside roles in the numerator. The common voltage-dependent factors can then be removed from the zero-total-current condition. Solving the remaining equality for the exponential of the membrane voltage gives the ratio of permeability-weighted concentrations displayed above. At V_m = 0, the apparent zero divided by zero in the current expression is resolved by its continuous limit; it does not mean that diffusion ceases when voltage is zero.

Consider an illustrative neuron at 37 °C with the earlier concentrations and the following permeability ratios: P_K = 1, P_Na = 0.04 and P_Cl = 0.10. The numerator is 1(4) + 0.04(145) + 0.10(10) = 10.8. The denominator is 1(140) + 0.04(12) + 0.10(120) = 152.48. The ratio is approximately 0.07083. Using the base-ten form, 61.5 log₁₀(0.07083) gives approximately −70.7 mV. Compare this with E_K ≈ −95 mV and E_Na ≈ +67 mV. The resting potential is much closer to the potassium equilibrium potential than to sodium's because potassium permeability dominates, yet the sodium contribution moves it significantly toward more positive values. Chloride provides a further contribution determined by both its concentration gradient and its permeability.

The same model allows direct prediction when extracellular potassium is increased to 8 mM. Holding every other quantity fixed gives numerator 14.8 and denominator 152.48, for a voltage near −62.3 mV. The resting voltage shifts by about +8.4 mV in this example, not the +18.5 mV predicted for E_K alone. The difference occurs because the other permeabilities contribute to the numerator and denominator, reducing the fractional change in the weighted ratio when potassium alone is altered. If sodium and chloride permeabilities were negligible, the voltage change would approach the full Nernst shift. Therefore an experimental plot of resting voltage against log external potassium can help reveal how strongly other pathways influence the resting state.

What if sodium permeability is doubled while the original ion concentrations and other permeability coefficients are restored? P_Na becomes 0.08. The numerator is 4 + 11.6 + 1 = 16.6, and the denominator is 140 + 0.96 + 12 = 152.96. The voltage is approximately −59.3 mV. This shift is not caused by a change in the equilibrium potential of sodium; the sodium concentration ratio has not changed. It is caused by an increase in the relative contribution of a pathway already far from equilibrium at rest. A question that holds concentrations fixed while altering a permeability is testing this distinction.

Suppose instead that every permeability doubles in proportion, including potassium, sodium and chloride. Both weighted sums double, so the ratio remains unchanged. The predicted passive steady voltage is identical. This mathematical invariance does not mean that the absolute rates of ionic movement remain identical. In the underlying current model the currents scale with permeability, and the membrane may have lower effective resistance. Moreover, a pump would need to support the larger ongoing ionic exchanges if the gradients are to remain steady over time. The voltage equation predicts one particular property, the passive zero-current voltage; it does not determine energetic cost or time course from the same ratio alone.

An illustrative calculation also exposes why a minor permeability coefficient can have an important effect. In the preceding example P_Na is only four percent of P_K, but the term P_Na[Na]_out is 5.8, larger than the potassium term of 4 in the numerator. The contribution of a species depends not only on the coefficient but on the concentration multiplying it. In the denominator, potassium overwhelmingly dominates because intracellular potassium is high. The resulting asymmetric influence explains why a small resting sodium permeability has a substantial effect on the voltage even though potassium remains the dominant permeant species by coefficient. It is misleading to look at percentages of permeabilities without looking at the associated concentration gradients.

The Goldman and conductance models should not be combined by substituting permeability for conductance in a weighted average of reversal potentials. The Goldman equation contains concentration-weighted sums inside a logarithm; the conductance model contains voltage-weighted sums outside a logarithm. They can both describe the same biological system under suitable approximations, but their parameters and assumptions differ. When a question gives conductances and reversal potentials and explicitly assumes a linear current relation, use the conductance balance. When it gives concentrations and relative permeabilities and invokes the constant-field assumption, use Goldman. When it asks for the equilibrium potential of one ion, use Nernst. Choosing an equation from its input quantities and assumptions is more reliable than memorizing superficial keywords.

Finally, the ordinary three-ion Goldman voltage equation omits electrogenic pump current and may omit other relevant species. A membrane in which substantial divalent calcium current contributes to the resting balance cannot generally be treated by inserting calcium into the same monovalent weighted formula. The calcium current must be described with its own valence-dependent relation or an appropriate conductance approximation, and the total current condition then solved. Similarly, a large independent pump current may shift the resting voltage away from the passive zero-current prediction. These qualifications are important for biological interpretation but do not demand a more specialized derivation at this stage. The governing principle remains current balance, and the equation is a particular tractable implementation of it.

7. Resting channels and the steady-state role of the sodium–potassium pump

The potassium gradient alone cannot produce a steady membrane voltage unless potassium is able to move through the resting membrane. In many neurons, resting potassium permeability arises substantially from potassium channels that are open over the relevant voltage range. Some belong to families commonly described as background or leak channels, including two-pore-domain potassium channels. Other potassium conductances, such as inward rectifiers, can contribute in particular cells. The term leak should not imply accidental membrane damage or absence of regulation. Channel proteins may be sensitive to pH, membrane tension, cellular signaling or other physiological conditions. Their activity is often part of how a neuron adjusts its resting voltage and excitability.

A potassium channel is selective because its molecular pore provides an energetically favorable route for potassium relative to other ions. Sodium is smaller in bare ionic radius, yet many potassium channels strongly favor potassium because selectivity depends on the energetic cost of shedding surrounding water and interacting with the pore, not simply on whether an ion fits through an opening. A simple sieve analogy is therefore incomplete. The relevant outcome here is that opening a potassium-selective channel can change potassium conductance much more than sodium conductance. If the resting voltage is above E_K, increasing such conductance tends to move the voltage toward E_K and reduce input resistance.

Resting sodium entry can occur through various background pathways, which differ among neurons. A small sodium conductance matters because sodium is far from equilibrium at a typical negative resting voltage. In some neurons, hyperpolarization-activated mixed-cation currents contribute to baseline behavior and the response to hyperpolarization. A hyperpolarizing perturbation can activate such a current, which then tends to pull the voltage back toward a more positive reversal potential. Thus the simple passive picture can be modified by subthreshold voltage-dependent pathways. It is useful to distinguish a stable leak conductance from a conductance recruited by a voltage displacement. Both may be present at rest, but the latter can make measured resistance and time course depend on the voltage and the duration of the test.

Chloride conductance provides another variable influence. If E_Cl is close to the resting voltage, chloride currents may be small at baseline while strongly opposing excursions away from that voltage. If intracellular chloride is higher, E_Cl becomes more positive and opening chloride channels can depolarize the cell. If intracellular chloride is lower, E_Cl becomes more negative. The concentration ratio depends partly on coupled transport processes. A potassium–chloride cotransporter can use the outward potassium gradient to favor removal of chloride under appropriate conditions; a sodium–potassium–chloride cotransporter can support chloride accumulation using gradients of sodium and other ions. These cotransporters have coupled stoichiometries and are not equivalent to an open chloride channel. They shape the concentrations that determine E_Cl, which then determines the direction of passive chloride current through channels.

The sodium–potassium ATPase is a primary active transporter of the plasma membrane. In its usual transport cycle, it exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions for each ATP hydrolyzed. The basic conformational cycle involves binding intracellular sodium, phosphorylation of the transporter, a transition favoring outward exposure and sodium release, binding extracellular potassium, dephosphorylation and return to a state favoring potassium release inside. The protein does not have to open as a continuous pore connecting the two solutions. Instead, changes in conformation couple the accessibility and affinity of ion-binding sites to the chemical steps of the ATP-driven cycle. This alternating-access mechanism permits sustained uphill transport.

The stoichiometry creates two different contributions to neuronal electrical function. First, the transport of sodium out and potassium in maintains the chemical gradients that allow passive currents to produce the resting voltage and later support rapid signals. Second, the export of three positive charges while two enter transfers one net positive charge outward per completed cycle. The pump is therefore electrogenic. It can directly contribute an outward current and tend to make the membrane more negative. These contributions operate on overlapping but distinguishable timescales. A rapid change in the pump's current can alter the voltage before substantial concentration changes occur. Sustained failure of the pump can gradually erode the gradients, altering both equilibrium potentials and excitability.

Suppose the conductance-weighted passive resting voltage in a model is −75 mV and the total linear passive conductance is 5.5 nS. Add a constant outward pump current of 11 pA, treating concentrations as fixed during the immediate change. The total passive current at a voltage V is 5.5 nS multiplied by V − (−75 mV). To offset the added outward current, the passive current must become −11 pA. Solving for the voltage shift gives −11 pA/5.5 nS = −2 mV. The new voltage is −77 mV. Blocking the pump instantly in this ideal model would remove the direct contribution, allowing the voltage to approach −75 mV. This example separates direct electrogenesis from the much larger indirect role of the pump in sustaining ionic asymmetry. The numerical magnitude is illustrative, not a universal pump effect.

If ATP production were interrupted, the initial effect would not necessarily be an immediate collapse of the entire resting voltage. Existing sodium and potassium gradients contain stored electrochemical energy. Passive pathways would continue to draw on those gradients even if the pump stopped. Over time, however, sodium accumulation, potassium loss and accompanying changes in osmotic balance and other transport systems would alter the gradients. Depending on the preparation, voltage changes can also recruit channels, increasing ionic exchange and accelerating deterioration. The precise course depends on the cell type, the size of the extracellular compartment, alternative energy pathways and the conductances present. A qualitative answer should distinguish immediate current removal from slower gradient dissipation, rather than assert a fixed latency or voltage change for all cells.

The pump's energetic burden can be related to measurable current. An outward electrogenic pump current of 16 pA corresponds to 16 × 10⁻¹² coulombs per second. Each completed cycle produces one net elementary charge of outward movement. Dividing by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs per cycle gives approximately 1.0 × 10⁸ cycles per second, and consequently about the same number of ATP molecules hydrolyzed per second under ideal stoichiometric coupling. Sodium export is three times that cycle rate, while potassium import is twice the cycle rate. This calculation does not estimate the entire energy consumption of a neuron, which includes many additional processes. It demonstrates why electrogenic current is not identical to the total ionic throughput of the pump and why electrical balance alone cannot establish the metabolic expenditure required to maintain gradients.

At long-term steady state, one should distinguish the current balance from mass balance for each ion. Zero total current ensures no ongoing accumulation of net capacitive charge, but sodium concentration could still change if its inward and outward fluxes do not balance. Potassium concentration could change independently. A fully steady ionic state requires appropriate balance of the species-specific fluxes, often involving several transport pathways besides the sodium–potassium ATPase. For example, a pump cannot independently choose its sodium and potassium fluxes because its transport stoichiometry links them. Other transport mechanisms may participate in maintaining intracellular volume and ionic composition. The simplified resting-voltage models treat concentrations as fixed inputs; the transport network is the reason such inputs can remain approximately fixed for a useful interval.

8. The membrane as a capacitor and resistor

The equilibrium equations explain where the voltage tends to settle, but do not tell us how quickly it gets there. When a neuron receives a brief input, its voltage does not instantaneously become equal to the reversal potential of the new pathway. The membrane stores separated charge, and this stored charge must change before the voltage changes. The physical description is capacitance. A capacitor consists of conducting regions separated by an insulating material. In the cell, the conducting regions are the intracellular and extracellular electrolyte solutions and the principal insulating material is the lipid bilayer. Ion channels provide conducting pathways in parallel with this insulating structure.

Capacitance is defined as the ratio between separated charge and the voltage difference required to produce that separation: C = Q/V in the linear approximation. A farad is a coulomb per volt, a large unit compared with the capacitance of a single cell. Neuronal capacitances are therefore commonly expressed in picofarads, where one picofarad is 10⁻¹² farads. Specific membrane capacitance is capacitance per unit area; an illustrative value for an unmyelinated biological membrane is approximately 1 μF cm⁻². The distinction between specific and total capacitance is important when comparing cells of different sizes. A larger cell can have a much larger total capacitance without any change in the basic electrical properties of its membrane per square centimetre.

The parallel-plate capacitor provides an intuitive model for the effects of membrane thickness. In that ideal geometry C = εA/d, where ε denotes the permittivity of the dielectric, A is plate area and d is the distance between the plates. Larger area allows more separated charge at the same voltage. Greater separation, with all else equal, reduces capacitance. A biological membrane is more complex than a pair of uniform metal plates, but the qualitative relationship helps explain why insulation around axons can reduce effective capacitance per unit length. The cell does not need to transport ions through an intact insulating lipid layer in order to alter its stored electrical charge; movement of ions through channels or from nearby regions can change charge accumulation on its surfaces.

For a constant capacitance, differentiating Q = CV with respect to time gives I_C = C dV/dt. A given current produces a faster voltage change in a smaller capacitance. Consider a 20 pF cell receiving 100 pA of net inward charge current. If ionic leakage is temporarily negligible, its initial voltage change is 100 pA/20 pF = 5 V s⁻¹, or 5 mV ms⁻¹. If the capacitance doubles to 40 pF, the initial rate halves to 2.5 mV ms⁻¹. The numerical conversion follows from picoamperes per picofarad giving volts per second. It is easy to lose a factor of one thousand when switching between seconds and milliseconds, so keeping units visible until the final conversion is useful.

Resistance describes the difficulty of current flow. For a linear conducting path, Ohm's law states that the voltage difference across the path equals the current multiplied by the resistance. A neuronal ionic conductance is more accurately represented by a resistor in series with a voltage source that represents the ion's reversal potential. The voltage across the resistor is then V_m − E_ion rather than V_m alone. Combining resistors in parallel increases the total conductance because each open pathway supplies an additional route for movement. Thus the total passive conductance equals the sum of individual conductances, and the corresponding total resistance equals the reciprocal of that sum. Blocking an existing leak pathway can increase resistance, whereas opening another pathway lowers it. The effect of a conductance change on the baseline voltage must be analyzed separately.

When current is deliberately injected into a neuron, the experimentally useful quantity is input resistance, defined for a linear response by the steady voltage change divided by the injected current change. It represents the combined ease with which the injected current can escape from the cell or flow to other regions. For an electrically compact cell with linear passive conductances, it equals the reciprocal of the total conductance. For a branched neuron the measured input resistance depends also on the recording location and the electrical connection to dendrites and axon. In a voltage-dependent membrane, the resistance estimated near one operating point can differ from that estimated at another. The investigator must therefore specify the range of applied currents and whether the response was measured at the beginning or end of a pulse.

The useful unit relationship is that a current in nanoamperes multiplied by a resistance in megaohms gives a voltage in millivolts. Equivalently, picoamperes multiplied by megaohms must be divided by one thousand to obtain millivolts. If a 50 pA current step produces a steady 10 mV depolarization, the measured input resistance is 10 mV/0.05 nA = 200 MΩ. If another cell produces only 5 mV under the same current, its input resistance is 100 MΩ. The different responses do not establish that their resting potentials are different. They indicate that the same input current is distributed through different effective conductances or pathways. A student should not confuse a cell's voltage before the stimulus with the ratio of its response to that stimulus.

A compact linear passive model combines these ideas into a charge-balance equation. Let the outward ionic current be G(V_m − V_rest), where G is the total passive conductance and V_rest is the resting voltage in the absence of injected current. Define I_inj as positive for inward current delivered by an electrode. Then C dV_m/dt = I_inj − G(V_m − V_rest). The terms have current units. The injected current adds positive charge, while a positive outward ionic current removes positive charge. If the voltage rises above rest, the passive ionic term opposes further rise. If the voltage falls below rest, the passive term becomes negative, which contributes inward charge current in the equation. The structure of this balance creates a restoring tendency toward rest.

At steady state under a constant injected current, the capacitive term vanishes because voltage is no longer changing. The model then gives ΔV_ss = I_inj/G = I_inj R_in. During the initial response, however, the capacitor must charge and the voltage depends on time. This creates a distinction between the eventual amplitude of a response and the amount of time available to reach it. A brief pulse can end before the steady amplitude is achieved, even if its strength would produce a substantial voltage change if maintained indefinitely. The duration of a signal is therefore a genuine physiological variable, not just a secondary feature of its amplitude.

A related distinction is between capacitive and ionic current in voltage-clamp experiments. An imposed voltage step requires charge to be delivered to the membrane capacitor, producing a transient current. Once the new voltage is established, the capacitive current becomes negligible in an ideal steady step, whereas ionic currents may continue. If an experiment reports a large transient at the onset of a voltage command, that transient must not automatically be assigned to a voltage-gated ion channel. Capacitive charging, access resistance and genuine channel kinetics can each affect the measured trace. In the simplest educational analysis, investigators identify and separate the capacitance-related component before interpreting the ionic component. This separation will become especially important in the next chapter's treatment of action potential experiments.

9. The membrane time constant and graded responses

To derive the time course of a passive response, write v = V_m − V_rest. The charge-balance equation becomes C dv/dt = I_inj − v/R. For a constant current step starting at t = 0 and an initial voltage displacement of zero, the solution is v(t) = I_inj R[1 − exp(−t/RC)]. Here R is the input resistance of the compact cell model and C is its total capacitance. The exponent must be dimensionless, so RC has units of time. The product τ = RC is known as the membrane time constant. A megaohm multiplied by a picofarad gives a microsecond; a gigaohm multiplied by a picofarad gives a millisecond. In practice it is often simplest to convert to ohms and farads first, then express the answer in milliseconds.

The exponential has a straightforward biological interpretation. At the instant current begins, the voltage has not yet changed, so the additional passive outward leakage is initially zero. Much of the applied current initially charges the membrane. As the voltage rises, the passive outward current grows and an increasing fraction of the applied current is balanced by this leakage rather than by additional charging. The rate of voltage change therefore declines. At long times, the voltage approaches a steady displacement at which the extra outward current equals the applied current. This gradual approach is not evidence that the stimulus is becoming weaker; it follows from the interaction between capacitance and leakage.

After one time constant the factor 1 − exp(−1) is approximately 0.632. The voltage has reached about 63 percent of its final displacement. After two time constants it reaches approximately 86.5 percent, after three about 95 percent, and after five about 99.3 percent. The time constant is not the time required for the response to become mathematically complete. An ideal exponential approaches its asymptote indefinitely. The percentages are convenient landmarks for analyzing recorded traces and estimating how much of a response can develop during a finite input.

For a cell with R = 200 MΩ and C = 100 pF, the time constant is 20 ms. A 50 pA current pulse would eventually produce a 10 mV voltage displacement. If the pulse lasts 5 ms, the model predicts a peak displacement of 10(1 − exp(−5/20)), approximately 2.21 mV. If it lasts 20 ms, the peak is approximately 6.32 mV. A pulse lasting 60 ms reaches approximately 9.50 mV. These examples illustrate why pulse duration matters even when current amplitude is identical. In an actual neuron a larger voltage excursion may activate voltage-sensitive conductances, changing the response away from this passive prediction. The computation is valid while the specified model remains a good description.

When the current pulse ends, the membrane may remain depolarized for a short interval because charge is still stored. The passive equation with no injected current becomes dv/dt = −v/τ. Its solution is v(t) = v_0 exp(−t/τ), with t measured from the end of the pulse and v_0 equal to the displacement at that instant. The voltage falls to 36.8 percent of its initial displacement after one time constant and about 5 percent after three time constants. Charge flows through the existing conductances until the baseline balance is restored. A return to baseline does not require the pump to move back every ion involved in the preceding displacement in real time. The pump maintains the gradients, while passive currents can discharge the membrane rapidly.

The charging and discharging forms give a way to estimate capacitance from physiological recordings. If a small current pulse establishes a 10 mV steady response and the trace reaches 6.32 mV after 12 ms, a single-exponential model gives τ = 12 ms. If the current is 100 pA, input resistance is 100 MΩ. Dividing τ by R gives 0.012 s/(100 × 10⁶ Ω) = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ F or 120 pF. The estimate is model-dependent. A branched neuron can exhibit several exponential components, and voltage-dependent channels can alter the shape. A poor fit should prompt reconsideration of the model instead of forcing a single time constant onto a complex trace.

The RC model also explains the difference between the effect of changing resistance and the effect of changing capacitance. If R increases while C stays fixed, a given sustained injected current produces a larger voltage displacement and the time constant increases. The initial slope in the ideal model, I_inj/C, does not change because the capacitor initially receives the same net current. The difference emerges as leakage begins to oppose charging. If C increases while R remains fixed, the final displacement is unchanged but the initial rise is slower and the time constant increases. An examination question that compares only steady amplitudes can therefore identify changes in resistance but cannot determine capacitance without additional time-course information.

In a linear passive cell, responses to independent injected currents can be added. This property is called superposition. If one current pulse produces a 3 mV displacement at a chosen time and another, under otherwise identical conditions, produces a 4 mV displacement at that same time, the combined response is 7 mV. The result assumes linearity and fixed membrane properties. Actual synaptic inputs often change conductance rather than simply delivering a fixed current. Their currents depend on the instantaneous voltage, and opening one population of channels can change the response to another. The principle of superposition is therefore a useful reference model, not a universal rule for every pattern of synaptic activation.

Temporal summation arises when successive responses overlap because the first has not yet decayed. Suppose a small idealized voltage displacement of 4 mV is produced at time zero and decays with a time constant of 20 ms. Ten milliseconds later about 4 exp(−0.5) = 2.43 mV remains. If a second identical instantaneous displacement is added under linear conditions, the combined displacement immediately afterward becomes 6.43 mV. If the second stimulus instead occurs 60 ms later, only about 0.20 mV remains and the combined peak is near 4.20 mV. These hypothetical values demonstrate how the timing of inputs determines integration. A long membrane time constant favors persistence of previous signals, while a short time constant favors faster tracking of changing inputs and less overlap.

A graded potential is a local voltage change whose amplitude can vary with the strength, duration and spatial distribution of the initiating input. It may be depolarizing or hyperpolarizing. Its generation can begin with sensory transduction, transmitter-gated conductance, a subthreshold voltage-sensitive current or artificial electrode stimulation. A graded potential is not defined solely by being small. It is characterized by a continuous dependence on input and, in the passive regime, attenuation over distance. A sufficiently strong graded response may recruit regenerative channels and initiate an action potential. That transition does not retroactively convert every preceding graded response into an action potential; it changes the mechanism that governs the subsequent electrical trajectory.

In sensory neurons, the graded stage provides a way to represent different stimulus intensities before signals are converted into spikes. In a dendrite, the graded stage provides a way to combine signals arriving from different sources. In either case, the ability of a response to influence the action potential initiation zone depends on both its own amplitude and the electrical properties of the intervening membrane. A large local voltage response in a distant branch may exert less influence on the soma than a smaller response nearby. The time constant explains persistence at a given location; the next section adds the spatial dimension required to understand this apparent paradox.

10. Electrotonic spread and the spatial properties of neurons

The electrically compact model is valuable when voltage changes throughout a small cell are nearly uniform. Real neurons are extended structures. A dendrite may carry a local input some distance from the soma, and an axon can extend far beyond the cell body. If the membrane voltage at one point becomes more positive than the voltage at a neighboring point, current flows through the conductive cytoplasm between those regions. This longitudinal movement redistributes charge and produces a voltage change beyond the original site. At the same time, some current escapes across the membrane through available ionic pathways. The combination of longitudinal conduction, membrane leakage and membrane capacitance is called passive or electrotonic spread.

Consider a uniform cylindrical process immersed in a conductive extracellular solution whose voltage is treated as constant. Its cytoplasm offers axial resistance to longitudinal current. For a cylindrical segment of radius a and length L, the ideal axial resistance is ρ_i L/(πa²), where ρ_i is intracellular resistivity. A thicker cylinder has a larger cross-sectional area and lower axial resistance for a fixed length. The surface membrane offers resistance to transverse current. If specific membrane resistance is R_m in ohm·cm², the membrane resistance of a segment is R_m divided by its surface area 2πaL. Increasing radius therefore increases membrane area available for leakage at a given length, even as it decreases axial resistance. The combined result, rather than either effect alone, determines how far a signal spreads.

It is convenient to define axial resistance per unit length, r_i = ρ_i/(πa²), and membrane resistance parameter r_m = R_m/(2πa). Their units are different: r_i has units Ω/cm and r_m has units Ω·cm. The resistance of a length L along the cytoplasm is r_i L, whereas its membrane resistance is r_m/L. The ratio r_m/r_i therefore has units of square centimetres. Its square root is the length constant λ, expressed in centimetres or another unit of length. Substitution yields λ = sqrt(aR_m/(2ρ_i)). In terms of diameter d = 2a, the expression is λ = sqrt(dR_m/(4ρ_i)). The formula assumes a uniform cylindrical process, linear passive properties and negligible variation of extracellular potential.

For a sufficiently long passive cable under steady conditions, the voltage displacement from the baseline at distance x from a maintained boundary displacement v_0 decreases as v(x) = v_0 exp(−x/λ). The length constant is therefore the distance over which the displacement falls to approximately 36.8 percent of its value at the starting point. After two length constants only about 13.5 percent remains, after three approximately 5 percent. This is a spatial relation for steady electrotonic spread; it should not be mistaken for the temporal decay equation simply because both contain exponentials. In one case the independent variable is distance and the scale parameter is λ. In the other it is time and the scale parameter is τ.

The derivation can be understood through conservation of current. If voltage varies along the process, Ohm's law gives axial current proportional to the negative spatial voltage gradient divided by r_i. Current leaving the cytoplasm across the membrane reduces the amount available to continue along the process. At steady state the membrane leak current per unit length is v/r_m. Combining the relationship between axial current and voltage gradient with conservation of current gives d²v/dx² = (r_i/r_m)v. The exponentially decaying solution follows for a semi-infinite process with a maintained displacement at its origin and no growing voltage far away. The solution depends on the boundary conditions: a sealed distal end, a branch point or a large soma can produce departures from the simplest exponential prediction.

As an illustrative calculation, suppose the length constant of an idealized cable is 0.5 mm and a local maintained input produces a 12 mV displacement at x = 0. At a distance of 0.25 mm the displacement is 12 exp(−0.5), approximately 7.28 mV. At 0.5 mm it is approximately 4.41 mV. At 1.0 mm it is approximately 1.62 mV. If membrane resistance per unit area rises fourfold while geometry and intracellular resistivity remain fixed, the length constant doubles to 1.0 mm. The predicted displacement at 1.0 mm then becomes approximately 4.41 mV. Increasing the membrane's insulation makes passive charge redistribution more effective over distance because less current escapes through the membrane along the way.

The square-root dependence on diameter is an important quantitative result. If two ideal unmyelinated cylindrical processes have identical specific membrane resistance and cytoplasmic resistivity, increasing diameter fourfold doubles the length constant. It does not quadruple it. Increasing intracellular resistivity fourfold halves the length constant. These predictions can be checked by considering the underlying physical tendencies. A thicker process reduces the opposition to longitudinal flow relative to membrane leakage, so more of the local signal reaches a distant site. A more resistive cytoplasm limits longitudinal flow, so less of the signal spreads. A change in membrane resistance influences both length constant and time constant if specific capacitance remains unchanged.

The membrane time constant for a uniform cable is τ = R_m C_m, where C_m is specific capacitance per unit area. The area factors cancel because membrane resistance decreases with area while capacitance increases with area. This product is dimensionally equivalent to the resistance-times-capacitance expression for a compact compartment. A change in diameter alone, with the same membrane properties, can therefore alter the length constant without changing the intrinsic passive membrane time constant. Real cells may show a different measured somatic time course because geometry influences how neighboring compartments exchange charge. The distinction between a local material property and a measured whole-cell property is essential when interpreting experiments on differently shaped neurons.

Transient electrotonic signals require the membrane capacitor to charge at successive locations as axial current spreads. A brief input therefore does not simply produce a scaled copy of its original waveform at every distance. Rapid voltage changes are preferentially attenuated because distant capacitances cannot charge instantaneously. The signal may become smaller, broader and delayed at remote sites. The steady exponential formula describes a maintained displacement after sufficient time, and cannot by itself predict the exact peak amplitude or latency of a brief pulse. This limitation is useful in examination questions that show both time traces and distance-dependent amplitude plots. A steady current experiment may be appropriate for measuring λ, while a brief-pulse experiment reveals additional temporal filtering.

Dendritic branching introduces further complexity. At a branch point, axial current can enter more than one daughter branch and can also flow toward the soma. Each branch has its own diameter, length, membrane conductance and termination. A large soma can act as an electrical load because its surface area and associated conductance and capacitance provide additional places for current to flow. As a result, anatomical distance alone does not establish the influence of a synapse on the soma or axon initial segment. Two inputs located the same number of micrometres from a recording site can have quite different effects if their branches have different electrical properties. For NET-level reasoning, the important approach is to trace the available paths and identify where current is likely to be lost or shared.

Myelin modifies the electrical properties of an axonal region by insulating the membrane. Effective transverse resistance rises and effective capacitance falls in the myelinated internode. The increased resistance favors longer passive spread, while reduced capacitance means less charge is required to change the local voltage. These changes facilitate the delivery of depolarizing current to the next excitable node. It is not necessary to derive a universal change in the product RC, because both factors are altered and effective circuit geometry matters. The regenerative features of saltatory conduction, including the concentration of voltage-sensitive channels at nodes, belong to the next chapter. The present physical explanation supplies the prerequisite understanding of why insulation can improve electrical communication.

Finally, passive electrotonic spread must be distinguished from propagation of an action potential. A local graded voltage response decays with distance in a passive cable because current is lost across the membrane. A regenerative impulse is renewed along its pathway by activation of voltage-gated currents, so its amplitude can be maintained over a much longer distance in a healthy conducting axon. Passive current nevertheless contributes to propagation because it helps depolarize nearby membrane toward threshold. The two processes are not competing explanations. Electrotonic spread delivers the local influence; regenerative membrane mechanisms restore the signal when the conditions for impulse initiation are met.

11. From graded voltage to the threshold for excitation

A neuron is excitable because a sufficient disturbance of its electrical baseline can engage regenerative membrane mechanisms. In an ideal passive system, a depolarizing input simply produces a graded voltage response, which decays when the input ends. In a real neuron, a range of subthreshold voltage changes may activate some voltage-sensitive channels, and sufficiently strong depolarization may cause inward currents to increase faster than competing outward currents. The transition into this positive-feedback process is the physiological basis of threshold. Its significance is causal: threshold identifies conditions under which the membrane can generate an impulse rather than merely respond passively to a stimulus.

The voltage recorded at the onset of a spike is often called the voltage threshold. This is an experimentally useful description, but it is not necessarily a constant intrinsic value of every neuron. A commonly used teaching diagram may label rest as −70 mV and threshold as −55 mV. Those numbers describe an illustrative cell under particular conditions. Actual initiation voltage varies with neuronal type, the location of the recording electrode, channel availability and the preceding voltage history. The action potential usually initiates at an electrically specialized region such as the axon initial segment in many central neurons. A somatic recording may therefore not precisely report the voltage where the regenerative event begins.

The resting voltage and threshold difference provide one useful measure of the depolarization required for firing. However, they do not by themselves determine the magnitude of current necessary to initiate firing. A cell with higher input resistance produces a greater steady displacement for the same injected current. If two idealized neurons both rest at −70 mV and have a fixed threshold at −55 mV, they require a 15 mV depolarization. In a compact passive model with input resistance 150 MΩ, the minimum long-duration current predicted to reach that voltage is 15 mV/150 MΩ = 0.10 nA or 100 pA. If the resistance is 300 MΩ, the predicted current is 50 pA. The calculation assumes the threshold is fixed, the membrane remains linear until threshold, and the current is delivered at the relevant site. It describes a useful idealization, not an exact rheobase for every cell.

A short pulse may fail to trigger a spike even if the same current would trigger one during a long pulse. In the earlier RC model, a current I applied for duration t produces a displacement IR[1 − exp(−t/τ)]. To reach a fixed threshold displacement ΔV_th by the end of the pulse, the minimum current in this simplified model is ΔV_th/{R[1 − exp(−t/τ)]}. For R = 150 MΩ, τ = 15 ms and ΔV_th = 15 mV, the long-duration limit is 100 pA. For a 5 ms pulse, the factor in brackets is about 0.283, so the predicted current is about 353 pA. For a 30 ms pulse the factor is about 0.865, giving approximately 116 pA. The need for a stronger brief input follows from the finite time required to charge the membrane capacitance. Active membrane processes can modify real strength–duration relationships, so the passive model should not be mistaken for a universal experimental curve.

The effect of a change in potassium permeability illustrates why excitability is a multi-variable property. Suppose a resting neuron expresses a potassium-selective pathway whose reversal potential is −95 mV. Opening more of those channels will usually move the voltage toward −95 mV and lower input resistance. For a cell whose threshold otherwise remains fixed at a more positive voltage, both effects tend to reduce the ability of a given depolarizing current to initiate a spike. Blocking some of those channels can produce depolarization and increased resistance, often increasing excitability over a suitable range. However, the response depends on how the remaining voltage-sensitive channels react to the new baseline. Strong persistent depolarization can reduce the availability of channels needed for regenerative firing. A direction-of-voltage prediction is therefore not always equivalent to a prediction of long-term firing ability.

An increase in extracellular potassium provides a closely related example. With concentrations and permeabilities otherwise unchanged over a short interval, the Nernst and Goldman equations predict that resting voltage generally becomes less negative. A modest increase may bring the neuron closer to initiation and enhance responsiveness. Severe or sustained depolarization can have a different effect if sodium-channel availability declines sufficiently. A cell may become depolarized yet less capable of generating full impulses. This phenomenon exposes the limitation of the tempting slogan that any depolarization necessarily excites a neuron. The detailed time dependence of sodium-channel activation and inactivation is reserved for the action potential chapter; here it is enough to recognize that channel state and membrane voltage jointly determine responsiveness.

An experimentally distinguishable form of reduced excitability is shunting. Imagine a cell at −70 mV with an input resistance of 200 MΩ. A 60 pA inward current would produce a 12 mV steady displacement in a linear model. Now open an additional conductance of 5 nS reversing at −70 mV. The original total conductance is 5 nS, and the new total is 10 nS. Because the additional conductance reverses at the baseline, the baseline remains approximately −70 mV, but input resistance falls to 100 MΩ. The same 60 pA current now generates only 6 mV of steady depolarization. A measurement of resting voltage alone would miss this large change in responsiveness. To infer excitability from a recording, an investigator should ask whether both baseline and input resistance have changed.

Threshold also depends on the spatial organization of an input. Suppose an idealized dendritic signal must pass a distance of two length constants before reaching a site capable of spike initiation. In the steady cable model, a 20 mV local displacement would be reduced to roughly 2.7 mV at that site. If the same local displacement occurs only half a length constant away, approximately 12.1 mV reaches the site. The steady model is not an exact representation of a transient synaptic potential, and real dendrites may possess active conductances. Nevertheless, the example shows that the local amplitude alone cannot tell whether a stimulus is effective. The location, electrical path and time course must be evaluated together.

A useful examination procedure is to separate three questions. First, what is the baseline voltage before the stimulus, and how is it influenced by gradients and resting channels? Second, what voltage change will the new input produce, considering reversal potential, input resistance, capacitance and distance? Third, will the relevant voltage-sensitive mechanisms be available to amplify that change into an impulse? The first two questions belong chiefly to the present chapter; the detailed regenerative answer to the third belongs to the next. Keeping the questions distinct prevents the common mistake of treating a membrane voltage as if it were an automatic declaration that the neuron is firing.

12. How experiments establish the ionic and electrical mechanisms

Direct measurement was essential in the development of modern neurophysiology. A potential difference across a cell surface cannot be established simply by placing two electrodes in the surrounding fluid unless the resulting signal is interpreted through an appropriate model. An intracellular recording uses an electrode that accesses the inside of the cell and a reference electrode that samples the surrounding solution. The recording amplifier compares the potentials at these locations. Before the intracellular electrode enters the cell, both recording points can be in the extracellular solution and their difference can be adjusted or checked against a baseline. When the electrode accesses the cytoplasm, a stable negative voltage is observed in a typical resting neuron. Withdrawal should return the recording toward the extracellular baseline if the electrode remains functional.

This apparently straightforward measurement depends on technical controls. An intracellular electrode can disturb the membrane or leak current. A voltage recorded from a damaged cell may be less negative than its undisturbed value because the injury adds an abnormal conductance. The electrode solution and extracellular solution can also generate a liquid junction potential, an offset arising from differences in ion mobilities where solutions meet. Changes in bath composition may alter this offset. Therefore an ion substitution experiment should control or correct electrode offsets rather than attribute every measured voltage change to the cell. A stable baseline, reproducibility across cells, recovery after washout and an appropriate reference are evidence that the observation reflects physiological behavior.

The squid giant axon became a valuable preparation for classical electrophysiology partly because its unusually large diameter made internal electrical access feasible. This experimental advantage does not make its ionic concentrations identical to those of mammalian neurons. Marine invertebrates occupy different ionic environments, and experiments must be interpreted using the compositions and temperature of their own preparations. Nevertheless, the underlying principles of selective ionic permeability and electrochemical driving force apply across animals. Comparisons of nerve and muscle preparations helped establish that resting potential depends strongly on potassium gradients in many excitable cells, while different species and tissues can have different quantitative permeability ratios.

One important historical prediction arose from treating the resting membrane as predominantly potassium-selective. If intracellular potassium and temperature remain fixed, the Nernst equation predicts a straight-line relationship between E_K and the base-ten logarithm of extracellular potassium, with a slope around 61.5 mV per tenfold change at 37 °C. A preparation behaving like a potassium-only membrane should follow this relationship. If another permeant ion contributes, especially when external potassium is low, the voltage may depart from the predicted line. This deviation is not evidence against electrochemical thermodynamics. It can indicate that the assumption of exclusive potassium permeability is inadequate.

An illustrative experiment can make the distinction precise. Use an isolated neuron bathed in solutions containing 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 mM potassium, with temperature fixed at 37 °C. Suppose intracellular potassium stays at 140 mM and other concentrations are held approximately fixed. The pure potassium Nernst model predicts approximately −113.5, −95.0, −76.4, −57.9 and −39.4 mV, respectively. Under the illustrative Goldman permeability ratios used earlier, the predicted voltages are instead approximately −76.2, −70.7, −62.3, −50.8 and −36.2 mV. The two models converge more closely at higher external potassium because the external potassium term becomes a larger proportion of the weighted numerator. These numbers are calculated teaching data, not reported measurements from an actual experiment. If observed values followed the second curve, the result would support the contribution of other resting permeabilities, though it would not uniquely identify the underlying channel proteins.

Designing such an experiment involves more than deciding which ion to vary. Increasing potassium by adding potassium chloride also changes chloride concentration and osmolarity. Replacing sodium with potassium may change sodium concentration, which affects the Goldman prediction in its own right. A carefully controlled experiment should specify the replacement ion, verify the resulting solution composition, keep pH and temperature consistent and consider the permeability of any substitute ion. Repeated measurements in a control solution and recovery after returning to it help distinguish reversible effects of ion substitution from progressive injury. It is impossible to interpret a concentration manipulation as isolated unless the other variables are either genuinely controlled or explicitly included in the model.

Goldman's 1943 theoretical work supplied a physically motivated description of passive ionic currents under a constant-field approximation. Hodgkin and Katz's 1949 work on squid axons used changes in the external ionic environment to study the electrical activity of the axon, with particular importance for the role of sodium in the active response. Their observations helped establish that different ionic permeabilities dominate at different stages of excitation. The appropriate conclusion for this chapter is not that sodium has no role at rest or that potassium never contributes during a spike. Rather, the relative influence of an ion depends on both its gradient and the permeability available at the time of measurement. This interpretation makes a testable prediction: manipulations of external potassium should often have a stronger effect on a potassium-dominated resting voltage, whereas manipulations of sodium can strongly influence the active voltage excursion when sodium permeability becomes large.

Current-clamp recordings provide a complementary test of the passive membrane model. In current clamp, the investigator specifies current and observes the resulting voltage. A family of small positive and negative current steps can reveal whether steady voltage changes are proportional to current near rest. The slope of a plot of voltage change against injected current estimates input resistance. The shape of the voltage transient estimates a time constant if a simple exponential is appropriate. A departure from linearity may reveal voltage-dependent conductances or changes in the effective electrical geometry. For example, if a prolonged negative step first hyperpolarizes the membrane and then produces a gradual return toward less negative values while the current remains constant, a purely passive single-resistor model is insufficient. The trace is consistent with recruitment of an opposing current, though additional experiments would be needed to identify the responsible channel.

Voltage clamp reverses the experimental arrangement: a feedback system supplies current to control membrane voltage, while the current needed to maintain the selected voltage is recorded. To estimate a reversal potential for a particular current, investigators can compare currents across several command voltages and identify where that current changes sign. However, a whole-cell recording can contain several ionic currents simultaneously. Selective pharmacological interventions, changes in ionic composition or subtraction of appropriate control traces can help isolate a component. If the reversal of an isolated component moves when external potassium changes by the amount expected from the Nernst equation, the observation supports potassium selectivity. An isolated component that reverses between E_K and E_Na and responds to changes in both gradients may instead be a mixed-cation conductance.

Patch-clamp recording can reveal channel activity at a finer scale, sometimes resolving discrete openings of single channel proteins or small groups of channels. Such measurements help distinguish the number of channels, the probability of opening and the current through an open channel. A macroscopic conductance increase might arise from more channels being available, a greater fraction of existing channels being open, or a change in single-channel conductance. Resting voltage measurements alone cannot separate these possibilities. For exam-level analysis, the important link is that molecular channel behavior provides a physical basis for the effective conductances used in the membrane circuit model. The models summarize the collective outcome; the measurements can reveal how that outcome is produced.

A second illustrative data analysis concerns pump inhibition. Suppose a neuron initially rests at −72 mV. Immediately after selective suppression of an outward electrogenic pump current, while concentrations remain effectively unchanged, the membrane settles near −69 mV. Over a much longer period it depolarizes further and its measured equilibrium potentials change. The two phases support a distinction between direct pump current and slower gradient maintenance. They do not establish that every later change is caused by the pump alone, because secondary voltage-sensitive currents, altered volume, ion exchange with other cells and metabolic disturbances can contribute. To isolate the mechanism, the experiment should track ionic concentrations or reversal potentials, compare an appropriate vehicle control and monitor input resistance. Inference is strongest when a predicted electrical change is accompanied by the independent measurements that distinguish alternative explanations.

13. Integrating the equations and interpreting biological changes

The same observed voltage can arise from different mechanisms. Consider two compact neurons both resting at −70 mV. The first has a large potassium conductance and a small but substantial inward sodium current; the second has smaller conductances of both species in the same relative balance. Their resting voltages could coincide even though their input resistances and energetic burdens differ. A third neuron might have different chloride regulation and a stronger outward pump current, yet arrive at the same measured baseline. This non-uniqueness is a common feature of biological inverse problems. Measuring an outcome does not automatically determine every process that produced it.

A practical method for analyzing such problems is to separate equilibrium, steady state and transient response. The first concerns a species-specific electrochemical reference voltage. The second concerns the sum of currents and, over longer intervals, the maintenance of concentrations. The third concerns the change in voltage when current is temporarily unbalanced. If a problem specifies a concentration change, calculate or predict the relevant Nernst potential first. If it then asks for the resting voltage, assess the conductances or permeabilities of all pathways. If it asks how quickly a stimulus changes the voltage, use a capacitive model and identify the appropriate resistance. If it asks whether the response reaches a distant region, include geometry and cable attenuation. These steps are logically connected, but each addresses a different physical quantity.

For example, suppose a neuron at −70 mV receives a perturbation that raises extracellular potassium while an independent intervention doubles a chloride conductance reversing at −70 mV. The potassium change tends to depolarize the passive balance because E_K shifts in a positive direction. The increased chloride conductance, evaluated at the original voltage, initially contributes little current. As potassium-driven depolarization develops, however, the chloride pathway supplies a counteracting current and lowers input resistance. Consequently, the combined voltage shift may be smaller than that produced by elevated potassium alone. The total effect cannot be obtained by adding two separately measured voltage shifts if the conductance change modifies the current–voltage relation. A current-balance calculation or controlled experiment is needed for a numerical answer.

A separate physiological example concerns differences between central nervous tissue and an isolated preparation. In tissue, activity in a group of neurons can transiently increase extracellular potassium. Nearby cells sharing the extracellular environment may experience altered driving forces even without direct synaptic connections to the active cells. Astrocytic uptake, spatial redistribution and other buffering processes help limit the disturbance. In a continuously perfused isolated-cell preparation, the bath can remove extracellular potassium more rapidly and maintain a nominal concentration. An investigator studying the same neuron under these two circumstances may therefore observe different responses during sustained activity. The difference is not necessarily due to a change in the identity of its channels. The local extracellular boundary conditions may have changed.

The resting voltage also participates in the operation of secondary active transport. Sodium entering through an appropriate coupled transporter can favor the uphill transport of another solute. If the sodium gradient deteriorates, the energetic advantage available for such transport diminishes. In some transport cycles, the membrane voltage itself contributes to the free-energy balance because the net transported charge is nonzero. Thus the ion gradients underlying excitability have consequences beyond impulse initiation. Conversely, metabolic state and solute transport can modify the gradients on which electrical behavior depends. This relationship connects neural electrophysiology with the earlier study of bioenergetics and membrane transport without requiring a separate treatment of every transporter.

The experimental and mathematical models in this chapter have intentionally different domains of validity. The Nernst equation is exact in thermodynamic form for a species at electrochemical equilibrium when activities are correctly specified. The ordinary Goldman voltage equation is an approximate passive zero-current model for a set of monovalent ions under constant-field assumptions. The linear conductance balance is useful when current–voltage relations are sufficiently linear over the interval examined. The RC response adds a fixed capacitance to a linear, electrically compact model. The simple cable expression extends passive behavior to an idealized uniform process and a specified spatial boundary condition. None of these replaces the others. Selecting the appropriate level of description is part of mastering neuronal excitability.

The transition to the following chapter is now clear. Resting ion gradients store electrochemical energy, selective pathways establish a baseline voltage, and the membrane's electrical properties shape local responses. A graded disturbance can deliver depolarization to an excitable region. When voltage-sensitive inward current becomes regenerative under suitable channel conditions, the membrane no longer behaves as a purely passive resistor and capacitor. The resulting action potential uses the same physical gradients and charge conservation principles but requires time-dependent conductances. The next chapter develops that dynamical mechanism, including repolarization, refractory periods and conduction. The present foundation allows those processes to be understood as consequences of familiar electrical and thermodynamic principles rather than as a list of memorized waveform phases.

14. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Resting voltage means all ion movement stops.

Correct concept. Rest is a zero-net-current steady state in an isopotential model, not equilibrium of each ion. Opposing currents and active transport may continue.

Why the error occurs. The term resting is often confused with thermodynamic equilibrium.

Examination reasoning. An exam may give nonzero sodium and potassium currents at rest; sum them rather than setting each to zero.

2. The bulk cytosol is strongly negatively charged.

Correct concept. Bulk solutions remain nearly electroneutral; a tiny separation near the membrane produces voltage.

Why the error occurs. A negative voltage is mistakenly interpreted as a large excess concentration of negative ions.

Examination reasoning. Distinguish charge stored by a capacitor from the total ion inventory.

3. A concentration gradient alone determines flow direction.

Correct concept. Both chemical and electrical terms determine passive movement.

Why the error occurs. Diffusion is first taught for uncharged solutes, so electrical forces may be overlooked.

Examination reasoning. Use the ion's valence and V_m − E_ion or the inward free-energy expression.

4. All ions move inward when the interior is negative.

Correct concept. Positive ions are attracted electrically, negative ions are repelled; concentration forces also matter.

Why the error occurs. The attraction of cations to negative voltage is generalized to anions.

Examination reasoning. For chloride, convert conventional current direction to particle movement correctly.

5. At equilibrium, concentrations must be equal.

Correct concept. A voltage may offset unequal concentrations.

Why the error occurs. Chemical equilibrium of neutral solutes is confused with electrochemical equilibrium of charged species.

Examination reasoning. Check whether V_m equals E_ion, not whether the concentration ratio equals one.

6. Nernst gives the resting potential of every neuron.

Correct concept. It gives equilibrium voltage for one species; multiple permeabilities and pump currents can contribute.

Why the error occurs. The same equation is often introduced using a potassium-dominated resting membrane, which obscures the one-ion condition.

Examination reasoning. Identify whether the question supplies one ion or several permeabilities.

7. Chloride uses the same GHK orientation as sodium.

Correct concept. Chloride is an anion; inside concentration appears in the numerator.

Why the error occurs. The visually similar cation terms encourage mechanical copying of the formula.

Examination reasoning. Reduce the equation to its chloride-only limit to check the sign.

8. Permeability and conductance are identical.

Correct concept. Permeability measures ease of crossing in a transport model; conductance describes current–voltage response.

Why the error occurs. Both properties describe an open pathway, but their mathematical definitions differ.

Examination reasoning. Use GHK for permeability inputs and a weighted voltage average for linear conductances.

9. The sodium–potassium pump is the sole direct origin of all resting voltage.

Correct concept. Selective passive pathways produce much of the baseline voltage using gradients maintained by the pump.

Why the error occurs. The pump creates the gradients and is electrogenic, encouraging an incorrect identification of those two functions.

Examination reasoning. Separate rapid loss of pump current from gradual loss of ionic gradients.

10. A larger membrane capacitance gives a larger steady response to a fixed current.

Correct concept. For a fixed linear resistance, capacitance changes the time course, not the steady voltage.

Why the error occurs. The capacitor analogy may suggest more stored charge must produce a larger final voltage under all conditions.

Examination reasoning. Calculate initial slope with I/C and final displacement with IR.

11. Higher input resistance means a less negative resting voltage.

Correct concept. Input resistance is the voltage response to small current changes; the baseline depends on current balance.

Why the error occurs. A more responsive cell is assumed to be more depolarized even before stimulation.

Examination reasoning. Compare two cells with identical reversal potential ratios but different total conductances.

12. After one time constant, charging is complete.

Correct concept. Charging reaches about 63.2%; discharge leaves about 36.8%.

Why the error occurs. The phrase time constant sounds like the full duration of the event.

Examination reasoning. Identify whether a trace rises toward a plateau or decays toward baseline.

13. The length constant and time constant are interchangeable.

Correct concept. Lambda measures spatial attenuation; tau measures temporal response.

Why the error occurs. Both equations contain exponentials, encouraging substitution of one scale for the other.

Examination reasoning. Check whether the independent variable has distance or time units.

14. Depolarization necessarily means excitation.

Correct concept. Depolarization can coexist with shunting or reduced availability of regenerative channels.

Why the error occurs. A movement toward threshold is mistaken for certainty of regenerative firing.

Examination reasoning. Consider resistance, threshold and channel state, not just baseline voltage.

15. A chloride channel at its reversal potential has no physiological effect.

Correct concept. Its conductance can shunt subsequent voltage changes even with zero initial current.

Why the error occurs. Zero current at the starting voltage is incorrectly generalized to all later voltages.

Examination reasoning. Recalculate input resistance when a pathway opens.

16. Every cell fires at −55 mV.

Correct concept. Threshold varies with channel availability, time, geometry and stimulus history.

Why the error occurs. A representative textbook diagram is mistaken for a universal physiological measurement.

Examination reasoning. Treat a fixed threshold as an explicit model assumption, not a universal biological constant.

17. A passive voltage signal remains constant along a healthy axon.

Correct concept. Passive spread attenuates; regenerative action potential propagation renews the signal.

Why the error occurs. Graded and regenerative signals both involve voltage changes, obscuring their distinct propagation mechanisms.

Examination reasoning. Distinguish the cable response from action potential conduction.

B 1. A recording gives −70 mV when the external reference is zero. What does this mean?

  1. The cytosol is at a lower electric potential than the bath.
  2. The whole cytosol contains 70 mM excess anions.
  3. The external solution has no ions.
  4. All ions are at equilibrium.
Answer and explanation

A. The cytosol is at a lower electric potential than the bath.

Voltage is a potential difference, not a concentration or a bulk net-charge measurement. Only a small separation of charge near the membrane is required. Individual ions can remain far from equilibrium.

B 2. Which feature allows ion gradients to persist across the resting neuronal membrane?

  1. Unrestricted diffusion through lipids
  2. A hydrophobic bilayer with selective protein pathways
  3. Identical internal and external solute concentrations
  4. Absence of all membrane proteins
Answer and explanation

B. A hydrophobic bilayer with selective protein pathways

The lipid core resists hydrated ions; channels provide selective passive routes and transporters maintain asymmetry. Unrestricted diffusion would dissipate gradients.

B 3. The typical mature mammalian neuronal concentration pattern is:

  1. High external K⁺ and high internal Na⁺
  2. Equal Na⁺ on both sides
  3. High internal K⁺ and high external Na⁺
  4. High internal free Ca²⁺ relative to outside
Answer and explanation

C. High internal K⁺ and high external Na⁺

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains high internal K⁺ and high external Na⁺. Free cytosolic calcium is usually far below extracellular calcium.

B 4. Which statement about potassium at E_K is correct?

  1. Potassium channels must be closed.
  2. K⁺ ions stop all microscopic motion.
  3. The inside and outside concentrations must be equal.
  4. Net passive K⁺ flux is zero.
Answer and explanation

D. Net passive K⁺ flux is zero.

At E_K the chemical and electrical influences balance. Individual ions may cross in both directions; neither channel closure nor equality of concentrations is required.

B 5. What is the valence z for chloride in the Nernst equation?

  1. −1
  2. +1
  3. −2
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

A. −1

Chloride carries one negative charge. Its negative valence reverses the sign relative to a monovalent cation with the same concentration ratio.

B 6. A K⁺-selective channel reverses at −95 mV. At −70 mV its conventional outward current is:

  1. Negative
  2. Positive
  3. Exactly zero
  4. Undefined because K⁺ is charged
Answer and explanation

B. Positive

I_K = g_K(V_m − E_K) is positive because −70 − (−95) = +25 mV. Positive outward current here represents potassium leaving.

B 7. Which quantity is explicitly absent from the Nernst equation for one ion?

  1. Absolute temperature
  2. Ion valence
  3. Membrane permeability
  4. Concentration ratio
Answer and explanation

C. Membrane permeability

Nernst predicts an equilibrium voltage from temperature, charge and activity ratio. Permeability determines how rapidly an open pathway can approach equilibrium, not the equilibrium voltage.

B 8. For a chloride-only membrane, the GHK expression reduces to which concentration ratio within ln, with positive RT/F prefactor?

  1. [Cl]out/[Cl]in
  2. [Na]out/[Na]in
  3. [K]in/[K]out
  4. [Cl]in/[Cl]out
Answer and explanation

D. [Cl]in/[Cl]out

The chloride terms are reversed relative to cations. This produces the same sign as applying z = −1 in the Nernst equation.

B 9. If all three relative permeabilities in the GHK equation are multiplied by the same factor, its predicted voltage:

  1. Remains unchanged
  2. Doubles
  3. Changes sign
  4. Becomes zero
Answer and explanation

A. Remains unchanged

The common multiplier cancels from numerator and denominator. Absolute currents and input resistance may change even though the passive zero-current voltage does not.

B 10. What is transported per normal Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase cycle?

  1. 2 Na⁺ out, 3 K⁺ in
  2. 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in
  3. 3 Na⁺ in, 2 K⁺ out
  4. 1 Na⁺ out, 1 K⁺ in
Answer and explanation

B. 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in

The pump normally exports three Na⁺ and imports two K⁺ per ATP hydrolyzed. One net positive charge leaves, so the pump is electrogenic.

B 11. The main long-term role of the sodium–potassium pump in resting potential is to:

  1. Open voltage-gated sodium channels
  2. Eliminate all ion leakage
  3. Maintain the ionic gradients
  4. Force every neuron to −70 mV
Answer and explanation

C. Maintain the ionic gradients

The pump supports ionic asymmetry against passive exchange. Its net outward current can also make a direct contribution, but it does not set a universal voltage.

B 12. Capacitance is defined by:

  1. I/V
  2. V/I
  3. Q×V
  4. Q/V
Answer and explanation

D. Q/V

Capacitance is charge separation per unit voltage. Conductance is current per voltage; resistance is the reciprocal of conductance in a linear model.

B 13. At the beginning of a small injected current step into a passive cell, what determines the initial voltage slope?

  1. Injected current divided by capacitance
  2. Only the potassium concentration
  3. Current multiplied by time constant
  4. Only the final input resistance
Answer and explanation

A. Injected current divided by capacitance

The initial extra leak is zero at the starting voltage in the linear model. The capacitive equation gives dV/dt = I/C at that instant.

B 14. Opening an additional linear conductance in parallel generally has what effect on input resistance?

  1. Increases it
  2. Decreases it
  3. Leaves it unchanged in all cases
  4. Makes capacitance infinite
Answer and explanation

B. Decreases it

Conductances in parallel add. Increasing total conductance reduces its reciprocal, the resistance, even if the new pathway reverses at baseline.

B 15. A membrane time constant of 20 ms means a passive step response has reached approximately what fraction of its final value after 20 ms?

  1. 37%
  2. 50%
  3. 63%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

C. 63%

For charging, the fraction is 1 − e⁻¹ ≈ 0.632. The 37% figure describes decay after one time constant.

B 16. In an ideal passive cable, the length constant is the distance at which a maintained voltage displacement is:

  1. Twice its initial value
  2. Zero
  3. 63% of initial value
  4. 37% of initial value
Answer and explanation

D. 37% of initial value

The steady spatial response is v(x) = v₀ exp(−x/λ). At x = λ, the remaining fraction is e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368.

B 17. Which is a property of graded potentials in the passive regime?

  1. Amplitude can vary with stimulus strength.
  2. Every response has the same amplitude.
  3. They necessarily propagate without loss.
  4. They occur only after a spike.
Answer and explanation

A. Amplitude can vary with stimulus strength.

Graded potentials vary with input strength and duration and can attenuate with distance. Regenerative propagation is a different mechanism.

B 18. What is the usual direct effect of increasing external K⁺ with other conditions fixed in a K⁺-dominated resting membrane?

  1. Hyperpolarization
  2. Depolarization
  3. No change in E_K
  4. A compulsory action potential of fixed duration
Answer and explanation

B. Depolarization

Increasing [K⁺]out makes E_K less negative and usually depolarizes the membrane. Whether a spike occurs depends on the initiation mechanisms.

B 19. A current through a mixed Na⁺/K⁺ channel is zero at its reversal potential. Which inference is justified?

  1. Both Na⁺ and K⁺ are at equilibrium.
  2. Both ions must stop moving.
  3. Their total conventional current is zero.
  4. All membrane currents are zero.
Answer and explanation

C. Their total conventional current is zero.

The net current through that channel can vanish while oppositely directed ionic charge currents persist. Other membrane pathways may also carry current.

B 20. Which experiment directly estimates input resistance near rest?

  2. Counting all channels in a tissue section
  3. Measuring extracellular osmolarity only
  4. Applying small known current steps and recording steady voltage changes

D. Applying small known current steps and recording steady voltage changes

The ratio ΔV/ΔI estimates input resistance under linear conditions. The other measurements cannot establish the electrical response to injected current.

  3. −2 pA
  4. −28 pA
Answer and explanation

B. +2 pA

I_K = 3×10 = +30 pA. I_Na = 0.2×(−140) = −28 pA. Their sum is +2 pA; the sum, not either current alone, determines the net charge change.

C 5. With g_K = 3 nS, E_K = −90 mV, g_Na = 1 nS and E_Na = +60 mV, what is the passive zero-current voltage?

  1. −75 mV
  2. −60 mV
  3. −52.5 mV
  4. −15 mV
Answer and explanation

C. −52.5 mV

The weighted numerator is −270 + 60 = −210 nS·mV. Divide by 4 nS to obtain −52.5 mV. The simple unweighted average −15 mV ignores conductance differences.

C 6. Use the monovalent GHK equation at 37 °C with [K]out/in = 4/140 mM, [Na]out/in = 145/12 mM, P_K = 1, P_Na = 0.04, P_Cl = 0. What is V_m?

  1. About −71.1 mV
  2. About −95 mV
  3. About +71.1 mV
  4. About −35 mV
Answer and explanation

A. About −71.1 mV

The weighted ratio is (4 + 5.8)/(140 + 0.48) = 9.8/140.48. Taking 61.5 log₁₀ of that ratio gives about −71.1 mV. E_K alone would be about −95 mV.

C 7. A neuron has passive zero-current voltage −70 mV and total conductance 10 nS. An additional constant outward pump current of 20 pA is imposed. Predict the new voltage, with gradients fixed.

  1. −68 mV
  2. −90 mV
  3. −50 mV
  4. −72 mV
Answer and explanation

D. −72 mV

The passive inward current required to balance the pump is −20 pA. The voltage shift is −20/10 = −2 mV. The pump shifts the baseline more negative.

C 8. A 100 pA injected current gives a steady 15 mV response. What is the input resistance?

  1. 15 MΩ
  2. 150 MΩ
  3. 1.5 GΩ
  4. 1500 GΩ
Answer and explanation

B. 150 MΩ

100 pA = 0.1 nA. ΔV/I = 15/0.1 = 150 MΩ. Confusing picoamperes and nanoamperes creates a factor-of-one-thousand error.

C 9. A cell has R = 250 MΩ and C = 80 pF. What is τ?

  1. 2 ms
  2. 200 ms
  3. 20 ms
  4. 0.02 ms
Answer and explanation

C. 20 ms

Convert to SI: 250 × 10⁶ Ω × 80 × 10⁻¹² F = 0.020 s = 20 ms. It is the time to reach 63.2% of a sustained linear step response.

C 10. A passive step reaches 12 mV at steady state and τ = 10 ms. How large is the response 10 ms after the step begins?

  1. 7.6 mV
  2. 4.4 mV
  3. 12 mV
  4. 1.2 mV
Answer and explanation

A. 7.6 mV

Charging gives 12(1 − e⁻¹) = 7.59 mV. 4.4 mV is the fraction remaining after one time constant during discharge, not the charging value.

C 11. After the end of a current pulse, a 10 mV displacement decays with τ = 20 ms. What remains 40 ms later?

  1. 6.3 mV
  2. 3.7 mV
  3. 8.6 mV
  4. 1.35 mV
Answer and explanation

D. 1.35 mV

Decay is 10 e^(−40/20) = 10e⁻² ≈ 1.35 mV. The response remains positive but is much smaller after two time constants.

C 12. A passive cable has λ = 0.8 mm. If its specific membrane resistance is increased fourfold with other properties fixed, new λ is:

  1. 0.2 mm
  2. 1.6 mm
  3. 3.2 mm
  4. 0.8 mm
Answer and explanation

B. 1.6 mm

λ is proportional to the square root of specific membrane resistance. A fourfold increase doubles λ, rather than quadrupling it.

C 13. A maintained displacement is 15 mV at x=0 in a cable with λ=0.4 mm. What is the displacement at 0.8 mm?

  1. 5.5 mV
  2. 7.5 mV
  3. 2.0 mV
  4. 13.0 mV
Answer and explanation

C. 2.0 mV

The distance is 2λ. The predicted displacement is 15e⁻² ≈ 2.03 mV. This applies to a steady ideal cable, not necessarily the peak of a brief transient.

C 14. Two uniform passive processes have the same specific membrane properties and cytoplasmic resistivity. Diameter B is four times diameter A. Which relationship follows?

  1. λ_B = 2λ_A, same intrinsic τ
  2. λ_B = 4λ_A, same τ
  3. λ_B = λ_A, τ_B = 4τ_A
  4. λ_B = λ_A/2, same τ
Answer and explanation

A. λ_B = 2λ_A, same intrinsic τ

The length constant scales with the square root of diameter. The local membrane time constant R_mC_m does not contain diameter, although whole-cell recordings can reflect geometry.

C 15. A neuron with 100 MΩ input resistance receives 100 pA. A conductance of 10 nS that reverses at baseline opens. Assuming a passive compact cell, the new steady voltage displacement is:

  1. 20 mV
  2. 10 mV
  3. 0 mV
  4. 5 mV
Answer and explanation

D. 5 mV

Original conductance is 10 nS. Adding 10 nS halves resistance to 50 MΩ. The current produces 0.1 nA × 50 MΩ = 5 mV. The unchanged baseline does not imply unchanged excitability.

C 16. Two idealized cells have the same 10 mV steady response to the same current step. Cell B has twice the capacitance of A. Which observation is predicted?

  1. B has a larger final response.
  2. B has half the initial voltage slope and twice τ.
  3. B has twice the initial slope.
  4. B has a lower input resistance.
Answer and explanation

B. B has half the initial voltage slope and twice τ.

Identical steady response at the same current implies the same R. Doubling C halves I/C and doubles RC. The final response is unchanged.

C 17. An isolated current reverses at −90 mV. Doubling external K⁺ shifts reversal by +18.5 mV at 37 °C. Which interpretation is most strongly supported?

  1. The channel is necessarily an ATPase.
  2. The channel carries only chloride.
  3. The isolated current is consistent with predominantly K⁺ selectivity.
  4. The whole cell must rest at the reversal voltage.
Answer and explanation

C. The isolated current is consistent with predominantly K⁺ selectivity.

The shift matches the potassium Nernst prediction. It supports K⁺ selectivity of the isolated current, but does not establish a molecular identity or whole-cell resting voltage.

C 18. A drug removes a pump current. The neuron rapidly depolarizes by 2 mV; after a much longer time its ion gradients change. What is the best explanation?

  1. Initial loss of electrogenic current followed by impaired gradient maintenance.
  2. Immediate elimination of all concentration gradients.
  3. The pump is a passive potassium leak channel.
  4. The later phase proves that the initial phase is unrelated to transport.
Answer and explanation

A. Initial loss of electrogenic current followed by impaired gradient maintenance.

The pump transfers net charge directly and sustains concentration gradients over time. The rapid electrical effect and slower ionic effect can be distinguished, although additional secondary mechanisms may contribute.

C 19. In a current-clamp experiment a constant hyperpolarizing step gives an initial negative peak followed by a partial recovery while the current remains unchanged. Which model conclusion is best?

  1. A single ideal passive RC model explains any such trace.
  2. Capacitance becomes zero.
  3. The Nernst equation cannot be valid.
  4. A time-dependent opposing current may be recruited.
Answer and explanation

D. A time-dependent opposing current may be recruited.

A passive linear single-compartment RC response under constant current approaches its steady value monotonically. A sag is consistent with a recruited opposing current, but identification needs further evidence.

C 20. A neuron rests at −70 mV with fixed model threshold −55 mV, R = 150 MΩ and τ = 15 ms. What is the approximate current to reach threshold after a 5 ms step?

  1. 100 pA
  2. 353 pA
  3. 35 pA
  4. 15 pA
Answer and explanation

B. 353 pA

The long-duration current is 15/150 = 0.1 nA. Divide by 1−exp(−5/15) ≈ 0.2835, giving about 0.353 nA. Actual thresholds depend on active channel kinetics.

C 21. A model neuron has K⁺ conductance 4 nS, Na⁺ conductance 1 nS and Cl⁻ conductance 1 nS, with reversal potentials −90, +60 and −60 mV. An intervention raises E_K to −70 mV and doubles g_Cl. Which pair of passive steady voltages is predicted before and after the intervention?

  1. −60 mV, −48.6 mV
  2. −60 mV, −40 mV
  3. −72 mV, −60 mV
  4. −90 mV, −70 mV
Answer and explanation

A. −60 mV, −48.6 mV

Before intervention the weighted sum is −360 + 60 − 60 = −360; dividing by 6 gives −60 mV. After intervention it is −280 + 60 − 120 = −340; dividing by 7 gives −48.6 mV. Doubling chloride conductance partially offsets the depolarizing effect of the E_K change; changing E_K alone would give −46.7 mV.

C 22. Two cells with the same baseline receive identical 50 pA current steps. Cell A reaches a 10 mV plateau with τ = 20 ms. Cell B reaches 5 mV with τ = 20 ms. Which combination accounts for these observations in the compact passive model?

  1. B has twice the resistance and half the capacitance.
  2. B has half the resistance and the same capacitance.
  3. B has half the resistance and twice the capacitance.
  4. B has the same resistance and twice the capacitance.
Answer and explanation

C. B has half the resistance and twice the capacitance.

The plateau is IR. A has 200 MΩ and B has 100 MΩ. Since τ is equal, C_B must double: C_A=100 pF and C_B=200 pF. This distinguishes the independently measurable steady resistance from the RC product.

C 23. At 37 °C a selective K⁺ current reverses at −95 mV in 4 mM external K⁺. A separate mixed current reverses at −35 mV and changes to −29 mV when external potassium doubles. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The mixed current is purely potassium-selective.
  2. The first current should shift to about −76.5 mV; the second current's smaller shift is compatible with contributions from other ions.
  3. The first current should shift to −113.5 mV; the second cannot carry K⁺.
  4. Neither current can be passive because their reversals differ.
Answer and explanation

B. The first current should shift to about −76.5 mV; the second current's smaller shift is compatible with contributions from other ions.

Doubling external potassium gives +18.5 mV for a pure K⁺ pathway at fixed internal concentration. The mixed current shifts by only +6 mV, consistent with additional permeant species, although the shift alone does not uniquely identify them. The two currents' reversals are not the whole-cell resting voltage.

C 24. A neuron rests at −70 mV, with threshold assumed −55 mV. Its input resistance is 200 MΩ and its capacitance 100 pF. A drug opens an extra 5 nS conductance reversing at −70 mV. For a 20 ms, 100 pA current pulse, which pair of peak voltage displacements is predicted before and after treatment?

  1. 20 mV, 10 mV
  2. 12.6 mV, 8.65 mV
  3. 12.6 mV, 6.3 mV
  4. 6.3 mV, 12.6 mV
Answer and explanation

B. 12.6 mV, 8.65 mV

Before treatment R=200 MΩ, τ=20 ms and the steady displacement is 20 mV; at 20 ms the response is 12.64 mV. After the added conductance, R=100 MΩ, τ=10 ms, steady displacement=10 mV and the response is 10(1−e⁻²)=8.65 mV. Neither reaches the model's 15 mV threshold; the shunt alters both amplitude and time constant.

C 25. The calculated steady electrotonic displacement from a dendritic source is 18 mV at x=0. A soma lies 0.6 mm away. In condition A λ=0.3 mm; in condition B λ=0.6 mm. If a sustained somatic displacement of 5 mV is necessary to reach threshold, what does the ideal passive model predict?

  1. Both conditions cross threshold.
  2. Neither condition crosses threshold.
  3. Only A crosses threshold.
  4. Only B crosses threshold.
Answer and explanation

D. Only B crosses threshold.

In A, x/λ=2 and the soma receives 18e⁻²≈2.44 mV. In B, x/λ=1 and the soma receives 18e⁻¹≈6.62 mV. Only B reaches the stated model threshold. Actual transient input and geometry may modify the prediction.

C 26. A study finds that adding extracellular KCl depolarizes an isolated neuron. A second experiment substitutes K⁺ for some Na⁺ while keeping osmolarity constant, and produces a different voltage shift. Which follow-up best distinguishes the predicted ionic effects from recording artifacts?

  1. Measure actual bath K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻, correct liquid junction offsets, compare GHK predictions for each composition and verify reversibility.
  2. Use only the first experiment because it produced a larger shift.
  3. Conclude that the Nernst equation fails whenever bath composition changes.
  4. Compare absolute voltages without recording temperature or reference stability.
Answer and explanation

A. Measure actual bath K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻, correct liquid junction offsets, compare GHK predictions for each composition and verify reversibility.

Both protocols alter more than a single independent variable: adding KCl changes chloride and osmolarity, whereas substitution changes sodium. The mixed-permeability model must use the actual compositions. Junction offsets, temperature and reversibility controls reduce alternative interpretations.

17. Original State SET and integrated practice

SET 1. At equal intracellular and extracellular concentrations of a monovalent ion, its equilibrium potential is:

  1. Zero
  2. +61.5 mV
  3. −61.5 mV
  4. Not defined
Answer and explanation

A. Zero

The ratio is one and log(1)=0. A channel could still carry current if the actual membrane voltage differs from zero.

SET 2. Which pair correctly identifies a mechanism and its immediate consequence at typical rest?

  1. Opening K⁺ leak: increases resistance
  2. Opening Na⁺ leak: tends to hyperpolarize
  3. Opening K⁺ leak with V_m > E_K: increases outward current
  4. Pump inhibition: instantly eliminates all K⁺ gradients
Answer and explanation

C. Opening K⁺ leak with V_m > E_K: increases outward current

A K⁺ pathway at V_m above E_K carries positive outward current; increasing conductance increases that current at fixed voltage. The other statements reverse effects or confuse timescales.

SET 3. A chloride channel has E_Cl = −50 mV; V_m = −70 mV. In which direction do chloride particles move when it opens?

  1. Inward
  2. Outward
  3. No net movement
  4. Always toward the higher concentration independently of voltage
Answer and explanation

B. Outward

V_m − E_Cl = −20 mV, so conventional outward current is negative. Negative particles moving outward produce an inward conventional current. The voltage moves toward −50 mV.

SET 4. Assertion: resting voltage can persist briefly after pump inhibition. Reason: pre-existing gradients store electrochemical free energy.

  1. Both true, and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true, and reason explains assertion

The existing gradients continue to drive passive currents briefly. Long-term maintenance requires active transport, so pump inhibition does not have to collapse the voltage instantaneously.

SET 5. Which variable has units of distance in the passive cable model?

  1. τ
  2. C_m
  3. R_m
  4. λ
Answer and explanation

D. λ

Lambda is a length constant. Tau has time units; specific capacitance and specific resistance are electrical properties per area or area-adjusted quantities.

SET 6. A 25 pF cell has an initial slope of 4 mV/ms during a small current step. Estimate injected current.

  1. 1 pA
  2. 10 pA
  3. 100 pA
  4. 1000 pA
Answer and explanation

C. 100 pA

4 mV/ms = 4 V/s. Multiplying by 25 pF gives 100 pA. This assumes the initial added passive leak is zero.

SET 7. Two identical idealized voltage increments of 3 mV are separated by one time constant. What is the combined displacement just after the second?

  1. 3.0 mV
  2. 4.1 mV
  3. 4.9 mV
  4. 6.0 mV
Answer and explanation

B. 4.1 mV

The residual first response is 3e⁻¹ ≈ 1.10 mV; adding the second gives 4.10 mV. Full 6 mV would require simultaneous increments under linear conditions.

SET 8. Which control is most important when comparing resting voltage in two solutions differing in external K⁺?

  1. Only cell color
  2. Only the size of the electrode holder
  3. Only the experimenter's preferred hypothesis
  4. Maintain or account for other ion concentrations, osmolarity, temperature and recording offsets.
Answer and explanation

D. Maintain or account for other ion concentrations, osmolarity, temperature and recording offsets.

Ion replacement can alter other gradients and junction potentials. Those variables must be controlled or explicitly included in the interpretation.

SET 9. A small increase in extracellular K⁺ depolarizes a neuron. Why does this not guarantee increased firing during prolonged exposure?

  1. Persistent depolarization may reduce available regenerative sodium channels.
  2. Nernst potential does not depend on K⁺.
  3. Depolarization is identical to hyperpolarization.
  4. Potassium cannot cross neuronal membranes.
Answer and explanation

A. Persistent depolarization may reduce available regenerative sodium channels.

Baseline depolarization may initially favor reaching threshold; prolonged depolarization can reduce sodium-channel availability and impair firing. Voltage alone is insufficient to predict excitability.

SET 10. Which sequence correctly links the three principal electrical models?

  1. RC model sets all ion gradients; Nernst predicts cell geometry.
  2. Nernst predicts spike frequency; Goldman predicts ATP hydrolysis.
  3. Nernst specifies ion equilibrium voltages; a current-balance model predicts resting voltage; RC behavior predicts passive time course.
  4. All three predict the same property using interchangeable parameters.
Answer and explanation

C. Nernst specifies ion equilibrium voltages; a current-balance model predicts resting voltage; RC behavior predicts passive time course.

Each model answers a different question. Equilibrium potentials inform current balance, and a capacitive balance determines voltage change over time.

18. Revision, recall and mastery

Resting voltage emerges from selective ion permeability and maintained ionic gradients. The Nernst equation supplies each species' equilibrium voltage. GHK or a suitable conductance model establishes the passive zero-current voltage. An electrogenic pump maintains gradients and can contribute an additional direct current. Membrane capacitance and resistance shape local graded responses; the cable length constant describes steady spatial attenuation. Initiation of a regenerative impulse depends on the graded signal reaching a sufficiently excitable region.

High-yield equations

  • E_ion = (RT/zF) ln(a_out/a_in).
  • V_m = (RT/F) ln[(P_K[K]_out + P_Na[Na]_out + P_Cl[Cl]_in)/(P_K[K]_in + P_Na[Na]_in + P_Cl[Cl]_out)] for the usual monovalent passive GHK model.
  • I_ion = g_ion(V_m − E_ion), for a locally linear pathway.
  • C dV/dt = I_inj − I_out.
  • τ = RC; ΔV(t) = IR[1 − exp(−t/τ)] for a constant current step.
  • λ = √(aR_m/(2ρ_i)); v(x) = v₀ exp(−x/λ) for a steady ideal semi-infinite cable.

Active recall

  1. Define V_m using an explicit reference convention.
  2. Explain why bulk electroneutrality is compatible with a negative resting potential.
  3. Derive E_ion from the equality of electrochemical potentials.
  4. Calculate E_K when external potassium doubles.
  5. Explain the reversed orientation of chloride terms in GHK.
  6. Differentiate equilibrium potential, reversal potential and resting potential.
  7. Explain why a small sodium conductance can influence baseline strongly.
  8. Distinguish the pump's direct and indirect contributions.
  9. Derive the compact passive RC step response from charge conservation.
  10. Predict what changes when R doubles or C doubles independently.
  11. Explain the physical origin of the cable length constant.
  12. Describe two experimental controls for ion substitution.
  13. Predict the effect of a shunting conductance on a later input.
  14. Explain why threshold is a transition rather than a universal voltage.

Mastery checklist

  • Explain how a tiny charge separation establishes voltage without disrupting bulk electroneutrality.
  • Determine ionic current and particle flux directions for cations and anions.
  • Choose the Nernst, GHK or conductance model from its inputs and assumptions.
  • Predict how altering permeability or concentration changes steady voltage.
  • Distinguish passive and active transport effects on different timescales.
  • Calculate the amplitude and temporal course of a subthreshold current response.
  • Interpret voltage–current, concentration–voltage and distance–voltage plots.
  • Recognize limitations of fixed-threshold and uniform-cable approximations.
