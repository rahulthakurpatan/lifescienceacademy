1. Why transcription does not finish gene expression

Consider a liver cell making an enzyme that will operate in the cytosol. The gene lies in a chromosome inside the nucleus. The DNA is not transported to the cytosol every time the enzyme must be produced. Instead, a temporary RNA copy is synthesized and prepared for translation. This arrangement protects the genetic archive while permitting a changing supply of working instructions. Yet it introduces an additional problem: the RNA must cross the nuclear boundary, reach the translation machinery, and remain available long enough to contribute to protein production. A perfectly copied RNA that cannot leave the nucleus may therefore produce very little protein. The connection between transcription and translation is not simply a line of arrows in the central dogma; it is a sequence of physically distinct events, each of which can fail independently.

The nuclear envelope comprises two membranes, but movement of nuclear-encoded messenger RNA to the cytoplasm normally occurs through nuclear pore complexes rather than directly through the lipid phase. The RNA backbone contains negatively charged phosphate groups and is strongly hydrated. A naked RNA would face an energetically unfavorable environment in the hydrophobic core of a lipid bilayer. Furthermore, a long polymer has a size and flexibility quite unlike those of a small diffusible metabolite. Cells overcome both difficulties by maintaining aqueous connections across the envelope and using proteins that recognize appropriate RNA cargoes. Export is therefore a problem of selective passage through a specialized boundary, not a problem of making RNA lipid soluble.

Why should the cell select cargo rather than simply allow every RNA molecule to leave? During the lifetime of a typical eukaryotic transcript, many intermediates exist that are not appropriate substrates for cytoplasmic ribosomes. A transcript may still contain sequences normally removed by splicing, or its 3′ end may be incorrectly formed. It may be an abortive product made from a promoter that failed to support productive transcription. Nuclear RNA includes molecules whose final roles are in the nucleus itself, such as many small nuclear RNAs and nucleolar RNAs. Even an accurately processed RNA may have a destination other than translation. Selectivity allows a cell to preserve nuclear functions while delivering suitable cargo to the cytoplasm.

The word mature must be interpreted in relation to a particular RNA class and biological purpose. A typical mammalian protein-coding transcript receives a modified 5′ end, undergoes splicing when introns are present, and forms a defined 3′ end. A transfer RNA acquires its own ends, fold and nucleotide modifications, while ribosomal RNA associates with other RNAs and proteins. These products cannot all be tested by one universal molecular checklist. A mature histone messenger RNA need not possess the same kind of polyadenylated 3′ end as a typical enzyme-encoding message. An intronless gene does not need to undergo splicing to produce an exportable transcript. Some RNAs even undergo additional maturation after an initial transport event.

There are three distinct spatial questions in this chapter. The first is whether an RNA leaves the nucleus at all. The second is whether the exported RNA reaches the appropriate place within the cytoplasm. The third is whether the RNA survives inspection and remains available for its biological activity. The answers influence one another, but they are not interchangeable. A message found in the cytoplasm may accumulate at the wrong pole of a developing cell. A message at the correct pole may remain translationally inactive. A defective message may leave the nucleus successfully yet be destroyed after a ribosome discovers an inappropriate stop codon. These possibilities explain why measuring the total quantity of RNA alone rarely settles the question of gene expression.

The bacterial situation clarifies what the eukaryotic boundary contributes. Bacteria generally lack a membrane-enclosed nucleus, and their ribosomes can begin translating a message before its synthesis has ended. This does not mean that bacterial RNA is unregulated or randomly distributed. Bacteria also possess RNA-binding proteins, nucleases, and mechanisms that influence localization and RNA lifetime. However, they do not require the canonical nuclear-pore-dependent export route described here. When a problem asks why nuclear RNA export is necessary, the decisive contrast is the compartmental organization of the cell rather than the presence or absence of RNA maturation.

In previous chapters, the individual chemical reactions that produce mature RNA were considered. The purpose of the present chapter is to follow their consequences for transport. We will treat the RNA not as an isolated sequence of nucleotides but as a moving molecular assembly. By the end of the journey, the same RNA sequence will have been associated with several different sets of proteins. These changes in association give the molecule different abilities at different stages. This view makes it possible to explain how processing history, spatial organization and quality control become part of the expression of a gene.

2. The RNA–protein particle and its changing identity

A newly transcribed messenger RNA associates with proteins throughout its life. The resulting assembly is called a messenger ribonucleoprotein particle, or mRNP. It is useful to distinguish the RNA molecule from the particle that contains it because the sequence of the RNA can remain unchanged while the behavior of the particle changes dramatically. A protein may shield part of the backbone, recognize a sequence, promote folding, recruit another protein, or influence the rate at which a nuclease approaches the RNA. An export receptor associated with the particle can permit interactions with the nuclear pore that would be inefficient for an unassisted transcript.

Binding is selective but rarely permanent. Proteins can recognize the chemical features of RNA through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds, base stacking and contacts with a particular three-dimensional surface. A long RNA contains many potential binding sites, and the accessibility of each depends on its structure and other proteins already occupying it. An association can be lost because a competitor has higher affinity, because the RNA folds differently, or because an enzyme changes the conformation of the interacting proteins. The term remodeling refers to such changes in the organization or protein composition of an RNA-containing complex. Remodeling does not necessarily change the nucleotide sequence.

During synthesis by RNA polymerase II, the growing RNA emerges gradually rather than appearing as a complete molecule. Its 5′ region can already associate with proteins while downstream sequences have not yet been synthesized. The 5′ cap provides a binding site for the nuclear cap-binding complex, commonly represented by CBP80 and CBP20 in mammals. This complex contributes to several aspects of RNA metabolism. Its association links the physical 5′ end of the message with proteins that participate in maturation and downstream handling. Importantly, the cap is not a stand-alone passport sufficient to make every RNA exportable. Other properties of the particle still matter.

Splicing changes both the nucleotide sequence and the protein composition of a transcript. During typical spliceosomal splicing in metazoans, exon junction complexes can be placed near newly joined exons. An exon junction complex is a protein assembly positioned approximately 20–24 nucleotides upstream of many exon–exon junctions. It is not the spliceosome itself, nor is it a piece of intron that remains in the RNA. It supplies information about a processing event that has already taken place. RNA-binding and processing factors can help connect the remodeled mRNP with export machinery. Later, when ribosomes move through the coding region, they can displace exon junction complexes encountered in their path. Thus a mark associated with nuclear processing can influence cytoplasmic decisions without requiring permanent modification of the DNA.

Formation of the 3′ end has comparable consequences for the particle. For a typical polyadenylated message, cleavage establishes the mature endpoint and poly(A) polymerase adds an adenine-rich tail. Nuclear poly(A)-binding proteins recognize this tail and influence its organization. The tail is not just an extra length of RNA; it participates in protein associations that change during the RNA's lifetime. A defective cleavage reaction can keep the transcript associated with the transcription site or leave it with an inappropriate set of proteins. Consequently, failure of a processing factor may resemble a transport failure even if the nuclear pore itself is intact.

The word coupling describes how these stages influence one another. If a processing step promotes recruitment of an export-related factor, efficient processing raises the probability that the RNA will leave the nucleus. Coupling need not imply that a particular processing reaction and export occur at precisely the same instant. Rather, they are causally connected through the assembly of the RNA particle. Some associations are made during transcription, others after release from the gene, and some are exchanged close to the pore. In different organisms and genes the details and timing can differ.

A useful conceptual distinction is between an adaptor and an export receptor. An adaptor contributes to selecting the RNA or linking its processing state to the transport machinery. An export receptor interacts with the selective environment of the nuclear pore and helps the cargo pass. The same protein can participate in more than one molecular interaction, so the terms describe roles rather than an absolute classification for every protein. Nevertheless, this distinction is valuable in experiments. A mutation that destroys RNA binding in an adaptor may prevent loading of an otherwise functional export receptor. A receptor mutation that preserves adaptor binding but destroys pore interaction would cause a different defect even though both mutations can increase nuclear RNA accumulation.

Once an mRNP crosses into the cytoplasm, some nuclear factors must be removed and replaced by proteins appropriate for the message's new environment. This exchange allows exported RNA to be recognized by translation factors, localization machinery or cytoplasmic surveillance systems. If nuclear proteins remained irreversibly attached, receptors could be trapped on cargo and fail to return for reuse. The cell would then face both a shortage of export factors and a problem of making the message functional. Reversible association and regulated remodeling are consequently central to the design of the export system.

Consider an experimental mutant with a normal transcript sequence, normal splicing and a normal poly(A) tail but with a protein association defect. It would be tempting to call the RNA fully functional on the basis of its sequence. That description would miss the reason for its poor expression. The experimental unit relevant to export is an RNA–protein particle, and correct assembly is an additional requirement beyond correct sequence. Conversely, finding a protein bound to an RNA does not prove that the interaction is required for transport. The binding may be redundant or incidental. Evidence of necessity requires a perturbation and a measurement that links altered association to altered RNA movement.

3. How a nuclear pore selects and translocates RNA

A nuclear pore complex spans the inner and outer nuclear membranes where they meet. It creates a regulated passage between the nuclear interior and the cytoplasm. Many of its constituent proteins are nucleoporins. Some nucleoporins contain numerous phenylalanine–glycine motifs, usually abbreviated FG repeats. These flexible regions participate in the selectivity of the passage by interacting with nuclear transport receptors. The relevant idea for RNA export is that the pore presents a molecular environment through which suitable receptor-bearing cargo can move more readily than an equivalent cargo without an appropriate transport factor.

Do not picture the pore as a simple rigid sieve. Size matters, but a large particle with appropriate transport interactions may be admitted whereas a smaller protein or RNA assembly without such interactions may move inefficiently. Shape, receptor binding and local interactions influence permeability. The FG regions form a dynamic selective barrier; models of its precise physical organization differ. It is sufficient at examination level to understand that transport receptors make reversible contacts with these regions and that this makes facilitated passage possible. There is no need to assume that every FG repeat binds an export receptor permanently or that all molecules follow one rigid path.

Long mRNAs can be associated with numerous proteins and can adopt compact or extended arrangements. They do not have to lose every associated protein before entering the pore. Instead, the particle changes its organization as necessary, and the RNA can be threaded or rearranged during passage. The chromosome and transcription machinery remain in the nucleus. The nuclear envelope itself is not consumed or opened for each transcript. Pore-mediated transport is a reusable process.

An export event can be broken into several distinguishable stages. First, the mRNP assembles with suitable export factors. Next, it reaches the nuclear face of a pore and interacts with components of the passage. Movement through the selective environment follows. Finally, factors on the cytoplasmic side promote release of the cargo and recycling of the machinery. These stages provide a framework for interpreting mutant phenotypes. If the defect occurs before docking, many transcripts may accumulate within the nucleoplasm. If a particle can dock but cannot complete release, a fraction may remain associated with pores. A microscopy image with limited resolution cannot by itself distinguish these events reliably.

The physics of passage and the direction of net export are also separate. Random thermal motion can move a receptor-bearing particle within the pore. Reversible interactions with FG nucleoporins do not inherently specify which side the particle must reach. Directionality arises because the cargo is assembled in one compartment and disassembled preferentially in the other. This asymmetry biases successful transport and makes backward movement of a released particle less likely. Different classes of nuclear cargo use different sources of biochemical asymmetry. For the major cellular mRNA route, ATP-dependent RNA–protein remodeling is particularly important; for many exportin-mediated pathways, the compartmental distribution of Ran-bound nucleotide states provides the main bias.

The distinction is important in an energy-inhibition experiment. Depleting ATP may interfere with preparation of an mRNP or release of its receptors and may also disturb numerous unrelated cellular processes. Observing inhibition does not establish that a pore contains an ATP-driven motor that mechanically pulls every nucleotide across the boundary. The reaction at a particular stage must be identified with more selective evidence. Similarly, the ability of a particle to make transient contacts with pore proteins is not proof of productive export. It may bind without crossing, enter and return, or reach the opposite side without being released.

Nuclear pores are used by both incoming and outgoing cargoes. A cell must deliver newly synthesized nuclear proteins inward while exporting many RNAs outward. This shared use does not require two totally separate pore populations. Direction and selectivity can be determined by cargo-specific receptors and their regulation on the two sides. Perturbation of a general pore constituent may therefore affect multiple types of traffic, whereas perturbation of a particular RNA export adaptor could show a narrower phenotype. Comparing RNA and protein localization in a mutant can help distinguish broad pore failure from a cargo-selective defect.

These principles explain why an apparently normal nuclear envelope does not prove normal transport. An electron micrograph can reveal the existence of pores but may not reveal whether the correct receptors are loaded, whether the molecular interactions inside the passage work, or whether cargo is properly released. Conversely, a cell with altered RNA localization need not have damaged nuclear membranes. Correct interpretation requires relating the spatial observation to the stage and molecular function being tested.

4. Building an export-competent messenger RNP: TREX and receptor recruitment

A cell cannot inspect a long mRNA by reading its entire nucleotide sequence and comparing it with an ideal reference before every export event. Instead, many of its export decisions depend on the molecular consequences of transcription and processing. A message that undergoes appropriate maturation is more likely to acquire proteins that support export. A molecule that has failed a maturation step may retain proteins associated with an earlier state or fail to acquire new ones. In this way the cell uses the history recorded in the RNA–protein assembly to help distinguish possible cargoes. The mechanism improves fidelity without requiring a separate sequence template against which every message is checked.

The transcription–export complex, abbreviated TREX, provides an important link between the production of a transcript and its preparation for export. The name describes the functional coupling of two stages that might otherwise seem independent. In well-studied eukaryotic systems, TREX contains a THO complex and an ATP-dependent RNA helicase family member, known as UAP56 or DDX39B in mammals and Sub2 in budding yeast, together with associated export adaptors. Mammalian ALYREF, sometimes called Aly/REF, is a representative adaptor. The exact constituents, the order of recruitment and the contribution of individual components vary with species and transcript. For most examination questions, recognizing the functional roles is more informative than memorizing every subunit.

An RNA helicase is often introduced as an enzyme that separates paired nucleic acid strands. The ATPases involved in mRNP formation have a broader relevant function: they can help reorganize RNA–protein contacts. When ATP binds, the enzyme can adopt a conformation suited to particular RNA interactions. ATP hydrolysis and product release allow the enzyme to change its association and proceed through another cycle. Such cycles can support the loading or displacement of proteins without requiring the helicase to travel the entire length of the RNA like a motor. This distinction avoids the misleading picture of UAP56 pulling a message bodily out of the nucleus.

During maturation, interactions involving the cap-binding complex, splicing-associated proteins and 3′ processing factors can promote the recruitment of TREX and related adaptors. These associations provide multiple possible routes into the export pathway. The receptor then recognizes the assembled cargo through RNA and protein contacts. When an adaptor interacts with both the message and the receptor, it helps raise the local concentration of the receptor near suitable RNA. It can also alter which surfaces of the receptor are available for RNA binding. Thus adaptor recruitment can increase the likelihood that a mature particle acquires an export-competent organization.

For bulk cellular mRNA in mammals, the principal receptor is a heterodimer called NXF1–NXT1, historically known as TAP–p15. The corresponding pair in budding yeast is Mex67–Mtr2. A heterodimer is an assembly formed by two different proteins rather than two identical subunits. NXF1 provides important RNA and nucleoporin interaction surfaces; association with NXT1 supports its function. The receptor binds RNA in a manner that usually does not require a unique universal sequence motif shared by all exported messages. The use of adaptors allows a common export pathway to serve transcripts with very different coding and untranslated sequences.

There is a useful consequence of separating cargo selection from pore interaction. Imagine a cell with ten thousand different mRNAs but only a limited collection of transport factors. If each message required a completely unique pore receptor, the system would need a large and costly repertoire of specialized transport machinery. Instead, multiple processing and RNA-binding interactions can converge on a common receptor. This is similar to a biochemical pathway in which a diverse set of upstream substrates is converted into a shared intermediate, although here the convergence involves protein assembly rather than identical covalent chemical reactions. The resulting system retains specificity at the RNA-binding stage and economy at the transport stage.

The common machinery does not mean that every message is equally dependent on each adaptor. A transcript that recruits NXF1 efficiently through one RNA-binding protein may tolerate the loss of another protein better than a transcript that relies heavily on the missing adaptor. In a cell population, removal of an export-related factor may therefore reduce cytoplasmic levels for only a subset of mRNAs. Such a pattern should not be treated as proof that the factor has no export function. Redundant pathways, different RNA structures, and differences in processing can explain partial rather than universal effects.

Intronless messages provide a particularly instructive test of the processing-dependent model. An intron-containing gene that fails to splice may accumulate an inappropriate transcript, but an intronless gene is not defective simply because it cannot undergo splicing. Many normal intronless mRNAs recruit export factors through alternative associations with cap-related machinery, RNA sequence elements or RNA-binding proteins. This shows why exon junction complexes cannot be treated as compulsory export labels on every mRNA. The key concept is the acquisition of a suitable RNA–protein configuration, not a fixed list of modifications imposed on all transcripts regardless of origin.

One can test the distinction between adaptor recruitment and receptor action by designing two conceptual mutants. In the first, an adaptor binds RNA normally but cannot interact with NXF1. In the second, NXF1 associates with the RNA but its relevant FG-binding function is impaired. Both may reduce cytoplasmic RNA. However, in the first mutant an RNA immunoprecipitation assay would predict reduced receptor association with cargo; in the second, the association could remain relatively normal while interactions with pore proteins or transport efficiency decrease. A combined molecular and spatial experiment is needed because the final phenotype alone is shared. This kind of reasoning is characteristic of Part C problems.

5. From docking to cytoplasmic release: the ATP-dependent directionality of bulk mRNA export

Once NXF1–NXT1 has been recruited to a competent particle, the receptor can help it interact with nucleoporins. At the nuclear entrance, proteins of the nuclear basket may contribute to docking and to the timing of cargo passage. Other associated proteins help link the mRNP to the pore. These interactions are transient and should not be interpreted as permanent attachments. If a mature message were held indefinitely at the entrance, it would not reach cytoplasmic ribosomes even though recruitment of export machinery had been successful.

Receptor contacts with FG repeat regions help permit movement through the central passage. Because the receptor can make and break interactions, the cargo can explore available conformations and positions. There is no reason to expect every encounter with a pore to succeed. A molecule may approach, bind and leave again before crossing. Even among successful encounters, the time taken can vary. The concept of facilitated translocation therefore concerns the probability and kinetics of movement rather than a compulsory mechanical sequence with one fixed transit duration.

An important final step occurs at the cytoplasmic face of the pore. The conserved DEAD-box RNA ATPase called Dbp5 in yeast and DDX19 in vertebrates participates in remodeling the exported mRNP. Gle1 and the small molecule inositol hexakisphosphate, commonly abbreviated IP6, help regulate the ATPase activity in well-studied systems. Cytoplasmic pore-associated proteins position the relevant machinery near emerging cargo. This localized arrangement matters because it restricts a critical remodeling reaction preferentially to the cytoplasmic exit rather than allowing it to occur at random on either side.

Dbp5/DDX19-mediated remodeling contributes to displacement of export factors and other nuclear mRNP proteins. The cargo can then be released into the cytoplasm, while recyclable proteins return to their places of action. The receptor-bearing configuration that was favorable for pore transit is lost. A newly released message is consequently less likely to retrace the same route spontaneously. This establishes a biochemical asymmetry between the two sides of the pore. ATP consumption is important for establishing and maintaining the organization of the system, even if thermal motion contributes to the physical passage of the particle.

To understand how an asymmetric reaction produces net transport, imagine a reversible binding equilibrium at the nuclear entrance and a reversible movement between entrance and exit. If the cargo remained identical throughout, particles could enter and leave in either direction according to concentration differences and binding energetics. Now suppose that when a cargo arrives at the cytoplasmic side, an enzyme rapidly removes the receptor. The reverse reaction from cytoplasmic free mRNP to receptor-bearing particle becomes less likely under the local conditions. The number of particles available to move backward is reduced. Continuously making exportable particles in the nucleus and consuming that exportable state at the exit sustains a net flux. This is a conceptual kinetic model, not a claim that every successful export event consumes a fixed number of ATP molecules.

ATP-dependent remodeling occurs at more than one stage. ATPases associated with nuclear assembly and cytoplasmic release should not be conflated. UAP56/Sub2 contributes to formation of a transport-ready particle, whereas DDX19/Dbp5 is particularly associated with its final remodeling near the cytoplasmic side. A mutation in the nuclear enzyme may lead to inefficient loading of the receptor. A defect in the cytoplasmic enzyme may permit association with receptor and pore but impair cargo release. Distinguishing the molecular roles is important even when both mutations eventually lead to similar reductions in cytoplasmic mRNA.

Energy depletion in intact cells complicates interpretation. ATP participates in transcription, splicing, RNA remodeling, protein phosphorylation and many other processes. An ATP-depleted cell may make less RNA, process it inefficiently, alter cytoskeletal organization, or fail to maintain normal nuclear transport. Therefore the conclusion that a particular export factor uses ATP should not rest solely on an experiment in which total cellular ATP is reduced. Stronger evidence comes from comparing functional and ATPase-defective forms of an individual factor under matched conditions, and from testing whether restoration of the functional protein reverses the phenotype.

The principal NXF1 route is conventionally contrasted with classical exportin-dependent transport because its directional cycle does not directly rely on the RanGTP gradient in the same manner. This is an important examination distinction, but it should not be turned into a statement that Ran has no influence whatsoever on the ability of a living cell to export mRNA. Many nuclear proteins that support gene expression must themselves be imported by Ran-regulated pathways. A severe Ran perturbation may indirectly influence the production or activity of the mRNA export apparatus. An experiment that disrupts Ran globally is therefore more complex than a diagram comparing two isolated transport cycles.

The receptor can be considered a reusable resource. If an individual message permanently holds one or more copies of the export receptor after reaching the cytoplasm, the available nuclear pool may eventually fall. Receptor recycling therefore affects the capacity of the pathway, not just the fate of the particular message. If a large burst of transcription temporarily exceeds the rate of receptor reuse and cargo release, newly synthesized transcripts may spend longer in the nucleus. Such a delay need not indicate an abnormal DNA sequence. Transport depends both on the molecular suitability of cargo and on the available capacity of shared machinery.

At the end of the export cycle, the RNA's 5′ and 3′ ends remain present unless a separate processing or degradation reaction has changed them. The defining change is the particle's association with proteins. Nuclear cap-binding and poly(A)-binding factors may be exchanged for cytoplasmic counterparts during subsequent mRNP maturation. Translation initiation, localization and surveillance can then act on the exported message. Passage through the pore is therefore best considered a transition between two functional molecular states, rather than the final event in the life of an mRNA.

6. Exportin-mediated RNA transport and the Ran nucleotide cycle

Many nuclear transport pathways rely on proteins belonging to the karyopherin family. Some of these proteins mediate import of cargo into the nucleus, whereas others promote export and are commonly called exportins. Their binding to cargo is regulated by the small GTPase Ran. A GTPase binds guanine nucleotides and can catalyze hydrolysis of guanosine triphosphate to guanosine diphosphate and inorganic phosphate. Ran does not itself act as a general RNA receptor. Its nucleotide state alters the interactions of transport receptors, allowing them to bind or release cargo in the correct compartment.

A useful starting point is the distribution of RanGTP and RanGDP. In the nucleus, the guanine nucleotide exchange factor RCC1 associates with chromatin and promotes release of GDP from Ran so that GTP can bind. The cytoplasmic environment contains Ran GTPase-activating protein, or RanGAP, which accelerates hydrolysis of Ran-bound GTP. These spatially separated activities maintain a relatively high RanGTP environment in the nucleus and a lower RanGTP environment in the cytoplasm. RanGDP can return to the nucleus with assistance from nuclear transport factor 2, NTF2, and undergo nucleotide exchange again. The system is maintained by biochemical reactions rather than by a permanent membrane barrier impermeable to every form of Ran.

For a typical exportin pathway, cargo and RanGTP bind cooperatively to the receptor in the nucleus. A stable export complex can then interact with nucleoporins and move through the pore. In the cytoplasm, RanGAP promotes GTP hydrolysis, assisted by additional factors in relevant pathways, and the complex dissociates. Cargo is released where it is needed and the receptor can return for another round. Nuclear import receptors respond to RanGTP differently: in many classical import pathways, nuclear RanGTP promotes release of cargo that was bound in the cytoplasm. Thus RanGTP does not universally promote cargo binding or cargo release; the direction of its effect depends on the receptor cycle.

The energy used to maintain this cycle is associated with the conversion of GTP to GDP and the regeneration of the nucleotide gradient. A useful conceptual question is why the hydrolysis reaction is located predominantly on the cytoplasmic side. If it occurred efficiently while the export complex was still in the nucleus, cargo might be released prematurely and the receptor would fail to carry it to the other compartment. Conversely, if Ran remained in the GTP-bound state indefinitely after reaching the cytoplasm, the receptor might retain its cargo and fail to recycle normally. Correct spatial organization is thus as important as the biochemical ability to hydrolyze GTP.

The term gradient can cause confusion. In this context it refers to the relative distribution of Ran nucleotide states between the two compartments. It is not an electrical potential across the envelope like a membrane potential generated by ion channels. Nor does it mean that a molecule of RNA is physically dragged by free GTP moving from high to low concentration. Instead, a cargo-binding equilibrium in one compartment and a cargo-release reaction in the other bias the cycle. This is a general principle of molecular transport: energy can be used to change affinity rather than applied directly as a mechanical force to a cargo.

Predict the outcome if RCC1 function decreases while other components initially remain intact. Nuclear RanGDP-to-RanGTP exchange becomes less efficient. The concentration of RanGTP available to stabilize exportin cargo complexes falls, and assembly of these complexes becomes less favorable. Import reactions can also be affected because some of their nuclear cargo-release events depend on RanGTP. A decrease in nuclear export of tRNA under these conditions would be compatible with a Ran-dependent mechanism, but it would not establish that the effect is selective for tRNA. Ran supports multiple transport processes.

A different perturbation is to impair RanGAP function. RanGTP hydrolysis in the cytoplasm becomes less efficient. Export complexes that reach the cytoplasm may fail to release cargo normally, and the nuclear-cytoplasmic difference in nucleotide states may eventually diminish. Notice that a mutation increasing the persistence of the GTP-bound form does not necessarily improve transport. Too stable a binding state can impede turnover of a cycle, just as an enzyme that binds its product too tightly may catalyze fewer reactions per unit time. Efficient transport requires both assembly and disassembly.

The comparison with NXF1-mediated mRNA export should now be precise. Both routes use the same broad cellular boundary and both require reversible interactions with pore constituents. Both use energy to maintain asymmetry and recycle machinery. However, the main exportin route is coupled directly to compartment-specific RanGTP binding and hydrolysis, whereas the major NXF1-dependent mRNA route uses RNA–protein assembly and ATPase-mediated remodeling to generate directional release. An examination problem may use the same nuclear-pore image for both pathways and ask which intervention specifically impairs one route at its proximal transport step. The student should identify the molecular energy-coupling event rather than merely identify the destination.

7. Transfer RNA export: recognition of a folded RNA cargo

Transfer RNA solves a different biological problem from messenger RNA. An mRNA carries a sequence of codons that can be read by ribosomes. A tRNA is an adaptor that connects an anticodon with a particular amino acid when correctly charged. Its function depends critically on its folded structure and chemically defined ends. Nuclear genes encoding tRNAs are generally transcribed by RNA polymerase III, and their initial products often require trimming, addition of the CCA sequence when it is not encoded, nucleotide modification and, for selected tRNAs, intron removal. These processing reactions produce or prepare a compact RNA structure capable of interacting with the translation machinery.

Because a tRNA is a distinct class of cargo, its route out of the nucleus need not be the same as the bulk mRNA pathway. Exportin-t is a well-characterized tRNA export receptor in vertebrates, and Los1 is a corresponding receptor in budding yeast. It belongs to the karyopherin family and cooperates with nuclear RanGTP to bind appropriate tRNA cargo. Structural features of the tRNA, including aspects of its folded acceptor and T-arm regions and the state of its ends, contribute to recognition. This form of recognition illustrates a more direct relationship between the three-dimensional organization of an RNA and its transport competence than is usually found in the broad adaptor-mediated selection of diverse messenger RNAs.

Imagine two tRNA precursors whose primary sequences differ only at a terminal processing site. The first is trimmed to produce an appropriate acceptor stem and properly formed ends. The second retains a long unprocessed extension. Even if both RNAs contain the correct anticodon, the extension can disrupt the normal structural surface or make the ends unsuitable for exportin recognition. Therefore the anticodon is not the sole determinant of tRNA export. The receptor must recognize a molecule with a suitable overall organization. The ability of a tRNA to base-pair with a codon cannot compensate automatically for every defect in maturation or transport.

The export cycle begins in the nucleus, where a suitable tRNA and RanGTP cooperate in forming an exportin complex. Reversible interactions between the receptor and pore constituents permit translocation. At the cytoplasmic side, hydrolysis of Ran-bound GTP and subsequent rearrangement of the transport machinery promote dissociation. The liberated tRNA can participate in further maturation or other steps necessary for its use in translation. The receptor returns for reuse. The spatial nucleotide-state cycle discussed previously thus has a direct example in RNA biology rather than being merely a general scheme for protein trafficking.

The examination-level model should include exceptions. Not all tRNAs follow an absolutely identical sequence of maturation and transport. In budding yeast, the processing of certain intron-containing tRNAs involves splicing at the mitochondrial surface after their initial nuclear export. Cytoplasmic tRNAs can also return to the nucleus in some eukaryotic systems, for example during changes in nutritional conditions or through normal recycling pathways. Several export routes can overlap in function, and loss of Los1 or exportin-t does not necessarily eliminate all tRNA export. These observations are important because they demonstrate that a transport pathway can have multiple routes and that the destination of a molecule can change over its lifetime.

A common error is to infer that every tRNA must be fully modified, spliced and aminoacylated before it can exit the nucleus. Nuclear export requirements depend on the organism, tRNA species and pathway. In particular, aminoacylation is an important step in preparing tRNA for its decoding function, but it should not be stated as a universal requirement for initial nuclear export. The correct approach is to identify the specific route being discussed, and distinguish recognition for transport from recognition for amino acid charging or binding to the ribosome.

A tRNA export defect may produce a consequence different from a messenger RNA export defect. Suppose a cell has normal cytoplasmic levels of a particular messenger RNA but a shortage of cytoplasmic tRNAs. Protein synthesis can still decline because the ribosome lacks an adequate supply of aminoacylated adaptors. If specific tRNA species become limiting, the effects could differ among coding sequences according to their codon composition and the availability of alternative decoding tRNAs. This connects RNA transport with the later topics of genetic code and translational fidelity without implying that codon recognition itself occurs at the nuclear pore.

To distinguish the two classes experimentally, one could examine the location of representative mature tRNAs and polyadenylated mRNAs in the same cell. A selective increase in the nuclear fraction of tRNA with preserved bulk mRNA localization would support a cargo-specific defect. A general disturbance of nuclear pores or of cellular energy balance might instead alter both distributions. Because a tRNA may undergo additional processing after export, the probes used in such an experiment must distinguish the RNA species relevant to the hypothesis. A probe that recognizes an intron-containing precursor does not necessarily report the distribution of the same molecules as a probe specific for mature tRNA.

8. Different destinations for ribosomal, small nuclear and other RNAs

Ribosomal RNA illustrates why RNA export cannot be defined solely as delivery of a naked nucleotide chain. In eukaryotes, much of rRNA synthesis and early ribosomal subunit assembly takes place in the nucleolus. The rRNAs associate with ribosomal proteins, many of which have been synthesized in the cytoplasm and imported into the nucleus. The growing preribosomal particles must eventually reach the cytoplasm, where the small and large subunits complete relevant maturation steps and participate in translation. Export of such assemblies involves cargo-specific adapters and transport factors, including Ran-dependent export mechanisms in well-studied systems.

A representative example is the adaptor Nmd3, which helps link a pre-60S ribosomal subunit to the export receptor CRM1, also called exportin 1 or XPO1. This illustrates the difference between receptor and adaptor once again. The RNA-containing ribosomal precursor is not simply recognized as a generic mRNA. Rather, an appropriate assembly state permits interaction with an adaptor that communicates with the export machinery. Additional factors can participate, and export routes for the two ribosomal subunits should not be assumed to be identical. For this chapter, the important comparison is between transport of an assembled preribosomal particle, a folded tRNA and a typical messenger ribonucleoprotein.

If the large subunit export pathway fails, the presence of normal mRNA in the cytoplasm does not guarantee normal protein synthesis. The ribosome requires both subunits for productive translation. A cell may accumulate immature ribosomal particles in the nucleus, display an altered subunit balance, and synthesize fewer proteins. This phenotype must be distinguished from a defect in rRNA transcription itself. For example, an experiment comparing the abundance of newly synthesized rRNA with the distribution of ribosomal proteins and precursor particles could help separate failure to make the particle from failure to deliver it. The nucleolus is an assembly site, not the normal site of cytoplasmic protein translation.

Several small nuclear RNAs have a more surprising itinerary. Although their eventual role is associated with nuclear processes such as splicing, some spliceosomal snRNAs in animals undergo an initial cytoplasmic stage during their maturation. Representative RNA polymerase II-derived spliceosomal snRNAs are exported with the help of cap-associated factors and CRM1-dependent machinery. They associate with characteristic Sm proteins in the cytoplasm and undergo further maturation before the resulting snRNPs are imported into the nucleus. It is incorrect to assume that every RNA with a nuclear final function must remain inside the nucleus from the moment it is transcribed.

U6 snRNA provides a useful contrast because it is synthesized by RNA polymerase III and does not undergo the same canonical cytoplasmic Sm-core assembly route as the representative Pol II-derived spliceosomal snRNAs. Its processing and association with Lsm proteins follow a different organization. The distinction matters not because an examination student should memorize every accessory factor, but because the use of the term snRNA identifies a functional class rather than a universal pathway of transport. RNA identity, processing and assembly state jointly determine the relevant itinerary.

Other RNA molecules may be retained in the nucleus because their normal roles require that location. Small nucleolar RNAs contribute to the processing and modification of other RNAs in the nucleolus, and many long noncoding RNAs are enriched at nuclear locations where they perform their functions. Their nuclear enrichment should not automatically be diagnosed as defective export. In fact, inappropriate export of a normally retained RNA could itself disrupt function. Conversely, some noncoding RNAs act in the cytoplasm. The absence of an open reading frame encoding a conventional protein cannot be used as a reliable criterion for deciding whether a molecule will be exported.

The broad principle across these examples is that the cell controls RNA traffic according to function and assembly history, rather than following a single rule that all RNA must travel outward. Some molecules are intended for one-way delivery to the cytoplasm, some return to the nucleus, some remain mainly nuclear, and some move between compartments in response to physiological conditions. These diverse routes allow eukaryotic cells to organize the steps of gene expression in space as well as in time.

One way to reason about these routes is to ask two separate questions. What molecular entity crosses the nuclear pore, and what does that entity become afterward? For an mRNA the answer might be an export-competent mRNP that becomes a cytoplasmic translation substrate. For a tRNA, it may be a folded RNA bound to an exportin that becomes available for later charging or other maturation. For a preribosomal subunit, it is a large RNA–protein assembly that continues maturation. For certain snRNAs, it is a precursor that must return to the nucleus after interacting with cytoplasmic assembly machinery. Classifying by these transitions is more useful than merely drawing all RNA types on one arrow from nucleus to cytoplasm.

9. Cytoplasmic RNA localization: delivering the instruction rather than the protein

Arrival in the cytoplasm does not end the problem of spatial organization. The cytoplasm contains many regions with different structures and functions. A migrating cell builds a leading edge, a neuron possesses processes that can extend far from its cell body, and an egg can establish distinct regions that will contribute to different embryonic structures. A uniform concentration of every protein in such cells would not reproduce their functional asymmetry. One strategy is to synthesize proteins close to the sites at which they are needed by transporting the corresponding mRNAs.

A localization element is a sequence or structural feature of an RNA that contributes to its intracellular destination. Such an element is often called an RNA zipcode. Many known examples are in the 3′ untranslated region, although localization information can also occur elsewhere. The term untranslated does not mean functionless. A 3′ UTR can interact with RNA-binding proteins without changing the amino acid sequence of the protein encoded upstream. A mutation in this region may therefore alter the spatial organization of gene expression even if the protein sequence is completely unchanged.

The transport-competent cytoplasmic RNA particle contains RNA-binding proteins and, in many examples of directed transport, connections to cytoskeletal motors. Microtubules and actin filaments provide polarized tracks. Kinesins and dyneins are major microtubule motor families, whereas myosins move on actin. Motor proteins couple ATP-dependent conformational changes to movement along their tracks. The precise motor and adaptor combination depends on the cell type and RNA cargo. A student should not infer that every kinesin family member moves toward the plus end, or that an anatomical destination such as the cell periphery always corresponds to the same microtubule end in every cell. The organization of the tracks must be specified.

Directed motion is not the only way to establish enrichment. An RNA can diffuse through the cytoplasm and be captured at a destination by local binding partners. Alternatively, molecules can be produced or distributed relatively broadly but degraded preferentially outside one region. If the RNA is protected at a particular site, its steady-state abundance becomes higher there even without a motor delivering every molecule. These mechanisms can coexist. A motor can deliver cargo to a region, an anchoring protein can retain it, and local RNA stabilization can prolong its residence. A static localization image cannot identify which of these contributions was decisive.

The difference between directed transport and selective stabilization can be illustrated using a hypothetical cell divided into two equal regions. Suppose the same number of RNA molecules enters each region per minute, but the degradation rate is much lower in the first. At steady state the first region contains more RNA because its molecules remain there longer. An observer seeing the final distribution might conclude that RNA was transported preferentially toward that region, but this conclusion would be unjustified without measuring movement or lifetime. A localization phenotype is a spatial outcome, not a direct demonstration of the mechanism producing it.

One well-known model is localization of beta-actin mRNA toward the leading edge of motile fibroblasts. A zipcode in the 3′ UTR contributes to association with zipcode-binding proteins, notably ZBP1. Localization of this message helps coordinate actin production with regions of cell movement. Deleting or altering the localization element can disrupt the distribution of the RNA without changing the actin coding region. The consequences can include altered local protein availability and cell polarity. However, actin protein itself also has mechanisms for assembly and redistribution, so loss of RNA localization should not be interpreted as proof that no actin can ever reach the leading edge.

A second model arises during early development of Drosophila. Localization of maternal bicoid mRNA toward the anterior region of the egg establishes a spatial source of Bicoid protein after translation begins. The protein distribution contributes to developmental patterning. Here, transporting or anchoring the message can specify where a regulatory protein first appears, which influences the formation of positional information. The biological implication differs from the fibroblast example: rather than supporting a continually moving cell edge, maternal RNA localization helps organize the body plan of a developing organism. Both examples nevertheless demonstrate the same broad principle of establishing local protein production through RNA placement.

Neurons illustrate the importance of distance and time. Some RNAs are delivered into axons or dendrites where local translation can contribute to cellular responses. A signal acting at a distant process need not always wait for a new protein to be synthesized near the nucleus and transported from the cell body. Local RNA can provide an available template whose translation is regulated by conditions at the destination. Not every neuronal protein is made locally, and RNA localization is only one aspect of neuronal organization. The point is that placing the instruction close to its site of use can change the temporal and spatial properties of a response.

An RNA that is actively transported can remain translationally repressed during transit. This may prevent premature synthesis of the encoded protein away from its intended destination. Upon arrival, changes in RNA-binding proteins or local signaling can permit translation. The RNA may then remain anchored or may be released again. Localization, anchoring and translation are distinct steps. A mutation affecting an anchoring protein might allow RNA to arrive transiently but fail to accumulate. A mutation affecting translational activation might allow normal RNA enrichment but produce insufficient local protein. A motor defect might prevent RNA from reaching the region in the first place. Each would require different evidence for diagnosis.

The endoplasmic reticulum supplies another important comparison. Many messages encoding secreted or membrane proteins become associated with ribosomes at the ER because the emerging polypeptide contains a signal recognized by the signal recognition particle. This is a protein-targeting mechanism that can bring the translating mRNA–ribosome complex to the ER. It should not be confused with a zipcode-mediated mechanism in which the RNA sequence or structure itself is recognized for localization. Some RNAs also associate with the ER through other interactions. The distinction illustrates how an RNA can acquire a spatial distribution either directly through RNA-binding machinery or indirectly through the protein being synthesized from it.

For Part C reasoning, always ask which property a mutation changes. A coding-region mutation that abolishes a signal peptide can disturb cotranslational targeting even if a localization element remains intact. A 3′ UTR mutation can disrupt an RNA-binding site without modifying the encoded peptide. A motor mutation can alter many cargoes and cell structures simultaneously. An RNA-binding protein mutant that specifically loses affinity for a zipcode may have a narrower effect. Appropriate control experiments are necessary to separate direct changes in RNA positioning from secondary changes in cell organization or RNA abundance.

10. Nuclear retention and the decision between repair, export and removal

RNA molecules differ in how long they remain in the nucleus. Some are retained because their normal function requires a nuclear location. Others are delayed because their assembly is still in progress. Still others fail to leave because they are defective and interact with quality-control machinery. These categories are biologically different even if the molecules appear in the same compartment under a microscope. A good interpretation begins by identifying what a particular transcript normally does and what processing state the experiment has detected.

Consider an intron-containing pre-mRNA while its last intron is still being removed. It is not necessarily defective; it may simply be an intermediate on a normal pathway. If splicing proceeds, the RNA can acquire the appropriate protein associations and enter the export pathway. However, if the splice donor is mutated so that removal becomes inefficient, the transcript may spend longer in a state that is not suited for export. Retention provides time during which processing can potentially be completed, but it also limits the delivery of an inappropriate template to the translation machinery. A delay thus has different implications depending on whether it is reversible and whether the transcript can eventually mature.

Retention can arise through more than one mechanism. Inefficient loading of an export receptor can leave a molecule unable to interact effectively with the pore. Alternatively, RNA-associated proteins can actively promote nuclear retention even when some export-related factors are present. Incompletely processed messages may remain near their transcription sites, associate with nuclear structures, or be delayed near the nuclear basket of the pore. The relative importance of these mechanisms differs across organisms and RNA substrates. The result is not a perfectly uniform gate that examines all messages in precisely the same way.

An important model system is budding yeast, where the nuclear basket-associated protein Mlp1 has been implicated in restricting the escape of some unspliced transcripts. The mammalian nuclear basket has corresponding proteins, including TPR, but the details of nuclear surveillance cannot be transferred uncritically from one species to another. The examination-level lesson is that components located at the nuclear entrance to a pore can contribute to cargo discrimination as well as to physical transport. A defect in such a retention mechanism could increase the cytoplasmic abundance of certain abnormal RNAs even when normal messages still cross the pore.

When a molecule cannot complete its required processing, degradation provides a way to prevent indefinite accumulation. The nuclear RNA exosome is an important RNA-processing and decay complex. In this context, exosome refers to a multiprotein complex involved in RNA metabolism; it is not an extracellular membrane vesicle. Its associated catalytic activities can degrade RNA from the 3′ end and, in relevant eukaryotic systems, perform internal cleavage through particular catalytic components. Accessory proteins help bring RNA substrates to the complex and facilitate processing of structured molecules. The composition of the accessory machinery differs among yeast, mammals and other organisms.

A nuclease cannot necessarily distinguish a valuable message from a defective message just by recognizing the chemical structure of a phosphodiester bond. The same kinds of bonds occur in both molecules. Selectivity instead depends in part on the accessibility of RNA ends, RNA-binding proteins, particular sequence or structural features, and the pathways that deliver the RNA to a nuclease. This is one reason why correct mRNP assembly protects an RNA in a functional manner. The proteins associated with the RNA can influence not only its transport but also its vulnerability to degradation.

The exosome illustrates another important distinction between processing and complete destruction. A nuclease can remove a specific extension from an RNA precursor, thereby generating a functional product, or it can continue degrading an RNA until the original molecule no longer exists as a usable template. Whether the final result is maturation or elimination depends on the substrate, the enzymes involved and regulation of their access. Accordingly, detecting a nuclease associated with an RNA does not by itself establish that the RNA is being destroyed. One must determine whether a correctly formed product appears or whether the original RNA and its useful products disappear.

A defective 3′ processing signal can illustrate the competition between maturation and surveillance. Normally, a suitable cleavage event helps establish the transcript's 3′ end and contributes to formation of a competent particle. If cleavage fails, the transcript may extend beyond its normal endpoint and remain associated with transcription or nuclear processing machinery. Its ability to recruit the correct export factors may be diminished, and it may become susceptible to nuclear degradation. Several outcomes are possible: some molecules may use an alternative cleavage site, some may accumulate transiently, and others may be destroyed. A single mutation does not guarantee that every transcript produced from the gene follows exactly the same fate.

Retained introns require similarly careful interpretation. Some intron-retaining RNAs are unstable or poorly exported; others are regulated transcripts that remain nuclear until conditions favor processing. Still others can appear in the cytoplasm and may be translated or recognized by cytoplasmic surveillance. A retained intron is not always an accidental defect, and the presence of an intron in a cytoplasmic RNA is not proof that the nuclear quality-control system has completely failed. The effect depends on whether intron retention is normally part of the gene's expression pattern and whether the resulting transcript remains functional.

The concept of quality control also does not mean that RNA production is perfect in a healthy cell. Gene expression generates intermediate and sometimes nonproductive molecules. Surveillance reduces their harmful consequences and helps maintain useful RNA populations. Even normal RNA species eventually undergo turnover. The cell does not need to preserve each correctly synthesized molecule indefinitely; it needs to maintain sufficient functional molecules while responding to changing conditions. A nuclear decay pathway that removes abnormal products therefore contributes both to fidelity and to the regulation of the size of the RNA population.

Consider a mutant lacking an important nuclear decay activity. Defective nuclear RNAs may increase in abundance because they are no longer efficiently degraded. Their export need not improve. If the mutation affects the nuclease but leaves retention intact, much of the abnormal RNA may simply accumulate inside the nucleus. A different mutant that compromises retention might allow some defective RNA to reach the cytoplasm, where it can encounter additional surveillance. The two mutants distinguish the gate that delays or prevents transport from the machinery that destroys a substrate. These functions are often coupled, but they are not synonymous.

This reasoning also explains why a strong nuclear signal may not appear in every processing or export mutant. If defective transcripts are degraded very quickly, their nuclear concentration may remain low even though their probability of reaching the cytoplasm is severely reduced. When a second mutation slows degradation, the otherwise hidden population may become visible. Consequently, failure to observe accumulated nuclear RNA does not establish that export is normal. It is necessary to consider how production, retention, maturation and decay jointly determine the number of molecules detected.

11. Translation-dependent surveillance: nonsense-mediated mRNA decay

The preceding mechanisms address whether an RNA is prepared for export. They cannot guarantee that every exported message will direct synthesis of the correct protein. Some defects become apparent only when the message is read by a ribosome. A premature termination codon is an important example. A base substitution can convert an amino acid codon into a stop codon, or a frameshift can bring an inappropriate stop into the reading frame. Alternative splicing can also produce a message with an early stop without requiring a permanent change in genomic DNA. Translation-dependent quality control helps limit the consequences of such messages.

Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, usually abbreviated NMD, is a eukaryotic surveillance pathway that reduces the abundance of many transcripts whose translation terminates in an inappropriate context. The word nonsense refers to the termination signal rather than to a message that lacks all meaningful information. A premature stop codon can be read correctly as a stop by the translation apparatus while still being inappropriate for the intended full-length protein. If the message remains abundant, translation may produce shortened polypeptides that lack necessary domains. NMD can reduce repeated synthesis of these abnormal products by lowering the availability of the RNA template.

The crucial feature of NMD is its dependence on translation in the canonical pathway. A splice junction or a nucleotide substitution does not by itself cause the pathway to operate without the relevant molecular context. The ribosome encounters a termination codon, and proteins associated with the RNA and translation termination contribute to deciding whether that event is appropriate. Thus nuclear RNA retention and NMD represent distinguishable opportunities for surveillance. A defective message that never reaches a translating ribosome may be eliminated through another route; it should not automatically be assigned to canonical NMD.

In mammals, exon junction complexes provide a particularly useful model for explaining why the position of a stop codon matters. During splicing, complexes are generally deposited about 20–24 nucleotides upstream of exon–exon junctions. When a ribosome translates through the coding sequence, it can displace complexes that lie in its path. If the normal stop codon is encountered after all relevant coding-region complexes have been displaced and there is no suitable downstream NMD-promoting context, the message can undergo ordinary termination and remain available for further translation.

Now suppose that a stop codon appears sufficiently far upstream of a downstream exon junction. A ribosome terminates before reaching the downstream complex. An exon junction complex remaining beyond the terminating ribosome can help recruit or stabilize interactions involving NMD factors. A widely used empirical mammalian rule is that a stop located more than roughly 50–55 nucleotides upstream of the final exon–exon junction often promotes NMD. The distance is measured to the junction, not to the 3′ end of the poly(A) tail and not directly to the center of the exon junction complex. It reflects the relationship between the terminating ribosome, the position of the junction-associated complex and the ability of the complex to remain downstream.

UPF1 is a central NMD factor and an ATP-dependent RNA helicase. UPF2 and UPF3 participate in many pathways of NMD, linking RNA-associated information to the surveillance machinery. In a commonly taught mammalian model, the termination event involves the eukaryotic release factors eRF1 and eRF3, and appropriate interactions stimulate phosphorylation of UPF1 by the kinase SMG1. Phosphorylated UPF1 can promote recruitment of RNA degradation machinery. Different downstream routes can involve internal cleavage, deadenylation, decapping and exonucleolytic degradation. This framework explains how an event in translation becomes coupled to removal of the message without requiring an extensive catalogue of all pathway proteins.

The mechanism has an important negative consequence for interpretation of protein measurements. Suppose an early stop codon is introduced into a gene and very little shortened protein is detected. One might initially think that the mutant RNA is never translated. In fact, limited translation may have occurred and initiated NMD, reducing the number of templates available for later translation. Alternatively, the shortened protein may be intrinsically unstable. A measurement of protein abundance alone cannot distinguish these explanations. Measurements of RNA synthesis, RNA stability and translation are needed to understand which layer of surveillance contributed.

The junction-distance rule is a useful predictor, not a universal law. A premature stop in the last exon often escapes the classic downstream-EJC-dependent mechanism because there is no later splice junction. A stop close to the final junction may also escape if the relevant complex is displaced or not retained. Other features of a transcript, including the termination environment and 3′ untranslated region, can influence NMD independently of a downstream exon junction. The pathway differs among species, and budding yeast does not follow the mammalian EJC model as a universal requirement. Therefore an examination question using the 50–55 nucleotide rule should specify an appropriate mammalian context and should not demand an unconditional prediction for all genes.

NMD can also influence the abundance of some normal cellular transcripts. Some regulated alternative splice forms contain termination contexts that promote NMD, making the combination of splicing and decay a means of controlling gene expression. This does not mean that every transcript targeted by NMD originates from a mutation or that every such event is harmful. The pathway responds to molecular features, and cells can exploit these features for regulation. The detailed control of particular gene circuits belongs to a later discussion, but recognizing this broader role prevents an overly narrow definition of RNA quality control.

A practical way to test an NMD hypothesis is to compare otherwise similar reporter RNAs with different stop-codon positions. If the message bearing an early stop decreases in abundance, whereas an otherwise matched message with a stop in the final exon remains relatively stable, the result is consistent with a positional effect. Reducing UPF1 activity or preventing relevant translation can help test whether the loss depends on NMD. These interventions have their own broader cellular consequences, so appropriate controls and rescue experiments strengthen the conclusion. The essential logic is to show that the RNA is produced, reaches a context in which it can be translated, and undergoes accelerated loss because of the surveillance mechanism.

The distinction between nuclear and cytoplasmic defects becomes particularly important when a mutation changes splicing. An altered splice product may retain an intron, be poorly exported, or contain a premature stop after successful export. It can therefore be sensitive to nuclear surveillance, NMD, or both in different fractions of the RNA population. The student should resist choosing one mechanism merely because the mutation is a splice-site mutation. The relevant evidence is the actual processed RNA sequence, its location, the timing of its loss, and its dependence on translation and specific quality-control factors.

12. RNA degradation as a regulated fate rather than an accidental disappearance

When discussing RNA surveillance it is easy to confuse the trigger for degradation with the chemical reactions that carry it out. The trigger identifies an RNA or a state of the RNA as suitable for removal. Nucleases execute cleavage and degradation. A single nuclease can be used by multiple recognition pathways, and a recognition pathway can use more than one route of degradation. The molecular logic of specificity is therefore not always located in the catalytic enzyme itself.

An endonuclease cuts within an RNA chain. An exonuclease removes nucleotides progressively from an accessible end. These terms describe the position and direction of the reaction rather than whether the RNA was originally normal or defective. In many eukaryotic cytoplasmic pathways, shortening of a poly(A) tail can facilitate subsequent degradation. Removal of the cap creates a 5′ end accessible to suitable 5′-to-3′ exonucleases such as XRN1. Other substrates can be degraded from the 3′ end by exosome-associated machinery. Internal cleavage can generate new ends that become substrates for both directions of exonucleolytic removal.

The order of reactions is not identical for every RNA or every surveillance pathway. For example, an internal cleavage event can bypass the need to wait for complete shortening of a poly(A) tail before the fragments are degraded. Conversely, normal messages can undergo regulated deadenylation and decay without a premature stop codon. A decrease in poly(A) signal does not necessarily mean that the entire RNA has already disappeared. It could reflect shortening of the tail below the detection threshold of a particular probe or a transition to a decay intermediate.

Quality control also extends beyond recognition of early termination. A ribosome can stall on an RNA or reach the end of a coding template without encountering a proper stop. No-go and nonstop surveillance refer to other classes of problems associated with translation. Their triggers differ from a premature termination event recognized by NMD. At the level required for this chapter, it is enough to distinguish the nature of the defect and recognize that cells have several ways to monitor whether an RNA is functioning appropriately. Detailed pathways of ribosome rescue and protein quality control belong to the later translation and gene-regulation chapters.

The overall biological advantage is that errors need not have permanent effects. A cell can destroy a defective RNA while retaining the DNA sequence from which it was transcribed. If the defective molecule arose through a temporary error in processing, later transcripts may be normal. If the genomic sequence itself contains a mutation, repeated transcription can generate new defective molecules, and surveillance may continue to remove them. RNA decay does not repair the DNA mutation. It changes the amount of mutant information expressed as RNA and potentially protein. This is a useful connection with earlier chapters on DNA repair and later chapters on genetics.

When a decay pathway is inhibited, more RNA can be detected even if the rate of transcription remains constant. When an export pathway is inhibited, nuclear RNA may increase, cytoplasmic RNA may decrease, or both may decline if nuclear degradation is enhanced. These different outcomes illustrate why RNA quantity is not a direct measure of transcription and why localization measurements should be combined with temporal information. The following sections use simple quantitative and experimental reasoning to distinguish such possibilities.

13. Classical observations and experimental evidence for RNA transport

The transport mechanisms described above were established by combining evidence of several kinds. Direct visualization can show that an RNA-containing assembly occupies a pore. Genetic perturbation can reveal a requirement for a particular protein. Biochemical association can identify possible physical interactions, and controlled alteration of a cargo can test the importance of its processing state or localization sequence. None of these methods by itself answers every question. A strong mechanism emerges when observations that address different stages of the process are consistent with one another.

The unusually large messenger RNPs produced at Balbiani ring loci in the salivary glands of Chironomus provided a valuable model for direct observation. Their size makes them suitable for examination using electron microscopy. Research in the 1990s, including studies by Mehlin, Daneholt and colleagues, examined the organization of these particles as they moved through nuclear pores. The particle adopted different shapes at different positions relative to the pore, and its extended form during passage could be related to its compact form in the nucleoplasm. The work provided visible evidence that a large RNA–protein particle changes its organization during export. The observation concerns a particular well-studied cargo and should not be converted into a claim that all mRNAs necessarily enter every pore in an identical orientation or configuration.

Visa and colleagues examined the cap-binding component CBP20 in Balbiani ring particles in a 1996 immunoelectron microscopy study. An antibody directed against a protein can provide information about the location of that protein in prepared cells. Association of CBP20 with the RNA-containing particle was detected during nuclear stages and during movement across the pore. This result supports the idea that a protein recruited during transcription can remain associated with the cargo across subsequent events. It does not establish that CBP20 is sufficient to export a message by itself. Nor does finding the protein at the pore prove that it supplies energy for passage. The strength of the observation lies in connecting stages that might otherwise have been treated as unrelated.

Genetic and biochemical analysis supplied a complementary line of evidence for the export receptor. Segref and colleagues described the yeast protein Mex67 in a 1997 study of nuclear messenger RNA export. Its association with RNA and nuclear pores, together with the behavior of mutant cells, supported a direct role in transport. The combination is particularly informative because a protein located at a pore could otherwise be involved in pore assembly, structural maintenance or the movement of entirely different cargo. Conversely, an RNA-binding protein could be involved in splicing or decay without ever contacting the transport channel. Evidence connecting the protein to both RNA cargo and the pore narrows the possible explanations.

A frequently used approach in such genetic analysis is to compare the cellular distribution of polyadenylated RNA in cells carrying a functional factor and cells in which its activity is impaired. If polyadenylated RNA accumulates in the nucleus after the perturbation, this suggests a defect in normal delivery to the cytoplasm or in a closely coupled step. Additional evidence is needed to exclude changes in transcript synthesis, maturation, tail length or decay. A temperature-sensitive allele, for example, may allow an investigator to compare cells before and after a shift to a restrictive temperature, but the temperature change can also affect other processes. Matched wild-type cells exposed to the same conditions are essential for meaningful interpretation.

Studies of tRNA export in 1998 demonstrated the value of a different experimental system. Arts, Fornerod and Mattaj characterized a protein capable of interacting with tRNA in a RanGTP-dependent manner. Their work included analysis of transport in Xenopus oocytes, whose large nuclei provide a convenient model for examining movement between nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments. Providing additional receptor promoted export in a cargo-selective fashion and relieved competition in the system. Related work by Kutay and colleagues independently identified exportin-t. These observations support the existence of a dedicated transport receptor for tRNA rather than a single universal exporter for all RNA molecules.

The distinction between competition and simple concentration effects is important. If a limited transport factor is occupied by excess suitable cargo, another substrate using the same factor may be transported less efficiently. Increasing the concentration of the limiting factor can relieve this competition. In contrast, if an unrelated cargo follows an entirely independent route, a selective competition effect is less expected, although very large perturbations can still alter shared pore capacity or other cellular resources. Competition experiments are therefore most useful when paired with evidence of receptor binding and specificity.

Classic RNA localization work also depended on altering the cargo rather than just observing it. In 1994, Kislauskis and colleagues examined the sequences responsible for intracellular localization of beta-actin mRNA using chimeric reporter messages and approaches that interfered with the RNA localization element. Changes in localization and cell organization could occur without a corresponding loss of total actin RNA or protein. This distinction illustrates why amount and location are independent properties. A molecule can be abundant and still fail to contribute efficiently to a spatially organized biological process.

A later study by Ross and colleagues characterized a protein recognizing the beta-actin zipcode. Mutating an important part of the RNA element impaired both its interaction with the protein and its localization function. These findings help connect a cis-acting RNA feature with a trans-acting RNA-binding factor. Here cis means that the relevant element is physically part of the cargo RNA; trans refers to a separately produced factor capable of interacting with suitable cargoes. A reporter experiment tests whether the information travels with the RNA itself, while a protein perturbation tests the machinery that reads or responds to that information.

These examples together suggest a general strategy for understanding intracellular transport. Localization by microscopy establishes where a cargo is found. Altering a receptor or an RNA element tests whether that component contributes to the observed distribution. Binding assays can test a proposed interaction. Restoring the original component can strengthen a causal interpretation. Comparing cargoes that use different pathways tests specificity. The combination is powerful because every method has alternative explanations when used alone. Students should apply this pattern to unfamiliar experimental situations rather than memorize historical names as disconnected facts.

14. A quantitative model of nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA abundance

Transport can be analyzed using a simple balance of molecular production and loss. Let N represent the mean number of molecules of a particular export-competent RNA in the nucleus of a cell, and C represent the mean number of corresponding molecules in the cytoplasm. These variables describe counts, not concentrations. Let s be the rate at which molecules enter the nuclear export-competent pool, expressed as molecules per minute. In this model the rate includes the net effect of successful synthesis and maturation. It is therefore not necessarily equal to the rate at which RNA polymerase initiates transcription.

Assume that export from this nuclear pool follows an effective first-order rate law with constant k_e, measured in inverse minutes. Then the number leaving the nuclear pool through export per minute is k_e N. Let nuclear decay also follow a first-order law with constant k_n. Its flux is k_n N. In the cytoplasm let the decay constant be k_c, so that loss from the cytoplasmic pool occurs at rate k_c C. For this introductory model we neglect return of the exported RNA to the nucleus, cell division, changing volumes and variations in receptor availability. The aim is not to describe every RNA but to understand the consequences of a few separate processes.

Conservation of molecules in each pool gives dN/dt = s − k_e N − k_n N and dC/dt = k_e N − k_c C. The first equation states that nuclear abundance increases through entry and decreases through export or nuclear decay. The second states that cytoplasmic abundance increases through export and decreases through cytoplasmic decay. Adding the equations gives d(N + C)/dt = s − k_n N − k_c C. The export term cancels because a successful export event moves a molecule between pools rather than destroying it. This cancellation is a central insight. If an experiment measures only the total number of RNA molecules, some effects of transport can be hidden by redistribution.

At steady state, average counts no longer change with time, although individual molecules continue to be produced, exported and degraded. Setting the first derivative equal to zero gives s = (k_e + k_n)N. Therefore N_ss = s/(k_e + k_n), where the subscript ss indicates steady state. Setting the second derivative equal to zero gives k_e N = k_c C. Substituting the first result yields C_ss = s k_e/[k_c(k_e + k_n)]. The units are consistent: s has units of molecules per minute, and division by an inverse-minute rate produces a molecular count.

The ratio C_ss/N_ss is especially useful in this model. Dividing the second result by the first cancels the common factor s/(k_e + k_n), leaving C_ss/N_ss = k_e/k_c. This equation demonstrates that a low cytoplasmic-to-nuclear count ratio can result from inefficient export or rapid cytoplasmic degradation. It does not uniquely diagnose a defect in transport. If an independent measurement shows that k_c is unchanged, the ratio can provide information about relative export efficiency under the assumptions of the model. If k_c is not known, the same ratio can be generated by many combinations of export and decay rates.

As an example, consider hypothetical educational values s = 60 molecules per minute, k_e = 0.20 per minute, k_n = 0.10 per minute and k_c = 0.05 per minute. Nuclear steady-state abundance is 60/(0.20 + 0.10) = 200 molecules. The export flux is 0.20 × 200 = 40 molecules per minute, and the nuclear decay flux is 0.10 × 200 = 20 molecules per minute. Their sum equals the input of 60. Cytoplasmic abundance is 40/0.05 = 800 molecules. The combined count is 1,000 and the ratio C/N equals four. The interpretation is that more molecules are present in the cytoplasm because the balance of arrival and disappearance permits a relatively large cytoplasmic pool.

Now reduce k_e to 0.05 per minute while leaving all other parameters unchanged. The nuclear count becomes 60/0.15 = 400 molecules. Export flux becomes 0.05 × 400 = 20 molecules per minute; nuclear decay flux becomes 40 molecules per minute. Cytoplasmic abundance becomes 20/0.05 = 400 molecules. The total is now 800 rather than 1,000, and the C/N ratio falls from four to one. Although export is not itself destruction, slower export exposes more molecules to competition with nuclear degradation, lowering the combined steady-state abundance. A student who thinks that export inhibition must always leave total RNA unchanged will miss this indirect consequence.

The role of nuclear decay can be demonstrated by setting k_n equal to zero. Provided k_e is greater than zero, N_ss becomes s/k_e and the steady-state export flux is exactly s. Cytoplasmic abundance is s/k_c regardless of the value of k_e. In this idealized limiting case, slowing export increases the nuclear pool sufficiently that the eventual export flux still matches production. A transient reduction in cytoplasmic delivery can occur while the nuclear pool grows. At a later steady state, however, the cytoplasmic count can recover. Real cells have finite capacity, nuclear surveillance, time-dependent responses and other losses, so this mathematical limit is not a guarantee that slowing export has no biological effect.

An instructive alternative is to increase cytoplasmic decay rather than alter transport. Starting from the first numerical example, change k_c from 0.05 to 0.20 per minute. The nuclear count remains 200 because neither input nor nuclear losses changed. Cytoplasmic count becomes 40/0.20 = 200. The ratio falls from four to one, exactly as in the earlier export-defect example, but the nuclear abundance differs. Thus a ratio alone does not distinguish the two mechanisms, whereas absolute compartment-specific counts plus information on synthesis and lifetime are more informative.

The model also provides a probability interpretation of nuclear fate. If each molecule in the nuclear pool experiences two independent competing first-order hazards, export at rate k_e and decay at rate k_n, its probability of eventual export is k_e/(k_e + k_n). The probability of nuclear destruction is k_n/(k_e + k_n). These probabilities sum to one. In the initial example the export probability is two-thirds. After reducing k_e to 0.05 per minute it becomes one-third. This formulation explains why a longer residence in a compartment can lower the yield of cytoplasmic molecules when surveillance competes with export.

For a single compartment losing molecules according to dM/dt = −kM with no new input, integration gives M(t) = M(0) exp(−kt). The half-life t_1/2 is the time at which M(t) is half of M(0), giving t_1/2 = ln(2)/k. In a cytoplasmic pool with k_c = 0.05 per minute and no continued influx, the predicted half-life is approximately 13.86 minutes. However, stopping transcription is not necessarily equivalent to stopping influx into the cytoplasm. Previously synthesized nuclear RNA can continue to be exported. A decay curve for total or cytoplasmic RNA after transcription inhibition may therefore contain more than one kinetic contribution.

In a nucleus with constant competing export and decay rates and no new input, the half-time for disappearance from that nuclear pool is ln(2)/(k_e + k_n). For the initial example it is approximately 2.31 minutes. This is not the chemical half-life of the RNA against degradation. Some molecules disappear from the nuclear measurement because they move to the cytoplasm intact. The distinction between residence time and degradation lifetime is especially important in pulse-chase experiments. A rapid fall in nuclear labeled RNA could be evidence of efficient export, rapid nuclear decay or a combination.

All of these results depend on the simplifying assumption that the effective rates are constant and proportional to pool size. If export factors become saturated, the export flux need not continue increasing linearly with N. If the cell is dividing, dilution introduces additional apparent losses. If multiple maturation intermediates are included, more pools are required. If the measured values are concentrations rather than counts, nuclear and cytoplasmic volumes must be taken into account. In particular, a bright nuclear fluorescence signal per unit area does not directly provide a nuclear-to-cytoplasmic molecular count ratio without suitable calibration. The simple balance model is valuable precisely because it exposes the assumptions a more detailed interpretation would need to examine.

15. Distinguishing synthesis, processing, export and stability experimentally

An experiment designed to investigate low protein abundance should first establish which level of gene expression is changing. A lower rate of transcription can reduce the number of RNA molecules available for every later step. Inefficient maturation can reduce the entry of transcripts into the export-competent nuclear pool. Inefficient export can shift their spatial distribution. Increased decay can shorten their residence in either compartment. Defective translation can reduce protein output even if the RNA is abundant at the correct location. A single endpoint measurement cannot generally distinguish all these cases.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization uses labeled probes that recognize complementary sequences in RNA. For an individual transcript, probes against exonic sequences can identify molecules carrying those sequences, while probes against intronic regions can help reveal nascent or intron-retaining RNAs. If multiple probes bind each target, individual RNA molecules can in suitable experiments appear as separate fluorescent spots. An exon probe alone cannot guarantee that a molecule is fully spliced because the exons are also present in many precursor molecules. A probe spanning a particular exon–exon junction can help identify a particular splice product, subject to probe performance and sequence specificity.

Oligo(dT) probes can be used to examine the broad distribution of polyadenylated RNA. A strong nuclear increase in such a signal after an export-factor perturbation is a useful clue, but it is not a complete inventory of all RNA classes. tRNAs, most mature rRNAs and many other RNA species lack the long poly(A) tails typical of many messenger RNAs. Furthermore, altered tail lengths can influence the hybridization signal. A gene-specific exon probe or other independent measurement is useful when deciding whether a change reflects redistribution of molecules rather than a change in the properties of their tails.

Subcellular fractionation provides a complementary approach. Nuclear and cytoplasmic material can be separated and the RNA content examined, for example by reverse transcription followed by quantitative PCR. The major interpretive challenge is that no physical separation is automatically pure. A nuclear RNA detected in a cytoplasmic preparation could arise from contamination during separation. Conversely, cytoplasmic material can remain associated with a nuclear fraction. Appropriate compartment markers, controls for recovery, and normalization to the number of cells or calibrated external standards are needed if absolute changes are being interpreted. Fractions should not simply be normalized to unrelated markers that themselves change under the perturbation.

A pulse-chase experiment helps identify the temporal order of events. During a brief pulse, newly synthesized RNA is labeled. During the chase, additional labeling ceases or is reduced so that the previously labeled population can be followed. In a simple export model, the label appears first in the nuclear compartment and later in the cytoplasm. If a mutant shows normal early nuclear labeling but much less later cytoplasmic label, a transport defect or loss of labeled RNA before or after arrival is plausible. Measuring the total labeled material at successive times helps distinguish redistribution from destruction. If most labeled material remains in the nuclear fraction, the evidence supports delayed export more directly than if all label simply disappears.

Consider four hypothetical datasets measured in comparable cells after a short labeling pulse. At the first sampling time all have 100 labeled nuclear molecules and no measurable labeled cytoplasmic molecules. Later, the control has 20 nuclear and 60 cytoplasmic molecules; the remainder are no longer detected. Mutant A has 70 nuclear and 10 cytoplasmic molecules. Mutant B has 15 nuclear and 5 cytoplasmic molecules. Mutant C has 20 nuclear and 60 cytoplasmic molecules but much lower protein abundance from the same message. Mutant A is compatible with nuclear retention or a slow export step. Mutant B shows greater loss of labeled material and requires investigation of decay in both compartments. Mutant C does not demonstrate a defect in RNA delivery in this particular experiment; a translation or protein-stability defect is worth examining.

Mutations in RNA processing sequences can be studied with paired reporter genes that differ only at the candidate element. If a splice donor is altered, measuring intron retention and the cellular distribution of the transcript provides more insight than measuring reporter protein alone. A decrease in cytoplasmic RNA accompanied by increased intron-containing nuclear RNA supports the possibility that a processing defect has impaired delivery. Restoring the splice donor and recovering normal maturation and distribution strengthens the conclusion. However, the mutation may also influence the abundance of alternative products, so both the identity and amount of the RNA must be examined.

Experiments on a zipcode similarly benefit from separating necessity from sufficiency. If deleting a candidate element from an otherwise localized RNA prevents enrichment at the destination, the element may be necessary for that behavior in the tested context. If adding the element to a reporter that is normally diffuse causes it to become localized, the element is sufficient to contribute to localization in that reporter context. Neither result implies that the element functions independently of all trans-acting proteins or of cellular organization. Mutations that disrupt RNA structure may have effects beyond changing one recognition site, making a restoration experiment particularly useful.

To distinguish active transport from diffusion and anchoring, a time-resolved observation is more valuable than a single fixed image. Directionally persistent movement along a cytoskeletal structure and sensitivity to an appropriate motor perturbation support a motor-dependent mechanism. However, changing cytoskeletal organization can also alter cell shape and the position of anchoring sites. A decrease in destination enrichment therefore requires interpretation in conjunction with particle trajectories, local residence times and general cell integrity. If arrival continues but residence at the destination becomes shorter, defective anchoring may be a better explanation than failure of transport.

Finally, a rescue experiment tests whether restoring a candidate function reverses a phenotype. Suppose removal of an RNA-binding protein results in nuclear accumulation of mature mRNA. Re-expression of the functional protein may restore cytoplasmic delivery. A variant that binds RNA but cannot recruit the export receptor may fail to rescue. This comparison is more mechanistically informative than restoration with the functional protein alone, because it tests the proposed interaction. It remains necessary to verify that both variants are expressed and located appropriately. A failure to rescue could otherwise reflect instability of the variant rather than loss of the interaction under study.

The general lesson is to match the readout to the proposed molecular step. The rate of transcription, the appearance of mature RNA, the association of transport factors, the distribution between compartments, the residence at a particular cytoplasmic location and the rate of disappearance are distinct observables. Combining several of them reduces ambiguity. This is why difficult questions in RNA biology often provide data from more than one method and ask for the conclusion supported by their intersection rather than by the most dramatic measurement alone.

  4. 100
Answer and reasoning

A. 200 — N=s/(k_e+k_n)=60/0.3=200 molecules. Dividing by export alone ignores nuclear loss; adding rates in the numerator is dimensionally wrong.

37. Numerical — For N=200 molecules, k_e=0.2/min and k_c=0.05/min, find the steady-state cytoplasmic count.

  1. 200
  2. 800
  3. 4,000
  4. 40
Answer and reasoning

B. 800 — Export flux is 40 molecules/min. At steady state C=40/0.05=800. The value 40 is a flux, not a count.

38. Numerical — Two cells each have C/N=2. Cell A has k_c=0.10/min; cell B has k_c=0.04/min. Under the model, which export rate is correct?

  1. A 0.20/min; B 0.08/min
  2. A 0.05/min; B 0.02/min
  3. Both 2/min
  4. Both 0.1/min
Answer and reasoning

A. A 0.20/min; B 0.08/min — C/N=k_e/k_c, so k_e=2k_c. An identical ratio does not imply identical rates if cytoplasmic decay differs.

39. Numerical — A nuclear molecule faces k_e=0.15/min and k_n=0.05/min as competing first-order hazards. What fraction is expected to export eventually?

  1. 1.00
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.75
Answer and reasoning

D. 0.75 — The export probability is 0.15/(0.15+0.05)=0.75. The remaining quarter is expected to undergo nuclear decay.

40. Numerical — An isolated cytoplasmic RNA pool has no new influx and k_c=0.10/min. Its approximate half-life is:

  1. 10 min
  2. 69.3 min
  3. 0.69 min
  4. 6.93 min
Answer and reasoning

D. 6.93 min — t_1/2=ln(2)/0.10=6.93 min. The reciprocal rate of 10 min is the mean lifetime for this model, not its half-life.

41. Numerical — The last exon junction is at nucleotide 500. Two mammalian reporter stop positions are 420 and 480 under otherwise matched conditions. Which prediction is most appropriate?

  1. Both must be degraded in every cell
  2. Neither can undergo NMD
  3. 420 more likely NMD by the classic junction rule
  4. 480 more likely because it is closer to the junction
Answer and reasoning

C. 420 more likely NMD by the classic junction rule — The stop-to-junction separations are approximately 80 and 20 nt. The first supports a retained downstream EJC; the second is below the usual 50–55 nt threshold. These are probabilistic predictions.

42. Numerical — At steady state s=40 molecules/min, k_e=0.1/min, k_n=0 and k_c=0.05/min. If k_e halves and a new steady state is reached, what changes?

  1. N halves; C doubles
  2. Both halve
  3. Both stay unchanged
  4. N doubles; C stays 800
Answer and reasoning

D. N doubles; C stays 800 — With no nuclear decay, N=s/k_e rises from 400 to 800. Export flux equals s at both steady states, so C=s/k_c=800. The transient behavior can differ.

43. Numerical — After a pulse, 120 labeled nuclear molecules exist. Later, 35 nuclear and 65 cytoplasmic labeled molecules are detected. What fraction has disappeared from the measured pools, assuming complete recovery of intact labels and no new labeled molecules?

  1. 16.7%
  2. 29.2%
  3. 54.2%
  4. 83.3%
Answer and reasoning

A. 16.7% — 120−(35+65)=20 molecules, and 20/120≈16.7%. The fall in nuclear count alone overestimates loss because export transfers intact molecules.

44. Experimental — A mutant has normal nascent RNA, fully spliced nuclear RNA, reduced NXF1 association with cargo and lower cytoplasmic RNA. Which is the strongest proximal hypothesis?

  1. Loss of a cytoplasmic zipcode only
  2. Failure of receptor recruitment
  3. Failure of DNA replication only
  4. Accelerated protein degradation only
Answer and reasoning

B. Failure of receptor recruitment — Normal synthesis and splicing plus reduced receptor loading support an assembly defect. The other choices do not explain the reduced cargo–receptor association.

45. Experimental — A mutant mRNA binds NXF1 normally but the receptor's FG-binding surface is impaired. Which result would support the hypothesized defect?

  1. Loss of exon junctions from DNA
  2. Normal receptor loading with reduced pore passage
  3. Absence of all RNA synthesis
  4. Increased tRNA aminoacylation alone
Answer and reasoning

B. Normal receptor loading with reduced pore passage — Receptor loading is intact, but engagement with the selective barrier is impaired. This separates cargo recognition from translocation.

46. Experimental — Depletion of DDX19 causes pore-associated mRNPs and reduced released cytoplasmic RNA. Which rescue comparison best tests the proposed role?

  1. Wild-type tRNA versus mutant DNA ligase
  2. Two unrelated fluorescence colors without perturbation
  3. Wild-type DDX19 versus an expressed ATPase-defective variant
  4. A different promoter versus no promoter
Answer and reasoning

C. Wild-type DDX19 versus an expressed ATPase-defective variant — A matched functional-versus-catalytic mutant rescue tests whether ATPase-dependent release is required. Expression and localization of both variants must be checked.

47. Experimental — An RNA FISH image shows high nuclear oligo(dT) signal after a treatment. Which additional result most specifically supports redistribution of mature mRNA?

  1. Total ATP falls
  2. Cell nuclei look larger
  3. A tRNA anticodon is unchanged
  4. Gene-specific mature RNA counts rise in nucleus and fall in cytoplasm while early synthesis is similar
Answer and reasoning

D. Gene-specific mature RNA counts rise in nucleus and fall in cytoplasm while early synthesis is similar — Compartment counts of mature RNA with synthesis controls support an export phenotype. Oligo(dT) alone can be influenced by tail length and RNA composition.

48. Experimental — A splice donor mutant has little cytoplasmic reporter RNA. Nuclear exosome inhibition causes strong accumulation of unspliced nuclear reporter but does not restore cytoplasmic RNA. What follows?

  1. Nuclear decay removed some retained RNA, but its inhibition did not restore export competence
  2. NMD was proven to act in the nucleus before translation
  3. The mutation must be repaired at the DNA level
  4. Exosome inhibition restores all RNA functions
Answer and reasoning

A. Nuclear decay removed some retained RNA, but its inhibition did not restore export competence — The two measurements separate nuclear destruction from export. Accumulation after nuclease inhibition does not establish successful maturation or delivery.

49. Experimental — In a matched reporter pair, deleting a 3′ UTR element abolishes peripheral enrichment but total RNA remains unchanged. Adding the element to a diffuse reporter induces enrichment. What does this support?

  1. The element contributes to localization and is sufficient in the tested reporter context
  2. The element changes the protein sequence necessarily
  3. The element is a universal export receptor
  4. The element acts independently of all proteins
Answer and reasoning

A. The element contributes to localization and is sufficient in the tested reporter context — Deletion supports necessity in the original context, and addition supports sufficiency in the reporter context. Neither proves universal independence from cellular machinery.

50. Experimental — Live tracking shows RNA arrives at the cell edge at the same rate in mutant and wild type, but mutant particles leave the edge sooner. Which step is most directly implicated?

  1. RanGTP formation alone
  2. Anchoring or local residence
  3. Transcription initiation
  4. Nuclear pore assembly alone
Answer and reasoning

B. Anchoring or local residence — Normal arrival with shortened residence points to retention at the destination, not necessarily impaired directed transport or nuclear export.

51. Experimental — A motor inhibitor reduces RNA enrichment at the cell edge but also changes cell shape. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The RNA must be degraded by NMD
  2. No RNA can be localized by diffusion
  3. The result is compatible with motor dependence but indirect effects must be controlled
  4. Motor dependence is conclusively proven
Answer and reasoning

C. The result is compatible with motor dependence but indirect effects must be controlled — A perturbation of cytoskeletal motors can alter the geometry and anchors as well as transport. Track movement and control cell organization before making a strong causal claim.

52. Experimental — After RCC1 impairment, tRNA export declines. Bulk mRNA export also declines later. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. NXF1 must directly bind RanGTP in every mRNA cycle
  2. RCC1 is a tRNA nuclease
  3. All RNA export mechanisms are identical
  4. Ran may directly affect tRNA export and indirectly disturb mRNA export machinery
Answer and reasoning

D. Ran may directly affect tRNA export and indirectly disturb mRNA export machinery — A global Ran perturbation affects many nuclear import and export processes. Later effects on NXF1-dependent transport do not establish direct Ran dependence.

53. Experimental — A reporter with an early stop has normal early nuclear production, reduced cytoplasmic abundance and increased stability following UPF1 depletion. Which hypothesis is strengthened?

  1. The reporter cannot be transcribed
  2. RNA exportin-t is necessarily defective
  3. The nuclear pore no longer exists
  4. Translation-dependent NMD contributes to the loss
Answer and reasoning

D. Translation-dependent NMD contributes to the loss — The early stop and dependence on a core NMD factor support accelerated surveillance after delivery. Translation dependence and appropriate rescue controls can further strengthen this inference.

54. Integrative — A pre-60S export adaptor mutant leaves cytoplasmic mRNA abundance unchanged but lowers protein output. Which other observation fits?

  1. Accumulation of immature large-subunit particles in the nucleus
  2. Universal loss of the 5′ cap from all mRNAs
  3. A lower DNA copy number as the obligatory cause
  4. Every RNA zipcode becomes a stop codon
Answer and reasoning

A. Accumulation of immature large-subunit particles in the nucleus — Ribosomal subunit availability limits translation even if mRNA delivery works. The other alternatives do not follow from a large-subunit export defect.

55. Integrative — A normally nuclear lncRNA becomes cytoplasmic after deletion of a retention element but total RNA abundance is unchanged. What is the most defensible consequence?

  1. Its genomic DNA has been exported
  2. Its nuclear function may be impaired because localization changed
  3. Its total abundance proves its function is intact
  4. It is now necessarily translated
Answer and reasoning

B. Its nuclear function may be impaired because localization changed — Spatial misplacement can impair a nuclear role without changing total counts. Cytoplasmic localization alone does not imply protein coding or DNA movement.
