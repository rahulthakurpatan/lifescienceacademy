1. Why a single strand can be a molecular machine

The previous topic introduced the chemical alphabet of nucleic acids and explained how complementary DNA strands store information. RNA uses almost the same alphabet, but the outcome is remarkably different. A messenger RNA can present a sequence for translation, a transfer RNA can carry an amino acid, and a ribosomal RNA can help catalyse the formation of a peptide bond. The same class of polymer performs these different tasks because a nucleotide sequence is not only a message: it is also a set of chemical instructions for folding.

Imagine a flexible chain carrying four kinds of small flat plates. Some plates can recognise one another through hydrogen bonding; neighbouring plates can stack; the chain itself carries negatively charged groups and polar hydroxyl groups. When parts of the chain encounter complementary partners, they can form a short double helix. A second complementary region may form elsewhere. These local structures can then pack together, much as independently formed pieces of a puzzle fit into a larger object. The resulting object can present a binding pocket, an exposed sequence, or a chemically reactive group in a particular orientation. The analogy stops there: real molecules fluctuate continuously, and there may be several competing structures rather than a single rigid answer.

Historically the messenger role did not explain everything biochemists found in cells. Transfer RNA provided an early clear example of a small RNA with an organised three-dimensional shape. The demonstration that RNA could catalyse chemical reactions overturned the assumption that biological catalysts must all be proteins; Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman received the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this discovery. Structures of the ribosome subsequently provided a striking explanation for the catalytic significance of RNA in the universal machinery of translation. These milestones illustrate the same reasoning: a claim about molecular function becomes more convincing when it can be linked to chemical groups, physical structure and experimentally testable predictions.

This chapter follows that connection rather than treating RNA classes as a list to memorise. Start with the chemical consequence of the ribose 2′-hydroxyl, build the vocabulary of paired and unpaired regions, consider why certain conformations are energetically favourable, and then use those concepts to explain the specialised architecture of cellular RNAs. The central examination skill is to predict what changes when a base, an ion, a structural contact or a binding partner is altered. Detailed transcription, processing and regulation pathways belong to Unit 3; general nucleic-acid melting and DNA topology are developed in the next chapter.

2. The ribose backbone: how a single hydroxyl changes the rules

RNA contains ribonucleotides. Each has a nitrogenous base attached to the 1′ carbon of ribose through a β-N-glycosidic bond. The purines adenine and guanine attach through N9; pyrimidines cytosine and uracil attach through N1. The primes distinguish sugar atom numbers from base atom numbers. A ribonucleoside is just base plus sugar; addition of one or more phosphates gives a ribonucleotide. In a polymer the 3′ oxygen of one ribose and 5′ oxygen of the next are connected through a phosphate. This 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkage provides an asymmetric backbone and therefore a direction. By convention a sequence is written from 5′ to 3′. A 5′ end can have a phosphate, a triphosphate, a cap or a hydroxyl depending on how it was produced; the direction does not depend on which group is present.

The 2′-hydroxyl distinguishes ordinary RNA from DNA, which has a hydrogen in its place. This group influences the physical geometry of the backbone. Ribose is not a flat pentagon; it can adopt puckered conformations. RNA duplexes generally prefer the C3′-endo sugar pucker, associated with the A-form helix. The steric and hydration consequences of the hydroxyl make the usual B-DNA geometry unfavourable for ordinary double-stranded RNA. The preference is not a statement that every ribose in a folded RNA must be C3′-endo. Local turns, unpaired regions and noncanonical interactions require conformational diversity. The 2′-OH also offers an additional hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor to interact with bases, neighbouring sugars, water and proteins. Its presence helps make the tertiary architecture of RNA richer than a model based only on complementary sequences would suggest.

There is a chemical cost to this versatility. In alkaline solution, deprotonation of the 2′-OH can generate a nucleophilic 2′-oxygen. It can attack the adjacent phosphorus on the 3′ side of the same ribose, forming a transient pentacoordinate phosphoryl-transfer configuration. Departure of the next nucleotide's 5′ oxygen breaks the backbone and gives an upstream fragment bearing a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and a downstream fragment with a 5′-OH. Opening the cyclic phosphate subsequently gives 2′- or 3′-phosphate products. The initial event is intramolecular transesterification, not simply water attacking a phosphodiester. This distinction explains why DNA, lacking the attacking 2′-OH, is much more resistant to the analogous alkaline cleavage. Efficient attack depends on geometry: the nucleophile and leaving group must approach an in-line arrangement around phosphorus.

This chemical susceptibility does not mean RNA must be short-lived under all biological conditions. A compact tRNA may persist because of its structure, modifications, binding partners and limited access to degradative enzymes, while many messenger RNAs are actively turned over. Intrinsic chemical hydrolysis and enzyme-mediated turnover are distinct phenomena. Similarly, the 2′-OH can participate in useful catalytic mechanisms rather than merely cause unwanted decomposition. Certain small ribozymes exploit the same nucleophilic group to cleave their substrates at defined positions.

A useful prediction follows from this comparison. Replace the ribose at an intended self-cleavage site with 2′-deoxyribose while leaving the surrounding base sequence unchanged. If cleavage requires the site's 2′-oxygen as nucleophile, this substitution blocks that particular chemical pathway. It may also perturb local folding, so an experiment must test whether the overall fold remains comparable. The correct conclusion is not that DNA cannot undergo any backbone cleavage; it is that this specific RNA transesterification mechanism cannot occur at a site without its attacking group.

3. Base recognition, stacking and A-form geometry

Complementarity begins with the arrangement of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors on the edges of the bases. In standard Watson–Crick pairing, A recognises U through two hydrogen bonds, and G recognises C through three. Each pair combines a purine with a pyrimidine and fits a roughly common geometry. In an RNA duplex the participating strand segments are usually antiparallel. Within one long chain they can therefore pair when the chain bends back on itself. Pairing two regions of separate RNA molecules is also possible, as in guide RNA recognition of a target or the association of complementary viral strands.

Hydrogen bonds explain much of the specificity, but they are not the complete explanation for stability. A free base in aqueous solution can already hydrogen bond with water. Forming a pair replaces some water interactions with base–base interactions; the net free-energy change also includes solvent reorganisation, stacking, ionic interactions and entropy. Aromatic surfaces stack when bases lie above one another in a helix. Interactions between these surfaces, including dispersion and changes in solvent exposure, are important contributors to helix stability. As a result, a sequence with more G–C pairs often forms a stable duplex, but the exact order of nearest-neighbour base pairs and the loop and salt conditions also matter. Counting hydrogen bonds alone cannot rank arbitrary RNA folds.

An RNA helix is commonly a right-handed A-form helix with approximately eleven base pairs per turn, a rise of about 2.6–2.8 Å per base pair and a pitch of roughly 28–30 Å. These values are idealised averages, not fixed constants for every RNA stem. The bases are tilted and displaced from the helical axis compared with B-DNA, giving A-form RNA a deep narrow major groove and a broad shallow minor groove. The geometry changes the accessibility of base edges to proteins, ions and other RNA segments. A partner may recognise the exposed minor-groove geometry, the phosphate backbone or a loop rather than needing to enter the narrow major groove. RNA–DNA hybrids generally adopt an A-like conformation, although the DNA strand can exhibit sugar puckers distinct from the RNA strand.

RNA expands the base-pairing vocabulary beyond A–U and G–C. The G–U wobble pair is particularly common; its offset geometry permits hydrogen bonding but introduces a different local shape and chemical pattern. A G–U pair can be selected for a functional contact, rather than being an accidental pairing error. Other noncanonical pairs use alternative edges of bases, such as Hoogsteen or sugar-edge interactions, and may be important in loops, turns and long-range contacts. A pair that is noncanonical in the Watson–Crick sense is not necessarily weak or biologically irrelevant. The word wobble in a structural description of G–U pairing is related to, but not identical with, the special rules for codon–anticodon recognition in translation.

To read an example, take the segment 5′-AUGC-3′. Its exact complementary partner is 3′-UACG-5′, written in the usual direction as 5′-GCAU-3′. The reverse operation is necessary because of antiparallel alignment. An RNA in which half the positions are adenine need not contain an equal number of uracils: a single strand may have many unpaired nucleotides. Equality of A and U and of G and C follows for an entire fully Watson–Crick-paired duplex when both strands are counted, not for arbitrary RNA.

4. Secondary structure: reading stems, loops and junctions

The primary structure is the covalent sequence and chemical identity of nucleotides. Secondary structure is the pattern of base-paired segments, including the unpaired regions that connect them. A stem is a run of paired bases forming a local helix. In a hairpin, the two strands of the stem are joined by a terminal loop. A hairpin can arise in a single strand when an inverted repeat is separated by a short sequence that can bend. For example, 5′-GCGC GAAA GCGC-3′ can form a four-pair stem enclosing the four-residue GAAA loop. The first G pairs with the last C; the second C pairs with the second-last G, and so on. The stem is antiparallel even though the entire molecule is one covalent strand.

A loop is not simply a structurally meaningless absence of pairing. It has a conformational cost because a segment that once had many possible arrangements becomes constrained. At the same time a loop can contain characteristic stacking and hydrogen bonds that stabilise a particular bend. A four-nucleotide loop is called a tetraloop. GNRA (N is any nucleotide; R is a purine), UNCG and CUUG families contain familiar examples of stable tetraloops. Not every four-residue sequence is equally stable, and the closing base pair influences the resulting geometry. A tetraloop can serve as a compact cap, a recognition surface or the starting point for a tertiary contact.

A bulge differs from a terminal loop. A bulged nucleotide or several nucleotides interrupt one side of an otherwise paired stem, with no unpaired counterpart on the other side. Such an interruption can cause a kink, alter coaxial stacking or expose a base for ligand recognition. An internal loop occurs when both strands have intervening unpaired residues, which may be equal or unequal in number. Its bases may actually form noncanonical pairs in the detailed three-dimensional structure. Thus a dot in a conventional secondary-structure diagram means no designated secondary pair, not necessarily no molecular interaction. A junction is a connection between several helices. Three-way and four-way junctions allow an RNA to branch in space; some junctions favour stacking of two helices into a longer quasi-continuous helix.

Learn to read a dot-bracket representation as a series of instructions. Each opening parenthesis identifies a paired position; its matching closing parenthesis identifies its partner; a dot represents a position unpaired in the chosen model. The string ((((....)))) has twelve characters and four pairs. Match the innermost parentheses first: positions 4 and 9; then 3 and 10; 2 and 11; 1 and 12. Four pairs consume eight nucleotides, leaving four unpaired. The notation says nothing about whether the pairs are G–C, A–U or G–U until the sequence is supplied, and says little about their exact spatial arrangement. In a biological sequence different proposed dot-bracket strings may all be chemically plausible, so the task is to choose between alternatives using physical reasoning and evidence.

Consider a mutation at position 2 of the example hairpin. Changing C to A makes A–G at positions 2 and 11, disrupting the expected Watson–Crick pair. The stem may become less stable, shift register, or form a noncanonical arrangement. Changing position 11 from G to U restores the possibility of an A–U pair. If the original function also returns, that is evidence that maintaining a pair at those positions mattered more than preserving the original individual bases. It is not proof of the complete fold, because the new bases may alter stacking, protein recognition or a competing structure. This kind of compensatory substitution is central to testing RNA architecture.

5. Pseudoknots and the limits of a flat structure diagram

A simple hairpin has nested pairs: if the outer pair is (i,j), the inner pair (k,l) obeys i < k < l < j. Both pairs can be drawn as arcs on the same side of a horizontal sequence line without intersecting. A pseudoknot arises when the paired regions interleave. In a common H-type pseudoknot, nucleotides in a hairpin loop pair with a complementary region outside that hairpin. If the first pair is (i,j) and a second pair is (k,l), the ordering i < k < j < l produces crossing arcs. It is the crossing of connectivity in this linear representation, not necessarily a literal knot in the polymer, that defines the feature.

For example, take a 14-nucleotide model with pairs (1,9), (2,8), (4,14), and (5,13). The first two form one stem; the latter two form another. The endpoints 1, 4, 9 and 14 interleave, so a single ordinary parenthesis alphabet cannot describe the full set unambiguously. An extended notation is ((.[[..))...]] where square brackets denote the second stem. Verify it position by position: the two round pairs close at 8 and 9; the square pairs close at 13 and 14. The unpaired positions remain dots. In three dimensions the stems can pack against one another, sometimes almost coaxially. The model is deliberately short to make connectivity easy to inspect, not a claim that every such sequence forms a stable pseudoknot.

Pseudoknots can help hold distant parts of a molecule in a precise arrangement. Some viral RNAs use structured regions, including pseudoknots, to influence ribosomal movement and reading-frame selection; some catalytic RNAs use them to organise an active site; riboswitches can use them as part of a binding architecture. These biological examples explain why predicting an RNA fold by simply finding the longest local inverted repeat can fail. If an algorithm assumes all base pairs must be nested, it cannot represent crossing pairs in the ordinary model, even when they are biologically essential.

A related interaction is the kissing-loop complex. Here bases in two hairpin loops pair with one another, either within one molecule or between separate molecules. It can initiate association or help connect remote structural domains. Depending on the connectivity and chosen representation, kissing-loop interactions may create crossing pair patterns. Not all tertiary contacts are pseudoknots, however: a base from one loop may fit into another helix's minor groove without forming a conventional crossing secondary pair. A student should therefore distinguish a description of connectivity from a description of three-dimensional packing.

6. Tertiary folding: building three-dimensional architecture

The secondary structure provides a scaffold, but many molecular functions require residues that are far apart in the sequence to approach one another in space. Tertiary structure is the spatial arrangement of the helices, loops, junctions and individual bases within a molecule. A four-arm secondary diagram does not tell us the angle between arms or which loop touches which helix. When the molecule folds, some stems stack end-to-end while others lie against one another. These contacts can create pockets with a shape, electrostatic potential and arrangement of hydrogen-bond groups that selectively bind another molecule.

Several recurring structural motifs show how this construction works. In coaxial stacking, the end of one helix stacks on the end of a second; the bases can form a nearly continuous stacked column even though the covalent chain may pass through a junction. This helps explain how a bent or branched secondary structure can contain long structural axes. In a base triple, a third base contacts a pair by using an available edge. In an A-minor interaction, an adenine residue from another segment contacts the minor groove of a helix. The interaction takes advantage of the chemical surfaces exposed by the A-form helix. In a ribose zipper, hydroxyl groups and backbone-associated contacts contribute to association between segments. A GNRA tetraloop can dock into an appropriate receptor elsewhere in the RNA, making a long-range connection. These motifs are not an extra alphabet to memorise; they demonstrate why the same Watson–Crick scaffold can support different tertiary arrangements.

Electrostatics impose a major constraint. At physiological pH, internal phosphate groups are negatively charged. Packing distant portions of the backbone close together increases electrostatic repulsion. Monovalent cations such as K⁺ and Na⁺ reduce the effective repulsion through their surrounding ionic atmosphere. Divalent Mg²⁺ is especially effective in supporting compact tertiary structure because it has a higher charge and can participate in local interactions, either directly or through its hydration shell. Specific metal-ion sites can additionally stabilise a catalytic conformation or participate in chemistry. One must distinguish general charge screening from a metal performing an indispensable active-site function. Not every magnesium dependence is evidence of direct metal catalysis.

Suppose an RNA has a well formed hairpin even in a solution containing only monovalent salt. Its tertiary docking event brings several negatively charged segments together. Adding Mg²⁺ may increase the fraction of compact molecules without changing the principal hairpin base pairs. If a structural probe reports mostly unchanged paired regions but an end-to-end distance decreases, these observations fit a change in tertiary packing rather than wholesale replacement of secondary structure. Conversely, some RNAs form new base pairs as they collapse, so the hierarchical model of folding is a useful tendency and not an absolute rule.

RNA can also participate in nonstandard conformations. Guanine-rich sequences may form stacked G-quartets around monovalent cations, especially potassium, producing a G-quadruplex. Some RNA sequences can form triplex-like arrangements through third-strand contacts. Left-handed Z-RNA is possible for suitable double-stranded sequences and conditions, although most regular RNA duplexes are A-form. Such alternatives are valuable as exceptions to the idea that all paired RNA must look alike. They do not need to dominate a NET/SET treatment: recognise the structural principles and avoid confusing these special architectures with the general A-form preference.

Finally, many natural RNAs form ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs). A protein can bind a hairpin, recognise a modified base, stabilise a junction or position an RNA next to another substrate. Assembly can change the RNA's structure rather than merely placing an unchanged RNA inside a passive shell. The complete particle has an organisation beyond the tertiary fold of one RNA molecule. The ribosome, spliceosome and telomerase exemplify how nucleic acids and proteins can cooperate as molecular machines.

7. Folding energetics, competing structures and kinetics

The principles established for thermodynamics in the previous subunit now become useful. For a folding transition at constant temperature and pressure, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Here ΔG is the free-energy difference between the chosen folded and reference unfolded states, ΔH is their enthalpy difference, ΔS is their entropy difference and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. Use compatible units; if ΔH is in kJ mol⁻¹, convert ΔS to kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ before multiplying. Folding often has favourable enthalpic contributions from stacking and specific interactions, opposed by a loss of conformational entropy. Solvent and ion rearrangements also contribute, so one should not assume the total entropy change is identical to the conformational entropy change of the chain alone.

As a simple two-state calculation, assume ΔH = −75 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −0.220 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ are constant in the temperature range considered. At 298 K, ΔG = −75 − 298(−0.220) = −9.44 kJ mol⁻¹. At 350 K, ΔG = −75 + 77 = +2 kJ mol⁻¹. The model predicts that folding is favoured at the lower temperature and disfavoured at the higher one. In an ideal two-state intramolecular transition with these simplifying assumptions, the temperature at which ΔG = 0 is 75/0.220 = 341 K, or about 68 °C. Real RNA can have intermediates, temperature-dependent enthalpy, changes in ions and separate transitions; a measured midpoint need not describe the unfolding of every structural element at once. The broader treatment of melting is reserved for 1.B.05.

For local secondary structure, nearest-neighbour reasoning is more useful than counting individual hydrogen bonds. A stack contains two successive base pairs, and its stability depends on their identities and order. A four-pair stem contains three adjacent base-pair stacking steps. Add appropriate stacking contributions and the energetic costs or benefits of loops, bulges, junctions and terminal effects to estimate the free energy of a proposed structure. Empirical parameters depend on experimental conditions. In a simplified problem, let three stack contributions be −2.2, −3.1 and −2.2 kcal mol⁻¹, with a loop penalty of +4.5 and an initiation correction of +0.5 kcal mol⁻¹. The illustrative sum is −2.5 kcal mol⁻¹. If disruption of one pair removes two of those stacks, simply subtracting the hydrogen bonds in that one pair would not account for the energetic consequence. These supplied values are fictional teaching parameters, not a prediction for a named biological sequence.

At equilibrium, two structures of the same RNA can be compared by the Boltzmann ratio P₂/P₁ = exp[−(G₂−G₁)/(RT)], assuming each G represents the free energy of a defined state or state ensemble and the states are in thermal equilibrium. R is the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 0.008314 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At 300 K, let structure B have a free energy 5 kJ mol⁻¹ higher than A. RT = 2.494 kJ mol⁻¹, so PB/PA = exp(−5/2.494) ≈ 0.135. If those are the only two states, PA = 1/(1+0.135) ≈ 0.881 and PB ≈ 0.119. A less stable conformation is not forbidden: it can be occupied a significant fraction of the time, especially when the free-energy difference is comparable to RT. This matters when only one conformation has an accessible ligand-binding site.

Free-energy differences describe equilibrium preferences; activation barriers govern the rates at which those preferences are reached. An RNA may rapidly form a stable local stem that must partially unfold before it can reach the biologically functional structure. That local stem can become a kinetic trap. A molecule trapped for seconds or minutes need not be at the global minimum of free energy. The order in which sequence segments appear during transcription can determine which contacts form first; a downstream complementary region cannot compete until it exists. RNA-binding proteins and RNA chaperones can help remodel trapped structures. Some helicases use ATP to promote strand separation or rearrangement. Folding therefore has both a thermodynamic landscape and a kinetic pathway.

The distinction becomes especially important in regulatory RNAs. A ligand can favour a binding-competent conformation by lowering its free energy. A nascent transcript may instead become committed to a regulatory outcome before complete equilibrium is reached. Two sequences with identical equilibrium structures can have different response times if their barriers differ. An experiment that measures only the final equilibrium cannot distinguish such kinetic effects; measurements over time or changes in the order of ligand addition are needed. In examinations, separate the questions ‘which state is more populated at equilibrium?’ and ‘which state appears first?’

8. Messenger RNA: a readable message that also folds

The job of mRNA is to present the ordered nucleotide information that a ribosome translates. Its coding region is read in triplets called codons. An open reading frame (ORF) is a continuous series of codons with the potential to specify a polypeptide, conventionally bounded by initiation and termination signals. The untranslated regions (UTRs) are parts of the transcript outside the translated coding region. They are not necessarily without function: they can carry sequences and folded elements controlling translation, localisation or stability. Even coding regions can form local secondary structures. A molecule does not have to be unfolded from end to end to be a messenger.

A typical mature nuclear eukaryotic mRNA has a 5′ cap containing 7-methylguanosine linked to the transcript by an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate bridge. Most such mRNAs also have a poly(A) tail at the 3′ end. Cap-binding and poly(A)-binding proteins contribute to protection and efficient translation; their interactions with other initiation factors can bring the ends into proximity. The resulting protein-mediated association is not the same as forming a covalently circular RNA. Histone mRNAs in animals provide a classic exception to the general polyadenylation pattern: many replication-dependent histone mRNAs use a specialised 3′ stem-loop. Some noncoding RNAs also carry caps or tails, so neither modification alone establishes that a transcript encodes protein.

Many bacterial mRNAs lack the conventional eukaryotic m⁷G cap and long stabilising poly(A) tail, and some carry several coding regions on one transcript. The Shine–Dalgarno sequence in many bacteria can pair with a complementary region of 16S rRNA to position the initiation site. Its accessibility depends on local secondary structure. Not all bacterial transcripts use a canonical Shine–Dalgarno sequence; leaderless initiation and other mechanisms exist. In typical eukaryotic cap-dependent initiation, the small subunit and factors scan towards an appropriate start codon; a stable hairpin in the leader can interfere with scanning. Exceptions such as internal ribosome entry demonstrate that structured RNA may also promote initiation in appropriate contexts.

The structural prediction is straightforward. Suppose a bacterial transcript has a ribosome-binding site 5′-AGGAGG-3′. If a nearby sequence 5′-CCUCCU-3′ forms a stable stem with it, the bases needed to pair with the rRNA become less accessible. A mutation disrupting the complementary region, while leaving the binding site sequence intact, can increase translation without changing the encoded protein. But a measured increase could also reflect altered RNA abundance or stability. Compare translation efficiency per transcript, measure the folding state and check RNA levels before claiming a direct initiation effect.

In vivo the messenger is continually remodelled by proteins and translating ribosomes. Local structure can protect a region from particular nucleases or create a recognition site for a nuclease; different structures can increase or decrease stability. RNA decay is therefore not a simple monotonic function of predicted folding energy. The important principle is that the sequence has two kinds of information: the nucleotide order used for coding and the structural possibilities used in regulation. Synonymous substitutions preserve an amino acid sequence but can change mRNA structure and consequently gene expression.

9. Transfer RNA: from cloverleaf to L-shaped adaptor

Translation requires a physical link between two different chemical languages. An amino acid has no simple direct complementarity to a three-nucleotide codon. Transfer RNA solves the problem by placing an amino-acid attachment site at one end and a sequence that can recognise the codon at the other. A mature conventional tRNA often has approximately 75–90 nucleotides, but the size varies, particularly in some mitochondrial tRNAs. Its secondary structure resembles a cloverleaf because several stems surround a central junction. The tertiary fold resembles an L because the stems stack and loops contact one another, bringing particular arms together while keeping the two functional ends separated.

Begin at the acceptor stem. In a typical tRNA, the first seven nucleotides pair with residues near the 3′ end. The single discriminator nucleotide (position 73 in standard numbering) precedes the conserved 3′-CCA terminus (positions 74–76). The terminal adenosine, A76, provides a ribose hydroxyl to which the amino acid is attached through an ester bond. Depending on the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase class, initial aminoacylation can occur at the 2′ or 3′ hydroxyl, with interconversion possible. The important contrast with the phosphodiester backbone is that the amino acid attaches through its carboxyl group to the terminal ribose, not through a peptide bond to a base. CCA may be encoded or added post-transcriptionally.

The D arm includes residues such as dihydrouridine, often abbreviated D. Dihydrouridine lacks the fully aromatic planar pyrimidine ring of uridine and can influence stacking and flexibility. The anticodon arm contains three consecutive nucleotides at conventional positions 34–36 that pair with the mRNA codon. The variable region lies between the anticodon and T arm; some tRNAs have a longer variable arm than others. The TΨC arm takes its name from a conserved sequence commonly including ribothymidine, pseudouridine and cytidine. RNA can therefore contain modified bases not represented by the four canonical letters. Pseudouridine is a uridine isomer with a carbon–carbon glycosidic linkage that changes its interaction possibilities. Methylated bases and other modifications can affect local structure, recognition or decoding.

In the tertiary structure, the acceptor stem stacks approximately with the T stem, and the D stem stacks approximately with the anticodon stem. Contacts between the D and T loops create the elbow of the L. Conserved interactions, such as contacts involving G18 and Ψ55 and G19 and C56 in conventional tRNAs, help organise this region. At opposite tips sit the 3′-CCA end and the anticodon. This spatial separation allows the ribosome to monitor codon recognition in the small subunit while positioning the aminoacyl end in the catalytic region of the large subunit. It is not merely decorative symmetry: changing the arm lengths or disrupting elbow contacts can impair positioning even if the anticodon still reads the correct sequence.

Recognition by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases is a second structural problem. A synthetase must select its amino acid and appropriate tRNA. The anticodon is an identity element for many tRNAs but is not universally the decisive signal. Features in the acceptor stem, discriminator base and overall geometry can also be critical. A classic example is the G3–U70 wobble pair in the acceptor stem of many alanine tRNAs, which is an important alanine identity determinant. Therefore changing an anticodon can change decoding preference without necessarily changing amino acid charging specificity. Conversely, disrupting an identity element may alter charging while leaving the anticodon unchanged.

The functional consequence is a two-step logic: synthetases assign amino acids to tRNAs; codon–anticodon pairing places those charged tRNAs on mRNA. A mischarged tRNA can still recognise the correct codon, yet deliver the wrong amino acid. The ribosome does not independently determine the chemical identity of every amino acid by reading the mRNA. Fidelity depends strongly on the upstream charging and editing systems. The enzymatic details belong to Unit 3, but the structural lesson belongs here: recognition surfaces at different positions on one RNA can encode distinct identities.

10. Anticodon orientation, wobble and modified bases

A codon is written 5′ to 3′, but its anticodon pairs antiparallel. For the mRNA codon 5′-AUG-3′, an exact complementary anticodon is 3′-UAC-5′, conventionally written 5′-CAU-3′. A frequent examination error is to give UAC without specifying direction when the question asks for the anticodon in 5′ to 3′ order. The 5′ nucleotide of the anticodon, position 34, pairs with the third nucleotide of the codon. The middle anticodon base pairs with the second codon position; anticodon position 36 pairs with the first codon position. This orientation explains why a chemical modification at position 34 affects recognition of synonymous codons that differ at their third position.

Francis Crick's wobble hypothesis explains how a set of tRNAs smaller than the number of sense codons can recognise multiple codons. Some pairing flexibility is permitted at the codon third position. In a simplified classical model, G at the 5′ end of the anticodon can recognise C or U in the codon, U can recognise A or G, and inosine (I), derived from adenosine by deamination, can recognise U, C or A. C and A are usually treated as recognising G and U, respectively, under the simple rules. Modifications and organism-specific decoding systems can restrict or expand these options. Thus these rules are examination models with meaningful biological exceptions, not universal guarantees for every unmodified or modified tRNA.

Consider 5′-GAA-3′ as an anticodon. Written in pairing orientation it is 3′-AAG-5′. The corresponding codons are 5′-UUC-3′ and, with G–U wobble at the third codon position, 5′-UUU-3′. Both specify phenylalanine in the standard genetic code. Replacing G34 with a C would restrict the simple pairing prediction to the codon ending in G, namely UUG, which encodes leucine; such a replacement does not guarantee that the altered tRNA would be properly charged with leucine. Charging identity and decoding identity are distinct as explained above.

A base modification can make the anticodon loop more ordered, improve stacking and influence how it interacts with the ribosomal decoding centre. Other modifications stabilise the global tRNA structure or help recognition by proteins. The presence of a modification does not mean a fifth ordinary genetic alphabet is used by the ribosome for all messages. Modified residues are generally produced after RNA synthesis and can have context-specific effects. To interpret an experimental result, determine where the modified base lies and which molecular interaction is likely to be affected.

11. Ribosomal RNA: scaffold, decoding centre and catalytic core

A ribosome must bring an mRNA, two or more tRNAs and many protein factors into a reproducible geometrical relationship while still allowing these components to move. Its RNA core provides much of that architecture. Ribosomal RNAs contain numerous helices, junctions, noncanonical pairs and tertiary contacts that assemble with proteins into small and large subunits. Their organisation reflects the same principles used in tRNA, but on a much larger scale. Instead of thinking of rRNA as a large string filling empty space, think of it as an intricate molecular framework that forms essential binding and catalytic surfaces.

In bacteria the 70S ribosome has a 30S small subunit containing 16S rRNA, and a 50S large subunit containing 23S and 5S rRNAs. A typical cytosolic eukaryotic 80S ribosome has a 40S small subunit with 18S rRNA and a 60S large subunit with 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs; the large rRNA designation varies in some lineages, such as 25S in yeast. Organellar ribosomes have different compositions. S denotes a sedimentation coefficient, not molecular weight or length. One Svedberg equals 10⁻¹³ s. Sedimentation depends on mass, shape, buoyancy and friction, so the S values of subunits are not additive. A 30S particle plus a 50S particle therefore need not be 80S.

The small subunit provides the decoding environment where the mRNA codon pairs with the anticodon of a tRNA. Conserved regions of small-subunit rRNA contribute to evaluating the geometry of base pairing, especially the first two codon positions. In bacteria, 16S rRNA also contains the region that recognises a Shine–Dalgarno sequence in many transcripts. The small subunit's structure therefore contributes to both positioning and fidelity. In the large subunit, the peptidyl-transferase centre positions the 3′ ends of adjacent tRNAs. The amino group of the aminoacyl-tRNA in the A site attacks the carbonyl carbon of the ester linking the growing peptide to the P-site tRNA. The result is transfer of the peptide to the A-site tRNA, with formation of a new peptide bond. This reaction is aminolysis of an activated ester rather than a direct condensation of two free amino acids in water.

Structural and biochemical evidence identifies the large-subunit rRNA as the central catalytic component of the peptidyl-transferase centre. In bacteria this is principally 23S rRNA. It positions the reacting groups and contributes to the environment that supports catalysis; the substrate tRNA's terminal ribose hydroxyl also contributes to the chemistry. Ribosomal proteins remain crucial for assembly, stability, regulation and function, so the statement ‘the ribosome is a ribozyme’ does not mean the complete ribosome is protein-free. It means that the catalytic centre for peptide bond formation is fundamentally RNA-based. Distinguish this reaction from ATP-dependent tRNA charging and GTP-dependent movements during translation.

To understand this experimentally, consider a mutation in a conserved region of 23S rRNA that impairs peptide bond formation while leaving assembled particles detectable. That result suggests a functional role but does not alone demonstrate direct catalysis; the mutation might alter substrate positioning or neighbouring structure. High-resolution structural localisation of the residue, binding measurements and kinetic tests can distinguish these explanations. The same logic applies to antibiotics that bind rRNA-containing sites: their effects can reveal an important region, but inhibition may arise from distorted geometry or interference with motion rather than chemical destruction of the catalytic centre.

12. Regulatory RNAs: recognition by complementary sequence and shape

The mRNA, tRNA and rRNA examples demonstrate that an RNA can function as a message, adaptor and catalytic scaffold. Regulatory RNAs use the same structural chemistry to influence other molecules. Many small regulatory RNAs function within proteins that hold a guide strand in a favourable conformation for target recognition. A target sequence must be complementary enough to the guide and accessible in its own structure. Thus a single mutation can alter recognition even when it does not change the number of transcripts produced.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are commonly about 22 nucleotides in their mature form. In a typical animal pathway, the precursor has a stem-loop structure, which is processed to yield a short duplex; one strand is loaded as a guide into an Argonaute-containing complex. The precursor is often imperfectly paired, with bulges or mismatches. These structural features matter for recognition by processing enzymes, although the entire processing pathway is treated in Unit 3. In many animal miRNA interactions, positions 2–8 at the 5′ end of the mature guide constitute the seed region and are important for initial recognition of target sites. Complementarity outside the seed, site context and target accessibility also matter. Partial complementarity frequently produces translational repression and RNA destabilisation. In plants, many miRNAs use much more extensive pairing and can direct target cleavage. Do not impose the animal seed-only model on all organisms.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are commonly generated from double-stranded RNA precursors or supplied as short duplexes. A typical Dicer product has approximately 21–23 nucleotides per strand and short 3′ overhangs, often two nucleotides. The guide strand in an Argonaute complex recognises a complementary target. Extensive complementarity, particularly across the region necessary for cleavage, can support endonucleolytic slicing by a catalytically competent Argonaute. Not every Argonaute protein is a slicer, and the outcomes of miRNA and siRNA pathways overlap. The structural contrast is more reliable than the oversimplification ‘miRNA represses, siRNA always cleaves’: typical precursor architectures, pairing patterns and protein context differ.

A target may contain a perfect guide-complementary sequence and yet respond weakly because the site is buried in a stable local stem. Opening the stem imposes an energetic cost. For a simplified example, if guide–target duplex formation contributes −12 kcal mol⁻¹ and opening the target costs +8 kcal mol⁻¹, their combined contribution is −4 kcal mol⁻¹ before any protein binding or other energetic terms are included. A second target whose duplex energy is only −10 kcal mol⁻¹ but opening costs +2 has a combined contribution of −8 kcal mol⁻¹. The second can be favoured despite weaker isolated duplex formation. These invented values illustrate coupled equilibria; real RNP recognition involves kinetics and other contacts as well. The comparison teaches why sequence matching is necessary but sometimes insufficient.

Other regulatory and functional RNAs show variations on this principle. Small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), as components of small nuclear RNPs, use sequence pairing and folded structures to recognise splice sites and organise the spliceosome. The U2 snRNA interaction at the branch site leaves the branch-point adenosine available for reaction; U2 and U6 participate in the catalytic RNA network. Small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) guide many rRNA modifications using sequence complementarity and characteristic motifs. Box C/D snoRNPs guide many 2′-O-methylations; box H/ACA snoRNPs guide many pseudouridylations. In these complexes RNA guides the site, while associated proteins provide the relevant catalytic activity. piRNAs are another class of small guide RNAs, associated with PIWI proteins and often important in transposon repression, notably in animal germlines; they are not simply Dicer-generated siRNAs.

Long noncoding RNAs are an operational class generally longer than 200 nucleotides, not a single structural type. They may contain folded domains that recruit proteins, participate in RNA–RNA pairing or organise molecular interactions. Bacterial small RNAs often use partial pairing to alter target translation or stability, frequently with RNA-binding proteins. The guide RNA of a CRISPR system provides a structured protein-binding scaffold plus a sequence for target recognition. Telomerase RNA contributes a template and a scaffold to the telomerase RNP; its reverse-transcriptase protein performs DNA synthesis. The common principle is to ask which part of a transcript is a recognition sequence and which part forms the scaffold. Detailed regulatory pathways and technologies belong to later units.

13. Riboswitches: converting ligand binding into a structural decision

A riboswitch is a cis-acting regulatory RNA element that responds to a ligand through a change in its own structure or structural occupancy. Many bacterial examples lie in untranslated regions of mRNAs. A typical riboswitch contains an aptamer region, which recognises a ligand, and an expression platform, which controls a downstream regulatory outcome. The two parts are functionally distinguishable but often share nucleotides. They need not be independent physical modules. Specific hydrogen bonds, stacking interactions, electrostatic contacts and pocket geometry allow a folded RNA to recognise a small metabolite without requiring a protein receptor for the primary binding event.

Consider a hypothetical transcriptional OFF switch. Without ligand, a segment of the nascent RNA pairs with a complementary segment to form an antiterminator. This sequesters bases that would otherwise be needed for a terminator stem. When the ligand binds, it stabilises an alternative aptamer arrangement; the terminator can form instead. In a bacterial intrinsic termination context, an appropriate stem-loop followed by a U-rich tract can promote termination. The result is reduced production of the downstream transcript. A translational OFF switch can instead regulate initiation by hiding a ribosome-binding site in a stem. Other riboswitches act in the opposite direction or use other mechanisms. Ligand binding by itself does not tell us whether expression goes up or down: inspect the expression platform.

Known ligand classes include thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), glycine and purines. TPP-responsive riboswitches also exist in some eukaryotes and can affect RNA processing. These examples connect the molecular binding pocket with feedback regulation of metabolite levels. If the product of a biosynthetic pathway is abundant, a switch can reduce synthesis of enzymes that make more of it. However, regulatory direction depends on the biological wiring. A switch controlling transport or degradation can have a different logic from one controlling biosynthesis.

For one simple binding site at equilibrium, dissociation constant Kd = [RNA][L]/[RNA·L], with concentration units, where L is free ligand. Let total RNA = [RNA] + [RNA·L]. Rearrangement gives the bound fraction θ = [L]/(Kd + [L]). For Kd = 20 nM, free ligand of 5 nM gives θ = 0.20, 20 nM gives 0.50 and 80 nM gives 0.80. This derivation assumes a single noncooperative site, equilibrium, and a known free rather than total ligand concentration. It is not a universal formula for the fraction of full-length transcripts terminated. Folding kinetics, polymerase speed, competing states and ligand residence time may affect the actual gene-expression response. An aptamer mutation can change ligand affinity while leaving the platform intact; a platform mutation can change expression while leaving binding nearly normal. These two observations distinguish recognition from signal transmission.

14. Ribozymes: how folding creates catalytic activity

An enzyme accelerates a chemical reaction by lowering its activation free energy, not by changing the equilibrium constant of the uncoupled reaction. The catalytic molecule need not be a protein. An RNA can fold into an active site that brings a substrate and catalytic groups together, helps orient a nucleophile, stabilises developing charge or participates in acid–base chemistry. The range of chemical groups available to RNA is smaller than that available to proteins, but nucleotide bases, ribose hydroxyls, phosphate groups, water and bound metal ions provide enough possibilities for several important reactions.

Cech's work on the Tetrahymena group I intron demonstrated self-splicing. In the classic reaction an external guanosine (or a guanosine nucleotide) provides a 3′-OH nucleophile that attacks the phosphate at the 5′ splice site. The freed 3′-OH at the end of the upstream exon then attacks the phosphate at the 3′ splice site, joining the exons. The intron provides a folded architecture that selects and positions reaction partners. Group II introns use a different initiating nucleophile in the usual branching pathway: the 2′-OH of a particular internal adenosine attacks the 5′ splice site, forming a 2′–5′ linkage and lariat intermediate. A subsequent reaction joins the exons. Some group II introns instead initiate through hydrolysis. The resemblance between group II splicing and spliceosomal chemistry is an important connection, but it is not proof by itself of a complete evolutionary history.

Altman's work on bacterial RNase P showed that an RNA component can catalyse processing of precursor tRNA. The usual cellular bacterial enzyme is an RNP; under suitable in vitro ionic conditions, the RNA component can carry out cleavage without its protein partner. RNase P removes the 5′ leader of pre-tRNA by hydrolysis, producing a mature 5′ phosphate and an upstream 3′-OH product. This differs from the cyclic-phosphate chemistry of many small self-cleaving ribozymes. Eukaryotic RNase P often has additional protein components, and protein-only RNase P forms exist in some systems. Thus RNase P provides evidence for RNA catalysis, but one should not claim that every enzyme with that name necessarily has catalytic RNA.

The hammerhead ribozyme is a useful model for connecting structure to cleavage chemistry. Three helical regions organise a conserved catalytic core; the substrate strand contains a ribose hydroxyl positioned to attack its adjacent phosphate. Cleavage yields a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and 5′-OH. The folded active site favours the in-line arrangement and provides catalytic interactions. Metal ions can help the RNA adopt its active architecture, and their chemical role depends on the ribozyme and conditions. General acid–base catalysis by nucleobases is important in several small ribozymes. Avoid the tempting universal statement that every RNA catalyst operates by the same two-metal-ion mechanism. The hepatitis delta virus ribozyme is another self-cleaving RNA; it has a compact pseudoknotted architecture and uses a catalytic cytosine in its active-site chemistry. These examples reinforce the connection between tertiary structure and function.

The ribosome's peptidyl-transferase centre is a different type of ribozyme: it catalyses amide bond formation rather than phosphodiester cleavage or ligation. The catalytic machinery of the spliceosome also includes essential RNA interactions. In contrast, telomerase is an RNA-containing enzyme whose reverse transcriptase protein performs polymerisation; its RNA serves as template and scaffold. A molecule can be indispensable to catalysis without itself being the catalytic chemical component. This distinction is especially important in questions that offer several RNPs as distractors.

How can an investigator distinguish an RNA catalyst from a contaminating protein enzyme? A persuasive design uses purified or independently synthesised RNA, demonstrates the expected products and reaction dependence on specific RNA sequence or structure, and includes appropriate negative controls. A reaction that vanishes when a crucial base is changed but returns after restoration of the active structure is stronger evidence than observation of cleavage in a crude cell extract. For a trans-acting catalyst, turnover of multiple substrate molecules can establish repeated catalytic action under appropriate conditions. A self-splicing RNA may react only once because its substrate is part of the same molecule; this does not invalidate its catalytic role.

The discovery of ribozymes helped motivate the RNA-world hypothesis, in which RNA-like molecules could have combined information storage with catalysis before modern DNA–protein systems evolved. The existence of RNA catalysts and the RNA core of the ribosome make that possibility plausible. They do not establish that every step of life's origin is known or that an ancient RNA-only organism has been observed. For this syllabus, the meaningful lesson is the chemical dual capacity of RNA, not speculative details of prebiotic pathways.

15. How we know a proposed RNA structure is real

A sequence may have many complementary regions, so a plausible drawing is a hypothesis rather than a finished structural determination. The strongest reasoning combines independent evidence. Comparative analysis examines related RNAs whose sequences have diverged while their functions remain conserved. If two positions consistently change together in a way that preserves complementary pairing, they may form a conserved structural contact. For example, one species has G–C, another A–U and another C–G at corresponding positions. Such covariation supports pairing more strongly than identity of one conserved base. Common ancestry and alignment errors can create misleading patterns, so independent substitutions and proper positional alignment matter. A perfectly conserved pair may lack observable covariation even if it is genuinely paired.

Compensatory mutagenesis is a complementary experimental approach. Imagine a stem with a G–C pair. Mutant 1 changes G to A, producing A–C; mutant 2 changes C to U, producing G–U, which may retain partial pairing; the double mutant produces A–U. If the two single mutants alter the structure or function and the double mutant restores it, the result supports the proposed pairing. Because G–U can be a legitimate wobble pair, the second mutant may have a smaller effect than the first. A stronger design can choose substitutions that more clearly disrupt both single-mutant pairs. Include measurements of RNA abundance, folding and activity, because the mutations might affect other features independently of pairing. Failure to rescue does not automatically disprove pairing: the original chemical identity or stacking environment might also be needed.

Chemical and enzymatic probing report accessibility and local conformation. Single-strand-preferring RNases and double-strand/stacked-region-preferring nucleases provide complementary information; for example, RNase T1 preferentially cleaves accessible unpaired guanosines, while RNase V1 preferentially cleaves structured or stacked regions. These are preferences, not perfect binary detectors. Dimethyl sulfate (DMS) can modify accessible Watson–Crick edges of adenine and cytosine under common probing conditions. Selective 2′-hydroxyl acylation analysed by primer extension (SHAPE) reports local backbone flexibility through reaction at ribose 2′-OH groups. Low reactivity may indicate pairing or rigid tertiary packing, while high reactivity often indicates flexibility. RNA-binding proteins can shield residues and confound simple interpretation. Probe-derived constraints improve secondary-structure models but do not alone specify every atomic contact.

Consider a proposed hairpin in which positions 3–6 pair with 15–18. Probing shows low SHAPE reactivity across both arms and high reactivity in the intervening loop. This is consistent with a hairpin. If a mutation in one arm increases reactivity across both arms, and a compensatory mutation restores low reactivity, the conclusion becomes stronger. Still, two competing folds might explain some of the same data. Use multiple probes, changes under different ionic conditions or direct structural information to discriminate. A nonreactive base is not automatically paired, and an accessible base is not automatically functionally irrelevant.

X-ray crystallography can resolve atomic arrangements in suitably ordered samples; nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) can examine structures and dynamics, particularly for smaller RNAs; cryogenic electron microscopy can resolve large assemblies such as ribosomes. These methods have different sample requirements and uncertainties. A structure may represent a particular ligand-bound or crystallised state rather than every state in solution. The yeast phenylalanine tRNA structure deposited as PDB 1EHZ is a real example of atomic structural evidence for the L-shaped architecture. Distance-sensitive fluorescence measurements can also report whether labelled sites approach one another. FRET efficiency increases strongly as donor and acceptor approach, approximately E = 1/[1+(r/R₀)⁶] in the standard Förster model, where r is the separation and R₀ is the characteristic distance at which E = 0.5. At r = 2R₀, E = 1/65 ≈ 1.54%; this illustrates why FRET is a sensitive distance reporter, not a direct measurement of a particular base pair. Detailed instrumentation is reserved for Unit 14.

Computational minimum-free-energy models propose structures from empirical energetic rules. A single lowest-energy drawing can hide other populated structures, and some common models omit pseudoknots. Partition-function approaches estimate ensemble pairing probabilities, which can be more informative when folds compete. Treat these calculations as predictions that require comparison with evidence. In an examination question, always ask which experimental observation is directly measured, what structural inference follows, and what alternative explanations remain.

16. DNA and RNA: comparing chemical tendencies, not absolutes

The differences between the two polymers can now be traced from atoms to function. DNA's deoxyribose backbone is relatively resistant to the neighbouring-group cleavage pathway and its long complementary duplexes support stable information storage. RNA's additional hydroxyl offers more interaction possibilities and a means of intramolecular cleavage. RNA also commonly contains uracil rather than thymine. In DNA, use of thymine rather than uracil helps repair systems recognise uracil formed by cytosine deamination. RNA may also contain ribothymidine and many other modified nucleosides; the ordinary alphabet is a starting point rather than a complete chemical inventory.

Ordinary cellular RNA is often a single covalent strand that forms local helices. However, some viral genomes are double-stranded RNA, and some RNAs form extensive intermolecular duplexes. Some viruses carry single-stranded DNA, and DNA itself can form hairpins and other non-B structures under suitable conditions. The categories ‘single-stranded RNA’ and ‘double-stranded DNA’ therefore describe common biological forms, not universal chemical restrictions. Similarly, RNA can be a genomic information store in many viruses; DNA can bind proteins and participate in structured regulatory complexes. Do not use common functions as absolute definitions.

Stability must be specified. Resistance to alkaline backbone cleavage is different from the free energy of duplex formation and different again from lifetime in a cell. Under comparable conditions, an RNA duplex may have strong base stacking and a stable A-form structure even though RNA has a chemically labile backbone. The next chapter addresses the roles of sequence, salt, temperature and molecular topology in greater detail. For now, when asked which polymer is more stable, decide what type of stability is being measured. The most productive way to understand RNA is to follow the causal chain: chemical groups allow local contacts; local contacts organise a fold; the fold presents surfaces that bind or react; the cellular context determines when the function occurs.

17. Original NET/SET practice: Part B and Part C

These are original questions designed to test the reasoning required at NET/SET level. They are not represented as official previous-year questions. Work out each answer before opening its explanation.

Part B — conceptual multiple-choice questions

B1. A 5′-AUGCC-3′ RNA segment forms a perfect antiparallel duplex. Which partner is written correctly in the 5′→3′ direction? (A. UACGG · B. GGCAU · C. CCGUA · D. TACGG)

Explanation: GGCAU. First write the complementary strand as 3′-UACGG-5′, then reverse it. UACGG has the wrong orientation, TACGG introduces T, and CCGUA is not the reverse complement.

B2. Which ribose group explains the characteristic alkaline cleavage of ordinary RNA? (A. base N9 · B. 5′-phosphate · C. 1′-H · D. 2′-OH)

Explanation: 2′-OH. Its deprotonated oxygen attacks the neighbouring phosphorus. A 5′ phosphate and base N9 are not that internal nucleophile.

B3. What best describes the common conformation of an RNA duplex? (A. Right-handed A-form · B. left-handed A-form · C. Z-form only · D. B-form only)

Explanation: Right-handed A-form. The ribose geometry favours it; B-form is common for hydrated DNA, and Z-RNA is a special exception.

B4. What is represented by a bulge? (A. two entire separate RNAs · B. all unpaired bases in an RNA · C. Unpaired bases on one side of a stem · D. a terminal loop)

Explanation: Unpaired bases on one side of a stem. A terminal loop closes a hairpin; an internal loop interrupts both strands.

B5. Which tRNA component directly bears its amino acid? (A. anticodon · B. Terminal A of 3′-CCA · C. TΨC loop · D. D loop)

Explanation: Terminal A of 3′-CCA. The amino acid is esterified to ribose; anticodon recognition and elbow contacts serve different functions.

B6. Which rRNA is a major component of the bacterial large-subunit peptidyl-transferase centre? (A. 18S · B. 16S · C. 5.8S · D. 23S)

Explanation: 23S. 16S is in the bacterial small subunit, 18S is typical of eukaryotic small subunits and 5.8S is a eukaryotic large-subunit component.

B7. Which interaction characterises a pseudoknot in a linear arc representation? (A. Crossing base-pair arcs · B. mandatory covalent circularisation · C. absence of base pairs · D. only nested arcs)

Explanation: Crossing arcs, with endpoints i < k < j < l. Nested arcs can describe ordinary stems; covalent circularisation is not required.

B8. Which RNA serves as template and scaffold rather than the polymerase catalyst of telomerase? (A. hammerhead RNA · B. RNase P RNA · C. Telomerase RNA · D. 23S rRNA)

Explanation: Telomerase RNA. Its associated reverse transcriptase protein performs DNA synthesis; the other options are associated with RNA-based catalysis.

B9. Which observation is most consistent with a typical animal miRNA seed interaction? (A. only its last nucleotide binds · B. Guide positions 2–8 recognise target · C. the guide attaches an amino acid · D. all targets must be perfectly matched)

Explanation: Positions 2–8 are important for many animal miRNA targets. Pairing elsewhere and context matter; perfect pairing is not universal and amino acid attachment belongs to tRNA.

B10. Which product pair is typical of hammerhead cleavage? (A. free amino acids · B. 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate only · C. two intact duplexes · D. 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and 5′-OH)

Explanation: Cyclic phosphate and 5′-OH. This is intramolecular transesterification; the hydrolytic product pair is characteristic of other cleavage pathways such as RNase P.

Part C — analytical, numerical and experimental reasoning

C1. A 16-nt RNA has pairs (1,16), (2,15), (3,14), (4,13), (5,12). Find the number of pairs and the fraction of paired residues. Is the structure nested?

Explanation: Five pairs; ten residues paired; 10/16 = 62.5%. All pairs nest, and the central six residues form a possible loop. Counting five divided by sixteen incorrectly uses pairs instead of residues.

C2. Two 12-nt model folds have free energies −8 and −5 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300 K. Estimate the equilibrium population of the lower-energy state, assuming only two states.

Explanation: RT = 2.494 kJ mol⁻¹. Higher/lower ratio = exp(−3/2.494) ≈ 0.300. Lower fraction = 1/1.300 ≈ 0.769, or 77%. The energy difference is not 3/8 of the population; the Boltzmann dependence is exponential.

C3. A four-pair stem has stacking terms −2, −3, −2 kcal mol⁻¹, loop +4 and initiation +1. Compute the illustrative folding free energy. Removing one of its internal pairs eliminates the first two stacks, all else fixed. What changes?

Explanation: Original ΔG = −2−3−2+4+1 = −2 kcal mol⁻¹. Removing two negative contributions increases ΔG by 5, to +3 kcal mol⁻¹. This artificial fixed-term model ignores reorganisation of the disrupted stem, but demonstrates why base-pair counting alone is inadequate.

C4. For folding, ΔH = −60 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −0.180 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Compute ΔG at 310 K and the ideal two-state midpoint.

Explanation: ΔG = −60+55.8 = −4.2 kJ mol⁻¹. Midpoint = 60/0.180 = 333.3 K ≈60.2 °C. The sign follows ΔH−TΔS, not ΔH+TΔS; a midpoint is not a guarantee that all tertiary contacts melt together.

C5. A pair of stem mutations gives activities WT 100%, left 8%, right 12%, double 89%. RNA abundance is the same in all cases. Which interpretation is best?

Explanation: A conserved interaction between those positions is strongly supported if both single changes disrupt complementarity and the double restores it. Equal abundance reduces one confounder. Activity recovery alone does not establish atomic geometry; probing provides further evidence.

C6. An RNA remains paired in monovalent salt; Mg²⁺ increases FRET between distant labelled regions while stem-probe signals barely change. What is the parsimonious interpretation?

Explanation: Mg²⁺ promotes a more compact tertiary arrangement without requiring major secondary structure changes. Higher FRET suggests shorter effective distance under controlled fluorophore conditions. It does not show direct metal catalysis or establish a specific base triple.

C7. Identify the structural class of pairs (2,10) and (5,14). What is wrong with describing them using two nested pairs?

Explanation: 2<5<10<14, so the pair arcs cross and constitute pseudoknot connectivity. Nesting would require the later opening to close first, which is not true here. A second bracket type or explicit pair list is required.

C8. An anticodon is 5′-IAG-3′. Using classical inosine wobble, which codons are recognised?

Explanation: Align 3′-GAI-5′ against 5′-CU(N)-3′. I pairs with U, C or A, giving CUU, CUC and CUA, all leucine codons in the standard code. CUG is not predicted under these simple rules. Modifications can affect actual recognition.

C9. A tRNA reads UUU correctly but is accidentally charged with leucine. If it participates in translation, what is inserted? Explain the level at which fidelity failed.

Explanation: Leucine. Correct codon pairing does not independently verify amino acid identity. The charging step failed; the ribosome selects the anticodon–codon complex rather than directly translating amino acid chemistry.

C10. An mRNA mutation strengthens a hairpin covering its bacterial ribosome-binding site. Protein output falls to 20% but RNA abundance stays at 100%. Predict a useful rescue experiment.

Explanation: Disrupt the complementary arm without changing the ribosome-binding site or coding sequence; verify reduced pairing and restored protein output per RNA. This tests accessibility rather than merely correlating a mutation with expression. A binding or initiation assay would strengthen the inference.

C11. Two targets pair with a guide at −14 and −11 kcal mol⁻¹. Opening their sites costs +10 and +3 respectively. Which is more favourable in this simplified coupled model?

Explanation: Target 1: −4; target 2: −8 kcal mol⁻¹. Target 2 is more favourable despite less favourable isolated duplex formation. Protein interactions and kinetic effects are omitted, so this does not by itself guarantee a larger cellular response.

C12. A single-site aptamer has Kd = 50 nM. At free ligand 150 nM, what fraction is bound? Does this equal the fraction of transcription terminated?

Explanation: θ=150/(50+150)=0.75. This is the equilibrium occupancy of an ideal binding site, not necessarily the fraction terminated, which also depends on the expression platform and transcription kinetics.

C13. An aptamer mutant retains ligand binding but no longer represses translation. A second mutant loses both binding and regulation. Which structural regions are suggested?

Explanation: The first may disrupt coupling or the expression platform; the second may disrupt the aptamer or global folding. Direct folding and affinity tests are needed; the result is inconsistent with assuming binding alone always determines regulatory output.

C14. Assertion: RNA can catalyse phosphodiester transfer reactions. Reason: specific RNA folds can position nucleophiles and leaving groups and stabilise a transition state. Assess both.

Explanation: Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. Mere possession of a 2′-OH does not make all RNA a catalyst; the active-site geometry and chemical environment are crucial.

C15. A deoxyribose substitution at a hammerhead substrate cleavage site abolishes cleavage; binding affinity is unchanged. Explain and contrast RNase P.

Explanation: The required 2′-OH nucleophile is missing, so the transesterification pathway is blocked. RNase P hydrolysis uses water rather than the substrate ribose's 2′ oxygen as its attacking nucleophile; the two product chemistries differ. Unchanged affinity supports but does not absolutely prove unchanged folding.

C16. A proposed stem has low SHAPE reactivity in both arms. Mutating one arm causes high reactivity in both; the double compensatory mutant restores low reactivity. Which conclusions are warranted?

Explanation: The changes support coupled folding of the two arms and restoration of a rigid/paired region. SHAPE measures local backbone reactivity, not a direct inventory of hydrogen bonds. The exact pairing register may need comparative or atomic structural evidence.

C17. An RNA has 30 G, 10 C, 25 A and 15 U in one strand. Can it be fully internally Watson–Crick paired? Find the maximum possible number of canonical pairs based only on composition.

Explanation: No. At most min(30,10)=10 G–C plus min(25,15)=15 A–U pairs =25 pairs or 50 of 80 residues (62.5%). Sequence order and loop constraints can lower the actual number. Wobble pairs are excluded by the question.

C18. A native 70S bacterial ribosome separates into 30S and 50S particles. A student claims the observation violates conservation of mass. Explain.

Explanation: Svedberg coefficients describe sedimentation, not mass. Shape and friction of associated subunits differ from those of separated particles, so S values are not additive. Mass conservation is not violated.

C19. The same RNA sequence produces a compact structure when slowly annealed and a different structure when rapidly cooled. Both persist for minutes. Does this contradict thermodynamics?

Explanation: No. Different folding histories can lead to metastable states separated by large barriers. Thermodynamics describes equilibrium populations; kinetic barriers determine access. Time-dependent relaxation or controlled annealing can test the hypothesis.

18. Consolidation and bridge to nucleic-acid stability

A nucleotide sequence is more than a series of letters. Its ribose chemistry makes an A-form duplex and permits local flexibility and specific additional contacts. Complementary regions produce stems; unpaired regions allow turns and junctions; interleaved pairing produces pseudoknots; stacking and tertiary interactions organise three-dimensional surfaces. The ion environment and folding history influence which of these surfaces are available. In mRNA, a fold can control accessibility; in tRNA, it separates recognition from amino-acid delivery; in rRNA, it organises a catalytic machine; in regulatory RNAs, it supports selective recognition; in ribozymes, it helps organise reactive groups.

As a final self-test, explain why the presence of Mg²⁺ can make a tertiary RNA fold more stable but cannot by itself prove that the ion performs catalysis. Draw a four-pair hairpin and name the base pairs in the correct direction. Explain why a compensatory double mutant is more informative than a single mutant. Trace the recognition of a codon from anticodon orientation to aminoacyl attachment. If these steps can be explained without consulting the definitions, the essential connections of this chapter are established.

The next chapter extends the same physical reasoning to nucleic-acid melting, renaturation, hybridisation and the topology of constrained DNA. In particular, separate chemical degradation from structural unfolding, and remember that the number of base pairs alone does not define the free energy of a molecule. These distinctions prepare the ground for the later study of protein conformations and biological catalysis.
