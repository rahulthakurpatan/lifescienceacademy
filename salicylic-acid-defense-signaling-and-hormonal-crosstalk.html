1. Why a plant needs salicylic acid

A healthy leaf must capture light, exchange gases, manufacture carbon compounds and maintain the physical integrity of its tissues. All these functions depend on living cells. A bacterium multiplying in the spaces between those cells or a fungus extracting nutrients from them threatens the same processes. Unlike a mobile animal, a plant cannot move away from an infection. Its cells must therefore detect danger and adjust their own activities while communicating with tissues that have not yet encountered the pathogen. Salicylic acid is one of the chemical regulators that connects local danger to a more extensive change in physiological priorities.

The first distinction to establish is between a pathogen and a pathogen-associated signal. The pathogen is an organism or infectious agent capable of causing disease in a susceptible host. A signal may be a microbial molecular pattern, a consequence of cellular damage or a change in the concentration of a plant metabolite. These are not interchangeable categories. Salicylic acid is predominantly an endogenous plant signal, not itself the invading organism or the general receptor that first recognizes all invaders. Its concentration and activity can change following upstream recognition. This chapter follows the hormone rather than cataloguing the entire machinery of pathogen recognition, which is treated separately in stress physiology.

Salicylic acid, abbreviated SA, is 2-hydroxybenzoic acid. It has an aromatic ring bearing a hydroxyl group adjacent to a carboxyl group. That relative arrangement distinguishes it from other hydroxybenzoic acid isomers. Its molecular formula is C7H6O3. The hydroxyl group attached to an aromatic ring makes SA a phenolic compound. Many phenolic compounds contribute to plant physiology, but not all are hormones. SA earns its place among hormonal regulators because its endogenous concentration, perception and downstream regulatory activity can be altered in ways that change plant responses. A structurally related chemical may fail to trigger the same response if it is not appropriately recognized or metabolized.

An increase in SA concentration is not identical to an increase in immunity. Immunity is an outcome of multiple processes, such as changes in gene expression, antimicrobial metabolism and the capacity to restrict pathogen multiplication. A leaf can contain considerable SA but fail to mount a normal transcriptional response if the required signaling protein is defective. Conversely, a leaf previously exposed to a signal may respond unusually rapidly to a subsequent infection without maintaining a large amount of every defense protein throughout the intervening period. This distinction between signal amount, signal interpretation and physiological output will guide the analysis of every mutant and experiment in the chapter.

SA is especially associated with defense against many pathogens that obtain nutrients from living tissue. Such pathogens are termed biotrophs. Hemibiotrophs can pass through phases with different relationships to host viability, and necrotrophs obtain nutrients from killed tissue. These terms classify lifestyles rather than hormone receptors. The distinction helps explain why a defense effective against one organism can be less useful against another, but it is not an absolute rule assigning each pathogen to precisely one hormone. A plant may experience wounding, water shortage and infection simultaneously. Its eventual phenotype reflects the integration of signals rather than the isolated action of an arbitrary hormone.

The history of the field illustrates the difference between discovering a chemical and demonstrating a biological signaling mechanism. Salicylate chemistry was known long before molecular plant biology. Later experiments on infected plants connected changes in endogenous SA with the induction of defense and resistance. Genetic studies identified plants unable to produce normal amounts of SA and plants unable to respond appropriately to it. These observations provided a causal framework in which synthesis, perception and downstream responses could be studied separately. The modern pathway is therefore not simply a list of metabolites inferred from their chemical similarity; it is supported by biochemical, physiological and genetic lines of evidence.

2. From chorismate to salicylic acid

The carbon skeleton of SA links defense to the aromatic metabolism of plants. Chorismate is produced through the shikimate pathway and serves as a branch-point precursor for aromatic amino acids and numerous specialized compounds. A branch point is a metabolite from which several competing biochemical routes depart. It is important to distinguish the existence of chorismate from the rate at which carbon flows toward salicylic acid. A plant does not convert all of its chorismate to SA during infection. It must continue to support other metabolic functions, and the abundance and activity of enzymes determine the distribution of metabolic flux.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, a major source of pathogen-induced salicylic acid is the isochorismate pathway. Isochorismate synthase 1, abbreviated ICS1 and also associated with the genetic locus SID2, operates in the plastid. It converts chorismate into isochorismate. This is an isomerization: the molecular arrangement changes, but the enzyme has not yet produced salicylic acid. A student who stops at the name isochorismate synthase and assigns the entire formation of SA to this protein misses the essential later reactions. ICS1 is necessary for efficient flux through the major induced route, but more than one protein participates in completion of the pathway.

Plastids possess membranes that separate their internal metabolite pool from the surrounding cytosol. An intermediate made in a plastid does not automatically become available to an enzyme outside it. EDS5 is a transporter associated with this boundary and is required for the export of isochorismate in the principal Arabidopsis pathway. The transport step is an integral part of hormone synthesis. In a mutant unable to export enough intermediate, activity of the plastid enzyme alone cannot guarantee the delivery of substrate to the subsequent cytosolic enzyme. Therefore a transporter mutation can look like a biosynthetic defect when the final product is measured, even though the transporter is not an enzyme forming or breaking the intermediate's covalent bonds.

The cytosolic protein PBS3 belongs to a family of enzymes capable of activating carboxyl-containing compounds before forming conjugates. In this pathway it uses ATP and glutamate to form an isochorismate-glutamate conjugate. The conjugation is not a storage reaction for already synthesized SA. Rather, the substrate is a precursor of SA. The distinction matters because other enzymes also form conjugates involving the mature hormone, but those reactions have different physiological consequences. The PBS3 product can decompose to form SA; in Arabidopsis, EPS1 facilitates the formation of SA from this intermediate. Thus glutamate, ATP, cellular compartmentation and a specialized catalytic step together connect primary metabolism with hormone production.

A diagram of the pathway should be interpreted spatially as well as chemically. The first conversion occurs inside the plastid, passage of the intermediate crosses the plastid boundary, and subsequent reactions occur in the cytosol. If an examination presents an ICS1 enzyme targeted incorrectly to another compartment, it is not sufficient to know its substrate specificity. One must also ask whether that compartment contains an accessible source of chorismate and whether the later reactions can receive the product. A correct enzyme in the wrong location can produce a deficient physiological phenotype. Conversely, experimentally redirecting a downstream enzyme can sometimes provide a means of testing which intermediate actually crosses a membrane.

The evidence for this pathway developed in stages. Identification of SID2 as a gene encoding isochorismate synthase in Arabidopsis linked a genetic requirement for resistance with a biochemical activity. Later studies examined the intermediate and the reaction catalyzed by PBS3, establishing how a eukaryotic plant completes a pathway that had previously been compared with bacterial SA synthesis. The bacterial analogy is useful up to a point: bacteria can employ a direct isochorismate lyase mechanism. One should not assume that the same enzyme necessarily performs the same final reaction in a flowering plant. Studies by Rekhter and colleagues and by Torrens-Spence and colleagues in 2019 resolved central parts of the plant route by examining intermediates and enzymatic transformations.

Plants also possess routes that connect phenylalanine metabolism to salicylate. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, or PAL, removes ammonia from phenylalanine to form cinnamate. Conversion of cinnamate-derived carbon to benzoate-related intermediates and subsequent hydroxylation can contribute to SA formation, with pathway details and relative importance depending on species. PAL is also important for other phenylpropanoid products. Consequently, inhibition of PAL can affect plant defense through more mechanisms than a decrease in SA. A reduction in resistance after disrupting PAL cannot by itself establish that the affected phenotype is exclusively caused by a decrease in salicylate.

Species comparisons require especially careful reasoning. Arabidopsis has become a model for detailed investigation of the isochorismate route; its convenient genetic tools do not mean that every crop uses the same dominant pathway. A plant may show a higher contribution from phenylalanine-derived carbon, different constitutive SA levels or distinct regulatory enzymes. An exam question may supply isotope-tracing observations in a species that has not been discussed in a textbook. The appropriate conclusion should follow the evidence rather than a memorized assumption that the exact Arabidopsis route is universally predominant. The shared biological concept is regulated production of a phenolic signal using aromatic metabolic precursors.

3. Free SA, storage and breakdown

To understand how defense starts and stops, consider the hormone pool within a leaf. Free SA is chemically distinct from its glucose conjugates, methyl ester and hydroxylated metabolites. An analysis reporting total salicylate after hydrolysis may include several conjugated pools. A measurement reporting only free SA is narrower. If two experimental treatments give the same total amount but distribute that amount differently between free and bound forms, their capacities to activate signaling need not be the same. Definitions of measured quantities therefore matter as much as the numbers.

Glycosyltransferases attach sugar groups to a range of plant metabolites. In SA homeostasis, conjugation with glucose can generate salicylic acid glucoside, often abbreviated SAG, and other conjugates such as the glucose ester. SAG is generally considered a less directly active storage form and can accumulate in vacuolar pools. The conjugation reaction changes polarity and interactions with membranes or transport systems. This makes it possible to reduce the free SA pool without immediately destroying the aromatic carbon skeleton. Hydrolytic release can, where appropriate, return hormone from a conjugate to a free pool. Different conjugates should not be treated as a single chemically uniform species or assumed to have identical rates of hydrolysis.

A separate transformation is methylation of the carboxyl group to form methyl salicylate, MeSA. This ester lacks the freely dissociating carboxyl proton of the acid and has different volatility and partitioning behavior. In tissues possessing an appropriate methyl salicylate esterase, hydrolysis regenerates SA. Consequently methylation can serve both as a mechanism lowering free SA in a source tissue and as a mechanism enabling movement of a derivative in particular biological contexts. A distant response depends not only on production of the mobile form but also on what happens after it arrives. If an esterase is absent, a tissue may receive a derivative without producing enough free hormone to respond normally.

Hydroxylation provides another route of inactivation. The Arabidopsis protein DMR6 has SA 5-hydroxylase activity, generating 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid. A related route generates 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid through SA 3-hydroxylation. These additional hydroxyl groups change the compound and its ability to act as free SA. The products can be further conjugated. It is helpful to distinguish this catabolism from simple reversible masking of the parent hormone. In an experiment inhibiting DMR6 activity, the expectation is reduced flux through that hydroxylation branch, not a general cessation of every pathway that removes SA.

Negative feedback can arise when the activity of a hormone promotes expression of an enzyme that limits its free concentration. This arrangement is useful for a plant because a defense program appropriate during infection may consume resources or impede growth when no longer required. SA-induced homeostatic reactions can limit the magnitude or duration of a response. Yet a feedback mechanism does not force all time courses to follow the same shape. If pathogen-dependent synthesis persists, an elevated concentration may be maintained despite substantial catabolism. If synthesis ends, the remaining conjugation and degradation capacity can lower free SA. An observed steady concentration may therefore conceal considerable simultaneous formation and removal.

For quantitative interpretation, let F represent the amount of free SA in a leaf region, expressed in nanomoles, and let every flux be measured in nanomoles per hour. A mass balance is dF/dt = v_synthesis + v_release + v_import − v_conjugation − v_catabolism − v_export. This equation is not an empirical kinetic law. It is bookkeeping for the free pool. When the derivative is zero, input and output fluxes balance; the individual fluxes need not be zero. If the amount is expressed per gram of fresh tissue, growth or changing water content can additionally alter the measured concentration. To avoid confusing production with dilution, experimental comparisons should use consistent normalization and sampling.

Consider an illustrative interval in which synthesis supplies 8 nmol/h, hydrolysis returns 2 nmol/h, and export is negligible. Conjugation removes 4 nmol/h and oxidative breakdown removes 3 nmol/h. The initial net accumulation is 3 nmol/h. If the starting pool is 12 nmol and rates remain constant for four hours, the calculated pool reaches 24 nmol. This simple result is not a prediction of real infection kinetics because feedback may change every flux. The arithmetic nevertheless helps identify the information needed to infer whether a rising hormone concentration is caused by faster synthesis or slower removal. Either could produce the same measured rise.

4. Local and long-distance hormone communication

Plant signaling has spatial structure. A cell near an infection may produce SA because its own upstream signaling pathway has been activated. A distant cell may produce SA because a signal generated elsewhere induces its local biosynthetic pathway. These two possibilities yield the same qualitative observation of increased SA in both sites, but they imply different transport mechanisms. Measurement of a compound in a distant organ does not prove that the measured molecules traveled intact from the infected leaf.

SA is a weak organic acid. The carboxyl group can occur in protonated and deprotonated forms according to the chemical environment. The distribution between these forms affects membrane partitioning. At typical cytosolic pH, the carboxylate form predominates; the extent of passive membrane crossing also depends on the membrane and any transport pathways present. It would be misleading to infer a universal polar transport system for SA solely from its acidic chemistry. Auxin transport involves specific influx and efflux systems and polarized localization of carriers. SA has its own movement and metabolism, and an analogy with auxin is limited to general principles of partitioning and compartmentation.

MeSA provides a particularly instructive example because the methyl derivative has chemical properties distinct from free SA. In tobacco, experiments on systemic acquired resistance demonstrated a role for MeSA production in an infected tissue and conversion back to SA in a responding tissue. These findings explain why blocking ester formation or hydrolysis can change resistance far from the initial infection. They do not show that MeSA is the only mobile immune signal of all plants. Plants differ in how they establish long-distance defense, and multiple signals may operate together.

N-hydroxypipecolic acid, abbreviated NHP, is a lysine-derived immune regulatory metabolite involved in systemic acquired resistance, particularly well characterized in Arabidopsis. It is produced through a route involving pipecolic acid and FMO1. NHP can induce changes in distal tissues that include activation of SA-related defense. An important distinction is that a signal moving from one location can stimulate the synthesis of a different signal at the receiving location. Thus the movement of NHP and the induction of SA biosynthesis need not be competing explanations. Both can occur as part of a connected signaling network.

Imagine a graft consisting of genetically different shoot regions connected through functional vascular tissue. The initially treated region can be made deficient in production of a candidate mobile signal, while the distant responding region has a normal biosynthetic capacity. Conversely, the responding region can be made deficient in the ability to perceive SA while the source region is normal. These complementary arrangements test different requirements. Failure when the source lacks a synthetic enzyme suggests a defect in generating or sending a signal. Failure when the receiver lacks a response component suggests that the distant tissue cannot interpret an arriving or locally synthesized signal. The graft must heal sufficiently to support exchange, and controls must rule out nonspecific effects of grafting or tissue damage.

A labeled-metabolite experiment addresses a different question. If isotope-labeled SA is supplied locally and labeled SA is subsequently recovered from distant tissue, some material derived from that supply has reached the distant tissue. Depending on the design, it may still have passed through an intermediate derivative. Unlabeled SA accumulation in that same distant tissue may indicate new synthesis from endogenous precursors. A careful experiment distinguishes intact isotopic species, tracks labeled derivatives and includes controls for contamination. Genetic necessity, chemical movement and physiological sufficiency are three related but separable claims. Systemic signaling is best understood when these forms of evidence are brought together.

5. NPR proteins and SA-dependent transcription

Once SA is present in the appropriate cellular pool, the next question is how it alters gene expression. A hormone receptor or perception component converts information about ligand availability into a change in protein activity, interaction or localization. In plant SA signaling the NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES family, commonly called NPR proteins, is central. NPR1 is a major positive regulator of many SA-dependent transcriptional responses. NPR3 and NPR4 participate in related SA-responsive regulation and can have distinct effects on transcription and protein homeostasis. It is important to avoid treating these three proteins as equivalent copies with identical functions.

NPR1 is often introduced as a transcriptional coactivator. A coactivator contributes to transcription by interacting with other regulatory proteins, rather than necessarily recognizing a specific DNA sequence on its own. TGA transcription factors recognize particular DNA sequence elements in promoters and participate in the expression of SA-responsive genes. The interaction between NPR proteins and TGA factors therefore provides a route by which the chemical signal can influence selected promoters. This explains why an experiment detecting SA binding by a purified NPR protein is mechanistically distinct from an experiment measuring transcription of PR1 in an intact plant. One tests a molecular interaction and the other tests an output of a larger pathway.

A useful simplified model begins with relatively low SA signaling in an unchallenged tissue. Basal defense is present, but large induction of selected pathogenesis-related genes is limited. Following infection, SA increases and NPR-centered transcriptional regulation changes. NPR1 can participate in activation of target genes with transcription factors, whereas NPR3 and NPR4 can participate in repression under low-SA conditions that is relieved through SA-dependent regulation. The precise contribution of family members depends on the target, cell context and signaling state. The modern view is therefore a receptor and regulatory network rather than a single linear chain in which every NPR protein has the same sign of action.

The evidence for direct SA perception has developed through biochemical binding studies, genetics and structural analyses. Early models emphasized regulation of NPR1 by SA-associated redox changes, while subsequent work demonstrated ligand binding by NPR family proteins and refined the structural basis of perception. A common textbook drawing portrays NPR1 as an inactive oligomer that becomes active after reduction and movement into the nucleus. This drawing conveys the important idea of regulated NPR1 state and nuclear function, but it should not be interpreted as a universal, fully resolved description of all molecular events or every experimental condition. SA binding, redox regulation, conformational changes and protein interactions together determine function.

Protein turnover is another level of regulation. The amount of an active regulator is a balance between synthesis, state conversion and degradation. Destruction of a signaling protein need not simply shut down a pathway in every context: controlled turnover can be associated with cycling of transcriptional complexes, while excessive loss of the same regulator can impair the response. This is why a mutation preventing all turnover is not automatically equivalent to increased resistance. A student should distinguish a direct measurement of the protein's abundance from an inference about the transcriptional activity of the remaining protein.

The expression of PATHOGENESIS-RELATED 1, or PR1, is frequently used as an indicator of the SA-dependent response. A marker is a measurable indicator associated with a process. It is not identical to the entire process. A higher PR1 transcript level supports the inference that a transcriptional defense program has been activated, but does not independently establish resistance against every pathogen. Disease outcome can depend on additional genes, microbial effectors, environmental conditions and the competence of tissues to execute defense. The best genetic studies measure both a molecular readout and pathogen performance. If a plant has a strong PR1 response but the pathogen still grows normally, the investigator should not ignore the second measurement.

A useful way to analyze mutant responses is to supply the product of a biosynthetic pathway externally. Consider an ICS1-deficient plant with otherwise functional perception machinery and an npr1 mutant impaired in a major downstream transcriptional route. Supplying SA can bypass much of the need for ICS1 in an appropriate assay. It cannot restore a missing functional NPR1 protein merely by making more ligand available. The prediction is that the biosynthetic mutant may regain an SA-responsive marker more effectively than the response mutant. This inference assumes adequate delivery, a noninjurious dose, and an output that actually depends on NPR1. Different genes may retain NPR1-independent responsiveness, so it is more accurate to make predictions about a specified output than to declare that npr1 has no SA response of any kind.

6. Systemic acquired resistance and immune priming

A plant challenged on one leaf may develop enhanced resistance in untreated leaves. This phenomenon is called systemic acquired resistance, or SAR, when a local immune-inducing challenge establishes a broader, persistent defensive state. The concept is often compared loosely with memory because past exposure changes a subsequent response. Unlike animal antibody-mediated adaptive immunity, however, SAR does not involve antigen-specific expansion of B or T lymphocytes. Its basis lies in plant physiological and transcriptional changes. The analogy is valuable only to communicate the temporal distinction between an initial challenge and a later response.

SAR can be studied by treating lower leaves with a pathogen that elicits local defense, leaving upper leaves uninoculated. After an appropriate interval, upper leaves are challenged with a test pathogen. Parallel plants receive a mock treatment on lower leaves before the same challenge. The important comparison is pathogen multiplication in the subsequently challenged upper leaves. If prior local treatment reduces subsequent pathogen growth, this supports induced systemic protection. To distinguish signaling from inadvertent spread of the initial inoculum, the investigator must ensure the upper leaves did not already contain the first pathogen. Similar plant age, environmental conditions and challenge inoculum are also essential.

SA is required for efficient establishment or execution of SAR in many well-studied systems. Local pathogen recognition can promote SA production, and distal tissues may activate their own SA synthesis and signaling. NPR1 contributes to a major transcriptional arm of systemic defense. NHP has emerged as a crucial component of the Arabidopsis SAR network and can participate in reinforcement of SA-associated responses. The biological system therefore includes feedback and communication between metabolites. A model that places only a single molecule in a one-way arrow from the infected leaf to every other leaf is generally too simple to explain the evidence.

Priming means that a later stimulus elicits a faster or stronger response because of a previous signal. This is different from continuously maintaining a high concentration of every defense product. A primed tissue can have nearly basal expression of a particular gene before the second challenge yet induce it much more strongly after challenge. This arrangement can reduce the physiological expense of keeping all protective pathways maximally active at all times. However, priming is not guaranteed to be free of cost, and not every example of elevated basal resistance is necessarily due to priming.

Quantitative data can separate these patterns. Suppose an illustrative experiment finds relative PR1 expression of 1 in untreated and unchallenged plants, 2 in previously induced but unchallenged plants, 5 in untreated plants after challenge, and 20 in previously induced plants after challenge. The basal difference is small compared with the challenge-dependent difference. The induced plant increases expression tenfold relative to its own baseline, while the control increases fivefold. This pattern is consistent with priming of the marker. The numbers alone cannot establish SAR unless they come from suitable distant tissue and are accompanied by a meaningful resistance readout. It is possible to show a transcriptional interaction without demonstrating protection.

SAR should also be distinguished from induced systemic resistance in the classical context of beneficial root-associated microbes. In many such systems, JA and ethylene signaling contribute strongly and SA is not the defining central requirement. There are exceptions and overlapping pathways. The terms describe physiological phenomena with different initiating contexts and typical signaling dependencies; they do not justify a universal chemical definition that excludes all overlap. A well-designed exam question may give the inducing organism, tissue and genetic dependence, which provide stronger classification evidence than a single hormone measurement.

7. SA, jasmonates, ethylene and ABA in combination

The previous chapter introduced jasmonates through the fatty-acid-derived JA-Ile signal and COI1–JAZ–MYC regulatory system. SA and JA differ in precursor chemistry, receptors and downstream transcriptional partners. A wound response and a biotrophic infection can nevertheless occur in the same plant. The resulting defenses need not be the arithmetic sum of two responses measured separately. Cross-talk describes the influence of one signaling pathway on another pathway's synthesis, perception, signal transmission or output.

A common experimental pattern is that SA treatment decreases the expression of certain genes induced by jasmonates. This is called antagonism at the measured output. In many Arabidopsis contexts the SA branch favors defenses associated with biotrophic or hemibiotrophic pathogens, whereas the JA branch participates in responses to herbivory and necrotrophic pathogens. Allocating defensive resources to the threat most relevant at a given time can be advantageous. Yet an antagonistic transcriptional relationship does not prove that the two hormones cannot coexist or that their concentrations must vary inversely. One pathway can reduce a selected downstream response while both hormones remain abundant.

Timing and concentration strongly influence conclusions about cross-talk. If SA is applied before, during or after jasmonate treatment, different regulatory proteins may already have accumulated. The same SA dose can consequently alter the amplitude, duration or onset of a JA-associated marker in different ways. An experiment that tests only a single dose at a single time provides a limited observation. It does not establish a universal interaction. Dose-response and time-course experiments help distinguish a transient delay from persistent suppression of the maximum response.

An informative four-condition comparison uses a mock control, SA alone, JA alone and the combined treatment. Suppose a JA-associated marker has illustrative values 1, 1, 12 and 4, respectively. The combination reduces the JA-induced output relative to JA alone. The effect cannot be described as loss of all JA signaling because the marker remains above the untreated value. If the same samples contain comparable JA-Ile concentrations after JA alone and combined treatment, the results favor a change at or downstream of JA perception rather than a simple decrease in ligand abundance. That inference still requires suitable measurements at the relevant sites and times. A reporter could be affected indirectly by tissue damage or general transcriptional inhibition, which controls must address.

Ethylene influences defense through pathways whose receptor system and signal-transduction logic differ from both SA and JA. JA and ethylene can cooperate in the activation of certain defense genes, including the classical PDF1.2-associated branch. JA signaling also has other outputs associated with MYC transcription factors; it is not a uniform response in all contexts. Thus a study measuring only PDF1.2 cannot necessarily describe the behavior of all JA-regulated genes. NPR-centered regulation, TGA-associated factors and additional transcriptional interactions can change the behavior of selected defense branches without directly preventing formation of JA-Ile.

Abscisic acid is central to water conservation and seed dormancy, as discussed earlier in this subunit. Under conditions of water shortage ABA can promote stomatal closure through its receptor, phosphatase and kinase network. Stomatal closure may also restrict entry of certain foliar pathogens, depending on their infection route. At another stage of infection ABA can attenuate particular SA-dependent defenses in some systems. These observations are not contradictory: they describe different sites of action and different measured outcomes. A change beneficial for restricting entry need not improve every post-entry immune response. Ethylene may alter ABA sensitivity in growth or germination contexts, while ABA and ethylene responses can interact differently in flooded or dehydrating tissues. A fixed reciprocal rule does not capture this biological diversity.

A plant simultaneously regulates growth through auxin, cytokinins, gibberellins and brassinosteroids. Growth requires carbon skeletons, energy, mineral nutrients and cell expansion. Defense can redirect these resources and can also alter signaling pathways that normally promote growth. The trade-off is therefore not purely a shortage of material, nor purely a competition between transcription factors. Both physiological allocation and signaling interactions contribute. In some cases a constitutively high-SA plant grows poorly, but small size alone cannot establish whether the cause is direct SA-dependent signaling, a metabolic perturbation or persistent disease. Comparing growth in a defense-signaling mutant with growth in a biosynthetic mutant may help separate these possibilities.

A practical interpretation rule follows from these examples: specify the two pathways, tissue, developmental state, timing, doses, measured outcome and relevant genotype before assigning the words antagonism or synergy. The same pair of hormones can cooperate for one response and oppose each other for another. The significance of cross-talk is that a plant can coordinate responses under changing demands, not that each hormone has a predetermined positive or negative relationship with every other one.

8. Evidence, controls and examination reasoning

The classical experiments on plant SA signaling provide a framework for reasoning from evidence. In studies of tobacco and other plants, accumulation of SA was observed in association with disease resistance and induction of pathogenesis-related proteins. Observation establishes correlation. To establish a requirement for SA, investigators used methods such as expression of bacterial salicylate hydroxylase, which reduces SA by converting it to catechol. Plants with reduced SA showed defects in some defense responses. An important limitation is that the introduced enzyme changes metabolism beyond simply subtracting the hormone. Catechol or other secondary consequences can affect phenotypes. Independent mutations in endogenous plant biosynthetic genes provide a valuable complementary test.

A mutant deficient in ICS1 has a mechanistic interpretation that differs from a transgenic plant expressing a degrading enzyme. The former reduces one route of synthesis, while the latter increases removal. Both may show reduced free SA after infection. Measurement of intermediates, enzyme expression and hormone rescue can help distinguish the two mechanisms. The most persuasive argument for a biosynthetic route combines genetic disruption with biochemical identification of the enzyme product and restoration by a functional gene. Rescue with an externally supplied hormone shows that a downstream response can still function, but does not alone identify the precise reaction normally catalyzed by the mutated protein.

An experiment with npr1 adds the receptor and response distinction. Imagine wild type, sid2 and npr1 plants treated with a solvent control or SA at a suitable concentration, and sampled after the same interval. Measure free hormone, PR1 transcript and growth of a defined test pathogen. If sid2 has reduced infection-induced SA but recovers PR1 expression with treatment, the evidence supports a lesion upstream of SA-dependent gene activation. If npr1 accumulates SA but fails to express PR1 adequately, the bottleneck is downstream of hormone availability for this output. The pathogen assay tests whether recovery of the marker corresponds to recovery of resistance, a separate conclusion that should be drawn only when data support it.

To interpret relative transcript abundance, a student may encounter quantitative PCR data. For a method using the delta-delta Ct approximation, target cycle thresholds are normalized to a stable reference gene, and fold change can be estimated as 2 to the negative delta-delta Ct power if amplification efficiencies are sufficiently similar and near ideal. Suppose untreated tissue has target Ct 29 and reference Ct 21, giving delta Ct 8. Induced tissue has target Ct 25 and reference Ct 21, giving delta Ct 4. The delta-delta Ct is negative 4, corresponding to an estimated sixteenfold increase. The calculation concerns relative transcript abundance, not the absolute number of PR1 protein molecules or the amount of SA. If reference expression changes with stress, normalization may be misleading. The quality of the biological experiment is not established by correct arithmetic alone.

A second illustrative dataset measures bacteria in distant leaves after challenge. The mock-pretreated wild type contains one million colony-forming units per gram of tissue, and the locally induced wild type contains ten thousand. The difference is a hundredfold, or two orders of magnitude. If an induced sid2 line contains eight hundred thousand, the same local challenge did not generate comparable protection in that genotype. One must still compare with the mock-pretreated sid2 baseline before attributing the difference specifically to lost induction. A mutant may differ in basal susceptibility. In logarithmic plots a unit difference represents a multiplicative rather than additive change, so equal visual distances correspond to equal ratios.

Experimental reasoning must distinguish necessity from sufficiency. When a loss-of-function mutation eliminates an outcome, the gene may be necessary under the tested conditions. When application of a chemical induces a response without the original stimulus, the chemical may be sufficient under the tested conditions. Neither claim automatically establishes that the gene is the sole mediator or that the applied dose reproduces physiological concentrations. Complementation, dose controls, independent alleles and direct biochemical assays make the argument stronger. A scientific conclusion states what has been established within the scope of the experiment and leaves other possibilities open.

9. A comparative framework for the nine hormone classes

The study of SA completes the major small-molecule hormone sequence in this subunit. The most transferable principle is that hormone action involves a chain of causal events: metabolic production, homeostatic control, spatial distribution, perception, intracellular signal processing and physiological output. Not every class implements these steps in the same manner. Ethylene is a gas, so reversible storage and movement of its precursor ACC differ from the storage and transport of a glucose-conjugated phenolic acid. Brassinosteroids frequently act close to their synthesis site rather than following a universal long-distance hormone transport pattern. Auxin distribution depends strongly on polarized transport in many developmental settings. These differences reflect chemistry and biological organization.

Some hormone pathways use regulated removal of a repressor. Auxin perception promotes destruction of Aux/IAA proteins, freeing ARF-associated transcriptional responses from repression. Bioactive gibberellins promote GID1-dependent recognition and turnover of DELLA repressors, allowing growth. Jasmonoyl-isoleucine promotes association of COI1 with JAZ proteins, leading to repressor degradation. Strigolactone perception through D14 and MAX2 targets SMXL-family proteins in relevant pathways. Despite a common design principle involving ubiquitin-dependent turnover, the ligand, perception partner, regulatory target and physiological output differ. A mutation stabilizing one repressor cannot be expected to affect all four hormone pathways in the same way.

Cytokinins use a phosphorelay involving histidine kinase receptors, phosphotransfer proteins and response regulators. ABA frequently uses a double-negative signaling arrangement: hormone-bound PYR/PYL proteins inhibit PP2C phosphatases that normally inhibit SnRK2 kinases. Ethylene receptors generally maintain suppression of ethylene responses in the absence of ligand, with CTR1 and EIN2 contributing to downstream regulation. Brassinosteroids signal through a membrane receptor kinase system involving BRI1 and a coreceptor, ultimately affecting phosphorylation of transcriptional regulators. SA provides an example of NPR-family-centered regulation of defense transcription. Learning the direction of regulation is more valuable than memorizing the pathway as an unexamined string of protein names.

When comparing hormone mutants, identify whether the affected component is involved in synthesis, activation, removal, movement, perception or downstream response. A biosynthetic lesion can often be bypassed with the corresponding active hormone, whereas a lesion in an essential perception component typically cannot. A mutant with a constitutively active downstream component can produce a response in low ligand conditions. A mutant lacking a positive regulator may have the opposite behavior. These are reasoning patterns rather than universal phenotype rules. For instance, removing a negative regulator can increase rather than decrease the output. A pathway diagram that specifies activating and inhibiting relationships allows a student to predict the direction of effects without relying on isolated facts.

Finally, all hormone signaling occurs within the constraints of plant physiology. Photosynthetic capacity, respiratory energy production, nitrogen assimilation, water relations and development shape the responses available to a tissue. A leaf that activates defense still needs resources for maintenance. A seed responding to ABA and GA cannot ignore the availability of stored reserves. A root exposed to nutrient deficiency may adjust architecture through multiple signaling classes. The nine-hormone framework is therefore not a taxonomy of isolated biological jobs. It is a collection of regulatory mechanisms that coordinate the behavior of a living organism across times, locations and environmental conditions.
