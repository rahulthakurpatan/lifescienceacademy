1. Why a plant needs salicylic acid

A healthy leaf must capture light, exchange gases, manufacture carbon compounds and maintain the physical integrity of its tissues. All these functions depend on living cells. A bacterium multiplying in the spaces between those cells or a fungus extracting nutrients from them threatens the same processes. Unlike a mobile animal, a plant cannot move away from an infection. Its cells must therefore detect danger and adjust their own activities while communicating with tissues that have not yet encountered the pathogen. Salicylic acid is one of the chemical regulators that connects local danger to a more extensive change in physiological priorities.

The first distinction to establish is between a pathogen and a pathogen-associated signal. The pathogen is an organism or infectious agent capable of causing disease in a susceptible host. A signal may be a microbial molecular pattern, a consequence of cellular damage or a change in the concentration of a plant metabolite. These are not interchangeable categories. Salicylic acid is predominantly an endogenous plant signal, not itself the invading organism or the general receptor that first recognizes all invaders. Its concentration and activity can change following upstream recognition. This chapter follows the hormone rather than cataloguing the entire machinery of pathogen recognition, which is treated separately in stress physiology.

Salicylic acid, abbreviated SA, is 2-hydroxybenzoic acid. It has an aromatic ring bearing a hydroxyl group adjacent to a carboxyl group. That relative arrangement distinguishes it from other hydroxybenzoic acid isomers. Its molecular formula is C7H6O3. The hydroxyl group attached to an aromatic ring makes SA a phenolic compound. Many phenolic compounds contribute to plant physiology, but not all are hormones. SA earns its place among hormonal regulators because its endogenous concentration, perception and downstream regulatory activity can be altered in ways that change plant responses. A structurally related chemical may fail to trigger the same response if it is not appropriately recognized or metabolized.

An increase in SA concentration is not identical to an increase in immunity. Immunity is an outcome of multiple processes, such as changes in gene expression, antimicrobial metabolism and the capacity to restrict pathogen multiplication. A leaf can contain considerable SA but fail to mount a normal transcriptional response if the required signaling protein is defective. Conversely, a leaf previously exposed to a signal may respond unusually rapidly to a subsequent infection without maintaining a large amount of every defense protein throughout the intervening period. This distinction between signal amount, signal interpretation and physiological output will guide the analysis of every mutant and experiment in the chapter.

SA is especially associated with defense against many pathogens that obtain nutrients from living tissue. Such pathogens are termed biotrophs. Hemibiotrophs can pass through phases with different relationships to host viability, and necrotrophs obtain nutrients from killed tissue. These terms classify lifestyles rather than hormone receptors. The distinction helps explain why a defense effective against one organism can be less useful against another, but it is not an absolute rule assigning each pathogen to precisely one hormone. A plant may experience wounding, water shortage and infection simultaneously. Its eventual phenotype reflects the integration of signals rather than the isolated action of an arbitrary hormone.

The history of the field illustrates the difference between discovering a chemical and demonstrating a biological signaling mechanism. Salicylate chemistry was known long before molecular plant biology. Later experiments on infected plants connected changes in endogenous SA with the induction of defense and resistance. Genetic studies identified plants unable to produce normal amounts of SA and plants unable to respond appropriately to it. These observations provided a causal framework in which synthesis, perception and downstream responses could be studied separately. The modern pathway is therefore not simply a list of metabolites inferred from their chemical similarity; it is supported by biochemical, physiological and genetic lines of evidence.

2. Chemical structure, ionization and biological availability

The behavior of a hormone depends in part on the molecular groups that it carries. Salicylic acid contains a carboxyl group attached directly to an aromatic ring, with a hydroxyl group on the neighboring ring carbon. Numbering begins with the ring carbon bearing the carboxyl group, so the hydroxyl group occupies position two. The structural term ortho describes this adjacent arrangement. The fact that a hydroxyl group appears at position two has consequences for molecular geometry and recognition by a protein-binding site. A compound carrying the hydroxyl group at position three is an isomer with the same elemental formula but a different spatial arrangement. A protein that binds one compound need not bind the other with equal affinity.

The simplest representation of the acid equilibrium is HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Here HA denotes the carboxyl-protonated form of SA, while A⁻ denotes the singly deprotonated carboxylate. In the range of pH ordinarily considered for cellular hormone movement, dissociation of the carboxyl proton is the relevant acid-base transition. The phenolic hydroxyl is not simply another carboxyl group. If a question depicts salicylic acid crossing a membrane in its electrically neutral form, identify which functional group bears the proton in this representation. The carboxylate form can interact differently with water, transport proteins and charged binding sites than the neutral acid.

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation relates the concentration ratio of these two forms to the pH and acid dissociation constant: pH = pKa + log10([A⁻]/[HA]). The pKa of the carboxyl group of SA is approximately 3 in dilute aqueous conditions. In an idealized comparison at pH 5, the ratio of anion to neutral acid is approximately 100:1, so the neutral fraction is about 1/101 or 0.99 percent. At pH 7 the ratio is about 10,000:1, and the neutral fraction is approximately 0.01 percent. These calculations assume that activities can be approximated by concentrations and that only the relevant acid-base transition is considered. A low proportion of neutral molecules does not mean that transport is impossible; neutral molecules can continuously be replenished by equilibration, and membrane proteins may facilitate movement of charged compounds.

The word free in a hormone measurement should not be confused with electrically neutral. Free SA usually means salicylate not chemically conjugated to another moiety, and includes the acid-base forms present in the sampled environment. Neutral SA represents only one protonation state within that pool. If a leaf extract is acidified during extraction, more SA may be present in the protonated form, but this does not indicate that the same fraction was neutral inside the intact cell before extraction. Analytical conditions can alter chemical speciation, which is one reason to distinguish a measurement procedure from the biological state that is being investigated.

Methylation and glycosylation are distinct from protonation. A proton can be added or lost in an acid-base equilibrium without an enzymatic change in covalent structure of the carbon skeleton. Methyl salicylate contains an ester group, and converting it back to salicylate requires ester hydrolysis. A glucoside contains a glycosidic linkage that must be cleaved to release the corresponding unconjugated molecule. These modifications therefore have different implications for persistence. Changes in pH can rapidly redistribute acid-base forms without any requirement for a biosynthetic enzyme, whereas a change in a conjugated pool generally depends on rates of formation, transport and enzymatic or chemical breakdown.

A comparison of SA and jasmonic acid further illustrates why chemical classification does not determine signaling specificity. Both are organic acids, but SA has an aromatic ring, whereas jasmonates are produced from fatty-acid-derived oxylipin metabolism. The active JA-Ile signal contains an amino acid conjugated to JA. Although both hormones can influence defense, their biosynthetic reactions, recognition proteins and physiological effects differ. Similarly, acetylsalicylic acid, commonly known as aspirin, is a chemically modified salicylate familiar from human pharmacology. Its existence does not mean that mammalian aspirin targets explain normal plant SA signaling. Plant hormone mechanisms must be inferred from plant biochemical and genetic evidence.

The molecular concentration of a hormone provides information about availability but does not directly report receptor occupancy. In a simple illustrative model of one ligand binding one site at equilibrium, the occupied fraction is [L]/(Kd + [L]), where Kd is the dissociation constant and [L] is the free ligand concentration. If the free concentration equals Kd, half of the sites are occupied in that model. If the concentration increases from Kd to nine times Kd, occupancy increases from 0.5 to 0.9, not ninefold. Actual NPR proteins participate in complexes with more complicated regulatory behavior, so this model must not be assigned as an experimentally established dose-response law for NPR1. Its purpose is to show why increasing a signal concentration and increasing a physiological response do not have to be proportional. Receptor amount, binding affinity and downstream amplification all influence the relationship.

An examination may describe two tissues containing the same free-SA concentration but different amounts of a positive response regulator. The simple occupancy fraction of the regulator, if its binding properties are unchanged, might be similar in both tissues, while the total amount of occupied regulator differs. If one tissue lacks the appropriate DNA-binding factor or has inaccessible target chromatin, even that increase in occupied regulator may not yield the same transcriptional response. Chemical availability is a necessary starting point, not the complete explanation of sensitivity.

3. From chorismate to salicylic acid

The carbon skeleton of SA links defense to the aromatic metabolism of plants. Chorismate is produced through the shikimate pathway and serves as a branch-point precursor for aromatic amino acids and numerous specialized compounds. A branch point is a metabolite from which several competing biochemical routes depart. It is important to distinguish the existence of chorismate from the rate at which carbon flows toward salicylic acid. A plant does not convert all of its chorismate to SA during infection. It must continue to support other metabolic functions, and the abundance and activity of enzymes determine the distribution of metabolic flux.

In Arabidopsis thaliana, a major source of pathogen-induced salicylic acid is the isochorismate pathway. Isochorismate synthase 1, abbreviated ICS1 and also associated with the genetic locus SID2, operates in the plastid. It converts chorismate into isochorismate. This is an isomerization: the molecular arrangement changes, but the enzyme has not yet produced salicylic acid. A student who stops at the name isochorismate synthase and assigns the entire formation of SA to this protein misses the essential later reactions. ICS1 is necessary for efficient flux through the major induced route, but more than one protein participates in completion of the pathway.

Plastids possess membranes that separate their internal metabolite pool from the surrounding cytosol. An intermediate made in a plastid does not automatically become available to an enzyme outside it. EDS5 is a transporter associated with this boundary and is required for the export of isochorismate in the principal Arabidopsis pathway. The transport step is an integral part of hormone synthesis. In a mutant unable to export enough intermediate, activity of the plastid enzyme alone cannot guarantee the delivery of substrate to the subsequent cytosolic enzyme. Therefore a transporter mutation can look like a biosynthetic defect when the final product is measured, even though the transporter is not an enzyme forming or breaking the intermediate's covalent bonds.

The cytosolic protein PBS3 belongs to a family of enzymes capable of activating carboxyl-containing compounds before forming conjugates. In this pathway it uses ATP and glutamate to form an isochorismate-glutamate conjugate. The conjugation is not a storage reaction for already synthesized SA. Rather, the substrate is a precursor of SA. The distinction matters because other enzymes also form conjugates involving the mature hormone, but those reactions have different physiological consequences. The PBS3 product can decompose to form SA; in Arabidopsis, EPS1 facilitates the formation of SA from this intermediate. Thus glutamate, ATP, cellular compartmentation and a specialized catalytic step together connect primary metabolism with hormone production.

A diagram of the pathway should be interpreted spatially as well as chemically. The first conversion occurs inside the plastid, passage of the intermediate crosses the plastid boundary, and subsequent reactions occur in the cytosol. If an examination presents an ICS1 enzyme targeted incorrectly to another compartment, it is not sufficient to know its substrate specificity. One must also ask whether that compartment contains an accessible source of chorismate and whether the later reactions can receive the product. A correct enzyme in the wrong location can produce a deficient physiological phenotype. Conversely, experimentally redirecting a downstream enzyme can sometimes provide a means of testing which intermediate actually crosses a membrane.

The evidence for this pathway developed in stages. Identification of SID2 as a gene encoding isochorismate synthase in Arabidopsis linked a genetic requirement for resistance with a biochemical activity. Later studies examined the intermediate and the reaction catalyzed by PBS3, establishing how a eukaryotic plant completes a pathway that had previously been compared with bacterial SA synthesis. The bacterial analogy is useful up to a point: bacteria can employ a direct isochorismate lyase mechanism. One should not assume that the same enzyme necessarily performs the same final reaction in a flowering plant. Studies by Rekhter and colleagues and by Torrens-Spence and colleagues in 2019 resolved central parts of the plant route by examining intermediates and enzymatic transformations.

Plants also possess routes that connect phenylalanine metabolism to salicylate. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, or PAL, removes ammonia from phenylalanine to form cinnamate. Conversion of cinnamate-derived carbon to benzoate-related intermediates and subsequent hydroxylation can contribute to SA formation, with pathway details and relative importance depending on species. PAL is also important for other phenylpropanoid products. Consequently, inhibition of PAL can affect plant defense through more mechanisms than a decrease in SA. A reduction in resistance after disrupting PAL cannot by itself establish that the affected phenotype is exclusively caused by a decrease in salicylate.

Species comparisons require especially careful reasoning. Arabidopsis has become a model for detailed investigation of the isochorismate route; its convenient genetic tools do not mean that every crop uses the same dominant pathway. A plant may show a higher contribution from phenylalanine-derived carbon, different constitutive SA levels or distinct regulatory enzymes. An exam question may supply isotope-tracing observations in a species that has not been discussed in a textbook. The appropriate conclusion should follow the evidence rather than a memorized assumption that the exact Arabidopsis route is universally predominant. The shared biological concept is regulated production of a phenolic signal using aromatic metabolic precursors.

Scientific evidence: Isochorismate synthase genetics; PBS3 biochemistry; EPS1 and PBS3.

4. How evidence separated hormone synthesis from perception

To see why the modern pathway is more informative than a list of reactions, reconstruct the questions that confronted investigators. First, is a compound associated with an immune response? Second, does it contribute causally to the response? Third, how does the plant produce the compound? Fourth, which molecules interpret its presence? These questions are logically distinct. It is possible to establish an association long before the molecular identity of a biosynthetic enzyme or receptor becomes known. A chapter organized by the modern pathway can obscure this chronological logic unless the evidence underlying the individual steps is examined.

Observations of salicylate accumulation during infection and the induction of pathogenesis-related proteins following exogenous application supported the hypothesis of a signaling role. However, external application exposes a plant to a chemical under conditions that can differ from normal endogenous production. Concentration, application route and timing can all be altered. A response to an external supply therefore supports sufficiency under the chosen experimental conditions. It does not alone establish a requirement for synthesis of endogenous SA during ordinary infection. Establishing a requirement called for independent reduction of hormone availability or disruption of its endogenous formation.

The use of bacterial salicylate hydroxylase provided one influential means of reducing SA within plant tissues. The enzyme converts SA to catechol, which means it both removes one molecule and produces another. The idealized reasoning was straightforward: if reduced endogenous SA is accompanied by impaired induced resistance, salicylate contributes to protection. However, a transgene may change multiple aspects of physiology. The accumulation of a degradation product can itself affect the response. Appropriate controls and independent loss-of-function mutants therefore matter. It is incorrect to discard the entire experiment because it has a limitation; it is equally incorrect to treat one experiment as definitive proof of every aspect of the model.

The genetic study of the SID2 locus offered a complementary strategy. Mutant plants were identified on the basis of defects in the defense-associated increase in SA. Characterizing the affected gene revealed an isochorismate synthase. This connected an independently defined phenotype with a specific biochemical conversion. Gene names and protein activities are different types of information: SID2 identifies a genetic locus, while ICS1 describes its enzyme function. A mutant name may have arisen from an observed phenotype before the enzymatic activity was understood. In a question using both terms, they should not be interpreted as two successive enzymes merely because their names differ.

The relationship between synthesis and downstream response becomes particularly clear through the npr1 mutant. If SA application is used to induce defense-associated gene expression, mutations that reduce that output despite hormone supply can be isolated. Some of these mutations affect response regulators rather than hormone production. The phenotype is only a starting point; subsequent genetic and molecular analyses are needed to identify the gene product. This strategy illustrates a general principle shared with other plant hormones. The response to an externally supplied signal can distinguish a failure of signal production from a failure to interpret it, provided uptake and metabolism of the treatment are considered.

Classic grafting approaches addressed yet another dimension: communication among tissues. In an experiment where the induced tissue and the responding tissue differ genetically, the movement of the signal must be distinguished from the capacity of the receiving cells to respond. The question is not simply whether SA is necessary in the whole plant. SA could be required in source tissues, receiving tissues, or both; alternatively, the long-distance signal could be a different metabolite that induces local SA production. The persistence of a distal response when an initially treated region lacks a particular metabolic capacity could argue against a strict requirement in that source tissue without ruling out the molecule's importance in the receiver.

Biochemical analysis filled an important remaining gap in the ICS pathway. Identification of isochorismate synthase established a necessary early step, but did not explain how the product became SA in Arabidopsis. A candidate downstream enzyme can be tested with purified or appropriately prepared substrates and the required cosubstrates, using appropriate negative controls. Detection of a new compound only when enzyme and substrates are present is evidence of enzyme-dependent formation. Structural identification of the product, analysis of mutants and localization of the proteins help connect the reaction demonstrated outside the plant with metabolic flux inside it. The PBS3 and EPS1 findings of 2019 exemplify this convergence.

Likewise, identifying a protein essential for SA-dependent transcription is not identical to establishing direct ligand binding. A regulator can be indispensable because it functions downstream of another receptor. Binding assays using purified protein and suitable structural analogues can directly test ligand association, but purification conditions may alter protein conformation. Structural studies reveal candidate interaction sites; mutations at such sites can then be examined for their effects on protein function in living cells. The NPR family model is strongest because several distinct approaches connect SA perception to transcriptional regulation rather than because one historical experiment answered every question.

When asked to interpret scientific history, avoid reasoning backward from today's model as though every early investigator already knew the later steps. Observing a chemical increase did not establish its biosynthetic origin, and identifying ICS1 did not establish every subsequent conversion. Demonstrating a defense phenotype did not identify all long-distance signals. This gradual resolution of different questions is precisely what allows the modern chapter to teach mechanisms using experimentally grounded causal links.

5. Regulation of biosynthetic flux and compartmentation

The isochorismate pathway is not simply switched off in healthy tissue and switched on at the same level in every infected cell. Basal SA can contribute to constitutive defense regulation, and an inducible increase is superimposed on the basal pool. A plant's prior nutritional status, genotype and developmental stage influence aromatic metabolism. Infection changes the transcriptional and post-transcriptional state of many cells, with only some cells receiving a particular combination of signals. The experimentally measured response in a whole leaf is an average across this heterogeneity. A large increase in a small region can be diluted in an extract of the entire organ, while a modest increase in many cells can produce a similar organ-average concentration.

In Arabidopsis, transcription factors such as SARD1 and CBP60g help coordinate induction of multiple SA-biosynthetic genes. Consider the advantage of coordinated induction. Increasing ICS1 activity alone may raise the plastid isochorismate pool, but if export or cytosolic conversion cannot keep pace, the additional flux will be limited. Increasing a downstream enzyme alone may have little effect if it has insufficient substrate. Changes in expression of several pathway components can therefore increase the capacity of the complete route more effectively than an isolated change at one step. Nevertheless, gene expression is a proxy for potential capacity, not a direct measurement of flux. Protein abundance, enzyme activity and metabolite supply are additional determinants.

The notion of a rate-limiting enzyme is useful if treated carefully. In a series of reactions, a slow step may exert a major influence on throughput under a particular condition. Its control can change when substrate concentrations, enzyme activities or removal rates change. A student who says that ICS1 is rate-limiting solely because it is the first named enzyme has not supplied evidence. An experiment that compares several independent changes in pathway capacity, measures relevant intermediates and evaluates actual SA accumulation provides a stronger basis for determining where control is concentrated. An upstream step may be crucial in one physiological state, whereas availability of a downstream component becomes important after it is strongly induced.

Consider an illustrative steady-flow system in which a plastid forms isochorismate at a rate of 10 arbitrary units per hour and EDS5-mediated delivery to the cytosol can sustain only 4 units per hour under the tested conditions. If no alternative reactions remove isochorismate, the plastid pool initially increases at 6 units per hour. It would be inappropriate to assume that this continues indefinitely: as the pool changes, substrate use, feedback and transporter flux may change. Nevertheless, the example shows that the plastid can contain a large amount of intermediate even when cytosolic product formation is limited. A high level of the intermediate is compatible with both active synthesis and impaired onward use.

Suppose a separate mutant has normal intermediate export but reduced PBS3 activity. In a simplified pathway one expects delivery of isochorismate to the cytosol without efficient conversion to the conjugate. Accumulation of a cytosolic precursor and depletion of its product would support such a model. In a real system, alternative reactions and feedback may prevent detectable accumulation, so absence of a large pool does not exclude a block. A robust conclusion would combine targeted metabolite measurements, gene complementation and direct enzyme assays. The direction of change in a metabolite pool is a useful prediction, not a substitute for all other forms of evidence.

PBS3-dependent conjugation also illustrates the role of energy in biosynthesis. Formation of an amide linkage between an organic acid and an amino acid generally requires activation of the acid group. The enzyme uses ATP in the reaction, thereby coupling an energy-requiring chemical transformation to nucleotide metabolism. The glutamate incorporated into the intermediate is not the main aromatic precursor of SA. The aromatic carbon framework originates through chorismate-related metabolism. In isotope-tracing questions it matters which atoms of an intermediate are retained in the product. Labeling an amino acid cosubstrate does not necessarily label the final aromatic molecule if the relevant part of the conjugate is eliminated during formation of SA.

The link with nitrogen metabolism is therefore more subtle than stating that SA is a nitrogenous compound. SA itself contains no nitrogen. Glutamate participates in formation of a transient biosynthetic intermediate, and carbon and nitrogen metabolism support cellular capacities for synthesis and defense. The absence of nitrogen from the final formula does not imply that nitrogen-containing substrates or proteins are irrelevant to its production. Conversely, nitrogen availability could influence defense indirectly through growth or metabolism without necessarily changing the specific enzyme reaction under investigation. These distinctions connect the SA pathway to the earlier unit topics without duplicating amino acid biochemistry.

Regulation also occurs at the level of output from the precursor pool. The shikimate pathway supplies aromatic amino acids required for protein synthesis as well as compounds that contribute to plant specialized metabolism. A change in precursor allocation may affect numerous products, and loss of one pathway enzyme can alter the availability of substrate for other pathways. Such redistribution is a potential confounding factor when phenotypes are inferred solely from metabolite abundance. An exam may describe a mutant with increased chorismate but reduced SA. Rather than assuming excess precursor guarantees high product, ask which route is able to use it. An abundant common substrate does not bypass a missing specific enzyme.

6. Free SA, storage and breakdown

To understand how defense starts and stops, consider the hormone pool within a leaf. Free SA is chemically distinct from its glucose conjugates, methyl ester and hydroxylated metabolites. An analysis reporting total salicylate after hydrolysis may include several conjugated pools. A measurement reporting only free SA is narrower. If two experimental treatments give the same total amount but distribute that amount differently between free and bound forms, their capacities to activate signaling need not be the same. Definitions of measured quantities therefore matter as much as the numbers.

Glycosyltransferases attach sugar groups to a range of plant metabolites. In SA homeostasis, conjugation with glucose can generate salicylic acid glucoside, often abbreviated SAG, and other conjugates such as the glucose ester. SAG is generally considered a less directly active storage form and can accumulate in vacuolar pools. The conjugation reaction changes polarity and interactions with membranes or transport systems. This makes it possible to reduce the free SA pool without immediately destroying the aromatic carbon skeleton. Hydrolytic release can, where appropriate, return hormone from a conjugate to a free pool. Different conjugates should not be treated as a single chemically uniform species or assumed to have identical rates of hydrolysis.

A separate transformation is methylation of the carboxyl group to form methyl salicylate, MeSA. This ester lacks the freely dissociating carboxyl proton of the acid and has different volatility and partitioning behavior. In tissues possessing an appropriate methyl salicylate esterase, hydrolysis regenerates SA. Consequently methylation can serve both as a mechanism lowering free SA in a source tissue and as a mechanism enabling movement of a derivative in particular biological contexts. A distant response depends not only on production of the mobile form but also on what happens after it arrives. If an esterase is absent, a tissue may receive a derivative without producing enough free hormone to respond normally.

Hydroxylation provides another route of inactivation. The Arabidopsis protein DMR6 has SA 5-hydroxylase activity, generating 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid. A related route generates 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid through SA 3-hydroxylation. These additional hydroxyl groups change the compound and its ability to act as free SA. The products can be further conjugated. It is helpful to distinguish this catabolism from simple reversible masking of the parent hormone. In an experiment inhibiting DMR6 activity, the expectation is reduced flux through that hydroxylation branch, not a general cessation of every pathway that removes SA.

Negative feedback can arise when the activity of a hormone promotes expression of an enzyme that limits its free concentration. This arrangement is useful for a plant because a defense program appropriate during infection may consume resources or impede growth when no longer required. SA-induced homeostatic reactions can limit the magnitude or duration of a response. Yet a feedback mechanism does not force all time courses to follow the same shape. If pathogen-dependent synthesis persists, an elevated concentration may be maintained despite substantial catabolism. If synthesis ends, the remaining conjugation and degradation capacity can lower free SA. An observed steady concentration may therefore conceal considerable simultaneous formation and removal.

For quantitative interpretation, let F represent the amount of free SA in a leaf region, expressed in nanomoles, and let every flux be measured in nanomoles per hour. A mass balance is dF/dt = v_synthesis + v_release + v_import − v_conjugation − v_catabolism − v_export. This equation is not an empirical kinetic law. It is bookkeeping for the free pool. When the derivative is zero, input and output fluxes balance; the individual fluxes need not be zero. If the amount is expressed per gram of fresh tissue, growth or changing water content can additionally alter the measured concentration. To avoid confusing production with dilution, experimental comparisons should use consistent normalization and sampling.

Consider an illustrative interval in which synthesis supplies 8 nmol/h, hydrolysis returns 2 nmol/h, and export is negligible. Conjugation removes 4 nmol/h and oxidative breakdown removes 3 nmol/h. The initial net accumulation is 3 nmol/h. If the starting pool is 12 nmol and rates remain constant for four hours, the calculated pool reaches 24 nmol. This simple result is not a prediction of real infection kinetics because feedback may change every flux. The arithmetic nevertheless helps identify the information needed to infer whether a rising hormone concentration is caused by faster synthesis or slower removal. Either could produce the same measured rise.

Scientific evidence: DMR6 biochemical evidence.

7. Local and long-distance hormone communication

Plant signaling has spatial structure. A cell near an infection may produce SA because its own upstream signaling pathway has been activated. A distant cell may produce SA because a signal generated elsewhere induces its local biosynthetic pathway. These two possibilities yield the same qualitative observation of increased SA in both sites, but they imply different transport mechanisms. Measurement of a compound in a distant organ does not prove that the measured molecules traveled intact from the infected leaf.

SA is a weak organic acid. The carboxyl group can occur in protonated and deprotonated forms according to the chemical environment. The distribution between these forms affects membrane partitioning. At typical cytosolic pH, the carboxylate form predominates; the extent of passive membrane crossing also depends on the membrane and any transport pathways present. It would be misleading to infer a universal polar transport system for SA solely from its acidic chemistry. Auxin transport involves specific influx and efflux systems and polarized localization of carriers. SA has its own movement and metabolism, and an analogy with auxin is limited to general principles of partitioning and compartmentation.

MeSA provides a particularly instructive example because the methyl derivative has chemical properties distinct from free SA. In tobacco, experiments on systemic acquired resistance demonstrated a role for MeSA production in an infected tissue and conversion back to SA in a responding tissue. These findings explain why blocking ester formation or hydrolysis can change resistance far from the initial infection. They do not show that MeSA is the only mobile immune signal of all plants. Plants differ in how they establish long-distance defense, and multiple signals may operate together.

N-hydroxypipecolic acid, abbreviated NHP, is a lysine-derived immune regulatory metabolite involved in systemic acquired resistance, particularly well characterized in Arabidopsis. It is produced through a route involving pipecolic acid and FMO1. NHP can induce changes in distal tissues that include activation of SA-related defense. An important distinction is that a signal moving from one location can stimulate the synthesis of a different signal at the receiving location. Thus the movement of NHP and the induction of SA biosynthesis need not be competing explanations. Both can occur as part of a connected signaling network.

Imagine a graft consisting of genetically different shoot regions connected through functional vascular tissue. The initially treated region can be made deficient in production of a candidate mobile signal, while the distant responding region has a normal biosynthetic capacity. Conversely, the responding region can be made deficient in the ability to perceive SA while the source region is normal. These complementary arrangements test different requirements. Failure when the source lacks a synthetic enzyme suggests a defect in generating or sending a signal. Failure when the receiver lacks a response component suggests that the distant tissue cannot interpret an arriving or locally synthesized signal. The graft must heal sufficiently to support exchange, and controls must rule out nonspecific effects of grafting or tissue damage.

A labeled-metabolite experiment addresses a different question. If isotope-labeled SA is supplied locally and labeled SA is subsequently recovered from distant tissue, some material derived from that supply has reached the distant tissue. Depending on the design, it may still have passed through an intermediate derivative. Unlabeled SA accumulation in that same distant tissue may indicate new synthesis from endogenous precursors. A careful experiment distinguishes intact isotopic species, tracks labeled derivatives and includes controls for contamination. Genetic necessity, chemical movement and physiological sufficiency are three related but separable claims. Systemic signaling is best understood when these forms of evidence are brought together.

Scientific evidence: MeSA-dependent systemic communication.

8. NPR proteins and SA-dependent transcription

Once SA is present in the appropriate cellular pool, the next question is how it alters gene expression. A hormone receptor or perception component converts information about ligand availability into a change in protein activity, interaction or localization. In plant SA signaling the NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES family, commonly called NPR proteins, is central. NPR1 is a major positive regulator of many SA-dependent transcriptional responses. NPR3 and NPR4 participate in related SA-responsive regulation and can have distinct effects on transcription and protein homeostasis. It is important to avoid treating these three proteins as equivalent copies with identical functions.

NPR1 is often introduced as a transcriptional coactivator. A coactivator contributes to transcription by interacting with other regulatory proteins, rather than necessarily recognizing a specific DNA sequence on its own. TGA transcription factors recognize particular DNA sequence elements in promoters and participate in the expression of SA-responsive genes. The interaction between NPR proteins and TGA factors therefore provides a route by which the chemical signal can influence selected promoters. This explains why an experiment detecting SA binding by a purified NPR protein is mechanistically distinct from an experiment measuring transcription of PR1 in an intact plant. One tests a molecular interaction and the other tests an output of a larger pathway.

A useful simplified model begins with relatively low SA signaling in an unchallenged tissue. Basal defense is present, but large induction of selected pathogenesis-related genes is limited. Following infection, SA increases and NPR-centered transcriptional regulation changes. NPR1 can participate in activation of target genes with transcription factors, whereas NPR3 and NPR4 can participate in repression under low-SA conditions that is relieved through SA-dependent regulation. The precise contribution of family members depends on the target, cell context and signaling state. The modern view is therefore a receptor and regulatory network rather than a single linear chain in which every NPR protein has the same sign of action.

The evidence for direct SA perception has developed through biochemical binding studies, genetics and structural analyses. Early models emphasized regulation of NPR1 by SA-associated redox changes, while subsequent work demonstrated ligand binding by NPR family proteins and refined the structural basis of perception. A common textbook drawing portrays NPR1 as an inactive oligomer that becomes active after reduction and movement into the nucleus. This drawing conveys the important idea of regulated NPR1 state and nuclear function, but it should not be interpreted as a universal, fully resolved description of all molecular events or every experimental condition. SA binding, redox regulation, conformational changes and protein interactions together determine function. Structural work supports a dimeric, transcriptionally active NPR1 complex rather than requiring that the final active form always be a single protein molecule.

Protein turnover is another level of regulation. The amount of an active regulator is a balance between synthesis, state conversion and degradation. Destruction of a signaling protein need not simply shut down a pathway in every context: controlled turnover can be associated with cycling of transcriptional complexes, while excessive loss of the same regulator can impair the response. This is why a mutation preventing all turnover is not automatically equivalent to increased resistance. A student should distinguish a direct measurement of the protein's abundance from an inference about the transcriptional activity of the remaining protein.

The expression of PATHOGENESIS-RELATED 1, or PR1, is frequently used as an indicator of the SA-dependent response. A marker is a measurable indicator associated with a process. It is not identical to the entire process. A higher PR1 transcript level supports the inference that a transcriptional defense program has been activated, but does not independently establish resistance against every pathogen. Disease outcome can depend on additional genes, microbial effectors, environmental conditions and the competence of tissues to execute defense. The best genetic studies measure both a molecular readout and pathogen performance. If a plant has a strong PR1 response but the pathogen still grows normally, the investigator should not ignore the second measurement.

A useful way to analyze mutant responses is to supply the product of a biosynthetic pathway externally. Consider an ICS1-deficient plant with otherwise functional perception machinery and an npr1 mutant impaired in a major downstream transcriptional route. Supplying SA can bypass much of the need for ICS1 in an appropriate assay. It cannot restore a missing functional NPR1 protein merely by making more ligand available. The prediction is that the biosynthetic mutant may regain an SA-responsive marker more effectively than the response mutant. This inference assumes adequate delivery, a noninjurious dose, and an output that actually depends on NPR1. Different genes may retain NPR1-independent responsiveness, so it is more accurate to make predictions about a specified output than to declare that npr1 has no SA response of any kind.

Scientific evidence: NPR-family perception; NPR1 structural evidence.

9. The logic of activation, repression and transcriptional competence

It is worth exploring the NPR pathway as a problem in regulatory logic, because the same principles recur throughout the plant hormone subunit. A positive regulator is a component whose activity promotes a specified output, whereas a negative regulator reduces that output. The words positive and negative are defined relative to the measured outcome. They do not refer to whether the protein is beneficial to the plant. A negative regulator can be physiologically indispensable because it prevents inappropriate activation in the absence of an inducing signal. Conversely, continuous activation of a positive regulator can be harmful if the response is expressed at the wrong time.

NPR1 is important for activation of numerous SA-responsive genes. A simplified model in which NPR1 supplies positive transcriptional regulation can be used to make useful predictions. Consider a target promoter that responds to NPR1 and appropriate TGA proteins. In a cell where the positive regulatory function of NPR1 is lost, increased SA alone may not produce normal promoter activity. If the TGA-dependent promoter recognition step is also essential, a loss of the relevant TGA factors may produce a similar defect even though NPR1 remains present. These two genotypes could therefore have similar reporter phenotypes for different molecular reasons. A marker assay can locate a broad functional defect but may not distinguish the two proteins without additional evidence.

The NPR3 and NPR4 proteins introduce another dimension. Experimental models of their role have included SA-dependent regulation of NPR1 turnover and direct repression of selected defense-associated genes through interactions with transcription factors. These are not necessarily descriptions of mutually exclusive processes. They describe different regulatory functions that can contribute under particular conditions. In the direct transcriptional repression model, low SA permits repression by NPR3/4-associated complexes, and SA-dependent perception weakens repression. That branch has the logical form of release from inhibition. The NPR1-centered positive branch provides activation. Coordinated reduction of repression and increase in activation can sharpen a response even though no single protein acts as a universal on/off switch.

A hypothetical two-branch network illustrates the consequences. Suppose a promoter's activity requires an available positive regulator and is also limited by a separate repressor. Removal of repression alone permits a basal level of transcription, but the maximum response requires the positive branch. Conversely, making the activator constitutively available may not produce the maximum response while repression persists. The combination of the two changes can produce a stronger output. In this example a plant lacking a negative regulator might show elevated basal expression but still retain an additional response to SA through the positive branch. If a question gives this phenotype, the correct interpretation is not that the plant has lost all hormone sensitivity. Rather, its basal state and dynamic range have changed.

The activity of a regulatory protein is rarely determined by its concentration alone. A protein can be present but have an inappropriate subcellular localization, a modification state that reduces interaction with a partner, or a conformation that fails to support a particular function. Redox conditions can influence the state of cysteine-containing proteins, and SA-associated changes in cellular redox regulation contribute to models of NPR1 function. Reduction and oxidation refer to electron-transfer chemistry, not a general synonym for activation and inactivation. Depending on the protein and reaction, the reduced or oxidized state may have different consequences. There is no universal biochemical rule that reduction always activates plant defense proteins.

This caution matters when reading historical diagrams of NPR1 oligomerization. The idea that SA-associated redox changes regulate the functional state of NPR1 helped establish the importance of cellular redox processes in defense. However, details of oligomer formation and dissociation, conditions during protein extraction and structural transitions have required refinement. A modern examination-level explanation should retain the robust principles: SA perception regulates NPR-family proteins, NPR1 participates in transcriptional activation, nuclear function and interaction partners are important, and redox state and protein turnover can modulate the response. It should not assign every observed SA response to a single obligatory sequence of oligomer dissociation events.

Direct ligand binding and transcriptional regulation require different measurements. For a purified protein, binding of radiolabeled or otherwise detectable SA may be assessed by comparing association with the protein against nonspecific background and competition by unlabeled ligand. Structural analogues may reveal chemical specificity. In an intact plant, the corresponding mutation can be tested for changes in gene expression and resistance. A variant that loses binding but retains normal abundance is particularly informative if the mutation does not cause general misfolding or loss of interactions with all partners. Without evidence of retained structural integrity, failure to bind could reflect damage to the protein rather than loss of a specific recognition function.

The same logic applies to nuclear localization. If a mutant form of NPR1 remains outside the nucleus and fails to activate PR1, the observations support a requirement for the appropriate intracellular localization in that assay. But if the mutation also prevents interaction with TGA factors, there may be more than one explanation. Restoring nuclear targeting while retaining the original mutation could help distinguish localization from partner-binding defects. Such a conceptual experiment does not require memorization of specialist techniques. It requires identifying independent variables and predicting which result would support each explanation.

Protein turnover provides a dynamic rather than static view of regulation. If a transcriptional complex must be renewed repeatedly during sustained activation, controlled removal of an existing component can permit a new complex to form. In other circumstances degradation primarily reduces the active protein pool and attenuates the response. A mutation that abolishes ubiquitin-dependent degradation may therefore alter timing and amplitude in ways that cannot be predicted from total protein abundance alone. Measurements taken at several times are more informative than a single late measurement. If a mutant initially shows normal activation but fails to maintain it, the defect differs from one that prevents the initial response altogether.

A complete description of SA signaling therefore connects the extracellular or cellular challenge to changing hormone production, ligand-dependent regulation, transcriptional competence and physiological execution. No observation at one level should automatically be extrapolated to the next. The presence of SA does not prove ligand binding, ligand binding does not prove productive transcription, and a transcript response does not prove resistance. Each step is testable. This hierarchy is an especially useful organizing principle when answering a multiple-statement examination question that mixes correct observations with an unjustified causal inference.

10. Systemic acquired resistance and immune priming

A plant challenged on one leaf may develop enhanced resistance in untreated leaves. This phenomenon is called systemic acquired resistance, or SAR, when a local immune-inducing challenge establishes a broader, persistent defensive state. The concept is often compared loosely with memory because past exposure changes a subsequent response. Unlike animal antibody-mediated adaptive immunity, however, SAR does not involve antigen-specific expansion of B or T lymphocytes. Its basis lies in plant physiological and transcriptional changes. The analogy is valuable only to communicate the temporal distinction between an initial challenge and a later response.

SAR can be studied by treating lower leaves with a pathogen that elicits local defense, leaving upper leaves uninoculated. After an appropriate interval, upper leaves are challenged with a test pathogen. Parallel plants receive a mock treatment on lower leaves before the same challenge. The important comparison is pathogen multiplication in the subsequently challenged upper leaves. If prior local treatment reduces subsequent pathogen growth, this supports induced systemic protection. To distinguish signaling from inadvertent spread of the initial inoculum, the investigator must ensure the upper leaves did not already contain the first pathogen. Similar plant age, environmental conditions and challenge inoculum are also essential.

SA is required for efficient establishment or execution of SAR in many well-studied systems. Local pathogen recognition can promote SA production, and distal tissues may activate their own SA synthesis and signaling. NPR1 contributes to a major transcriptional arm of systemic defense. NHP has emerged as a crucial component of the Arabidopsis SAR network and can participate in reinforcement of SA-associated responses. The biological system therefore includes feedback and communication between metabolites. A model that places only a single molecule in a one-way arrow from the infected leaf to every other leaf is generally too simple to explain the evidence.

Priming means that a later stimulus elicits a faster or stronger response because of a previous signal. This is different from continuously maintaining a high concentration of every defense product. A primed tissue can have nearly basal expression of a particular gene before the second challenge yet induce it much more strongly after challenge. This arrangement can reduce the physiological expense of keeping all protective pathways maximally active at all times. However, priming is not guaranteed to be free of cost, and not every example of elevated basal resistance is necessarily due to priming.

Quantitative data can separate these patterns. Suppose an illustrative experiment finds relative PR1 expression of 1 in untreated and unchallenged plants, 2 in previously induced but unchallenged plants, 5 in untreated plants after challenge, and 20 in previously induced plants after challenge. The basal difference is small compared with the challenge-dependent difference. The induced plant increases expression tenfold relative to its own baseline, while the control increases fivefold. This pattern is consistent with priming of the marker. The numbers alone cannot establish SAR unless they come from suitable distant tissue and are accompanied by a meaningful resistance readout. It is possible to show a transcriptional interaction without demonstrating protection.

SAR should also be distinguished from induced systemic resistance in the classical context of beneficial root-associated microbes. In many such systems, JA and ethylene signaling contribute strongly and SA is not the defining central requirement. There are exceptions and overlapping pathways. The terms describe physiological phenomena with different initiating contexts and typical signaling dependencies; they do not justify a universal chemical definition that excludes all overlap. A well-designed exam question may give the inducing organism, tissue and genetic dependence, which provide stronger classification evidence than a single hormone measurement.

Scientific evidence: NHP and priming.

11. Following the systemic signal: necessity, movement and priming

A source leaf and a receiving leaf need not have identical roles in a systemic response. The source must detect a local challenge and generate a signal that can influence other parts of the plant. The receiving tissue must be connected to the source in a way that allows communication and must possess the capacity to execute the induced response. Some molecules can function at both locations, whereas others are especially important in one. For example, the presence of SA in a distal leaf may reflect local production induced by another mobile signal. The distinction between a source requirement and a receiver requirement is fundamental to interpreting grafting experiments.

The physical structure of the plant provides multiple potential means of communication. Vascular tissues connect distant organs, and metabolites or signals can move through these connections. Some volatile compounds can also influence tissues through the air. This is why the detection of a volatile derivative such as MeSA does not automatically identify its exact path in every experiment. A sealed or separated treatment design can help investigate whether airborne communication contributes, while vascular connection experiments address another route. The observation that a compound has volatile properties is not by itself proof that it reaches a particular distal cell by volatilization rather than by movement in a fluid or by a combination of processes.

Methyl salicylate formation provides a reversible chemical connection between the SA pool at a source and the potential SA pool of a recipient. The source must contain substrate and a suitable methyltransferase. The recipient must have the capacity to convert MeSA back to free SA if its downstream response depends on the acid. This interpretation has particular experimental support in tobacco, including work involving salicylic acid-binding protein 2, an enzyme with methyl salicylate esterase activity. The conclusion must remain tied to the system studied. A plant that relies strongly on another systemic signal may show a different phenotype following disruption of the same methylation branch.

Consider a hypothetical comparison in which source plants are either normal or deficient in a methyltransferase required for MeSA generation, while receiving leaves are either normal or deficient in the relevant esterase. If the response in that species and assay depends primarily on MeSA movement followed by hydrolysis, both single defects should impair distal induction. Supplying MeSA to a normal receiver could bypass deficient production in the source, but supplying MeSA to an esterase-deficient receiver might not restore a response requiring free SA. Direct application of SA to the receiver could bypass the hydrolysis defect if the perception machinery is functional. These are testable predictions of the specified model, not universal results for every plant species.

NHP-dependent systemic responses show why a single-pathway model is insufficient. NHP is related to lysine metabolism rather than to the aromatic precursor chemistry of SA. The pathway to NHP includes formation of pipecolic acid and hydroxylation by FMO1. In Arabidopsis, NHP accumulation is linked with induction of SA-dependent defenses and systemic priming. If NHP moves to a receiving tissue, it can help stimulate a local increase in SA synthesis and SA-dependent transcription. Thus the receiving tissue may need ICS1 for an induced increase in SA even when the long-distance information was conveyed by a different metabolite. The genetic requirement for SA biosynthesis in a recipient is consistent with, rather than contrary to, an NHP-mediated communication model.

One useful distinction is between initiation and amplification. A weak signal can arrive in a receiver and activate local metabolic and transcriptional changes that produce additional signal molecules. This amplifies the initial input. Local production can make the final hormone pool far larger than the amount of material physically delivered from the source. The amount of labeled source-derived molecule recovered in a recipient therefore need not equal the magnitude of the eventual response. A small flux can have a substantial regulatory effect if the receiver contains a responsive network. Conversely, a large delivered flux may have little effect in a response-deficient receiver.

The need for amplification also explains how distance and tissue competence shape systemic effects. A leaf that has recently expanded may differ from an older leaf in vascular connectivity, metabolic capacity and hormone sensitivity. If two receiving leaves display different PR1 induction after a common local challenge, this does not automatically show that the mobile compound failed to reach one of them. It could indicate different amounts of local biosynthetic enzymes, response regulators or opposing hormonal signals. Measuring both the candidate mobile signal and the downstream response is necessary to distinguish the alternatives.

Immune priming can be analyzed as an interaction between prior treatment and later challenge. The experimental design therefore includes four combinations rather than only two: neither treatment, prior treatment alone, challenge alone and both treatments. If the prior treatment alone strongly elevates a marker and adding the later challenge produces no additional change, the pattern is consistent with persistent induction rather than necessarily with enhanced responsiveness. If the prior treatment alone has little effect but substantially increases the magnitude of the challenge response, the pattern supports priming. The distinction is particularly important when interpreting a single post-challenge measurement. Without the unchallenged groups, persistent activation and priming may be impossible to separate.

A physiological test of resistance should distinguish the two temporal stages as well. A reduction of pathogen load in a leaf that never received the original inducing agent can support systemic protection. But if the original treatment itself contains a substance that directly inhibits the test pathogen and reaches the receiving leaf in sufficient concentration, the interpretation needs additional controls. The plant's own immune response and a direct antimicrobial effect are not the same mechanism. Testing the candidate compound on the pathogen separately, alongside plant genotypes with known response defects, can help assess which explanation is more plausible. No single test replaces the combination of chemical, genetic and physiological evidence.

These considerations also place SAR in its correct biological context. It is a form of induced resistance, not a guarantee that every future pathogen will fail to infect the plant. Protection may vary with the challenge organism, the time since induction and environmental conditions. The responsive state is subject to metabolic and regulatory constraints. Distinguish broad enhancement of protection against a range of challenges from an absolute immune state. The former is a useful description of SAR; the latter is too strong and is not supported by the mere presence of systemic defense markers.

12. SA, jasmonates, ethylene and ABA in combination

The previous chapter introduced jasmonates through the fatty-acid-derived JA-Ile signal and COI1–JAZ–MYC regulatory system. SA and JA differ in precursor chemistry, receptors and downstream transcriptional partners. A wound response and a biotrophic infection can nevertheless occur in the same plant. The resulting defenses need not be the arithmetic sum of two responses measured separately. Cross-talk describes the influence of one signaling pathway on another pathway's synthesis, perception, signal transmission or output.

A common experimental pattern is that SA treatment decreases the expression of certain genes induced by jasmonates. This is called antagonism at the measured output. In many Arabidopsis contexts the SA branch favors defenses associated with biotrophic or hemibiotrophic pathogens, whereas the JA branch participates in responses to herbivory and necrotrophic pathogens. Allocating defensive resources to the threat most relevant at a given time can be advantageous. Yet an antagonistic transcriptional relationship does not prove that the two hormones cannot coexist or that their concentrations must vary inversely. One pathway can reduce a selected downstream response while both hormones remain abundant.

Timing and concentration strongly influence conclusions about cross-talk. If SA is applied before, during or after jasmonate treatment, different regulatory proteins may already have accumulated. The same SA dose can consequently alter the amplitude, duration or onset of a JA-associated marker in different ways. An experiment that tests only a single dose at a single time provides a limited observation. It does not establish a universal interaction. Dose-response and time-course experiments help distinguish a transient delay from persistent suppression of the maximum response.

An informative four-condition comparison uses a mock control, SA alone, JA alone and the combined treatment. Suppose a JA-associated marker has illustrative values 1, 1, 12 and 4, respectively. The combination reduces the JA-induced output relative to JA alone. The effect cannot be described as loss of all JA signaling because the marker remains above the untreated value. If the same samples contain comparable JA-Ile concentrations after JA alone and combined treatment, the results favor a change at or downstream of JA perception rather than a simple decrease in ligand abundance. That inference still requires suitable measurements at the relevant sites and times. A reporter could be affected indirectly by tissue damage or general transcriptional inhibition, which controls must address.

Ethylene influences defense through pathways whose receptor system and signal-transduction logic differ from both SA and JA. JA and ethylene can cooperate in the activation of certain defense genes, including the classical PDF1.2-associated branch. JA signaling also has other outputs associated with MYC transcription factors; it is not a uniform response in all contexts. Thus a study measuring only PDF1.2 cannot necessarily describe the behavior of all JA-regulated genes. NPR-centered regulation, TGA-associated factors and additional transcriptional interactions can change the behavior of selected defense branches without directly preventing formation of JA-Ile.

Abscisic acid is central to water conservation and seed dormancy, as discussed earlier in this subunit. Under conditions of water shortage ABA can promote stomatal closure through its receptor, phosphatase and kinase network. Stomatal closure may also restrict entry of certain foliar pathogens, depending on their infection route. At another stage of infection ABA can attenuate particular SA-dependent defenses in some systems. These observations are not contradictory: they describe different sites of action and different measured outcomes. A change beneficial for restricting entry need not improve every post-entry immune response. Ethylene may alter ABA sensitivity in growth or germination contexts, while ABA and ethylene responses can interact differently in flooded or dehydrating tissues. A fixed reciprocal rule does not capture this biological diversity.

A plant simultaneously regulates growth through auxin, cytokinins, gibberellins and brassinosteroids. Growth requires carbon skeletons, energy, mineral nutrients and cell expansion. Defense can redirect these resources and can also alter signaling pathways that normally promote growth. The trade-off is therefore not purely a shortage of material, nor purely a competition between transcription factors. Both physiological allocation and signaling interactions contribute. In some cases a constitutively high-SA plant grows poorly, but small size alone cannot establish whether the cause is direct SA-dependent signaling, a metabolic perturbation or persistent disease. Comparing growth in a defense-signaling mutant with growth in a biosynthetic mutant may help separate these possibilities.

A practical interpretation rule follows from these examples: specify the two pathways, tissue, developmental state, timing, doses, measured outcome and relevant genotype before assigning the words antagonism or synergy. The same pair of hormones can cooperate for one response and oppose each other for another. The significance of cross-talk is that a plant can coordinate responses under changing demands, not that each hormone has a predetermined positive or negative relationship with every other one.

Scientific evidence: SA–JA timing experiments.

13. Integrating hormonal decisions during competing demands

The interaction between SA and JA is one example of the more general problem of coordinating simultaneous physiological requirements. Consider a plant in which a new leaf is expanding while neighboring tissue is infected. The growing leaf needs cell wall synthesis, water uptake and cell expansion, whereas the infected tissue needs a defense response. It would be disadvantageous if every response were applied with equal strength to every cell. Spatial variation in hormone production and perception provides one way of directing different outputs to different locations. Temporal regulation provides another: investment in growth can slow during acute challenge and resume as the challenge subsides.

The auxin pathway is especially useful for illustrating this integration. Auxin promotes specific growth and developmental responses through regulated gene expression and, in appropriate tissues, rapid changes associated with cell expansion. Defense-associated SA signaling can influence auxin-related processes in certain plant-pathogen systems. If SA reduces an auxin-associated transcriptional response, this does not prove that all auxin has been removed from the plant. There may be a change in sensitivity, biosynthetic regulation, transport or a downstream target. A useful experimental comparison measures endogenous auxin, a selected auxin-responsive reporter and actual growth. If the concentration is maintained but reporter activity declines, the observations favor a change in responsiveness or downstream regulation over simple loss of ligand.

The same principle applies when a pathogen perturbs a plant's hormone network. A pathogen may produce a compound that resembles a plant signal or may change host gene expression. One well-established example is coronatine produced by certain strains of Pseudomonas syringae, which mimics aspects of JA-Ile signaling and can promote susceptibility in particular host contexts by influencing defense and stomatal regulation. The presence of this molecule does not imply that JA is intrinsically harmful to plants. JA has important physiological roles in many protective responses. A pathogen can exploit an existing regulatory interaction to its advantage in a specific infection. This distinction is important: the normal function of a pathway and the consequences of its manipulation by another organism are different questions.

Cytokinins provide another example of a link between defense and development. They regulate meristem activity, nutrient-dependent growth and leaf senescence. In some contexts cytokinin signaling can contribute to SA-associated defense responses, while pathogen-induced changes in cytokinin metabolism can alter nutrient distribution and tissue behavior. The overall effect depends on which species and tissues are studied. A question that describes cytokinin-induced elevation of a defense marker should be interpreted as evidence of a particular interaction rather than as a replacement of the primary SA perception mechanism. Cytokinin receptors remain part of a distinct phosphorelay system, even when their downstream outputs converge on defense-associated genes.

Gibberellin and brassinosteroid pathways both demonstrate that growth-promoting signals are regulated by protein activity rather than simply by the quantity of growth substrate available. GA promotes removal of DELLA growth repressors in many contexts. BR signaling changes kinase-dependent regulation of transcriptional factors such as BZR1 and BES1. Defense and growth networks can intersect with these regulatory systems. A change in the state of a shared downstream regulator can alter growth in a manner that is not predicted solely by total GA or BR concentration. Thus measuring a hormone concentration without examining its response machinery may not identify the cause of a growth phenotype.

Strigolactones offer a different perspective because their physiological roles include changes in branching in response to nutrient availability. Nutrient deficiency may alter the distribution of growth among shoots and roots, and that altered architecture can affect how resources and signals are distributed. The relationship between branching and immune defense is not captured by describing SL as either a universal promoter or inhibitor of SA signaling. In an integrated question, distinguish the directly specified SL-mediated change in bud outgrowth from secondary consequences of the altered plant architecture. Such a distinction prevents the chapter from attributing every correlated physiological change to direct molecular cross-talk.

ABA and ethylene provide particularly clear examples of context-dependent interactions. During water limitation, ABA can increase the capacity of a plant to conserve water through guard-cell regulation. A reduction in stomatal aperture may change both gas exchange and the opportunity for some microorganisms to enter through natural openings. Ethylene can influence guard-cell behavior in a context-dependent manner, and ABA and ethylene can affect each other's synthesis or signaling. A mutation with altered aperture and altered disease susceptibility must be analyzed at both the entry and post-entry stages. If a treatment improves resistance after surface exposure but not after infiltration that bypasses the entry barrier, the data support a contribution from entry-related physiology rather than necessarily an enhanced intracellular defense response.

The distinction between response and consequence extends to water use. Closing stomata can conserve water, but it can also reduce carbon dioxide availability for photosynthesis. Reduced carbon fixation could influence growth or metabolic investment over time. A short-term experiment measuring aperture does not directly establish the long-term cost in biomass, because compensatory changes in other leaves, environmental variation and changes in duration can alter the outcome. An integrated physiological analysis distinguishes immediate guard-cell signaling from longer-term carbon allocation. SA, ABA and ethylene can all influence some of these processes, but the chapter should retain the specific mechanism supplied by the data instead of assigning a blanket response to one hormone.

Consider a hypothetical two-stress experiment in which plants experience either drought, foliar infection or both. Four groups include the unstressed control. Free ABA, free SA, stomatal aperture, PR1 transcript abundance and pathogen burden are measured at defined times. If drought alone raises ABA and closes stomata, that supports the expected water-conservation response. If infection alone raises SA and PR1, that supports an immune-associated response. If combined treatment raises both hormones but lowers PR1 relative to infection alone, the observations suggest a cross-talk effect on the output or changes in the infection itself. They do not show that ABA prevented SA synthesis, because measured SA is elevated. Additional controls for pathogen entry and tissue damage are needed to establish a mechanism.

Growth-defense trade-offs can also be interpreted through comparisons among genotypes. Suppose one mutant constitutively accumulates high SA and is small, while a double mutant that additionally disrupts an important SA response regulator retains high SA but grows closer to normal. This pattern supports a contribution of signaling to the growth defect. If a different double mutant reduces SA synthesis and also restores growth, it supports a requirement for the elevated hormone pool. The degree of rescue matters: partial restoration implies that other mechanisms may contribute. In a real plant the mutations may have independent developmental effects, so comparisons with each single mutant and the wild type are essential.

In agriculture, these relationships explain why constitutive activation of one defense pathway is not automatically the best route to a healthy, productive plant. A treatment that improves resistance against one disease can be neutral or detrimental under a different threat, and continuously activated responses may carry growth costs. Inducible regulation and appropriately timed responses can be valuable because they balance readiness against ongoing physiological demands. This is a conceptual application of plant hormone biology, not a recommendation to apply a particular chemical or dose. Actual crop outcomes depend on the species, environment, developmental stage and pathogen. The general lesson for examination reasoning is that a hormone's physiological value cannot be ranked independently of the problem the plant faces.

The antagonism of ethylene and ABA during seed germination provides a concrete example of interactions at the level of response sensitivity. In Arabidopsis, genetic studies involving ETR1, CTR1 and EIN2 linked ethylene response capacity to the inhibition of germination by ABA. When ABA maintains dormancy, a functioning ethylene pathway can reduce the extent of inhibition in appropriate conditions. The inference is not that ethylene always destroys ABA. A seed can retain ABA yet become less sensitive to it. One experimentally supported mechanism involves increased expression or accumulation of ABI1 and ABI2, phosphatases that oppose ABA-dependent SnRK2 activity. An ethylene-related change in these components can reduce the output of ABA signaling without requiring that the hormone disappear. This example also clarifies why the sign of a mutation must be interpreted carefully: a loss of a positive ethylene-response component may enhance ABA sensitivity in a particular germination assay, whereas loss of a negative regulator in another branch may produce a different phenotype.

Ethylene can also influence the availability of SA itself. Arabidopsis studies have shown that EIN3 and EIL1 can repress SID2 expression, linking an ethylene-responsive transcriptional module to a major SA biosynthetic enzyme. Compare this with an experiment in which SA changes a JA-responsive marker while JA-Ile concentration remains unchanged. In the former example, the interaction can alter production of the second hormone; in the latter, the measurements suggest regulation at or downstream of availability. These examples show that cross-talk is not restricted to proteins binding each other's receptors. It can involve regulation of an enzyme's gene, modification of a phosphatase or a change in the activity of a transcriptional complex. They also explain why a particular inhibitory interaction should not be generalized to every ethylene- or SA-dependent response.

Evidence for these interactions: classical ABA–ethylene genetics, ABI1/ABI2 regulation and ethylene-associated repression of SID2.

14. Evidence, controls and examination reasoning

The classical experiments on plant SA signaling provide a framework for reasoning from evidence. In studies of tobacco and other plants, accumulation of SA was observed in association with disease resistance and induction of pathogenesis-related proteins. Observation establishes correlation. To establish a requirement for SA, investigators used methods such as expression of bacterial salicylate hydroxylase (NahG), which depletes SA by converting it to catechol. Plants with reduced SA showed defects in some defense responses. An important limitation is that the introduced enzyme changes metabolism beyond simply subtracting the hormone. Catechol or other secondary consequences can affect phenotypes. Independent mutations in endogenous plant biosynthetic genes provide a valuable complementary test.

A mutant deficient in ICS1 has a mechanistic interpretation that differs from a transgenic plant expressing a degrading enzyme. The former reduces one route of synthesis, while the latter increases removal. Both may show reduced free SA after infection. Measurement of intermediates, enzyme expression and hormone rescue can help distinguish the two mechanisms. The most persuasive argument for a biosynthetic route combines genetic disruption with biochemical identification of the enzyme product and restoration by a functional gene. Rescue with an externally supplied hormone shows that a downstream response can still function, but does not alone identify the precise reaction normally catalyzed by the mutated protein.

An experiment with npr1 adds the receptor and response distinction. Imagine wild type, sid2 and npr1 plants treated with a solvent control or SA at a suitable concentration, and sampled after the same interval. Measure free hormone, PR1 transcript and growth of a defined test pathogen. If sid2 has reduced infection-induced SA but recovers PR1 expression with treatment, the evidence supports a lesion upstream of SA-dependent gene activation. If npr1 accumulates SA but fails to express PR1 adequately, the bottleneck is downstream of hormone availability for this output. The pathogen assay tests whether recovery of the marker corresponds to recovery of resistance, a separate conclusion that should be drawn only when data support it.

To interpret relative transcript abundance, a student may encounter quantitative PCR data. For a method using the delta-delta Ct approximation, target cycle thresholds are normalized to a stable reference gene, and fold change can be estimated as 2 to the negative delta-delta Ct power if amplification efficiencies are sufficiently similar and near ideal. Suppose untreated tissue has target Ct 29 and reference Ct 21, giving delta Ct 8. Induced tissue has target Ct 25 and reference Ct 21, giving delta Ct 4. The delta-delta Ct is negative 4, corresponding to an estimated sixteenfold increase. The calculation concerns relative transcript abundance, not the absolute number of PR1 protein molecules or the amount of SA. If reference expression changes with stress, normalization may be misleading. The quality of the biological experiment is not established by correct arithmetic alone.

A second illustrative dataset measures bacteria in distant leaves after challenge. The mock-pretreated wild type contains one million colony-forming units per gram of tissue, and the locally induced wild type contains ten thousand. The difference is a hundredfold, or two orders of magnitude. If an induced sid2 line contains eight hundred thousand, the same local challenge did not generate comparable protection in that genotype. One must still compare with the mock-pretreated sid2 baseline before attributing the difference specifically to lost induction. A mutant may differ in basal susceptibility. In logarithmic plots a unit difference represents a multiplicative rather than additive change, so equal visual distances correspond to equal ratios.

Experimental reasoning must distinguish necessity from sufficiency. When a loss-of-function mutation eliminates an outcome, the gene may be necessary under the tested conditions. When application of a chemical induces a response without the original stimulus, the chemical may be sufficient under the tested conditions. Neither claim automatically establishes that the gene is the sole mediator or that the applied dose reproduces physiological concentrations. Complementation, dose controls, independent alleles and direct biochemical assays make the argument stronger. A scientific conclusion states what has been established within the scope of the experiment and leaves other possibilities open.

15. Measuring hormone pools, flux and receptor-dependent outputs

The choice of measurement determines what an experiment can demonstrate. A concentration measurement provides a snapshot of a chemical pool at a particular sampling time. A transcript assay measures the abundance of a selected RNA or a related signal. A protein assay may report abundance or modification state. A pathogen growth assay measures a physiological outcome. All are valuable, but their units and meanings differ. An experiment can show a strong molecular response without revealing how much protection the plant gained, while a resistance phenotype alone may not identify which molecular mechanism produced it.

Chromatographic methods separate molecules in a mixture according to their interactions with stationary and mobile phases. When coupled to mass spectrometry, identification and quantification can be strengthened using characteristic molecular ions, fragmentation patterns and standards. For SA metabolism, the distinction between free and conjugated pools requires the extraction and analysis to preserve or deliberately alter the relevant chemical forms. If the sample is hydrolyzed before analysis, a measured increase may represent free SA plus salicylate released from conjugates. If the untreated sample is measured separately, the difference between the two estimates may provide information about a hydrolyzable pool. The difference is meaningful only when hydrolysis and recovery are appropriately controlled.

Stable isotope standards are useful in analytical measurements because a labeled molecule can behave similarly to the unlabeled analyte during extraction while being distinguishable in a mass spectrometer. Adding a known amount of such a standard can help account for losses during sample preparation and variation in instrument response. This is conceptually different from feeding a labeled precursor to a living plant and tracing its incorporation into products. In the latter experiment the label is used to investigate metabolic fate. Confusing the two roles can lead to interpreting a technical normalization standard as though it were evidence of natural biosynthetic flux.

Imagine an illustrative pulse-labeling experiment in which a plant is supplied with a labeled precursor. The proportion of labeled SA increases over time. This supports transfer of label into SA, but the degree of enrichment depends on the fraction of the precursor pool that is labeled, alternative unlabeled sources and the time allowed for metabolism. A low percentage of labeled SA does not necessarily mean the pathway contributes little to overall production if the relevant precursor pool is poorly labeled. A high percentage does not establish a large absolute synthesis rate if the total amount of product is small. Comparing isotopic enrichment and total pool size together provides a more informative picture than either alone.

Consider a simplified comparison of two leaves. Leaf A contains 20 nmol of SA per gram of tissue and 50 percent of the measured SA is labeled; leaf B contains 100 nmol per gram and only 20 percent is labeled. Leaf A has the greater fractional enrichment, but it contains 10 nmol of labeled SA per gram, whereas leaf B contains 20. This example demonstrates why a percentage and an absolute amount can rank samples differently. Neither number alone reveals the actual enzymatic synthesis rate without knowing the labeling history, precursor enrichment and removal of product. For Part C reasoning, the correct response may be to identify a measurement that remains necessary rather than to choose a larger value as automatic evidence of greater pathway activity.

Time courses are essential for understanding regulatory feedback. If a free-SA measurement peaks at six hours and returns toward baseline by twenty-four hours, the result may reflect reduced synthesis, accelerated removal, export, conversion to a conjugate or a combination of these processes. Measuring the corresponding SAG pool can test whether reversible conjugation contributes to the decrease. Measuring hydroxylated metabolites can reveal another route. An increase in a product does not establish that a single enzyme generated all of it, since distinct enzymes may have overlapping activities. Comparisons of mutants and complemented lines are therefore particularly informative.

One can also use reporters to investigate the spatial pattern of hormone-associated transcription. A reporter driven by a selected promoter produces a measurable output wherever that promoter is active under the conditions of the experiment. A PR1 promoter reporter reveals the activity of a selected defense-associated regulatory region, not the exact location of all free-SA molecules. A signal from the reporter can persist after the inducing signal has declined if the reporter protein is stable. Conversely, a short-lived reporter may more closely follow recent promoter activity. A student interpreting fluorescence must distinguish promoter activity, reporter maturation and degradation, and hormone concentration.

A resistance assay introduces its own measurement considerations. Equal pathogen concentration in the inoculation suspension does not guarantee equal initial deposition or entry into two different plant genotypes. A genotype with smaller stomatal openings may allow fewer bacteria to enter through natural openings, while a direct internal inoculation may bypass that difference. Measuring pathogen burden at an early time point helps separate differences in initial establishment from differences in later multiplication. The experimental question determines which inoculation route is appropriate. If the goal is to investigate post-entry SA-dependent defense, controlling for entry is important; if the goal is to investigate the total physiological protection of an intact leaf, entry itself may be a relevant outcome.

Finally, biological replication differs from technical replication. Repeatedly measuring one leaf extract can estimate instrumental or assay variation but does not reveal the full variation among individual plants. Measuring different plants grown and treated independently provides a basis for biological comparison. Environmental factors such as humidity, leaf age and light can affect plant immunity and hormone responses. A convincing study controls or records these factors and uses adequate replication. An examination dataset may omit a particular control intentionally; recognizing that missing control is a substantive scientific answer, not an evasion of the question.

16. A comparative framework for the nine hormone classes

The study of SA completes the major small-molecule hormone sequence in this subunit. The most transferable principle is that hormone action involves a chain of causal events: metabolic production, homeostatic control, spatial distribution, perception, intracellular signal processing and physiological output. Not every class implements these steps in the same manner. Ethylene is a gas, so reversible storage and movement of its precursor ACC differ from the storage and transport of a glucose-conjugated phenolic acid. Brassinosteroids frequently act close to their synthesis site rather than following a universal long-distance hormone transport pattern. Auxin distribution depends strongly on polarized transport in many developmental settings. These differences reflect chemistry and biological organization.

Some hormone pathways use regulated removal of a repressor. Auxin perception promotes destruction of Aux/IAA proteins, freeing ARF-associated transcriptional responses from repression. Bioactive gibberellins promote GID1-dependent recognition and turnover of DELLA repressors, allowing growth. Jasmonoyl-isoleucine promotes association of COI1 with JAZ proteins, leading to repressor degradation. Strigolactone perception through D14 and MAX2 targets SMXL-family proteins in relevant pathways. Despite a common design principle involving ubiquitin-dependent turnover, the ligand, perception partner, regulatory target and physiological output differ. A mutation stabilizing one repressor cannot be expected to affect all four hormone pathways in the same way.

Cytokinins use a phosphorelay involving histidine kinase receptors, phosphotransfer proteins and response regulators. ABA frequently uses a double-negative signaling arrangement: hormone-bound PYR/PYL proteins inhibit PP2C phosphatases that normally inhibit SnRK2 kinases. Ethylene receptors generally maintain suppression of ethylene responses in the absence of ligand, with CTR1 and EIN2 contributing to downstream regulation. Brassinosteroids signal through a membrane receptor kinase system involving BRI1 and a coreceptor, ultimately affecting phosphorylation of transcriptional regulators. SA provides an example of NPR-family-centered regulation of defense transcription. Learning the direction of regulation is more valuable than memorizing the pathway as an unexamined string of protein names.

When comparing hormone mutants, identify whether the affected component is involved in synthesis, activation, removal, movement, perception or downstream response. A biosynthetic lesion can often be bypassed with the corresponding active hormone, whereas a lesion in an essential perception component typically cannot. A mutant with a constitutively active downstream component can produce a response in low ligand conditions. A mutant lacking a positive regulator may have the opposite behavior. These are reasoning patterns rather than universal phenotype rules. For instance, removing a negative regulator can increase rather than decrease the output. A pathway diagram that specifies activating and inhibiting relationships allows a student to predict the direction of effects without relying on isolated facts.

Finally, all hormone signaling occurs within the constraints of plant physiology. Photosynthetic capacity, respiratory energy production, nitrogen assimilation, water relations and development shape the responses available to a tissue. A leaf that activates defense still needs resources for maintenance. A seed responding to ABA and GA cannot ignore the availability of stored reserves. A root exposed to nutrient deficiency may adjust architecture through multiple signaling classes. The nine-hormone framework is therefore not a taxonomy of isolated biological jobs. It is a collection of regulatory mechanisms that coordinate the behavior of a living organism across times, locations and environmental conditions.

17. Solving multi-pathway hormone experiments

The final stage of hormone study is to recognize the causal structure of a problem without being told in advance which named pathway is relevant. Many examination questions present a mutant phenotype, a treatment response and several molecular measurements. Start by separating these observations into groups: ligand production, availability, receptor function, downstream signaling, physiological response and environmental context. A mutation affecting an enzyme upstream of the ligand may reduce several outputs that depend on that ligand. A mutation affecting a response component may leave ligand levels normal or elevated. In the latter case feedback could increase synthesis, so high hormone concentration is not necessarily evidence of enhanced response.

Consider a hypothetical Arabidopsis line that displays reduced PR1 expression after infection, and whose free-SA pool rises normally. In a second experiment externally supplied SA does not restore PR1 expression. The most parsimonious explanation is a defect affecting the response to SA rather than a simple shortage of SA. This could involve NPR1, a relevant transcription factor, a promoter-specific mutation or another component required for the measured output. The observations do not uniquely identify the gene. If the question supplies a choice between an ICS1 loss, an essential NPR1 loss and an enzyme that reduces SA catabolism, the response lesion is the best of those choices. If no choices are provided, the appropriate conclusion is broader: a functional response defect for the selected marker.

Now consider a line that shows low free SA after infection but has a normal amount of SAG when compared with the wild type. Several explanations remain possible. A lesion reducing biosynthesis might lower both pools, but a preexisting SAG reservoir could obscure the change in the sampled interval. Increased glycosylation could rapidly reduce free SA without immediately reducing total salicylate. Reduced hydrolysis of SAG could also limit replenishment of free SA. Increased oxidative breakdown might lower free SA while other pools persist. Measuring one time point and two metabolites is insufficient to distinguish these alternatives. A time course including synthesis-associated intermediates, hydroxylated products and a direct perturbation of candidate enzymes would make the conclusion stronger.

The same data can be expressed using a simple balance. Suppose a control leaf contains 30 nmol/g free SA and 70 nmol/g of a single SA-equivalent conjugated pool. A treated leaf contains 10 nmol/g free SA and 90 nmol/g of the same conjugate. Total SA equivalents in these two pools remain at 100 nmol/g. The difference in free SA is negative 20 nmol/g, balanced by a positive 20 nmol/g difference in the conjugated pool. This is consistent with redistribution toward conjugate, provided normalization and recovery are comparable. However, total equality alone does not prove that the same individual molecules were transferred directly; simultaneous changes in synthesis and breakdown can also produce the same final balance. An isotope experiment could address transfer more directly.

Cross-talk questions often require an appropriate reference condition. Suppose JA alone increases expression of a marker from 1 to 9 relative units, while combined SA and JA increase it from 1 to 5. If SA alone yields 1 unit, then the increase associated with JA in the absence of SA is 8, and the increase associated with JA in the presence of SA is 4. This represents a reduction of one-half in the induced increment under this illustrative linear-scale comparison. Calling the response a 44 percent reduction by comparing the raw values 9 and 5 gives a different result because the baseline is included. Either calculation can be meaningful if clearly defined, but they answer different questions. A quantitative problem should state whether it concerns total measured expression or the response above baseline.

An interaction can also be detected when both individual treatments change the outcome. On an explicitly chosen additive scale, suppose mock, SA, JA and combined treatments yield 2, 6, 10 and 12 units. The separate increases above mock are 4 and 8. An additive prediction gives 2 + 4 + 8 = 14 units. The combined response is 2 units below that prediction, even though it exceeds either individual response. This demonstrates that a combined response larger than each individual response is not automatically synergistic relative to an additive model. The interpretation depends on the definition of the expected independent effect and the appropriate measurement scale. Expression fold changes and probabilities may call for different models, so this example is deliberately restricted to the stated linear-scale assumption.

Another frequent error involves reading pathogen growth on a logarithmic axis. Imagine two treatment groups that start with equal bacterial counts of 10³ colony-forming units per gram and finish at 10⁵ and 10⁶, respectively. The first group exhibits a hundredfold increase and the second a thousandfold increase. The final burden in the first group is one-tenth of the second, but pathogen multiplication still occurred substantially in both. A claim of complete pathogen elimination is therefore false. Distinguish relative suppression, absolute suppression and complete prevention of growth. The biological outcome depends on the magnitude of the reduction, tissue damage and the duration of observation.

Hormone rescue experiments can be extended by using an output that is known to be controlled by a negative regulator. For example, in GA signaling, removal of DELLA repression is central to growth promotion. If a GA biosynthetic mutant is dwarf because too little active GA is available, supplying active GA may restore elongation. If the repressor itself is altered so that it cannot be removed appropriately in response to GA, exogenous hormone may fail to restore growth. Compare this with SA: an ICS1 defect can reduce hormone availability, while a major NPR1-dependent response defect cannot generally be bypassed by simple ligand addition. The same upstream-versus-downstream reasoning applies even though the molecular signs differ and SA signaling is not identical to DELLA regulation.

Finally, infer physiological outcomes from the relevant biological objective rather than memorizing slogans about beneficial or harmful hormones. A plant with high JA-responsive marker expression may be better equipped to respond to a particular chewing herbivore, yet that does not establish increased protection against every microbial pathogen. A plant with strong SA signaling may restrict a biotrophic infection, yet continuous defense activation can impede growth. A plant with high ABA may conserve water through stomatal closure, yet reduced gas exchange changes carbon assimilation. In each case the biological process being measured determines whether a given response is advantageous. This is the level of integration expected when a question combines metabolism, hormone signaling, transport and defense without explicitly naming which chapter provides the solution.

18. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Total SA equals free SA

Correction: Total can include conjugated pools. Why confused: Hydrolysis-based measurements may combine them. Exam trap: Equal totals with different free pools. Reasoning: Use form-specific measurements.

2. ICS1 directly produces SA

Correction: ICS1 produces isochorismate. Why confused: Pathway shorthand hides later steps. Exam trap: Normal ICS1 but defective PBS3. Reasoning: Track intermediate and compartment.

3. EDS5 is a synthesis enzyme

Correction: EDS5 transports the intermediate. Why confused: Transport loss also lowers product. Exam trap: Plastid retention of isochorismate. Reasoning: Separate movement and catalysis.

4. PBS3 creates inactive SAG

Correction: PBS3 conjugates isochorismate to glutamate. Why confused: Conjugation occurs at multiple steps. Exam trap: Choose PBS3 substrate. Reasoning: Identify whether the reaction precedes free SA.

5. PAL is the sole route in all plants

Correction: ICS predominates in induced Arabidopsis SA; species differ. Why confused: Earlier studies emphasized phenylalanine. Exam trap: A crop tracer experiment. Reasoning: Respect the studied species.

6. MeSA and SAG are identical

Correction: One is methyl ester, the other glucose conjugate. Why confused: Both modify SA. Exam trap: Choose esterase versus glucosidase. Reasoning: Identify the linkage.

7. DMR6 adds glucose

Correction: DMR6 performs SA 5-hydroxylation. Why confused: Removal routes are conflated. Exam trap: Predict 2,5-DHBA. Reasoning: Track the chemical transformation.

8. NPR1 necessarily binds promoter DNA itself

Correction: NPR1 co-regulates; TGA factors bind DNA. Why confused: Both occupy regulatory complexes. Exam trap: A mutated TGA site. Reasoning: Separate ligand recognition and DNA binding.

9. NPR3/4 always behave like NPR1

Correction: Their regulatory contributions differ. Why confused: Homologous names suggest identical functions. Exam trap: Loss of a repressor raises basal output. Reasoning: Determine the regulatory sign.

10. SA always rescues npr1

Correction: Ligand cannot generally replace an essential response component. Why confused: Synthesis-mutant rescue is overgeneralized. Exam trap: Compare sid2 and npr1. Reasoning: Locate the lesion relative to ligand.

11. PR1 induction proves resistance

Correction: PR1 is a marker, not pathogen burden. Why confused: Markers serve as convenient proxies. Exam trap: High PR1 but unchanged CFU. Reasoning: Measure each endpoint separately.

12. All distal SA physically traveled intact

Correction: Distal de novo synthesis can contribute. Why confused: Detection in two sites suggests movement. Exam trap: Mostly unlabeled distal SA. Reasoning: Distinguish movement and local synthesis.

13. MeSA is the only SAR signal

Correction: NHP and additional signals also contribute. Why confused: A landmark result is generalized. Exam trap: Residual SAR in another species. Reasoning: Limit conclusions to the tested context.

14. Priming means continuous maximal defense

Correction: Priming enhances a later response. Why confused: Post-challenge measurements obscure baseline. Exam trap: Four prior-treatment/challenge groups. Reasoning: Compare challenge-dependent increments.

15. SA and JA cannot coexist

Correction: Their interaction depends on context and output. Why confused: Antagonism is oversimplified. Exam trap: Both ligands high, one marker low. Reasoning: Separate concentration from response.

16. ABA always has one effect on disease

Correction: Entry and post-entry outcomes can differ. Why confused: Stomatal defense is confused with transcriptional defense. Exam trap: Surface versus internal inoculation. Reasoning: Identify stage and measured outcome.

17. Lower pathogen count equals eradication

Correction: Relative suppression is not zero organisms. Why confused: Log plots obscure absolute counts. Exam trap: 10⁵ versus 10⁷ CFU/g. Reasoning: Calculate ratio and retain actual count.

Part B — fundamentals and mechanisms

1. Which is the chemical identity of SA?

  1. 3-hydroxybenzoic acid
  2. Adenine derivative
  3. Diterpenoid
  4. 2-hydroxybenzoic acid
Answer and explanation

D. 2-hydroxybenzoic acid

Its hydroxyl group is adjacent to the ring carbon carrying COOH; changing its position changes the isomer. Adenine derivatives and diterpenoids characterize other hormone classes.

2. Which enzyme forms isochorismate in the major induced Arabidopsis route?

  1. ICS1
  2. PBS3
  3. DMR6
  4. FMO1
Answer and explanation

A. ICS1

ICS1 converts chorismate into isochorismate; PBS3 acts downstream, DMR6 hydroxylates SA, and FMO1 participates in NHP biosynthesis.

3. Which function is associated with EDS5?

  1. MeSA hydrolysis
  2. Plastid export of isochorismate
  3. Glycosylation of SA
  4. JA-Ile perception
Answer and explanation

B. Plastid export of isochorismate

EDS5 provides access of a plastid intermediate to downstream cytosolic reactions, not covalent modification of SA.

4. Which set describes PBS3 substrates?

  1. Methionine, ACC and oxygen
  2. Lysine and pipecolate
  3. Isochorismate, glutamate and ATP
  4. SA, glucose and GTP
Answer and explanation

C. Isochorismate, glutamate and ATP

PBS3 forms the isochorismate-glutamate intermediate using ATP; this differs from conjugating mature SA for storage.

5. What is a role of EPS1?

  1. Plastid export of isochorismate
  2. SA 5-hydroxylation
  3. Ethylene perception
  4. Facilitating SA formation from the PBS3 product
Answer and explanation

D. Facilitating SA formation from the PBS3 product

EPS1 accelerates conversion of the intermediate. EDS5 mediates export and DMR6 participates in SA hydroxylation.

6. What does phenylalanine ammonia-lyase produce?

  1. Cinnamate and ammonia
  2. Isochorismate and glutamate
  3. MeSA and glucose
  4. JA-Ile and CO2
Answer and explanation

A. Cinnamate and ammonia

PAL deaminates phenylalanine. Its phenylpropanoid roles extend beyond SA synthesis, so its mutant phenotype is not uniquely salicylate-specific.

7. Which SA derivative is a glycoside?

  1. JA-Ile
  2. SAG
  3. MeSA
  4. ACC
Answer and explanation

B. SAG

SAG is salicylic acid glucoside. MeSA is an ester, ACC an ethylene precursor and JA-Ile a jasmonate conjugate.

8. Which activity directly releases SA from MeSA?

  1. SA methyltransferase
  2. ACC oxidase
  3. Methyl salicylate esterase
  4. ICS1
Answer and explanation

C. Methyl salicylate esterase

Hydrolysis of the methyl ester restores the carboxylic acid. Methyltransferase performs the opposing formation reaction.

9. Which protein is associated with SA 5-hydroxylation?

  1. NPR1
  2. EDS5
  3. PIN1
  4. DMR6
Answer and explanation

D. DMR6

DMR6 forms 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid from SA. NPR1 regulates signaling, EDS5 transports an intermediate and PIN1 is associated with auxin transport.

10. Which description fits NPR1?

  1. Major SA-responsive transcriptional co-regulator
  2. Isochorismate synthase
  3. Universal MeSA transporter
  4. Ethylene receptor
Answer and explanation

A. Major SA-responsive transcriptional co-regulator

NPR1 mediates important SA-dependent transcription through regulatory interactions. It is not a biosynthetic or transport enzyme.

11. Which factors provide sequence-specific promoter recognition in key NPR pathways?

  1. GA20 oxidases
  2. TGA transcription factors
  3. Rubisco
  4. PIN carriers
Answer and explanation

B. TGA transcription factors

TGA proteins are DNA-binding transcription factors; NPR1 contributes to transcriptional regulation through such partners.

12. What does PR1 transcript measurement directly report?

  1. Number of viable bacteria
  2. NPR1 binding affinity
  3. Expression of a defense-associated gene
  4. Free SA concentration
Answer and explanation

C. Expression of a defense-associated gene

Transcript abundance is a marker of a selected output; hormone abundance, infection and binding require separate measurements.

13. What term describes enhanced distal protection after local immune induction?

  1. Phototropism
  2. Polar auxin transport
  3. Photophosphorylation
  4. Systemic acquired resistance
Answer and explanation

D. Systemic acquired resistance

SAR involves a change in protection at a distant site following a local challenge. The alternatives concern light responses, transport or energy conversion.

14. What is the immediate precursor of NHP?

  1. Pipecolic acid
  2. Cinnamate
  3. ACC
  4. Campesterol
Answer and explanation

A. Pipecolic acid

FMO1-dependent hydroxylation of pipecolate generates NHP. The alternatives belong to phenylpropanoid, ethylene and brassinosteroid metabolism.

15. Which statement about MeSA is accurate?

  1. It is chemically identical to free SA
  2. It contributes to systemic signaling in some systems
  3. It is the sole universal SAR signal
  4. It is a glucose conjugate
Answer and explanation

B. It contributes to systemic signaling in some systems

MeSA can move and be hydrolyzed in particular systems, but NHP and other signals also contribute. Esterification changes chemical identity.

16. SA-dependent defense is especially associated with which pathogen lifestyle?

  1. Photosynthesis
  2. Nitrogen fixation
  3. Biotrophy
  4. All necrotrophy without exception
Answer and explanation

C. Biotrophy

Many pathogens that feed on living tissues activate SA-associated defenses. This is a tendency, not an exclusive rule.

17. Which module perceives JA-Ile?

  1. NPR1–TGA
  2. PYR/PYL–PP2C
  3. GID1–DELLA
  4. COI1–JAZ
Answer and explanation

D. COI1–JAZ

COI1 and JAZ participate in jasmonate-dependent recognition. The other pairs are associated with SA, ABA and GA.

18. What does hormonal cross-talk mean?

  1. One pathway influences production, signaling or output of another
  2. Every hormone shares a single receptor
  3. The hormones cannot coexist
  4. All interactions are antagonistic
Answer and explanation

A. One pathway influences production, signaling or output of another

Cross-talk can occur at several regulatory levels and can increase or decrease particular outputs.

19. Which pathway involves PYR/PYL and SnRK2?

  1. Cytokinin
  2. ABA
  3. SA
  4. Ethylene
Answer and explanation

B. ABA

ABA receptor complexes inhibit PP2C, permitting kinase signaling. The other pathways have distinct perception modules.

20. Which statement correctly distinguishes ethylene?

  1. SA is made by ACC oxidase
  2. Both use NPR proteins as the primary receptor system
  3. Ethylene is a gas; ACC can move as a precursor
  4. Ethylene is stored as SAG
Answer and explanation

C. Ethylene is a gas; ACC can move as a precursor

Ethylene gas diffuses, while ACC is a precursor with its own transport and conjugation. SAG and NPR regulation concern SA.

Part C — analytical and experimental reasoning

21. sid2 has low induced SA and SA application restores PR1; npr1 has high SA but PR1 remains low after treatment. What fits?

  1. Both lack SA synthesis
  2. Both affect only transport
  3. Both affect pathogen entry
  4. Synthesis lesion in sid2, response lesion in npr1
Answer and explanation

D. Synthesis lesion in sid2, response lesion in npr1

Ligand bypasses a synthesis defect but cannot generally replace a necessary downstream response component. No entry measurement is supplied.

22. Plastid isochorismate forms at 15 nmol/h and is exported at 9 nmol/h; other sinks are negligible initially. Net change?

  1. +6 nmol/h
  2. −6 nmol/h
  3. +24 nmol/h
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

A. +6 nmol/h

Input minus output = 15 − 9 = 6 nmol/h. Feedback may subsequently change the rates.

23. X and Y have 100 nmol/g SA equivalents; free SA is 60 in X and 15 in Y. Which is justified?

  1. Y cannot synthesize SA
  2. Y has a smaller free pool
  3. Y necessarily has greater signaling
  4. X has a smaller total
Answer and explanation

B. Y has a smaller free pool

The total hides chemical partitioning; free pool sizes differ. Neither signaling output nor synthesis rate is directly measured.

24. In a MeSA-dependent system, the source lacks its methyltransferase but the receiver has normal esterase and signaling. Which bypasses the source lesion?

  1. Further reducing source SA
  2. Removing receiver NPR1
  3. MeSA application to receiver
  4. Blocking receiver esterase
Answer and explanation

C. MeSA application to receiver

Recipient MeSA delivery bypasses the failed production step and can provide SA after hydrolysis.

25. The receiving tissue lacks the relevant MeSA esterase but retains SA perception. Which treatment bypasses hydrolysis in the specified model?

  1. MeSA alone
  2. More source methyltransferase
  3. Blocking TGA
  4. Direct SA supply
Answer and explanation

D. Direct SA supply

Supplying the acid avoids the required ester cleavage; additional MeSA does not directly solve the receiver defect.

26. Locally supplied isotope-labeled SA is followed by abundant mostly unlabeled distal SA. What is the best inference?

  1. Distal synthesis is plausible; mobile signal identity remains unresolved
  2. All distal SA traveled intact
  3. NHP cannot participate
  4. The distal tissue cannot perceive SA
Answer and explanation

A. Distal synthesis is plausible; mobile signal identity remains unresolved

Unlabeled accumulation can reflect local synthesis. The experiment does not uniquely determine the signaling molecule or transport route.

27. Mock, JA, SA, SA+JA marker values are 1,11,1,6. Reduction of the JA-induced increment above baseline?

  1. 0%
  2. 50%
  3. 45.5%
  4. 90%
Answer and explanation

B. 50%

Increments are 10 and 5. Reduction is (10−5)/10 = 50%; raw-value reduction is a different measure.

28. SA+JA reduces PDF1.2, while JA-Ile remains unchanged compared with JA alone. What follows?

  1. All JA outputs disappeared
  2. The hormones cannot coexist
  3. Altered downstream regulation or responsiveness is plausible
  4. JA-Ile synthesis certainly stopped
Answer and explanation

C. Altered downstream regulation or responsiveness is plausible

The marker and measured ligand differ in behavior. This is compatible with regulation downstream of availability, not proof of one unique target.

29. Prior treatment gives marker 1 before challenge and 24 after; control gives 1 and 6. Which interpretation fits?

  1. Persistent maximal basal activation
  2. No challenge response
  3. Complete pathogen elimination
  4. Priming of the challenge-dependent marker
Answer and explanation

D. Priming of the challenge-dependent marker

Equal basal values with a stronger subsequent response support priming. Pathogen protection needs an independent assay.

  1. 100-fold lower final burden
  2. Twofold lower
  3. Zero viable pathogen
  4. 100-fold higher
Answer and explanation

A. 100-fold lower final burden

10⁷/10⁵ = 100. Both groups still contain viable organisms.

  2. 16-fold increase
  3. 4-fold decrease
  4. 2-fold increase

B. 16-fold increase

ΔCt values 8 and 4 give ΔΔCt −4 and 2^4 = 16, assuming appropriate normalization and efficiency.

32. A mutant retains normal total NPR1 but has impaired PR1 induction. Which test directly addresses localization?

  2. Measure bacterial growth in broth only
  3. Compare nuclear and extranuclear NPR1
  4. Measure water content only
Answer and explanation

C. Compare nuclear and extranuclear NPR1

Subcellular measurements test whether adequate protein reaches the nucleus. Total abundance alone cannot resolve this.

33. A separate transcriptional repressor is lost while the NPR1 branch remains functional. Which is possible?

  1. All SA responses must disappear
  2. SA synthesis must cease
  3. NPR1 becomes a biosynthetic enzyme
  4. Higher basal expression with further SA induction
Answer and explanation

D. Higher basal expression with further SA induction

Removing repression can raise baseline without destroying the positive signaling branch; exact output depends on the promoter.

34. A dmr6 mutant has intact biosynthesis. Direct expected defect?

  2. Loss of ICS1 activity by definition
  3. Loss of every glycosylation reaction
  4. Loss of COI1
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced SA 5-hydroxylation capacity

The lesion concerns the hydroxylation branch; feedback can affect other pathways but does not redefine the primary defect.

C. Entry-related barrier such as stomatal aperture

Bypassing entry removes the benefit, supporting an entry-related component. The exact barrier still requires testing.

  3. The response regulator necessarily synthesizes SA
  4. A signaling-dependent contribution to growth inhibition

D. A signaling-dependent contribution to growth inhibition

20. Revision, recall and mastery

Chapter summary

Salicylic acid is a phenolic defense regulator. Arabidopsis uses an ICS1-dependent pathway involving plastid export through EDS5, cytosolic PBS3 and EPS1-assisted conversion. Phenylalanine-derived routes vary among species. Free SA is regulated by glycosylation, methylation and hydroxylation. NPR-family proteins and TGA factors control selected defense transcription. MeSA and NHP can contribute to systemic immunity in appropriate contexts. SA–JA and ABA–ethylene interactions depend on tissue, timing, pathogen and response, while growth and defense must be coordinated.

Equations and quantitative recall

Free-pool change = input − output; pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]); labeled amount = total × fraction; ideal qPCR fold change = 2−ΔΔCt. State assumptions and units.

Active recall

1. Trace ICS1 to SA across compartments.

ICS1 → isochorismate → EDS5 export → PBS3 conjugate → spontaneous conversion/EPS1.

2. Distinguish SAG, MeSA and 2,5-DHBA.

Glucoside, methyl ester and hydroxylated catabolite.

3. Why is free SA not identical to neutral SA?

Free SA is unconjugated; neutral SA is a protonation state.

4. What reaction is associated with DMR6?

5-hydroxylation of SA.

5. Compare NPR1 and TGA roles.

Co-regulator and DNA-binding transcription factor, respectively.

6. Predict sid2 versus npr1 rescue.

SA may bypass sid2, but not a necessary NPR1-dependent response step.

7. Define SAR experimentally.

Prior local induction reduces pathogen burden after a separate distal challenge.

8. Why does MeSA arrival not guarantee a response?

Hydrolysis and downstream perception must be functional in relevant systems.

9. Connect NHP and SA.

NHP can activate distal SA biosynthesis and NPR-dependent priming.

10. Design the SA–JA factorial comparison.

Mock, SA, JA, both; measure ligand and specified output.

11. Distinguish priming and persistent induction.

Compare basal and challenge-dependent responses.

12. Contrast ABA and SA signaling.

PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2 versus NPR-family regulation.

13. Explain growth-defense tradeoff.

Consider resource allocation, regulatory interactions, time, tissue and stress.

Mastery checklist

  • Reconstruct SA production, compartmentation and homeostasis.
  • Compare synthesis and response mutants and explain rescue.
  • Distinguish PR1 markers, pathogen burden and systemic priming.
  • Explain context-dependent hormonal interactions.
  • Compare origins, receptors and responses of all nine hormone classes.
