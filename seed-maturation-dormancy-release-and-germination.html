1. From a developing embryo to an independent plant

A bean seed that remains dry in a cupboard for months and then produces a root within a few days of receiving water demonstrates a remarkable developmental transition. The young plant inside the seed has not been assembled from scratch after watering. Its principal embryonic axis and cotyledons were already organized before the seed separated from its parent. Yet that embryo did not continuously increase in size while the seed remained dry. It survived a period during which chemical reactions, transport and tissue growth were strongly restricted. The explanation requires us to distinguish building an embryo from preparing that embryo to survive outside the maternal plant. These are related but different developmental tasks. An embryo that has formed recognizable organs may still lack sufficient reserves or the capacity to tolerate water loss. Conversely, a mature seed can contain a structurally complete embryo that is deliberately prevented from completing germination until its surroundings are appropriate.

The biological challenge is not merely survival of an isolated cell. The future plant must preserve a coordinated multicellular organization, including a living root meristem and a shoot meristem, while it experiences a large alteration in its physical state. During development on the maternal plant, water and assimilates arrive from surrounding maternal tissues. After separation, this continuous supply is interrupted. At first, the embryo cannot obtain sufficient carbon from photosynthesis and cannot obtain minerals from a well-developed root system. A successful seed therefore has to accomplish three complementary objectives: establish a functional embryo, provision that embryo for the interval before independent nutrition, and regulate when growth is permitted to resume. The last objective explains why the first rainfall after seed dispersal is not invariably followed by seedling emergence. In an environment where a brief shower is followed by drought, delaying germination can protect the embryo from committing to growth during a false start.

Three kinds of tissue are particularly important in understanding this process. The embryo is the young sporophyte produced from the fertilized egg. In an ordinary diploid angiosperm with a Polygonum-type embryo sac, it is diploid and carries contributions from both parents. The endosperm, formed through fertilization of the central cell, is commonly triploid in that model and initially participates in supporting the developing embryo. The seed coat originates from the maternal integuments and has the maternal sporophyte genotype. Its physical and chemical properties can affect the conditions experienced by the tissues inside. These origins matter when interpreting a cross between two plant lines: a maternal influence on dormancy could arise through the seed coat, maternal environment or maternally contributed genes, and does not automatically demonstrate genomic imprinting. They also matter for experiments that remove a tissue, because removing the surrounding tissue alters several possible influences at once.

The developmental endpoint used in this chapter is visible protrusion of the embryonic radicle through the tissues that surround it. This is a convenient and biologically meaningful criterion for completed germination in many seeds. The radicle is the embryonic root, not a root produced de novo from an undifferentiated mass after imbibition. Its elongation often precedes extensive emergence of the shoot. Subsequent seedling establishment requires continued axis development, reserve consumption, acquisition of water and minerals, and eventually an effective photosynthetic apparatus. Agricultural tests sometimes adopt a later criterion, such as production of a normal seedling. Thus a laboratory report that describes eighty percent germination and a field observation that records sixty percent establishment need not contradict one another. The endpoints differ, and some seeds may complete germination but fail during the interval that follows. When examining an unfamiliar study, identify what event was scored before interpreting any percentage or graph.

2. Late embryogenesis, seed filling and storage tissues

During early embryogenesis, cell divisions and differentiation construct the pattern of the embryo. Late development shifts the relative emphasis toward growth, reserve storage and preparation for dispersal. The distinction is a change in developmental emphasis, not an instantaneous switch that all seeds undergo at precisely the same morphological stage. In a developing grain, the embryo and endosperm can be at different stages of differentiation while the maternal plant continues supplying organic and inorganic resources. Water enters developing tissues, cells enlarge, and the seed increases in dry mass as it converts imported nutrients into retained compounds. In an orthodox seed, dry-mass accumulation is generally followed by a marked decline in water content. Measuring fresh mass alone cannot identify how much material the seed has accumulated because fresh mass includes both water and dry matter.

Consider the conversion of imported assimilates into a storage reserve. Carbon fixed in maternal leaves reaches developing reproductive structures largely in transportable forms such as sucrose. Sugars delivered to the seed must cross boundaries between tissues, enter appropriate cells and be metabolized. In a starch-rich grain, sugar metabolism supplies substrates that are assembled into starch granules within plastids of storage cells. Packaging many glucose units into a polymer creates a compact carbon store and avoids the osmotic consequences of holding the equivalent amount of carbon as freely dissolved individual sugars. The storage process nevertheless requires enzymes, regulated metabolism and an adequate supply of precursors. If a mutation compromises transport into the seed, the developing storage tissue may remain poorly filled even when the maternal leaves photosynthesize normally. If the defect instead blocks conversion inside the storage tissue, imported carbon may accumulate in other forms. Both cases can lead to lighter seeds, but the causal defect is different.

Oil-rich seeds illustrate another solution to provisioning. Fatty acids are assembled into triacylglycerols, which accumulate in lipid droplets bounded by a phospholipid monolayer associated with proteins such as oleosins. Neutral lipids are hydrophobic and can store a large amount of chemical energy in a relatively compact package. Later, lipases release fatty acids from stored triacylglycerols; fatty acid oxidation provides acetyl-CoA, and in many oil-rich seedlings the glyoxylate cycle and gluconeogenesis contribute to conversion of stored carbon into transportable carbohydrate. These transformations allow tissues that cannot yet photosynthesize sufficiently to receive usable carbon. The detailed reaction sequence belongs to metabolic physiology; the developmental principle is that storage and mobilization are distinct programs separated in time. An embryo that consumes the oil while the seed is still filling would compromise its own ability to survive the later heterotrophic period.

Proteins stored in seeds provide nitrogen as well as carbon. Storage proteins are synthesized and accumulated in specialized compartments, including protein storage vacuoles and, depending on the species, other protein bodies. During early seedling growth, proteases and related processes make amino acids available for the synthesis of enzymes, membrane proteins and structural cellular components. A reserve is therefore not simply material that can be burned for ATP. It is also a source of atoms for new tissue. Phosphorus is commonly stored in phytate, often associated with mineral ions, and hydrolysis of this reserve during germination can make phosphate available for metabolic and biosynthetic use. The details of mineral nutrition vary among species, but the common developmental problem is to provide a growing embryo with accessible resources before uptake from the soil meets its needs.

Location of the reserve must be distinguished from its chemical identity. Wheat and maize retain a substantial endosperm at maturity. A pea or bean seed instead stores much of its reserve in enlarged embryonic cotyledons; the endosperm was present earlier in development but is largely consumed before maturity. Consequently, the presence of two large cotyledons in a bean is not evidence that endosperm never developed. Likewise, the thin persistent endosperm surrounding the Arabidopsis embryo is an important living compartment despite its small mass. Its functional contribution cannot be inferred from reserve mass alone. A tissue may regulate growth through a mechanical constraint or a signaling activity while contributing little stored carbon. A comparison of a cereal grain, a bean seed and an Arabidopsis seed therefore illustrates three different combinations of storage, protection and developmental control.

Seed filling is also sensitive to the timing and conditions of the maternal environment. A reduction in assimilate supply during the filling period can reduce final seed dry mass; the magnitude of the effect depends on whether the developing seed has alternative sources and whether the limitation occurs before or after most reserve deposition. A seed harvested prematurely can be large and watery but contain relatively little stored dry matter. This is why comparisons between seed batches must use comparable developmental stages. One way to separate filling from dehydration is to sample seeds successively during maturation, record fresh mass, and determine the dry mass of matched samples. A period of increasing dry mass indicates net accumulation of dry material, whereas a subsequent fall in fresh mass without an equivalent fall in dry mass indicates predominant water loss. These measurements do not identify particular biochemical reserves unless supplemented by compositional evidence, but they establish the chronological relationship between filling and drying.

3. Maturation dehydration and the preservation of a living embryo

Scientific reading: Arabidopsis maturation regulators · Seed desiccation tolerance and longevity

Removing water from a living tissue ordinarily threatens membrane organization, protein conformation and the reactions that maintain cellular homeostasis. Water interacts with polar groups in membranes and macromolecules, contributes to the three-dimensional environment in which proteins function, and provides a medium for solute movement. As water is removed, ions and metabolites become concentrated. Membrane lipids can change their packing, proteins can aggregate, and reactive chemical species can damage cellular structures. A seed that simply lost water without preparation would therefore be at substantial risk. The desiccation tolerance acquired during late development of an orthodox seed is an active property of its developmental history, not a universal consequence of having a seed coat.

In many orthodox seeds, abscisic acid (ABA) participates in establishing a maturation program in which growth is restricted, protective molecules accumulate and the seed becomes prepared for dehydration. Late embryogenesis abundant proteins, usually abbreviated LEA proteins, are examples of molecules associated with this protection. Many LEA proteins are highly hydrophilic, and their properties permit them to participate in stabilization of cellular structures under low-water conditions. Their functions are diverse and they should not be imagined as a single impermeable coating around every organelle. A second contribution comes from non-reducing sugars, including sucrose and, in many seeds, raffinose-family oligosaccharides. Interactions between sugars, membranes and proteins help maintain structural integrity; highly concentrated cellular constituents can also enter a glassy physical state with restricted molecular mobility. This does not mean that a seed contains ordinary window glass. Rather, the cytoplasm behaves as a highly viscous amorphous matrix in which large-scale molecular rearrangements become slow.

Restricting mobility lowers the frequency of some damaging reactions but does not eliminate all chemical change. Oxidation, spontaneous molecular lesions and gradual deterioration can still occur during dry storage. Protective systems and repair during subsequent hydration contribute to persistence. A dry seed is thus not biologically equivalent to an indestructible object. Its longevity depends on the degree of maturation, genetic properties, storage conditions and the damage that it can withstand or repair. A seed that tolerates drying when freshly mature may lose viability over time if the environment accelerates deterioration. Desiccation tolerance concerns the ability to survive water removal; longevity concerns how long viability can be maintained under specified conditions. A seed may possess both characteristics, but they are not interchangeable measurements.

The seed coat changes during maturation as well. Its component cell layers differentiate, and in many species outer tissues develop thickened walls, pigments or water-resistant structures. These changes can protect the embryo from mechanical damage and modify access of water, gases or light to inner tissues. A mature testa is often made partly of dead maternal cells, but the embryo must remain living if the seed is viable. Protective function does not require the coat itself to maintain high metabolic activity. The physical design of the coat differs between species and can influence the dispersal strategy. A hard, water-impermeable coat can impose a distinct block to germination, whereas a coat that allows water entry may still provide mechanical resistance or chemical influence. A thick coat is not necessarily impermeable; its permeability depends on its structure and composition rather than thickness alone.

A useful distinction in seed biology is between orthodox and recalcitrant storage behavior. Many familiar crop seeds, including pea and wheat, tolerate substantial drying at maturity and are classed as orthodox. Some seeds, such as those of cacao, remain sensitive to substantial dehydration and are classed as recalcitrant. They cannot be assumed to survive conventional drying and low-temperature storage procedures designed for orthodox seeds. This is a biological exception to the familiar dry-seed model rather than a defect in seed formation. Intermediate storage behavior also exists, so the two categories should not be treated as an exhaustive binary classification of every species. The important examination inference is conditional: a treatment that is useful for one storage behavior can be damaging for another. Viability measurements before and after dehydration, rather than the appearance of the dry seed alone, establish the extent of tolerance.

Desiccation tolerance also changes with developmental state. An orthodox seed that survives considerable drying in its mature condition may become progressively less tolerant as it proceeds through germination and begins sustained seedling growth. The emergence of a radicle is therefore not simply a reversible change in size produced by adding water. It marks a transition in the developmental state of the organism. A newly emerged root exposed to severe drying may die even though the same seed, prior to hydration, survived months of dry storage. The relationship explains why dormancy and environmental sensing can be advantageous. Once the embryo commits to growth, conditions that would have been survivable in the mature dry seed may become lethal. The timing mechanism reduces the chance of crossing that developmental boundary in a habitat that cannot sustain the seedling.

The coordination of maturation can also be studied genetically. In Arabidopsis, the transcriptional regulators LEC1, LEC2, FUS3 and ABI3 participate in overlapping aspects of the transition from a growing embryo to a mature seed. Their importance becomes apparent when a mutant embryo develops recognizable structures but fails to acquire the usual suite of maturation properties. A mutant can have reduced reserves, altered cotyledon characteristics, abnormal dormancy or reduced tolerance of dehydration. These outcomes indicate that the program controlling late development is not merely the passive consequence of an embryo becoming larger. Regulatory changes can alter several properties at once because the same regulatory component influences multiple genes and developmental processes.

ABI3 is particularly instructive because its association with ABA responses links signaling to maturation-specific gene regulation. If a severe abi3 mutant does not survive drying as efficiently as a corresponding wild type, it is not sufficient to conclude that the mutant has an abnormally impermeable coat. An informative comparison would examine the mutant and wild type before and after dehydration, alongside independent measurements of their maturation-associated reserves and protective properties. If the mutant remains viable before drying but loses viability disproportionately afterward, the observation directly identifies a deficit in surviving the water loss. Altered reserve accumulation could contribute to poor later growth but is a distinct observation from immediate loss of viability during dehydration.

The effects of these regulators may overlap without being identical. When several mutants share reduced accumulation of storage proteins, the simplest conclusion is that each affected gene contributes, directly or indirectly, to normal maturation. It does not follow that all four proteins bind precisely the same DNA sequences or act in a simple linear order. A rescue experiment that restores one maturation feature but not another is evidence that the developmental outputs can be separated. For example, renewed reserve deposition would not by itself establish that the seed has regained its capacity to tolerate drying. These are different outputs of a coordinated network. The regulatory evidence connects this chapter with the earlier study of developmental mutants, where multiple consequences of a single genetic change are described as pleiotropy.

A further distinction is between inhibiting germination and preserving the seed's developmental competence. An intervention that keeps every seed from protruding a radicle may appear to increase dormancy. If the same intervention damages the cells irreversibly, however, the failure to germinate is not dormancy. A regulatory program that enables dry storage must preserve the ability to resume growth when conditions become appropriate. This distinction explains why viability after release of a growth block is such an important measurement. Dormancy-related genes can be understood as components of a reversible developmental restraint, whereas genes involved in protection or repair can influence whether the embryo survives long enough for release to matter. They may interact, but the effects must be distinguished experimentally. The maturation program is successful not because growth stops indefinitely, but because it stops while the embryo remains capable of later growth.

4. Quiescence, dormancy and the definition of a living seed

Scientific reading: Dormancy classification and germination control · Tetrazolium viability assessment

A seed that has not germinated provides an observation, not a diagnosis. At least three explanations need to be considered. The embryo might be dead; it might be alive and capable of germinating once an external requirement is supplied; or it might be alive but prevented from completing germination by an internal developmental constraint. The second situation is often described as quiescence when an otherwise nondormant seed lacks suitable conditions, such as water. Dormancy refers to a block in a viable seed that prevents completed germination under conditions that would otherwise be favorable for that seed. The definition is operational because favorable conditions depend on species, seed history and the particular environment. Failure to germinate in a dark dish at a single temperature cannot by itself establish either death or dormancy.

The distinction is especially clear when seeds are hydrated. A nondormant dry seed and a physiologically dormant dry seed both appear inactive. After receiving water, the nondormant seed may complete germination. The dormant seed can also take up water and resume a range of metabolic activities without protruding its radicle. It is the failure to complete the developmental transition despite appropriate conditions, not an absence of all metabolic activity, that identifies the dormancy problem. A physically dormant seed has a different immediate limitation: its water-impermeable seed or fruit covering prevents sufficient hydration. Both situations delay germination, but they make different experimental predictions. Scratching an impermeable coat may permit water uptake and subsequent germination; the same treatment does not necessarily remove physiological inhibition in an embryo that was already hydrated.

Primary dormancy develops during seed formation and is present at dispersal or maturity. Secondary dormancy can arise after dispersal in a previously nondormant or dormancy-released viable seed exposed to conditions that promote its induction. A dry seed stored without water is not automatically in secondary dormancy. Its failure to germinate may merely reflect absence of water. To establish secondary dormancy, the investigator must show that the seed has acquired a reduced ability to germinate when returned to conditions that previously supported germination. This requirement makes the history of the seed essential. Germination behavior reflects not only the current environment but also the environmental signals received during development, dispersal and the period preceding the test.

The classical categories of dormancy describe several forms of constraint. Physiological dormancy involves regulatory restrictions on embryo growth and/or surrounding tissues; hormone status and sensitivity often contribute. Physical dormancy results from water-impermeable coverings. Morphological dormancy is associated with an underdeveloped embryo that must complete further development before germination. Some seeds combine morphological and physiological components, and others combine physical and physiological dormancy. Mechanical resistance of a covering can influence germination without meeting the specific definition of physical dormancy, because the covering may already allow water entry. These categories are useful for reasoning about treatments, but they should not be assigned merely because a seed looks small, hard or slow to germinate.

Viability, germination capacity and vigor answer different questions. Viability concerns whether the seed retains the potential to produce a living plant under appropriate conditions, including any required dormancy release. A germination percentage records the fraction meeting a defined criterion in a specified test. Vigor describes aspects of performance such as rapid, uniform germination and the ability to establish a seedling under less favorable conditions. A viable dormant seed may contribute to a low germination percentage in an unsuitable test. An aged nondormant seed may eventually produce a radicle but do so slowly and subsequently form a weak seedling. A batch with a high final germination percentage in favorable conditions can therefore have poorer vigor than another batch with the same final percentage.

The distinction can be explored using a tetrazolium viability assessment. Living tissues capable of reducing tetrazolium salts can produce a colored formazan product. The pattern of staining in critical embryonic regions provides evidence about living tissue. An embryo with a damaged root or shoot meristem may have substantial stained tissue and nevertheless lack the capacity to develop normally. A viability test must therefore be interpreted with attention to the essential tissues and the limitations of the assay rather than counted as a simple whole-seed color reaction. Comparing viability evidence, germination under several conditions and the outcome of a dormancy-releasing treatment helps discriminate among dead seeds, viable dormant seeds and nondormant seeds that were environmentally limited.

5. Environmental permission: after-ripening, chilling, light and temperature

Scientific reading: Borthwick et al., 1952

The removal of dormancy does not always coincide with the onset of germination. After-ripening is a change in germination behavior occurring during dry storage in certain physiologically dormant seeds. Such a seed may germinate more readily when finally hydrated, even though it did not grow during storage. This illustrates how the ability to respond to water can change while the seed remains dry. The rate of after-ripening depends on seed properties and storage conditions. Storage is not automatically beneficial: beyond a suitable period, damage associated with aging may reduce viability. A rise in germination percentage over the first months of storage followed by a decline over later months can consequently reflect two different processes, dormancy release followed by deterioration. Evidence of viability is needed to distinguish them.

Stratification refers to exposing hydrated seeds to a suitable temperature regime for a period that changes their dormancy status. Cold stratification is especially familiar from species in which a prolonged cool period precedes a favorable growing season. Other species require warm conditions or a sequence of temperatures. The hydration component is important because it allows physiological processes that differ from those in a very dry seed. In an experimental comparison, freshly harvested seeds can be divided into a moist-chilled group, a dry-chilled group and a moist control maintained at another suitable temperature. Afterward, all groups are tested under the same germination conditions. If moist chilling specifically improves germination, the difference provides evidence that the combination of hydration and temperature was important; it does not by itself establish which molecule changed.

Light can provide information about the position and surroundings of a seed. Small seeds with limited reserves may benefit from germinating near a soil surface, where a seedling has a better chance of reaching light before exhausting stored resources. In some light-sensitive seeds, red light promotes germination through phytochrome signaling and far-red light can reverse the effect of a preceding red pulse under suitable conditions. The classic red and far-red pulse experiments with lettuce demonstrated that the sequence of illumination mattered, supporting a reversible photoreceptor mechanism rather than a simple explanation based on energy received from light. The photoreceptor exists in interconvertible forms; red and far-red light favor different proportions of these forms. In a suitable experimental system, the last pulse can strongly affect germination outcome. Not every seed has a red-light requirement, and the response depends on the species, seed state, temperature and light regime.

Temperature has two distinguishable roles. It affects the rates of biochemical and physical processes during germination, and it can modify the developmental state that determines whether germination is permitted. A seed incubated below the temperature at which it can grow may fail to protrude a radicle simply because growth is too slow. A prolonged treatment can also alter dormancy, producing a different response when the seed is transferred to a common test temperature. To separate these effects, a student must distinguish a pretreatment condition from a condition during scoring. For example, two groups that receive different pretreatments but are then germinated at the same temperature test a persistent effect of pretreatment. Two groups germinated directly at different temperatures test the combined influence of the current temperatures on germination. An experiment that changes both factors without suitable controls cannot readily separate them.

Scarification is a treatment that alters the covering, often by producing a localized opening through which water can enter. A physically dormant seed that becomes hydrated and germinates after such a treatment gives evidence of a permeability constraint. To interpret the result, compare water uptake of untreated and treated seeds before scoring radicle emergence. If untreated seeds gain substantial water but do not germinate, whereas treated seeds gain similar water and do germinate, the benefit of scarification cannot be assigned solely to restoration of water entry. Mechanical restraint, changes in gas access or damage-related effects may have contributed. In contrast, a large difference in hydration before any growth occurs supports a role for the water barrier. This is an example of using an intermediate measurement rather than merely a final germination percentage to identify the step affected by a treatment.

6. The developmental balance between ABA and gibberellins

Scientific reading: ABA and GA signaling overview · GA response in Arabidopsis

A seed does not possess a single chemical switch that simply measures how much water is present. It integrates developmental signals with environmental information, and plant hormones are important components of that integration. Abscisic acid and gibberellins are particularly useful for understanding the transition between maturation and germination. ABA generally favors maturation-associated properties and restraint of germination, whereas biologically active gibberellins generally promote the processes that permit radicle emergence. These are tendencies within a regulated system rather than permanent labels attached to each hormone. A substance can participate in more than one developmental process, and its effect depends on the cells receiving the signal, their developmental competence and the presence of other signals.

The distinction between hormone concentration and hormone response is fundamental. A seed can have a high concentration of ABA but a weakened response if a mutation affects the machinery that perceives or transmits the signal. Another seed can have a modest ABA concentration yet be strongly inhibited because its responsive tissues are unusually sensitive. Conversely, a low concentration of active gibberellin may limit germination even when the tissues could respond normally to externally supplied GA. These alternatives explain why measurements of hormone quantity and tests of hormone responsiveness provide complementary information. An observed increase in germination after GA treatment does not, on its own, prove that the original seed was GA deficient. A sufficiently strong growth-promoting treatment may overcome other forms of restraint. To establish a specific biosynthetic defect, one needs additional evidence, such as a causal mutation, an independently measured hormone deficit and restoration of the phenotype when the active hormone is supplied.

At the level of a germinating seed, ABA can restrain growth of the embryonic axis, maintain dormancy-associated gene expression and influence the behavior of the covering tissues. The response does not require every cell to be affected in precisely the same way. Some seeds can accomplish an early event, such as rupture of the testa, while a later step, such as protrusion through the remaining endosperm, is prevented. This is important because recording only the first visible disruption of a covering can lead to an overestimate of completed germination. In Arabidopsis, for example, testa rupture and endosperm rupture can be distinguished. A seed may pass one morphological threshold without crossing the next. An experimental design that separately counts both events can reveal that a hormone treatment affects the latter step more strongly than the former.

Gibberellin contributes to germination through several related effects. It can enhance growth potential of the embryonic axis and promote the reduction of restraint imposed by tissues surrounding the radicle. In a cereal grain it also participates in the coordination of reserve mobilization. These functions solve different parts of the developmental problem. Increased growth potential enables the embryo to generate the expansion needed for protrusion. Reduced resistance of an enclosing tissue lowers the force that must be overcome. Mobilization of a reserve supplies substrates for continued metabolism and growth. These outcomes need not occur in identical order in all plants. A student should distinguish a change that is required for visible radicle emergence from a change that supports subsequent elongation of a seedling.

At an elementary molecular level, hormone signaling depends on receptors and downstream regulatory molecules. In the standard ABA signaling framework, ABA perception by PYR/PYL/RCAR-family receptors enables inhibition of certain PP2C phosphatases; this releases restraint on SnRK2 protein kinases and permits phosphorylation of downstream components. In relevant seed contexts, transcription factors and other targets influence the expression of genes that support ABA-responsive developmental states. The useful feature of this mechanism for examination reasoning is its inhibitory logic: inhibiting a negative regulator can produce an increase in a downstream response. A loss of a negative regulator is not expected to have the same effect as a loss of a positive kinase. The complete biochemical pathway, receptor diversity and physiological effects in other tissues belong to plant signaling and physiology; here the pathway explains why an altered seed response can result even when ABA abundance is unchanged.

The corresponding core GA response framework includes the GID1 receptor and DELLA proteins. In the presence of active gibberellin, association of GA with GID1 promotes recognition of DELLA growth repressors and, in the classical pathway, their removal through regulated proteolysis. When the abundance or action of DELLA repressors decreases, previously constrained growth-promoting programs can operate more effectively. Suppose a seed contains a stabilized DELLA protein that does not undergo normal GA-dependent removal. Increasing the quantity of GA might have little effect on that particular downstream block, because the defect lies in a repressor's responsiveness rather than in hormone supply. This differs from a GA biosynthetic defect with intact receptors and downstream machinery. The same general rule applies to many developmental signaling pathways: supplying a ligand is most informative when interpreted in light of the step that is defective.

The term ABA to GA balance is often used as a compact description of the regulatory state. It is helpful only if its limits are understood. A numerical ratio calculated from bulk hormone concentrations cannot capture the spatial distribution of hormones, the activities of different biologically active forms, or tissue-specific sensitivity. Identical ratios can arise from very different absolute concentrations, and a cell responds through its own signaling machinery rather than through a universal division operation. Thus a seed with an ABA concentration of two arbitrary units and a GA concentration of one arbitrary unit is not necessarily developmentally equivalent to a seed with concentrations of two hundred and one hundred. The quantities, their units, their biological activity and the response functions all matter. In conceptual questions the balance describes opposing influences; it is not a universal quantitative threshold with a fixed numerical value.

Developmental genetics provides a way to test this reasoning. Compare an ABA-deficient line, an ABA-insensitive line, a GA-deficient line and a line with severely impaired GA perception in the same suitable environment. If the first two lines both exhibit reduced restraint of germination, they may share a phenotype while differing in the cause. Supplying ABA might restore inhibition more effectively in the deficient line than in a strongly insensitive line. Similarly, adding active GA may restore germination in a GA-deficient line whose signaling machinery is functional, but not in a line with a complete loss of an essential GA response. Such predictions assume that the mutations affect the specified process and that the relevant growth step is otherwise intact. Partial mutations, redundant genes, dose-dependent responses and tissue differences can complicate real results, so complete rescue should not be asserted as a universal rule.

In Arabidopsis, DOG1, or DELAY OF GERMINATION 1, is a useful example of a dormancy-associated genetic regulator that reminds us that dormancy is not reducible to two hormones alone. Differences in genetic background and maturation environment can alter the depth of dormancy. DOG1-associated regulation and ABA-associated regulation interact in the control of whether a hydrated seed can complete germination. It would be misleading to claim that every seed's dormancy is determined by DOG1 alone or that all species use the same molecular components in identical ways. The appropriate NET-level interpretation is that dormancy is a developmental trait controlled by multiple genetic and environmental influences, which converge on the capacity for continued embryo growth. Differences between seed lots can therefore persist even when they are subsequently germinated in the same environment.

The evidence for the importance of hormone action is not limited to correlations between a high ABA measurement and low germination. Classical experiments with isolated seed tissues and subsequent studies of genetic defects make it possible to alter a candidate factor and observe the consequence. A controlled hormone treatment asks whether a specified signal is sufficient to modify the tested process under those experimental conditions. A loss-of-function mutant asks whether a component contributes to the normal process. Neither result automatically demonstrates that the component acts directly on every gene or every cell in the seed. Direct mechanistic claims require appropriate additional evidence. The conceptual framework is strengthened when independent methods converge on a common causal explanation rather than when a single correlation is repeatedly described.

7. Imbibition and the physical basis of water uptake

The first encounter between a mature dry seed and a favorable water source produces a physical change that precedes much of its renewed metabolic activity. Water interacts with hydrophilic groups in the seed's macromolecules and cell walls, and hydration can cause substantial swelling. This initial uptake is known as imbibition. It is not synonymous with active water pumping. A dry seed can develop a strong tendency to take up water because water has a lower chemical potential in the dry material than in the surrounding liquid. Passive water movement down this potential difference can occur even if the embryo is unable to perform the reactions required for growth. This is why a dead seed can become visibly swollen and why swelling alone is not a test of viability.

Water potential is a way of expressing the thermodynamic tendency of water to move. Under the usual convention, pure water at reference pressure and temperature has a water potential of zero. Contributions from solutes, pressure and interactions with matrices can change that potential. In dry seeds, interactions between water and dry matrices are particularly important. The often-used relation between solute potential and concentration is useful for dilute solutions but does not by itself describe the complex hydration of a dry seed. Once cells become substantially hydrated, osmotic differences and the hydrostatic pressure of cellular contents become more useful in understanding cell expansion. A growing plant cell requires a source of water and a wall that can yield in a controlled manner. Hydration can produce turgor, but turgor alone does not ensure irreversible extension if the wall remains rigid.

The condition of the seed covering affects the initial rate of uptake. A water-permeable covering allows water to reach internal tissues, whereas an impermeable covering can delay uptake until its properties change. Size, contact with the moist environment, temperature, internal composition and the presence of pores or specialized openings influence the kinetics. Water does not necessarily reach all tissues at the same instant. An initial gradient of hydration can exist between outer and inner regions, and tissues can swell differently because of differences in composition. A comparison of uptake rates between two species is therefore not a direct comparison of metabolic activity. The species may differ in seed size, covering permeability and the fraction of their mass made up of water-binding polymers.

A hydrated seed may initially lose soluble constituents to its surroundings. Membranes that were stabilized in a very dry state must reorganize as hydration proceeds, and temporary leakage can occur before normal barrier function is restored. Severe damage acquired during maturation or storage can increase or prolong leakage. An electrical conductivity assay of the surrounding water can therefore provide indirect information about the escape of charged solutes and, in appropriate comparisons, seed deterioration. It is not a direct measure of all possible damage. A seed with a more permeable covering may release more solute than a comparable seed with a restrictive covering, even if embryo damage is similar. Comparisons should use seeds of comparable type and size and control factors such as incubation duration and water volume. The most informative conclusion comes from combining leakage data with germination performance rather than using conductivity as an absolute definition of seed vigor.

A moist environment is not always physiologically equivalent to unlimited water availability. Dissolved salts and other solutes can reduce the water potential of the surrounding medium. If external water potential becomes sufficiently low, the seed may not hydrate to the extent required for the developmental events that lead to radicle emergence. Salts can also have ion-specific toxic effects, so a decline in germination in a saline solution cannot always be attributed to osmotic restriction alone. An investigator who wishes to distinguish these mechanisms can compare treatments with similar osmotic potentials but different chemical compositions, while recognizing that matching osmotic potential does not automatically make every other property of the solutions equivalent. This example connects seed biology with the physical chemistry of aqueous solutions without requiring a detailed course in plant stress physiology.

The three-phase model of water uptake provides a framework for organizing the sequence. Phase I is the rapid initial uptake associated with hydration of dry tissues. Phase II is an interval during which the net water content changes relatively slowly compared with the initial rise. During this interval a viable seed can reorganize cellular structures, reactivate metabolism, repair molecular damage and prepare for growth. Phase III is renewed substantial water uptake associated with extension of the embryonic axis and seedling growth after completion of germination. The phases refer to changes in water uptake rather than mutually exclusive lists of molecular events. Respiration may begin increasing during early hydration, and preparations for expansion may occur during the later part of the plateau. A dormant seed can remain in a phase-II-like state without entering the growth-associated phase under the tested conditions.

The graph should be interpreted as a schematic for individual seeds or appropriately synchronized populations. In a batch of seeds, different individuals can enter the rapid-growth phase at different times. The average water content may then exhibit a gradual increase rather than a sharp transition. If a student sees a population-level curve with a broad plateau, it is not possible to conclude that every seed remained at precisely the same hydration level for the entire period. The population average could combine individuals that had begun growth with others that had not. Recording radicle emergence separately for individual seeds gives temporal information that a pooled mass measurement cannot supply. A water uptake curve and a cumulative germination curve describe different variables and must not be treated as alternative labels for the same graph.

A practical consequence of these physical principles is that adequate moisture and adequate oxygen are both important. A seed completely submerged in stagnant water may encounter a lower supply of oxygen than a seed on a moist, aerated substrate. The extra water does not necessarily improve the capacity for ATP generation. Some species are adapted to germination or early growth under restricted oxygen, but it is incorrect to generalize such behavior to all seeds. The environmental requirements for germination are interactive rather than additive boxes on a checklist. For example, an elevated temperature can increase respiratory demand while also reducing oxygen solubility in water. The same level of water supply may therefore be compatible with growth in one temperature and aeration regime but limiting in another.

The degree of hydration also helps determine how seed priming differs from completed germination. A controlled hydration treatment can permit a seed to perform some early preparatory activities while stopping short of radicle protrusion. In a suitable orthodox seed, subsequent drying may be possible before the loss of drying tolerance associated with continued growth. The resulting seed may germinate faster on later hydration. Such a response does not mean that a seedling can always be dried back into a mature seed. The point at which drying causes irreversible damage depends on the species and developmental stage. A comparison of primed and untreated seeds should therefore record emergence timing and final viability as well as any effects on seedling establishment. This example applies the distinction between physiological preparation, completion of germination and later growth to a practical biological problem.

8. Metabolic reactivation and the phase-II interval

Scientific reading: Seed longevity and genome damage

Drying and storage do not erase the molecular organization of a mature viable seed. It retains a variety of proteins, nucleic acids, membrane components and organelles. When the water content increases, the cell must re-establish the physical conditions required for these components to operate together. Some enzymes already present in the mature seed can become active as hydration restores their appropriate environment. Other proteins have to be synthesized during germination. Some stored messenger RNAs can contribute to early translation, and new transcription also contributes to the developing response. The relative importance of these sources varies with the species, the quality of the seed and the particular phase of germination. The important distinction is between recovery of a pre-existing system and construction of new molecular components. Both processes can occur in a seed that has not yet shown visible radicle emergence.

ATP is required for a wide range of renewed cellular activities, including biosynthesis, active transport and repair. The embryo initially relies on internal reserves rather than on a fully functional photosynthetic apparatus. Soluble carbohydrates that are already available can be metabolized as respiration resumes. Mitochondrial activity and organization change during hydration, allowing increasing use of aerobic metabolism when oxygen is available. Glycolysis can provide ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation and supplies intermediates that connect to further metabolism. In aerobic respiration, mitochondrial electron transport supports a much larger ATP yield from complete oxidation of substrates than glycolysis alone. The details of ATP stoichiometry depend on the biological system and are not needed to infer why severe oxygen limitation often compromises sustained seedling growth. A seed can be wet yet fail to generate the energy required to continue development.

The seed also has to contend with damage accumulated while dry. Molecular lesions that did not prevent storage survival can become important when DNA is transcribed or replicated and when membranes must sustain ion gradients. Repair systems active during rehydration can contribute to successful germination. Aged seeds often germinate more slowly or less uniformly than fresh seeds, even when some retain viability. Delays can arise because components must be repaired or because damage has reduced metabolic efficiency. However, delayed germination is not diagnostic of a repair defect by itself; dormancy can produce a similar population-level delay. To distinguish these causes, compare fresh and aged seeds that have undergone the same dormancy-release treatment, assess viability and look for independent indicators of deterioration. This approach avoids attributing all delays to the most familiar mechanism.

Reactive oxygen species illustrate the need to distinguish amount and context in biological regulation. Molecules produced through oxygen metabolism can damage proteins, nucleic acids and membranes when they accumulate beyond the capacity of protective systems. At controlled levels, reactive species can also contribute to signaling and changes in the environment of growing cells. A simple rule that all oxidation is harmful or all oxidative activity promotes germination is therefore unsatisfactory. The location, amount, timing and balance with protective systems determine the effect. Within this chapter the relevant point is that reactivation of respiration and other metabolism creates both useful energy and potential sources of molecular damage. The successful transition requires the coordination of metabolism with maintenance of cellular integrity.

How can one establish that a particular metabolic event occurs before germination is completed? An experiment can collect samples at a succession of times after hydration, while recording the fraction of seeds with protruded radicles at each sampling time. Measurements of oxygen uptake, protein abundance or enzyme activity can then be compared with the developmental scores. If oxygen uptake increases before visible protrusion in a cohort, this establishes the chronological relation for that cohort. It does not establish that the measured increase is sufficient by itself to produce emergence. Inhibiting the process and examining whether emergence is impaired can provide additional evidence, but nonspecific toxicity or damage must be excluded before concluding that a particular pathway is required. Experiments using a targeted genetic defect and rescue can help strengthen such an inference.

The phase-II interval is particularly important for physiologically dormant seeds because metabolic reactivation and developmental permission can become separated. A viable dormant seed may hydrate and resume some respiration, transcription and protein synthesis, yet remain unable to produce the extension and tissue rupture required for emergence. It is therefore possible for two seeds to consume oxygen at a similar early rate but exhibit different final germination percentages. Conversely, a large metabolic rate measured late in an experiment may partly reflect that one group already contains growing seedlings while the other does not. Comparing groups at the same chronological time can therefore confound a treatment effect on cellular metabolism with a difference in developmental stage. A comparison at matched stages complements, but does not replace, a comparison at matched times. The appropriate choice depends on the question being asked.

The early seed is not merely an embryo operating in isolation. Its surrounding tissues may contain living cells that respond to hydration and influence nutrient supply, hormone status or mechanical resistance. For a species with a persistent living endosperm, analysis of a whole seed extract combines material from the embryo and endosperm and possibly other tissues. An increase in activity measured from the whole seed does not specify which tissue produced it. Tissue separation or appropriately localized observations are needed to assign activity more precisely. This follows directly from the genetic and developmental distinction among seed compartments established in the preceding chapter. It also illustrates why cellular location is essential when explaining how a hormone or metabolic signal affects the behavior of an intact seed.

9. Radicle protrusion: embryo growth versus covering resistance

Visible germination requires a physical as well as a biochemical event. The root end of the embryonic axis has to increase in length enough to protrude through the surrounding tissues. Before this can happen, cells of the axis must obtain water, generate or maintain turgor and undergo changes in their walls that permit extension. The root tip is initially enclosed by seed tissues whose resistance depends on their structure and developmental state. Even a living, hydrated embryo may not be able to extend through an enclosing structure if its growth potential is too low or the surrounding tissue offers too much resistance. Conversely, removal or weakening of a restraining tissue may permit an embryo that previously failed to emerge to continue growing.

This relationship can be represented qualitatively by two competing quantities: the effective force exerted by the growing embryo and the effective resistance of the tissue that must be displaced or ruptured. Under the simplified assumption that these quantities are compared at the same location and geometry, protrusion becomes possible when the former exceeds the latter. The representation is not a universal equation for predicting germination from hormone concentration. The force and resistance can vary in space and time, and a living tissue can change its mechanical properties while the embryo is expanding. Nonetheless, the comparison is useful because it distinguishes two mechanistic classes of explanation. A treatment can promote emergence by increasing embryo growth capacity, decreasing resistance or affecting both.

The covering nearest the radicle is not always simply a passive shell. In some endospermic seeds, the region of endosperm around the root end of the embryo undergoes a change in its mechanical properties during germination. Enzymes and wall-remodeling processes can contribute to changes that make protrusion possible. The contribution and identity of individual enzymes vary among species, and the detection of increased activity of an enzyme in an extract does not prove that it is the sole cause of tissue weakening. The timing and location of the activity are important. If a wall-remodeling process occurs in a tissue that does not lie in the path of the radicle, it may have a different developmental role. An experiment that tests local tissue resistance as well as biochemical activity provides stronger evidence for the proposed mechanism.

Consider an excised embryo from a dormant seed. If it elongates in conditions where the intact seed does not, the result suggests that a surrounding tissue or the interaction between that tissue and the embryo contributes to the intact seed's failure to germinate. This does not automatically demonstrate that the only problem was mechanical. Removing a tissue can alter chemical signaling, gas diffusion, nutrient availability and exposure to light as well as resistance to growth. If the isolated embryo remains unable to extend, a constraint associated with the embryo itself becomes a plausible explanation, but the excision procedure may also have damaged it. Appropriate controls include excised embryos from nondormant seeds of comparable developmental state and evidence that the isolation procedure permits normal growth under the chosen conditions. The key exam skill is to state what an experimental result supports, and to avoid overstating what it proves.

The emergence of the radicle often relies heavily on cell elongation, with cell division contributing to subsequent growth in a species- and stage-dependent fashion. It is misleading to claim that the root first appears because a completely new meristem is created by repeated divisions of unspecialized cells in the mature seed. Embryogenesis established the basic axis and meristematic regions in advance. Hydration and regulatory changes allow the existing organization to resume development. Cell division and elongation are related but distinguishable contributions to tissue growth. An inhibitor that interferes with cell division may have a different immediate effect from a treatment that prevents water uptake or wall extension. The exact phenotype depends on when the treatment is applied and on which process is required for the chosen developmental endpoint.

In a typical Arabidopsis germination sequence, swelling of the seed can be followed by rupture of the outer testa before the radicle breaks through the remaining endosperm. This model makes it possible to score two successive events rather than a single endpoint. A population of seeds can show a high frequency of testa rupture but a low frequency of endosperm rupture when the later step is inhibited. Such a pattern argues against the interpretation that all early hydration processes were completely blocked. It instead identifies a later transition at which to examine growth potential, covering resistance and regulatory status. In other species the separation of these visible events may be less obvious, and not every seed has the same surrounding tissues. The correct general concept is that the structures the radicle must traverse depend on the seed anatomy; the model-specific sequence is valuable precisely because it exposes the steps that can be measured separately.

The emerging radicle improves the young sporophyte's ability to establish contact with the surrounding substrate. As it elongates, it can anchor the plant and contribute to water and mineral acquisition. The transition to an independent plant, however, is still incomplete. The shoot has to develop sufficiently to support photosynthetic carbon gain, and stored reserves continue to contribute while the embryo makes that transition. Completion of germination should therefore be regarded as a milestone within development rather than the conclusion of all seed-related biological activity. A seed can meet the radicle criterion yet fail to produce a normal shoot because a later developmental process is impaired. This distinction allows examination questions to separate factors controlling germination from factors controlling seedling survival.

10. How a germinating cereal grain mobilizes its food supply

Scientific reading: Chrispeels and Varner, 1967, GA response · GA and ABA effects, 1967

A cereal grain provides a particularly clear example of communication between different seed compartments. A large portion of a mature barley grain consists of starchy endosperm. Much of the starchy endosperm is no longer living at maturity, yet its reserve can support the living embryo. The grain also contains a peripheral living aleurone layer derived from endosperm and a living embryo whose single cotyledon is specialized as the scutellum. The scutellum lies against the storage tissue and participates in nutrient transfer. Outside these compartments lie protective tissues, including the fused fruit wall and seed coat in the caryopsis. This combination of living and nonliving structures is not paradoxical. Stored starch can be retained in a nonliving tissue, while living tissues synthesize and secrete enzymes that release accessible substrates from it. The seed thus uses an extracellular or intertissue digestion strategy to make a reserve available to the growing axis.

In the classical account of cereal germination, the hydrated embryo contributes gibberellin signals that influence aleurone cells. Gibberellin stimulates the expression and production of hydrolytic enzymes in responsive tissues. These enzymes include alpha-amylase, which acts on starch. The enzyme is delivered to the region containing stored starch, where hydrolysis creates smaller carbohydrates that can be further metabolized. The products of reserve breakdown become available to the embryo, including through the absorptive activities of the scutellum. The important causal sequence is signal production, reception by a responsive living tissue, enzyme synthesis or activation, reserve hydrolysis, nutrient transfer and utilization by growing cells. If the aleurone cells cannot respond to GA, the presence of a large starch reserve does not by itself assure efficient mobilization.

The mechanism is more informative when the biochemical roles are distinguished. Alpha-amylase attacks internal alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds in starch chains, creating smaller molecules and additional chain ends. Other enzymes contribute to further breakdown, including reactions that act at chain ends or address branches. Consequently, alpha-amylase activity is a useful indicator of one component of mobilization but is not a complete measurement of all sugar production. An experiment in which amylase activity is high but sugar transfer to the embryo is defective might show accumulation of carbohydrate outside the growing tissue. Another experiment in which transport is intact but enzyme production is reduced may show retained starch. The two phenotypes are distinguishable by measuring the reserve remaining, the products formed and the carbon that reaches the embryo, rather than by scoring seedling length alone.

The elegant feature of classical cereal experiments was the ability to separate hormone production from hormone response. In 1967, Chrispeels and Varner reported studies of GA-dependent hydrolytic enzyme production using isolated barley aleurone layers. Removing the embryo from the system enabled the experimenter to supply a hormone under controlled conditions and ask whether the isolated responsive tissue could alter its enzyme production. The approach did not require the complete grain to germinate. It therefore separated an effect on the enzyme-producing tissue from the other processes that occur simultaneously in an intact germinating grain. Their findings supported a causal action of GA on enzyme synthesis and secretion, and related experiments showed that abscisin, now called abscisic acid, opposed the response. The experimental logic remains important even when the contemporary molecular signaling details are understood more fully.

To reconstruct the reasoning, imagine comparing isolated viable aleurone layers maintained under otherwise identical conditions. One group receives no added GA and the other receives active GA. Enzyme activity can be assessed in the surrounding medium as well as in the tissue itself. If the treated layers produce more secreted enzyme than the untreated layers, the difference supports the sufficiency of GA to promote the tested response under those conditions. If an inhibitor of protein synthesis prevents the increased activity, this adds evidence that protein synthesis contributes to the response. But an inhibitor that kills the cells would also prevent enzyme production, so the control for tissue viability is crucial. Comparison of internal activity and external activity is also valuable: a treatment can inhibit secretion without completely preventing enzyme synthesis. These distinct readouts allow the investigator to locate the affected step with greater precision.

ABA can oppose the GA-induced response in this system, but the observation should not be transformed into a rule that ABA directly destroys alpha-amylase molecules. A change in the amount of enzyme produced, a change in its secretion and an alteration of its catalytic activity are different possible mechanisms. If ABA is added to isolated aleurone tissue and the amount of enzyme subsequently secreted falls, the result is not equivalent to adding ABA to a preparation of purified enzyme and measuring its catalytic rate. The former experiment tests a cellular regulatory response; the latter addresses direct biochemical effects on the enzyme under the specified assay conditions. Keeping these experiments distinct prevents confusion between hormone signaling and enzyme inhibition, topics that are both common in examination questions but belong to different levels of explanation.

Although the textbook cereal model is valuable, it is not a universal anatomical description of seed germination. The embryo and scutellum may contribute to hydrolytic processes, and the relative contributions of different tissues can vary with species and developmental time. In pea, for instance, reserves are concentrated in living embryonic cotyledons rather than in a massive persistent starchy endosperm surrounded by a cereal-type aleurone layer. A pea seedling can therefore mobilize its reserves through a different spatial arrangement. Moreover, major reserve mobilization often continues after the radicle has protruded. A mutation that impairs starch breakdown may have a stronger effect on continued seedling growth than on the first visible event of germination, especially if adequate soluble substrates were already available. An experimentally observed effect on seedling size must not automatically be described as a failure of germination unless the radicle endpoint was actually measured.

The general lesson is that seed reserves are valuable only if their constituents become accessible at appropriate times. A storage polymer is not an immediately transportable food source, and an enzyme secreted into a storage compartment does not itself deliver ATP to the embryo. Hydrolysis makes smaller molecules accessible; transport delivers substrates; metabolism within living cells converts those substrates into usable energy and materials. Different tissues cooperate across these steps. When a question identifies a block in one step, the student should predict the consequences both upstream and downstream. A block in hydrolysis can cause retention of a storage polymer and reduced availability of smaller products. A block in transfer can permit formation of products but prevent effective delivery. A block in respiration can leave available carbohydrate insufficiently utilized. This causal separation is more reliable than memorizing a single diagram of an arrow from starch to seedling.

11. Dicot and monocot seedlings: different uses of the embryonic body plan

Scientific reading: Seedling establishment and reserve use

Once a radicle has emerged, growth of the embryo continues through the tissues already patterned during embryogenesis. In a typical eudicot embryo, two cotyledons are attached to the embryonic axis. The region between the root and cotyledonary node is called the hypocotyl, and the portion above the cotyledonary node is the epicotyl. The plumule includes the embryonic shoot region. During seedling emergence, differential growth of these regions helps determine the position of cotyledons relative to the soil. In common bean, a familiar example of epigeal emergence, hypocotyl elongation lifts the cotyledons above the soil. In pea, a familiar example of hypogeal emergence, the cotyledons remain below the ground while the shoot emerges through growth of the region above them. Both plants have two cotyledons. The difference concerns growth geometry and developmental deployment rather than the number of cotyledons.

The terminology epigeal and hypogeal describes the position of the cotyledons during emergence. It does not directly classify the seed according to the presence of a persistent endosperm, its dormancy mechanism or the chemistry of its reserves. A cotyledon that emerges above the soil can in some species become photosynthetic, contributing carbon during establishment. Cotyledons that remain underground can continue supplying reserves without serving as exposed photosynthetic organs. Neither arrangement implies that the mature seed lacked a root or shoot meristem. The underlying embryonic axis was present in both cases, and differential growth changes the position of its components relative to the substrate. A student interpreting a diagram should identify the cotyledonary node and trace which axis region elongated rather than relying only on whether a green structure is visible above the soil.

Grasses present a distinct arrangement. In maize, the scutellum is the specialized cotyledon associated with the storage tissue. The embryonic shoot is protected by a coleoptile, and the embryonic root region is associated with a coleorhiza. As germination proceeds, the root emerges through the protective region, and the shoot grows upward within its protective sheath. The coleoptile subsequently permits emergence of the developing foliage. These structures have specific names and roles and should not be confused with the organs they protect. The coleoptile is not the primary root, and the coleorhiza is not a photosynthetic cotyledon. The scutellum is not the entire endosperm. The anatomical distinction provides a useful way of connecting the number and identity of embryonic organs with the particular challenges of seedling emergence in a grass.

As the shoot reaches a favorable light environment, the young plant begins to depend increasingly on photosynthetic carbon assimilation. The transition is not necessarily completed as soon as green color appears. Initially, the shoot may still receive substantial resources from seed reserves while its leaves expand and its photosynthetic apparatus develops. Under darkness, many seedlings elongate using stored resources, but their capacity to persist is finite if they cannot establish a sufficient source of assimilated carbon. The developmental endpoint for establishment is therefore more demanding than radicle protrusion. A treatment that restores visible germination but leaves a seedling dependent on a depleted reserve does not necessarily restore successful establishment. This distinction is particularly important in genetic experiments where one phenotype is rescued at an early stage but another becomes apparent later.

Water and mineral acquisition also change during establishment. A newly protruded radicle can begin to explore the substrate; continued root growth increases the opportunity for uptake. The root system and shoot system then develop in coordination. If the shoot rapidly consumes water without a sufficient capacity for root uptake, the seedling may experience stress even though the seed initially germinated under adequate conditions. The seed-to-seedling transition therefore involves changing relationships between internal provision and external resources. The purpose of stored nutrients is not to replace those external resources permanently, but to support the developmental interval before the young plant can obtain them effectively. Detailed meristem organization, branching and organogenesis are taken up in Subunit 5.D, while the physiology of photosynthesis, mineral acquisition and translocation belongs to Unit 6.

The difference between germination and establishment can be examined with a simple hypothetical genetic example. Imagine a viable oil-rich seed whose embryo can protrude a radicle under favorable conditions but whose capacity to convert stored lipid carbon into transportable carbohydrate is substantially reduced. The first cells may have enough available soluble substrates to complete germination. However, continued growth in darkness can become restricted when those substrates are depleted. If supplying an accessible carbon source improves early growth, the observation supports the idea that carbon provision was limiting, although other effects of the added substrate must be considered. It does not show that photosynthesis is required for the initial germination event. The experiment separates a defect in reserve utilization during establishment from the regulatory or mechanical processes controlling completion of germination.

Seeds also differ in the size of their reserves and in the distance that a seedling must traverse before reaching a favorable light environment. A larger reserve can sometimes support a longer period of heterotrophic growth, but size alone does not determine survival. A seed may contain considerable reserves that are poorly mobilized, or its seedling may grow in a direction or manner that makes those reserves ineffective. A smaller seed that germinates at an appropriate depth and quickly establishes a photosynthetic shoot can succeed with much less stored material. These relationships help explain why environmental cues and seed provisioning evolve together. They do not require a universal rule linking a particular seed mass to a fixed depth of emergence. Such rules would ignore species differences in seed composition, seedling anatomy and environmental conditions.

12. Experimental reasoning: distinguishing the affected developmental step

A well-designed seed experiment begins by identifying the step at which a proposed mechanism is expected to act. If the question is whether a treatment releases dormancy, it is not enough to count the number of large seedlings at the end of a long incubation. Large seedlings can result from earlier emergence, faster growth after emergence or both. The experiment should record the time at which each radicle protrudes and, if necessary, measure subsequent growth separately. If the question concerns reserve utilization, measurements of storage compound abundance, hydrolysis products or seedling dry mass may be required. If the question concerns viability, the ability to germinate after appropriate treatment and evidence from a suitable viability test become important. Choosing a readout that corresponds to the biological question is an essential part of mechanistic reasoning.

A particularly useful design separates the seed's developmental history from its immediate environment. Suppose seeds of one genotype are produced under two different maternal temperature regimes. After harvesting and suitable handling, seeds from both groups are incubated at the same test temperature. If the resulting germination curves differ, the difference cannot simply be explained by the immediate test temperature because it was held constant. The maturation environments may have affected dormancy, reserve provisioning or other aspects of seed quality. To distinguish those possibilities, the investigator can compare seed dry mass, viability and responsiveness to a dormancy-release treatment. If both groups remain highly viable and the difference in germination largely disappears after an appropriate release treatment, an influence on dormancy becomes plausible. If the low-performing group contains many damaged embryos, the original contrast may instead include a difference in seed quality.

The same logic applies to a reciprocal cross between plants differing in seed dormancy. In a typical reciprocal cross, the nuclear genotype of the hybrid embryo can be the same for corresponding alleles while the identity of the maternal parent changes. The seed coat genotype changes with the maternal plant, and the parental contributions to the endosperm are reversed. The maternal environment may also differ. Therefore, a reciprocal-cross difference is evidence of a parent-associated effect, but its existence does not uniquely identify one mechanism. It may reflect maternal seed coat properties, contributions from the endosperm, maternally influenced embryonic regulation or the conditions of seed development. Assigning the effect to imprinting requires evidence beyond the phenotype of a reciprocal cross. Understanding seed compartment origins allows the student to recognize why multiple plausible explanations remain.

The comparison of intact seeds and isolated embryos is similarly valuable if used carefully. Suppose intact seeds from a dormant line fail to protrude a radicle, whereas isolated embryos from the same line extend readily in a nutrient-containing medium. The result supports a contribution from the covering tissues or from their interactions with the embryo. Now suppose a second experiment finds that removal of the outer seed coat alone does not restore emergence but removal of the inner endosperm does. This narrows the candidate region. It still leaves open whether the relevant contribution is physical, chemical or both. Mechanical measurements of resistance and appropriately controlled tissue-based signaling experiments can provide complementary evidence. If the removed tissues provide nourishment, the medium in which the isolated embryo is tested also matters. A rescue that depends on a rich medium cannot automatically be interpreted as restoration of the same developmental conditions present inside an intact seed.

To study hormone regulation, a factorial experiment can be more informative than a single treatment. Seeds can be assigned to a control condition, an ABA condition, a GA condition and a combined ABA-plus-GA condition. If each hormone has an effect, comparison of all four groups reveals whether the combined condition resembles one single treatment, gives an intermediate result or differs in another way. These outcomes can suggest relationships between regulatory processes. For example, a GA-dependent increase in the germination fraction that is diminished by added ABA is consistent with opposing influences. It does not determine whether one hormone changed the abundance of the other or whether two signaling pathways converged on a common growth process. Measurements of endogenous hormone levels or genetic perturbations can distinguish among additional hypotheses. One should not claim a direct molecular interaction from a combined-treatment outcome alone.

A genetic rescue experiment can place a defect within a broad causal sequence. A mutant that does not produce enough active GA can sometimes be rescued by supplied GA if the downstream system remains functional. If a second mutant lacks an essential response component, supplied GA is less likely to restore that response. Comparing these two outcomes helps distinguish an upstream deficiency from a downstream defect. However, a negative rescue result has alternative explanations: the hormone may fail to reach the relevant tissue, the applied form may not be active in the system, the dose or timing may be unsuitable, or the seed may have an additional lesion. Therefore, an absence of rescue is informative only when appropriate controls establish that the treatment was effective in a comparable responsive system. A wild-type control exposed to the same conditions and a known responsive line can help interpret negative results.

Experimental replication is important because seeds from the same maternal plant or the same seed lot can share developmental influences. Counting one hundred seeds from a single dish gives a useful count of the fraction in that dish but does not necessarily establish the variability among independently prepared dishes or maternal plants. A study that aims to generalize to a genotype should include appropriate biological replication, not simply a large number of seeds all sharing the same uncontrolled developmental history. Random assignment of comparable seeds to treatments reduces systematic differences between groups. Repeated observations at defined intervals help reveal whether a treatment changes timing, final fraction or both. The examination principle is to identify the experimental unit and the denominator of the measured outcome before interpreting a numerical difference as evidence for a mechanism.

Classical discoveries fit within this same reasoning framework. Borthwick and colleagues' 1952 lettuce experiments demonstrated reversibility of the response to red and far-red light. Their finding could not be explained simply by the total amount of illumination; the sequence of wavelengths mattered. The 1967 barley experiments of Chrispeels and Varner, by contrast, isolated a responsive tissue and tested a hormonal influence on the production of enzymes. One experiment separated spectral information from cumulative light exposure, while the other separated tissue response from the presence of the embryo. Both illustrate the value of designing an experiment that distinguishes competing causal accounts. Later molecular studies provide more specific identities for components of the pathways, but the basic experimental logic remains accessible and useful for solving NET and SET questions.

13. Quantitative interpretation of seed maturation and germination

Quantitative observations can help distinguish biological processes that look similar when described only in words. Consider a developing seed that has a fresh mass of 80 milligrams and a dry mass of 25 milligrams. The difference between those quantities is the mass of water, provided the drying method used to estimate dry mass removes water without a significant loss of other material. At a later developmental stage, the same type of seed may have a fresh mass of 60 milligrams and a dry mass of 45 milligrams. It has become lighter in fresh mass but contains more dry material. The observation is perfectly compatible with simultaneous reserve accumulation and maturation dehydration. An interpretation based only on fresh mass would miss the increase in retained matter, whereas an interpretation based only on dry mass would miss the loss of water. The two measurements together reveal a developmental transition.

There are two commonly encountered ways of expressing moisture content. On a fresh-mass basis, divide water mass by total fresh mass. On a dry-mass basis, divide water mass by dry mass. Both are meaningful, but they have different denominators. They therefore produce different numerical values even when they describe the same seed. The dry-mass basis may exceed one hundred percent because water mass can exceed dry mass; fresh-mass basis cannot exceed one hundred percent for a seed with nonnegative water and dry mass. The conversion follows directly from conservation of mass. If the fresh-mass water fraction is f, then water mass equals f times fresh mass and dry mass equals (1 − f) multiplied by fresh mass. Dividing those expressions gives a dry-mass water fraction of f divided by one minus f. The conversion is nonlinear and the fractions must not be confused with percentages. A numerical problem may deliberately give a moisture content without specifying the basis; in that case a unique numerical interpretation is not justified until the convention is established.

When a seed takes up water during germination, the investigator may record the increase in fresh mass relative to the initial fresh mass. This is an operational measure of mass gain, not necessarily an exact determination of the amount of water inside every tissue. Respiration can consume reserves and release carbon dioxide, and soluble compounds can leave the seed. Surface water adhering to seeds can also affect measured mass. During the initial period of hydration, changes in water mass often dominate the measured increase, but a more detailed mass balance is required if measurements extend through prolonged seedling growth. An experiment that compares a control group and a treatment that alters reserve metabolism should be especially cautious in interpreting their fresh masses as direct measurements of water uptake. The chemical and developmental changes in the seed can change dry mass as well.

In a germination test, a cumulative curve plots the fraction of seeds that have met a defined germination criterion by each observation time. If the initial batch consists of N seeds and G(t) have completed germination by time t, then the cumulative percentage is one hundred times G(t) divided by N. The count at each successive observation includes seeds counted earlier, so a correctly recorded cumulative curve cannot decline unless the definition or the denominator changes. This property allows a student to detect an incorrectly labeled graph. If a curve falls after a maximum, it may represent the number of living seedlings, the number germinating during a particular interval or another noncumulative measure, rather than cumulative germination. Once a seed has been counted as having protruded a radicle, later death does not erase that historical event from the cumulative count.

The final fraction that germinates and the speed of germination are related but distinct. A treatment can leave the final fraction unchanged while accelerating the times at which individual seeds meet the criterion. A second treatment can increase the final fraction without substantially changing the timing among those that germinate early. To quantify timing, one can calculate the mean germination time among seeds that germinate during the observation period. If n_i is the number newly germinating at observation time t_i, then the mean time is the sum of each product n_i times t_i, divided by the sum of n_i. The numerator has dimensions of seed counts multiplied by time, and the denominator has dimensions of seed counts, leaving units of time. It is a weighted arithmetic mean of observed germination times. Cumulative counts must first be converted into newly germinated counts; inserting cumulative counts directly would count earlier seeds repeatedly.

A limitation of mean germination time is that it describes the observed germinating subset, not all seeds originally tested. Suppose a treatment permits a small number of fast seeds to germinate but prevents most other seeds from doing so. The mean among its successful seeds could be shorter than that of a treatment with much higher final germination. It would be misleading to call the former treatment superior on the basis of the smaller mean alone. Final germination percentage and a measure of timing should be considered together. A related issue arises when some seeds remain ungerminated at the end of the observation period. Their actual time to germination is not known; they might germinate later, remain dormant or be dead. Assigning every ungerminated seed a germination time equal to the last observation would introduce an assumption that is not supported by the measurements.

A median-type measure such as the time at which half of the initial seed population has germinated must also specify its denominator. Some studies instead calculate the time at which half of the eventual germinating fraction has completed germination. These measures coincide when every seed germinates, but differ otherwise. If only forty of one hundred seeds germinate, the time to germination of half of the initial population is not observed, whereas the time to half of the eventual germinators can be estimated from the twentieth successful seed. A simple interpolation between observations is sometimes useful when a threshold is crossed between two measured times. Such an interpolation assumes approximately linear accumulation of germination over the interval, which need not reflect the exact behavior of individual seeds. It is an approximation rather than a direct observation of the time of the event.

When data are compared across treatments, a common experimental mistake is to use viability-adjusted germination in one group and total-seed germination in another. Suppose a test includes one hundred seeds, of which eighty are judged viable in an appropriate independent assessment, and sixty protrude their radicles. The observed germination percentage is sixty percent of the initial batch. The proportion of the estimated viable seeds that germinated is seventy-five percent, assuming the viability estimate applies to the tested batch and all germinating seeds were viable. The latter value can help investigate dormancy, but it is not the same as the observed germination percentage. Both values can be reported, with their denominators clearly stated. In an experiment using different representative samples for viability and germination, the adjusted proportion is an estimate rather than an exact classification of individual seeds.

Numerical reasoning also benefits from relating a graph to a mechanistic hypothesis. If two groups have nearly identical mass gain during initial imbibition, but one group has a much smaller proportion of radicles emerging, a purely initial water-permeability explanation becomes less convincing. The data instead suggest examining events after uptake, including hormone response, metabolism and resistance of covering tissues. This inference is conditional because equal whole-seed mass gain does not prove identical hydration of each tissue. Conversely, if one group shows little initial mass gain and a treatment that alters its coat restores both uptake and subsequent emergence, the data provide evidence for a permeability-related limitation. The goal of a graph-based question is to connect the measured variable to the biological process it can actually represent, while preserving uncertainty about mechanisms that have not been measured.

14. Developmental timing, adaptation and biological limits

The maturation and germination programs can be viewed as different solutions to the problem of allocating the same biological resources across successive stages. During seed filling, assimilates are used to build reserves and protective structures. During the mature dry interval, preservation of that investment takes priority over growth. During germination, reserves and stored information are reactivated and consumed, and structures that formerly protected the embryo may have to be weakened or displaced. A process that benefits the seed during one interval can therefore become a barrier during another. For example, a water-resistant covering may protect a seed during dispersal but must allow sufficient hydration before development can resume. Similarly, growth inhibition during maturation can protect the seed against premature germination, but that inhibition must be sufficiently relieved under appropriate conditions if the next generation is to become established.

Premature germination while the seed is still associated with the maternal plant illustrates this conflict particularly clearly. In cereal crops, preharvest sprouting can occur when mature or nearly mature grains germinate in wet conditions before harvest. A seed that has lost restraint of germination can begin mobilizing starch at an undesirable time. The consequence is not restricted to whether a root becomes visible. Changes in the chemical properties of the grain can reduce its suitability for particular uses. A cultivar selected for very weak dormancy may germinate promptly after sowing, but under certain environmental conditions the same characteristic can increase vulnerability to preharvest sprouting. Conversely, exceptionally persistent dormancy can reduce uniformity and predictability of crop establishment. These examples illustrate a tradeoff between developmental timing and environmental risk rather than a universally optimal degree of dormancy.

The term vivipary describes germination or continued development of offspring before separation from the parent in relevant plant contexts. In some plant groups, development proceeds in this manner as part of a normal reproductive strategy. In other cases, precocious germination can arise when the normal restraints on maturation-associated growth are impaired. These examples should not be treated as identical phenomena with identical molecular causes. A seed of a plant adapted to dispersal as a developing propagule may have a developmental program very different from that of a typical dry orthodox grain. The fact that both exhibit growth before detachment does not demonstrate that both lack ABA or possess the same dormancy-related mutation. The comparison is valuable because it reveals that the familiar mature, dry and dormant stage is an adaptation, not an indispensable universal stage for every flowering plant.

Dormancy can distribute germination across time. If all viable seeds germinate immediately after the first favorable signal, a subsequent unfavorable event can eliminate a large proportion of a cohort. If a portion remains dormant, it may contribute to later recruitment when conditions improve. Yet dormancy also entails costs: remaining in a soil seed bank exposes the seed to deterioration, pathogens, predation and the possibility of missing a favorable establishment opportunity. The advantage of delayed germination therefore depends on the environmental pattern, not merely on the existence of a dormant state. Even among seeds produced by the same plant, differences in maturation conditions and physiological state can lead to variation in the timing of germination. Such variation may influence the distribution of seedling establishment without requiring every seed to receive a completely different developmental program.

The maternal environment matters because developmental decisions made during seed formation can influence how the offspring responds later. Temperature, water availability and nutritional supply during maturation may affect properties such as seed reserve size, desiccation tolerance and dormancy. A seed that encounters an unfavorable environment during filling can show poor performance after dispersal even when germination conditions are otherwise suitable. This kind of carryover effect is distinct from an immediate response to the germination environment. It illustrates how development integrates information over time. A study of seedling behavior that ignores the history of the maternal plants may misattribute differences in germination to the genotype of the embryo or the current experimental treatment. Careful comparisons therefore use seeds with comparable history whenever the goal is to identify a specific cause.

Seed storage and conservation provide another practical application. Orthodox seeds can often be kept in suitable dry, cool conditions to preserve genetic resources, but the same approach cannot be applied indiscriminately to seeds that are sensitive to drying or cooling. The appropriate storage strategy depends on the biological properties of the species and the quality of the seed lot. A collection intended to preserve a plant population must retain viable material capable of producing future plants, not merely structures that still look like intact seeds. Periodic assessments of germination and viability can therefore be used to distinguish the physical presence of stored seeds from preservation of their developmental capacity. The fundamental distinction between a seed that is alive but dormant and one that has deteriorated is essential for interpreting these assessments.

The transition also connects developmental biology to physiological and genetic concepts from other units. Water potential explains initial hydration and contributes to expansion of growing cells. Enzyme kinetics helps explain how measured reserve hydrolysis changes when enzyme production or substrate availability changes. Cellular respiration provides the energetic basis for active cellular processes, while hormonal signaling illustrates how an external or developmental cue changes gene activity and tissue behavior. Inheritance and gene regulation clarify why a mutant seed can differ from a wild-type seed even when both encounter the same conditions. These connections are useful precisely because they explain stages in the developmental sequence. They do not require treating every metabolic pathway or every signaling component in full biochemical detail within the seed chapter.

Finally, the chapter closes the plant portion of early development by returning to the body plan formed during embryogenesis. Double fertilization establishes the embryo and supporting seed compartments. Patterning produces an organized embryonic axis. Seed filling and maturation provision that axis and enable survival during the interval before establishment. Dormancy and environmental responses regulate whether development can proceed. Germination restores active growth and exposes the root to the surrounding environment, while later growth progressively produces a self-supporting shoot and root system. The next curriculum topics concerning plant meristems and organogenesis build on this continuity. An understanding of the seed stage makes those later topics more coherent because the student recognizes that the meristems and tissue identities used during establishment were not invented at the moment of germination. They are the products of earlier developmental processes whose activity has been resumed and redirected toward independent growth.

  2. The later transition from testa rupture to radicle passage through endosperm
  3. All water uptake
  4. Initial seed formation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The early visible event is largely preserved while the subsequent event is strongly reduced. The data localize the phenotype rather than proving a particular molecular target.

21. A reciprocal cross changes seed dormancy although the corresponding hybrid embryo nuclear genotypes are the same. Which inference is warranted?

  1. Imprinting is conclusively proved
  2. The embryos have different numbers of cotyledons by necessity
  3. Paternal genotype can never contribute
  4. A parent-associated effect is plausible, including maternal coat or endosperm differences
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Reciprocal crosses alter multiple parent-associated features of seeds. Further evidence is required to separate imprinting from maternal environment, coat genotype and endosperm effects.

22. An abi3 line and wild type both have 95% viability before dehydration. After the same drying treatment, viability is 20% and 91%, respectively. What is most directly supported?

  1. Both lines have identical drying tolerance
  2. The mutant must lack a testa entirely
  3. The mutant is impaired in surviving this dehydration treatment
  4. The mutant was originally nonviable
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Before drying the viability is comparable; after drying the mutant loses much more viability. The experiment does not by itself locate the protective molecular mechanism.

23. A treatment inhibits radicle emergence in 95% of seeds, and nearly all remain unable to grow even after the treatment is removed and appropriate rescue conditions are tried. Essential embryonic tissue is unstained in viability tests. Which interpretation fits?

  1. The seeds are guaranteed to germinate next season
  2. Loss of viability rather than simple reversible dormancy
  3. The treatment improved seed vigor
  4. Primary dormancy is conclusively enhanced
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Failure after release and evidence of damaged essential tissues support nonviability. A reversible developmental restriction must preserve the potential to grow.

24. A researcher reports that a hormone treatment increases final seedling mass. What additional measurement is most useful for deciding whether the treatment specifically accelerated germination?

  1. Time of individual radicle protrusion
  2. Only the number of flowers on the parent
  3. Only final seedling color
  4. Total mass of the laboratory container
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Direct recording of radicle emergence distinguishes timing of germination from subsequent growth. Final biomass alone combines both effects.

25. A seed lot of 100 reaches 40 germinators at the end of a test. What can be said of the time to 50% of all initial seeds?

  1. It is exactly the time of the twentieth germinator
  2. It must be zero
  3. It was not observed in the test
  4. It equals the mean germination time
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The threshold is 50 seeds, and only 40 were observed. The twentieth germinator corresponds to half of the eventual observed germinators, a different denominator.

Advanced mixed-format practice

1. Assertion: A viable physiologically dormant seed may consume oxygen without radicle emergence. Reason: Hydration can reactivate metabolism before a regulatory restriction on completed germination is relieved.

  1. Assertion true, reason false
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both are true, but reason does not explain assertion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Hydration and respiratory reactivation can precede relief of the growth block. The reason explains why metabolism is compatible with failure of visible germination.

2. Consider statements: I. Endosperm and embryo have the same genetic origin. II. Seed coat is maternal. III. A persistent endosperm may restrain radicle protrusion. IV. All dicots lack endosperm at maturity. Which pair is correct?

  1. I and IV
  2. I and II
  3. III and IV
  4. II and III
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. II and III are correct. Endosperm and embryo arise through separate fertilization events, and many dicots retain endosperm. Thus any pair containing I or IV is incorrect.

3. Match A: physical dormancy; B: after-ripening; C: aleurone; D: scutellum with 1: dry storage change, 2: water-impermeable covering, 3: specialized grass cotyledon, 4: living enzyme-secreting layer. Correct order for A–D?

  1. 2,1,4,3
  2. 1,2,3,4
  3. 2,1,3,4
  4. 4,3,2,1
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A→2, B→1, C→4 and D→3. The aleurone and scutellum are different structures, and dry after-ripening is not a permeability barrier.

4. Assertion: A smaller mean germination time always proves a superior seed lot. Reason: Mean germination time is calculated only from seeds that germinated during the observation period.

  1. Both false
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both true and reason explains assertion
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. A fast minority can produce a short mean despite poor final germination. The mean describes successful germinators and must be interpreted alongside the final fraction.

5. Using the chapter's cumulative germination graph, choose the combination supported by the plotted values.

  1. A and B both reach 100% on day 4
  2. A has lower final germination than B
  3. A reaches 90% at day 6 and B at day 4
  4. A reaches 90% at day 4; B at day 6
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. The graph uses a fixed denominator and explicitly shows the same final percentage of 90% at different times. None of the other descriptions matches both curves.

MisconceptionCorrect interpretation and exam strategy
A dry seed must be dead.Drying can be developmentally tolerated by an orthodox seed. Test its potential for germination rather than infer death from appearance.
All seeds tolerate severe drying.Recalcitrant and intermediate seeds limit this inference. A storage-treatment question must identify seed behavior.
Maturation means embryo shape alone is complete.Reserve loading and acquisition of protective properties are distinct from early morphogenesis. Compare dry mass and viability.
A drop in fresh mass means reserves were lost.Fresh mass includes water. Examine dry mass before deciding whether reserve accumulation ceased.
Dormancy is any lack of growth.A viable seed lacking an external requirement can be quiescent; nonviability is another possibility. Look for favorable-condition evidence.
Physical dormancy means any tough testa.Strict physical dormancy involves a water barrier. Compare hydration before and after scarification.
After-ripening means germination in a moist cold environment.After-ripening in this context occurs during dry storage; moist chilling is stratification.
ABA and GA levels alone determine behavior.Tissue responsiveness and developmental state matter. Predict biosynthetic versus receptor-mutant phenotypes separately.
GA directly digests starch.GA is a hormone; enzymes hydrolyze starch. An isolated tissue assay distinguishes signaling and catalysis.
All imbibing seeds are viable.Physical hydration can occur in dead tissue. Look for growth or independent evidence of essential tissue viability.
Dormant seeds cannot respire.A hydrated physiologically dormant seed can resume metabolism without completing germination.
Testa rupture always completes germination.In Arabidopsis the remaining endosperm can still restrain the radicle. Score the correct morphological endpoint.
All reserve mobilization is completed before the radicle emerges.Much reserve mobilization may support later growth. Separate germination and establishment outcomes.
Pea never forms endosperm.The mature pea stores reserves chiefly in cotyledons, but endosperm develops earlier.

Integrated revision and active recall

Seed formation establishes an embryo, supporting endosperm and a maternal covering. Maturation prepares a coordinated embryo for dispersal by accumulating reserves, differentiating protective tissues and, in orthodox seeds, acquiring drying tolerance. Dormancy may be established before dispersal and changed by the environment afterward. Imbibition restores the physical conditions for metabolic activity, but a viable seed must overcome regulatory and mechanical constraints before the radicle emerges. Reserve mobilization then supports continued growth until the seedling can obtain resources independently.

Essential quantitative relationships

  • Water mass = fresh mass − dry mass.
  • Fresh-mass moisture percentage = 100 × water mass / fresh mass.
  • Dry-mass moisture percentage = 100 × water mass / dry mass.
  • Cumulative germination percentage = 100 × cumulative germinated count / initial count.
  • Mean observed germination time = Σ(nᵢtᵢ) / Σnᵢ; nᵢ is the newly germinated count.

  1. Trace the developmental origins of the three seed compartments.
  2. Explain why seed dry mass and fresh mass can change in opposite directions.
  3. Describe two mechanisms contributing to tolerance of maturation dehydration.
  4. Distinguish quiescence, primary dormancy and secondary dormancy.
  5. Predict water uptake and emergence after scarification in physical versus physiological dormancy.
  6. Explain why red–far-red reversibility is evidence for a photoreceptor.
  7. Compare an ABA-insensitive mutant and an ABA-deficient mutant.
  8. Sketch the three phases of water uptake and identify the growth-associated transition.
  9. Explain the role of growth potential and covering resistance in radicle emergence.
  10. Reconstruct the classical barley GA–aleurone–amylase model.
  11. Compare bean, pea and maize seedlings using their embryonic structures.
  12. Calculate mean germination time from newly germinated counts and explain the denominator.
  13. Explain why germination and seedling establishment are not equivalent.

Mastery checklist

  • Explain seed compartment function, storage behavior and maturation regulation without confusing tissue origin.
  • Interpret water uptake, viability, dormancy release and hormone-response experiments with appropriate controls.
  • Predict distinct effects on radicle emergence and subsequent seedling establishment.
  • Calculate moisture content, germination percentage and timing metrics using explicitly defined denominators.
  • Recognize monocot and eudicot developmental structures and link the embryonic axis to subsequent meristem activity.
