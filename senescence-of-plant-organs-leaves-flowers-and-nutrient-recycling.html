1. An organ has a developmental lifetime

A plant does not have to preserve every organ that it has constructed. During its early growth a wheat plant invests nutrients in leaves, which absorb sunlight and manufacture organic matter. Later, when the grains develop, the same leaves may become an important reservoir of nitrogen. A nitrogen atom incorporated into an enzyme in a leaf early in the season may eventually become part of a storage protein in a grain. For that transfer to take place, the plant must recover material from the enzyme, transform it into a transportable form and deliver it through living tissues. The yellow leaf that remains at the end of this process is not merely a damaged solar collector. It is the remnant of an organ whose role in the life of the plant has changed.

The final developmental phase of an organ, during which many of its normal functions decline and its constituents are remodeled or degraded, is called organ senescence. In leaves the decline often culminates in cell death and sometimes in shedding. It is important to recognize the phase that precedes those final outcomes. A senescing mesophyll cell can still carry out transcription, translation, respiration, intracellular transport and membrane transport. Some of these activities are required precisely because other activities are being discontinued. The capacity for carbon fixation may diminish at the same time as the capacity to degrade selected proteins increases. Senescence is therefore not equivalent to the simultaneous cessation of all metabolism.

Think of the developmental sequence of an individual foliage leaf. Cells in the primordium divide, and subsequently the leaf expands and differentiates. During expansion, it receives imported organic compounds as well as water and minerals. When the lamina matures and becomes a substantial photosynthetic surface, it commonly becomes a net exporter of assimilated carbon. In a later phase, photosynthetic production falls and the contribution of the leaf to other organs increasingly includes compounds liberated from its own structures. Eventually its viability and capacity for exchange cease. These phases describe a common pattern, not a rule that each transition occurs on a sharply defined day or at exactly the same point in every cell.

A source is an organ or tissue that makes a net contribution of a specified resource to the rest of the plant. A sink is a region that imports a resource for growth, metabolism or storage. The same organ can have different source and sink relationships for different compounds. A developing leaf can import sucrose before it can export much of its own, while also accumulating inorganic nitrogen delivered from roots. A mature leaf can provide sucrose to a root and, at a later time, supply amino acids to a developing seed. Source and sink are relational physiological terms, rather than permanent labels for particular botanical structures.

The distinction matters because leaves remain attached to a continuously changing system. A plant may produce new leaves while older leaves senesce. The older leaves may be shaded by the newer leaves and receive a smaller share of the light available to the canopy. If an older leaf costs more to maintain than it contributes by photosynthesis, continued investment in its photosynthetic apparatus may offer little advantage under those conditions. Recovering part of its nitrogen can allow the plant to build proteins in organs with a greater opportunity for productive growth. This explanation describes a biological tradeoff; it does not imply that an individual plant consciously calculates profit or that every instance of senescence improves its fitness.

A useful distinction is between the age of an organ and its physiological state. Two leaves that emerged on the same day may experience different irradiance, nutrient supply and water status. Their rates of maturation and functional decline may differ. Conversely, leaves of different ages may show similar chlorophyll contents at one time. Developmental age can influence the capacity to initiate senescence, but chronological age alone cannot establish the stage of the process. If the experiment concerns aging, the date of leaf emergence is relevant. If it concerns senescence, measurements of organ function and remodeling must accompany the age measurement.

The word senescence has a different emphasis in animal cell biology. An animal somatic cell that has entered cellular senescence may remain metabolically active despite a stable arrest of proliferation. A mature leaf mesophyll cell is often no longer proliferating substantially even before its organ begins to senesce. Arrest of division is therefore not an adequate criterion for recognizing leaf senescence. The relevant plant process is the developmental reorganization of an already differentiated organ, with changing patterns of biosynthesis, catabolism, transport and survival. An answer that applies the animal criterion directly to mature foliage misses this difference in scale and developmental context.

Nor should organ senescence be confused with the developmental death of selected cells during the construction of an organ. A differentiating tracheary element loses its protoplast so that its remaining wall can contribute to a conducting pathway. The death is part of the acquisition of tissue function. In contrast, mesophyll cells in an aging leaf lose much of the photosynthetic machinery that previously supported organ function. Both phenomena involve regulated degradation, but the relationship between that degradation and the function of the tissue differs. This distinction links organ senescence to the plant developmental cell death examples studied in the preceding chapter.

2. From visible yellowing to evidence of coordinated change

A green leaf becoming yellow provides an accessible starting point for studying senescence, but colour alone does not reveal why the change has occurred. Chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the blue and red regions of visible light. When its concentration falls, other pigments and the optical properties of the tissue make different contributions to the reflected light. Carotenoids, which were present in the green chloroplast, can become more conspicuous as chlorophyll disappears. In some autumn leaves, anthocyanins also accumulate. The appearance of yellow or red is consequently the result of a changing mixture of compounds rather than the transformation of all chlorophyll directly into yellow or red pigment.

Chlorosis refers to insufficient green pigmentation of tissue. It can arise when a plant fails to form or maintain normal quantities of chlorophyll. Mineral deficiencies, adverse environmental conditions, certain mutations and infection can all give rise to chlorosis. If a young leaf is chlorotic because its iron supply is inadequate, the biological problem differs from the coordinated dismantling of a previously functional chloroplast in an old leaf. In the first case, normal development or maintenance is limited. In the second case, mature cellular components are being degraded as the developmental role of the organ changes. Some stresses cause both chlorosis and premature senescence, which is why the processes must be distinguished experimentally rather than by assuming that one excludes the other.

An experiment to distinguish the two should examine the history of the tissue. A leaf that has always contained little chlorophyll cannot be assumed to have degraded a previously abundant pigment pool. Measurements taken from emergence through maturity and decline can distinguish low initial accumulation from accelerated subsequent loss. If young chlorotic leaves regain normal pigmentation after the limiting nutrient is supplied, the result supports a deficiency-related explanation. Recovery is not by itself an absolute criterion, because some early senescence-associated changes can also be reversible in certain systems. The experiment is strongest when recovery, age, cell viability and nutrient movement are assessed together.

The study of senescence progressed from descriptions of visible aging to chemical analyses of leaves and finally to measurements of the expression and activity of individual genes and proteins. The value of the molecular approach is that two apparently identical yellow leaves can be found to differ in their internal state. One may have activated enzymes involved in chlorophyll and protein turnover; the other may have lost pigment following abrupt injury. Comparing protein patterns and messenger RNA abundance at different ages can reveal that some cellular activities decline while others become more prominent. A purely passive model of aging, in which all cellular reactions simply weaken together, cannot easily account for such selective changes.

Senescence-associated genes are genes whose expression increases or changes characteristically in senescing tissues. Their products may participate in catabolism, stress responses, transport or regulation. A senescence-associated transcript is evidence about the state of the cell, not a direct measurement of the quantity of material exported from the organ. Transcript abundance depends on both synthesis and degradation of the RNA; protein abundance further depends on translation and protein stability. Moreover, related genes may respond to environmental stress independently of developmental senescence. A single marker should therefore be interpreted alongside the age of the organ and other physiological observations.

An instructive comparison can be made between an attached leaf and a detached leaf kept in darkness. The latter may lose chlorophyll rapidly and is experimentally convenient, but detachment removes its normal connections to sinks and roots. Darkness also stops photosynthetic carbon fixation. The resulting changes reflect carbon starvation, altered water relations and wounding responses as well as senescence-like degradation. An attached leaf placed in darkness has a different transport context. To infer the natural course of developmental senescence, observations in intact plants are essential. Detached leaves may still be useful for testing particular biochemical capacities provided the conclusions are restricted to that experimental system.

Studies of leaf senescence commonly measure chlorophyll content, net carbon assimilation, changes in proteins, nitrogen content, expression of selected genes, electrolyte leakage and sometimes fluorescence characteristics. These measurements address different questions. Chlorophyll reports pigment abundance. Carbon assimilation measures the net exchange of carbon dioxide under specified conditions. Leakage reports a loss of the ability of membranes to retain dissolved ions, whereas expression of a catabolic gene may precede such damage. An organ can score as strongly senescent by some measures before it loses membrane integrity. The temporal separation of these changes is a valuable clue to the distinction between active remodeling and terminal loss of viability.

An appropriate time course should begin before obvious visual changes. If measurements begin only after the leaf is yellow, early changes in photosynthesis or nutrient allocation will be missed. Because many chemical measurements destroy tissue, independent replicate leaves at equivalent positions and developmental stages must be collected on successive dates. Nondestructive measurements on the same leaf, such as carefully standardized fluorescence or gas exchange, can supplement the destructive samples. Sampling from the same region of the lamina is important if senescence progresses unevenly from tip to base or among sectors. Otherwise, a change in sampling position could be mistaken for a change over time.

3. Why chloroplasts become major sites of remobilization

The structure of a mature chloroplast explains why its dismantling matters for nutrient economy. The organelle is bounded by an envelope and contains a soluble compartment called the stroma. Inside are thylakoid membranes, on which photosynthetic pigments and protein complexes are organized. Light capture and the photosynthetic electron-transfer reactions occur in association with these membranes. The ATP and reducing power formed by photosynthetic electron transport support carbon assimilation in the stroma. The two regions are functionally linked: membrane reactions provide energy and reductant, while carbon metabolism provides an important route for using them.

The thylakoid membrane is not simply a layer of chlorophyll. The pigments are associated with proteins and organized into functional complexes. These complexes contain additional cofactors, including metal-containing groups and electron carriers. Large amounts of material are invested in producing and maintaining this apparatus. The stroma also contains abundant soluble enzymes, particularly ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, commonly abbreviated Rubisco. In many C3 leaves, Rubisco constitutes a substantial fraction of the soluble protein pool. Its amino acids represent a reservoir of nitrogen that can become useful elsewhere when the protein is degraded.

It follows that a decrease in the nitrogen content of a senescing leaf need not be explained by the removal of nitrate from the vacuole alone. A large part of the recoverable nitrogen may initially be locked within organic molecules. In a protein, the nitrogen is incorporated into amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds. The plant cannot move an intact chloroplast through the phloem to a grain. Instead, its constituents have to be dismantled to yield smaller molecules that can be metabolized or transported. Different degradation and transport steps are needed for different classes of molecules.

The change in chloroplast structure during natural senescence is often described as a transition towards a gerontoplast, a plastid characteristic of senescing tissue. It may show disorganization of internal membranes, a reduction of pigment-protein complexes and changes in lipid-rich plastoglobules. The exact appearance depends on the plant and the stage examined. The outer envelope need not disappear at the onset of thylakoid degradation. Therefore a micrograph showing altered grana inside a bounded organelle need not represent a completely dead cell. Comparing the internal condition of the plastid with the surrounding cytoplasm provides a more informative view of the stage of senescence.

As the capacity for carbon fixation declines, the consequences for light energy management become significant. Absorption of a photon by chlorophyll creates an excited state. In a functional photosynthetic apparatus, excitation can be transferred to a reaction centre and used to drive electron transfer. Other processes dissipate some of the excitation energy harmlessly as heat. If electron-transfer capacity and carbon metabolism are impaired, absorbed excitation can become excessive in relation to productive use. Formation of reactive oxygen species may increase in particular circumstances. This creates a reason for coordinating the loss of pigments with the breakdown of the proteins and membranes that normally house them.

Unbound chlorophyll and some of its coloured intermediates can promote damaging photochemical reactions. The ability to remove such compounds without allowing them to accumulate is especially valuable in an illuminated tissue. A regulated degradation pathway consequently differs from indiscriminate bleaching. The breakdown products must be processed, and in some cases transported into a compartment in which they can accumulate without interfering with photosynthesis or damaging the cytoplasm. A defect in an intermediate step may be more damaging than complete removal of the original pigment pool by a correctly functioning pathway.

Declining photosynthesis does not necessarily imply that the chloroplast has been completely dismantled. Closure of stomata during water limitation restricts carbon dioxide entry even if the internal photosynthetic apparatus remains largely intact. Loss of Rubisco can limit assimilation even if pigment content remains high. Damage to reaction centres may reduce photochemical efficiency independently of stomatal aperture. Consequently, chlorophyll concentration, chlorophyll fluorescence and carbon assimilation are complementary, not interchangeable, measurements. A leaf that retains its original green appearance can still have lost a considerable fraction of its capacity to fix carbon.

A senescing mesophyll cell requires energy to reorganize itself. Some ATP can be supplied by residual chloroplast activity during early decline, but mitochondrial respiration is particularly important as photosynthetic capacity falls. Respiratory substrates may come from soluble carbohydrates and from metabolism of products liberated during breakdown. Maintenance of ion gradients, synthesis of selected enzymes, vesicle movement and solute transport all require an organized living metabolism. Sudden destruction of all energy-generating systems would make orderly recovery of nutrients more difficult. This is one reason why loss of viability is a poor early definition of senescence.

4. The chemical pathway that removes chlorophyll

Chlorophyll a contains a magnesium ion coordinated within a modified tetrapyrrole macrocycle and a long hydrophobic phytol-derived ester group. The ring system is responsible for much of its characteristic light absorption, whereas the hydrophobic group contributes to its association with membranes and pigment-protein complexes. Chlorophyll b differs from chlorophyll a in a substituent on the ring system. This change alters its absorption spectrum and is related to its function in light harvesting. The structural differences between the two forms are important during senescence because the major breakdown route is organized around derivatives of chlorophyll a.

In the well-characterized senescence pathway of higher plants, chlorophyll b is first converted to chlorophyll a through reactions involving chlorophyll b reductase activities and a subsequent reduction of the hydroxymethyl intermediate. NON-YELLOW COLORING1 and related proteins participate in the initial conversion, and HCAR acts in the conversion of the intermediate towards chlorophyll a. This enables pigment originally present in chlorophyll b-containing antenna complexes to enter the major chlorophyll a degradation route. A defect in this conversion can change the persistence of antenna-associated pigments without necessarily preserving all aspects of chloroplast function.

Removal of the central magnesium from chlorophyll a yields pheophytin a. A major advance came when Arabidopsis STAY-GREEN proteins were shown to possess magnesium-dechelatase activity. Shimoda, Ito and Tanaka reported this biochemical function in 2016. The discovery illustrates why a visible phenotype does not by itself identify the reaction catalysed by a gene product. A mutant with persistent green pigment had provided a way of identifying the importance of the gene, but testing the ability of its product to remove magnesium was necessary to establish its direct biochemical role.

Pheophytin pheophorbide hydrolase, usually abbreviated PPH, then hydrolyses the phytol ester of pheophytin a, yielding pheophorbide a and phytol. The order is significant. In this representative pathway, the principal substrate of PPH is pheophytin rather than intact chlorophyll. Other routes of pigment turnover and reactions involving chlorophyllase exist in plants, so this sequence should be understood as the standard senescence-associated route rather than as a universal law for pigment degradation in every tissue. In an examination question about the Arabidopsis leaf pathway, magnesium removal should precede the PPH reaction.

The next essential change is the opening of the macrocycle. Pheophorbide a oxygenase, or PAO, catalyses a reaction that converts pheophorbide a into a red chlorophyll catabolite. Red chlorophyll catabolite reductase, RCCR, reduces this intermediate to a primary fluorescent chlorophyll catabolite. The conversion from a closed pigment ring to a linear tetrapyrrole profoundly changes the optical and chemical properties of the molecules. It also enables subsequent modifications that generate a diverse family of compounds called phyllobilins.

Products of the chloroplast pathway can undergo further enzymatic modifications outside the plastid, and many catabolites are ultimately stored in the vacuole. Under acidic conditions certain fluorescent chlorophyll catabolites can isomerize into nonfluorescent forms. Not every plant produces precisely the same final mixture, and some later derivatives can be coloured. The important point for interpreting leaf yellowing is that the abundant green pigment is progressively converted to forms that no longer make its original contribution to the absorption spectrum of the photosynthetic tissue.

A hypothetical block of PAO illustrates the difference between the absence of a reaction and the interruption of an entire coordinated system. Earlier steps could still produce pheophorbide a while subsequent processing becomes limited. The intermediate may accumulate, particularly if its production exceeds alternative removal routes. Because it is photoreactive, light-dependent cellular injury could occur. Reducing illumination would alter the opportunity for the accumulated compound to participate in photochemical reactions, although it would not restore PAO activity. Measuring intermediate abundance alongside damage is necessary to distinguish a direct chemical consequence of the block from unrelated effects of the mutation.

A defect at an earlier step has a different expected biochemical signature. If magnesium removal is impaired, persistence of chlorophyll a may be more conspicuous than accumulation of pheophorbide a. If the conversion of chlorophyll b is limited, the ratio of the two chlorophyll forms can change. These expectations assume that the perturbed gene is important in the tested tissue and that other reactions do not fully compensate. A single observation of colour cannot distinguish all the possibilities. A combination of pigment separation, genetic analysis and measurements of photosynthetic performance is much stronger evidence.

The fate of chlorophyll nitrogen should not be confused with the much larger potential contribution from chlorophyll-binding and stromal proteins. The major nutritional advantage of safely dismantling the pigment-protein complexes is that their protein components can be made available for turnover. The linear catabolites can retain nitrogen atoms originally present in chlorophyll. It is therefore incorrect to infer that each atom of chlorophyll nitrogen must be recovered and exported just because the visible green colour has disappeared. The chemical fate of the pigment and the fate of the surrounding proteins are distinct questions that can be investigated separately.

5. Releasing protein nitrogen without prematurely killing the cell

A peptide bond is an amide linkage formed between amino acid residues. Its hydrolysis yields products with newly exposed amino and carboxyl groups. Proteases accelerate the cleavage of peptide bonds, but proteolysis does not instantly yield a pool composed entirely of free amino acids. Initial cleavage can generate large fragments, followed by smaller peptides. Additional peptidases can release individual amino acids. For nitrogen remobilization, the eventual products must be accessible to the metabolism and transport machinery of the living cell.

Protein turnover takes place throughout leaf life. A photosynthetically active chloroplast repairs or removes damaged components, and a healthy cytoplasm replaces proteins as their activity or stability changes. Senescence changes the balance of these activities. The plant reduces investment in many photosynthetic proteins while increasing its reliance on pathways that recover their constituents. Net decline of a protein pool occurs when the amount degraded exceeds the amount synthesized. This simple distinction is important: measuring the decrease of a protein does not by itself tell whether degradation has accelerated, synthesis has slowed, or both have occurred.

Proteases are distributed among several cellular compartments. A protein within the chloroplast stroma may be degraded by plastid-associated enzymes, or material derived from the chloroplast may reach other compartments for degradation. The large central vacuole of a leaf cell provides an acidic, hydrolytic environment that can contribute to the recovery of cellular components. The tonoplast separates this environment from the cytoplasm. Maintaining that separation during early remodeling allows degradative activities to occur without exposing every essential cytoplasmic structure to the same conditions.

Autophagy is one means by which material can reach a lytic compartment. In macroautophagy, a membrane expands around selected or bulk cytoplasmic material, eventually enclosing it in a double-membrane autophagosome. Fusion with the vacuole releases the inner vesicle and its contents into the vacuolar lumen, where the cargo is broken down. In plant cells, portions of chloroplast stroma containing proteins can be delivered through autophagy-related routes. This is particularly relevant when a leaf is mobilizing a large protein reserve without immediately destroying every chloroplast or the entire cell.

Autophagy is sometimes incorrectly classified as a cell death mechanism whenever it is observed in a senescing tissue. Consider a cell experiencing carbon limitation. By degrading some cellular constituents, it can release substrates for metabolism and maintain functions needed for survival. The same pathway can contribute to nutrient export from an old organ. If disrupting autophagy causes an earlier loss of viability, the observation is consistent with a protective role of autophagy rather than evidence that autophagy normally causes that death. The relationship between a degradation mechanism and the fate of a cell must be established by perturbing the mechanism and measuring the consequences.

The distinction between autophagic structure abundance and autophagic flux is useful here. Imagine that a cell forms ten autophagosomes per hour and clears ten per hour; the average number present might remain constant. A mutation that blocks vacuolar delivery could cause autophagosomes to accumulate, even if their formation rate has not increased. Conversely, very efficient completion of the process could produce few visible structures at a particular instant. Evidence of abundant vesicles is not, by itself, a measurement of the rate at which cellular material is successfully recycled.

A useful test of the contribution of autophagy to organ senescence is to compare an autophagy-deficient mutant with its wild type and an appropriately complemented line. Compare leaves of equivalent age before the onset of terminal damage. If a mutant loses viability early but transfers less previously acquired nitrogen to its seeds, early yellowing or death cannot be interpreted as more efficient senescence. A causal interpretation needs information about the nitrogen pool, the transport destinations and the length of time for which the source tissues remained viable. A separate assessment of seed development is also necessary because impaired sinks could reduce the apparent amount of nitrogen they receive.

Guiboileau and colleagues used nitrogen isotope labeling to examine whole-plant nitrogen redistribution in Arabidopsis lines defective in autophagy. Their study provides a connection between intracellular recycling machinery and a whole-organism resource outcome. Importantly, the relationship can be tested even when the amount of nitrogen available to the plant differs between growth conditions. This design is more informative than concluding from chlorophyll alone that a plant has successfully mobilized its stored nitrogen. It also demonstrates why the timing of premature organ decline must be separated from the biochemical efficiency with which constituents are recovered.

The nitrogen released by protein degradation is chemically useful but not necessarily in the form most suitable for transport. Amino acid metabolism can redistribute amino groups among different carbon skeletons. Some reactions release ammonium, which must be managed because its uncontrolled accumulation would disturb cellular chemistry. Glutamine synthetase uses ATP to incorporate ammonium into glutamine from glutamate, and glutamine can subsequently contribute to transfer of nitrogen to other compounds. Asparagine is another important nitrogen transport form in many plants. It contains two nitrogen atoms per four carbon atoms, making it a relatively nitrogen-rich organic transport compound. The relative importance of these forms depends on species, tissue and environmental conditions.

Transport from the vacuole or plastid to the cytosol is an additional requirement. Soluble degradation products must cross the relevant membrane or be generated in a compartment from which they can be moved. Membrane transporters can use concentration gradients or couple transport to ion gradients. The proton gradients underlying many plant transport processes are maintained through energy-consuming activities. Thus proteolysis, metabolic conversion and membrane transport form a connected chain; successful degradation of a protein in a leaf does not prove that its nitrogen has reached a seed. A defect at any step can change the overall efficiency of resource recovery.

6. Recovery of RNA, membrane lipids and other cellular reserves

Proteins are important stores of nitrogen, but the composition of a leaf is far more varied than its protein content suggests. A plant cell contains ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, messenger RNA, DNA, structural polysaccharides, soluble sugars, organic acids and several classes of lipid. Many of these compounds are required for normal photosynthesis and growth; some become available for reuse when their original functions are no longer needed. Understanding senescence requires considering which chemical bonds must be broken, which elements can be recovered from the products, and which structures should remain functional while recovery proceeds.

RNA is a particularly relevant resource because every nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a sugar and one or more phosphate groups depending on its state of phosphorylation. A substantial fraction of cellular RNA is associated with ribosomes. A decrease in the number of ribosomes associated with a particular biosynthetic activity can make some RNA available for degradation. Ribonucleases cleave RNA into smaller products, and other enzymes can convert these products into compounds that enter nucleotide salvage or degradation pathways. Salvage denotes reuse of a component to synthesize a new nucleotide rather than synthesis of all its atoms from simpler precursors. Recovery of phosphate may require hydrolysis of phosphorylated intermediates.

An increase in ribonuclease activity during senescence is biologically understandable, but the cell must retain some capacity for making RNA and protein. Messenger RNAs encoding catabolic enzymes and transport proteins must be produced and translated at appropriate times. It would be counterproductive for all ribosomes and nucleic acids to disappear at the moment when the cell begins to activate new metabolic functions. The apparent paradox is resolved by the distinction between selective changes in cellular pools and complete destruction. A senescing leaf can reduce its capacity for constructing photosynthetic structures while retaining enough gene expression machinery to carry out the remodeling that follows.

Phosphate is not confined to nucleic acids. It is also present in phosphorylated metabolic intermediates, phospholipids and other cellular compounds. Free inorganic phosphate released from these molecules can enter metabolic reactions, participate in energy metabolism, or be transferred out of the cell through suitable transport systems. If phosphate acquisition from soil is limited, such recovery may help support other organs. The magnitude of this benefit depends on how much phosphorus the source organ contains, how accessible its various chemical pools are and whether recipient tissues can obtain or use the remobilized phosphate. One should not assume that all phosphorus is associated with RNA or that all forms are recovered to the same extent.

Membrane lipids are important in the physical transformation of a senescing leaf. Thylakoid membranes are rich in galactolipids as well as other lipid classes. Their hydrophobic components help create the environment in which membrane proteins and electron carriers operate. Removal of a substantial proportion of those lipids alters membrane organization, even if the protein components have not disappeared at an identical rate. The chloroplast envelope and other cellular membranes have different compositions and roles, and their preservation or degradation may follow different schedules.

Lipases and other lipid-degrading enzymes can release fatty acids and polar head groups. Fatty acids are energy-rich molecules; their metabolic use requires conversion into activated derivatives and subsequent breakdown. In plants much fatty acid beta-oxidation takes place in peroxisomes. The resulting products can contribute to other metabolic pathways. The energetic outcome depends on subsequent metabolism, and not every fatty acid liberated from a senescing membrane is immediately oxidized. Some can be incorporated into other lipid pools or stored transiently. During plastid remodeling, changes in plastoglobules reflect in part the altered handling of hydrophobic constituents. Their occurrence does not indicate a single obligatory destination for every membrane lipid.

The term recycling may suggest that nothing is lost. In a real organism, however, recycling is incomplete. A cell expends energy to mobilize and transport material, and respiration converts part of its organic carbon into carbon dioxide. Some molecules remain in structures that cannot readily be recovered before the organ dies. Others are converted into relatively stable end products that accumulate in the senescent tissue. When a leaf falls, the substances still present in its walls and contents enter the litter rather than being transferred directly to a growing organ. Resource remobilization is therefore a process with an efficiency that can be measured; it is not a synonym for total conservation of each element within the living plant.

Cellulose provides a useful contrast with the soluble reserves of a leaf. It is a polymer of glucose units arranged in chains that associate within a wall. Cell walls contribute to the mechanical integrity of the tissue and maintain the arrangement of cells around intercellular spaces and vascular bundles. Much wall material remains present even in fallen leaves. Extensive degradation of all walls at an early stage could undermine the organization of the tissue and disrupt transport pathways. A considerable decrease in photosynthetic protein content can occur without a corresponding decrease in every structural polymer. The chemical accessibility and functional importance of a pool both influence its potential contribution to nutrient recovery.

Other cellular components are also not equally dispensable. The plasma membrane is needed to regulate exchanges with the surroundings; mitochondria help supply energy; vascular tissues carry substances between organs. Their function during much of senescence makes the remaining organization of the leaf important to the plant. A leaf is not an inert bag that releases soluble material as soon as its contents are hydrolysed. Until the terminal stage it is an organized tissue in which catabolism is coupled to cellular homeostasis and controlled movement of solutes.

The fate of a recovered atom should be distinguished from the fate of its original molecule. A carbon atom that began in a fatty acid could eventually be released as carbon dioxide during respiration. A nitrogen atom initially found in a leaf protein could reach a seed as part of an amino acid that is different from the original residue. A phosphate group removed from a nucleotide could enter ATP and later another phosphorylated compound. These changes mean that a simple measurement of the disappearance of one biochemical class cannot establish the ultimate destination of all its constituents. Tracer experiments and measurements of total element content provide complementary information.

7. The path from a senescing mesophyll cell to a growing sink

A recovered solute is useful to another organ only if it leaves the source and enters the transport network. The mesophyll is separated from the vascular tissues by other living cells and by a network of extracellular and intracellular spaces. Solutes can move between cells through plasmodesmata, which provide cytoplasmic connections, or cross membranes to enter and leave the apoplast. The apoplast comprises extracellular spaces and walls outside the plasma membrane. The symplast comprises the interconnected cytoplasms of living cells. The contributions of these routes depend on the anatomical arrangement and the transport properties of the plant.

Phloem is the major long-distance pathway for many organic solutes that move from source to sink. Its conducting cells in flowering plants are sieve elements, functionally supported by associated companion cells. The sieve elements provide a low-resistance route for bulk movement of phloem sap, and living companion cells help maintain and load the transport system. In an old source leaf, amino acids and sugars generated in the lamina must become available to the phloem through appropriate unloading and loading steps at the cellular level. Xylem, by contrast, is primarily involved in the long-distance delivery of water and dissolved mineral nutrients from the root, although transfers between xylem and phloem and redistribution of particular solutes also occur.

The movement of phloem sap can be understood through a pressure difference between source and sink regions. Accumulation of osmotically active solutes in source phloem tends to draw water into it, frequently from nearby xylem. This increases turgor pressure. Removal of solutes at the sink reduces the tendency for water to remain in that part of the pathway. A hydrostatic pressure difference along the conducting tissue can drive bulk flow. The individual molecules are not each propelled along the entire length of a sieve tube by a separate motor. Loading and unloading can involve metabolically dependent transport, whereas long-distance flow is driven primarily by the pressure gradient.

A senescing leaf may experience declining photosynthesis but continue to export organic carbon. Sugars can derive from ongoing carbon fixation or from degradation of pre-existing carbohydrate reserves, including starch. The amount of sucrose available for export reflects both production and local consumption. Some carbon must be retained for respiration and other metabolic activities. A measurement of low net carbon dioxide uptake, therefore, cannot be taken as proof that sucrose export has completely ceased. Conversely, abundant soluble sugar in the tissue does not guarantee rapid export if its movement into phloem or consumption by sinks is limited.

Amino acids in the cytosol of source cells face an analogous transport problem. Their presence is a necessary precursor for some forms of nitrogen transfer, but it is not the final event. Movement into the vascular system, maintenance of the long-distance path and uptake by recipient tissues must all occur. An amino acid transporter can contribute to loading or retrieval of solutes from the apoplast. In a simplified source cell, an increase in amino acid production accompanied by decreased export capacity could cause the local pool to rise. In that situation, a high free amino acid concentration is not evidence for greater delivery to the seeds. It may instead indicate an imbalance between production and removal.

A growing seed is not simply an empty container into which solutes diffuse until equilibrium is reached. Seed tissues undergo rapid biosynthesis and differentiate into structures with distinct developmental functions. Imported nitrogen can be incorporated into storage proteins or other compounds, lowering the concentration of the free substrates available for metabolism. Imported carbon can contribute to starch, lipid, cell wall and other pools. The rate of utilization, unloading capacity and size of the tissue influence its demand for assimilates. A well-connected sink with active metabolism can receive substantial material over a prolonged period.

Sink strength is commonly understood as a combination of the size of a sink and its metabolic or transport activity per unit size. A large organ with little ongoing growth may have a different demand from a small rapidly developing organ. Two grains may differ in their competitive access to the resources of the plant if one has a stronger capacity for growth or a more favorable vascular connection. This does not mean that the plant distributes all resources in direct proportion to sink mass. Connectivity, transport limitations and the identity of the nutrient also matter. The concept provides a framework for explaining how an organ's developmental state influences the supply it receives.

Removing a reproductive sink is a useful experimental way to examine source–sink relations, but interpretation requires care. If seeds are removed, less nitrogen can accumulate in them simply because the destination has been removed. The plant may redirect resources to vegetative structures or storage organs, and the removal may change hormonal signals and photosynthetic feedback. Leaves might retain nutrients longer or respond differently depending on the species. An experiment showing delayed yellowing after pod removal can support a relationship between reproduction and senescence, but it does not establish that a single signal from the pod controls all aspects of leaf aging.

In a perennial, buds, stems and roots may serve as storage sinks at the end of a growing season. An aging leaf can transfer nutrients to these structures before it falls. Those nutrients may be remobilized again when new growth begins. This creates a temporal dimension to the source–sink relationship. The sink for a nutrient during autumn can become a source for the same nutrient during spring. The relevant comparison is not simply between old leaves and seeds but between disposable organs and persistent or developing structures that will support later stages of the life cycle.

A plant can acquire mineral nitrogen from soil during the same interval in which it recovers nitrogen from aging leaves. The nitrogen content of a developing organ represents the combined effects of these and potentially other sources. If a mature seed contains more nitrogen than was present in one particular old leaf, that does not imply that the measurements violate conservation. Other leaves, stems, roots and new uptake may contribute. To estimate the share that came from a defined earlier pool, an investigator can label that pool and follow its distribution. Whole-plant analysis must account for the possibility that resources are passing through temporary storage tissues rather than moving in one uninterrupted step from a specific leaf to a seed.

The vascular connection also explains the importance of timing. If an organ loses its connection to the living plant before its mobilizable nutrients have been exported, direct recovery of those nutrients through the original phloem becomes impossible. Some material can later be recovered indirectly from decomposed litter through the soil, but that is a different process, dependent on ecological nutrient cycling and root uptake. Thus delaying physical separation for a suitable period can allow further remobilization in some contexts. Retention of a completely nonfunctional organ, however, has no inevitable advantage, especially when environmental hazards favor its removal.

8. Measuring nutrient remobilization by mass balance

The ability to describe nutrient movement qualitatively becomes much more powerful when it is linked to a quantitative balance. For an isolated chemical element, atoms are not created or destroyed in ordinary biological reactions. Carbon can be transferred to atmospheric carbon dioxide, nitrogen can leave an organ in organic compounds and phosphorus can move to another tissue, but the number of atoms must be accounted for within the chosen system boundary. The boundary could be a leaf, a group of leaves or the whole plant. A calculation must state which boundary has been chosen before describing a gain or loss.

Nutrient content denotes the total quantity of an element in a defined tissue. Concentration expresses the quantity relative to another measured quantity, commonly dry mass. If the nitrogen concentration in a leaf is C milligrams per gram of dry mass and its dry mass is M grams, the product C times M gives the total milligrams of nitrogen in the leaf. This relationship follows directly from cancellation of units: milligrams per gram multiplied by grams gives milligrams. It provides a straightforward means of converting concentration data into the quantity required for a mass balance.

A fresh leaf loses water as it wilts. Its fresh mass can decrease substantially even when its nitrogen content has not changed. Concentration expressed per gram of fresh mass can therefore increase because the denominator has fallen. Dry mass is more appropriate for many comparisons of nutrient pools because it excludes most variation in tissue water content. Yet dry mass is not constant either. Carbon dioxide released during respiration and substances exported to other organs reduce the dry mass of a senescing tissue. Some tissue may also be physically lost. Calculations based solely on nutrient concentration per dry mass consequently remain vulnerable to denominator effects.

Let the total amount of nutrient in the mature green leaf be N initial and the total amount in the same defined leaf cohort at the end of the senescence period be N final. The apparent amount withdrawn from that pool is N initial minus N final. Dividing by N initial converts the change to a fraction of the initial pool. Multiplication by 100 expresses the fraction as a percentage. This defines an apparent resorption efficiency when the chosen stages and sampling are appropriate. The calculation measures net disappearance from the sampled tissue, not automatically successful arrival at a particular sink.

Suppose a mature leaf contains 20 milligrams of nitrogen and the attached senescent leaf contains 8 milligrams immediately before it falls. The net reduction is 12 milligrams, which corresponds to 60 percent of the original amount. If 9 milligrams of previously present nitrogen are recovered in seeds and 2 milligrams in roots, 11 milligrams of the original pool have been identified in those destinations. The remaining difference of 1 milligram cannot be assigned without further evidence. It might be in other tissues, reflect uncollected material or be within measurement uncertainty. The calculation demonstrates how leaf resorption and seed recovery answer related but distinct questions.

Now consider what happens if only concentration is measured. A mature leaf has a dry mass of 1.00 gram and contains 20 milligrams of nitrogen per gram. The senescent leaf has a dry mass of 0.60 gram and contains 10 milligrams of nitrogen per gram. Its total nitrogen has declined from 20 milligrams to 6 milligrams. A concentration-only calculation would suggest that half the nitrogen has disappeared. The content-based calculation instead gives a decline of 14 milligrams, or 70 percent. The difference arises because dry mass was also removed from the original leaf. This hypothetical example illustrates why the dry mass loss correction discussed by Vergutz and colleagues is necessary when comparable concentration data are used to estimate resorption.

The correction can be derived without introducing an additional biological assumption. Write the green concentration as Cg, the green dry mass as Mg, the senescent concentration as Cs and the senescent dry mass as Ms. The final-to-initial content ratio is CsMs divided by CgMg. By rearranging the factors, it can be expressed as the ratio Cs/Cg multiplied by Ms/Mg. The latter quantity is the dry mass correction factor. When senescence reduces dry mass, this factor is less than one. Subtracting the corrected ratio from one yields the fraction of the original content that is no longer in the leaf. A factor of one corresponds to unchanged dry mass.

Concentration can sometimes increase despite a net decrease of nutrient content. For example, a leaf that initially contains 10 milligrams of an element in 1 gram of dry matter has a concentration of 10 milligrams per gram. If its mass falls to 0.4 gram while 6 milligrams of the element remain, the concentration rises to 15 milligrams per gram. The total amount has nevertheless fallen by 4 milligrams. An examination problem may exploit the tendency to equate rising concentration with net import. The direction of change in a concentration is determined by the relative changes of both the numerator and the denominator.

The difference between resorption efficiency and resorption proficiency is another useful distinction. Efficiency describes the proportion of a starting pool withdrawn, whereas proficiency is often expressed as the nutrient concentration remaining in the senesced tissue. Consider two leaves that end with the same nitrogen concentration. If their initial concentrations or changes in mass differ, the fraction of their initial nitrogen that they have withdrawn may differ as well. Neither measurement makes the other unnecessary. A high efficiency can still leave a substantial residual concentration if the initial tissue is especially nutrient-rich. A low final concentration does not indicate the magnitude of the initial investment.

Several assumptions determine how reliably field data can be interpreted. The sampled green and senescent leaves must be comparable in identity and developmental history. A young expanding leaf cannot necessarily substitute for the mature stage, because its nutrient content is changing through growth and import. Fallen leaves collected after rain may have lost soluble materials through leaching after separation. Insects may remove portions of the lamina. These losses are not equivalent to transport into living sinks. Where possible, investigators sample attached leaves near abscission and account for differences in leaf mass, size and tissue loss.

A negative apparent resorption efficiency is mathematically possible if the final nutrient content exceeds the initial content. It may reflect continued accumulation, redistribution into the leaf, contamination, differences between sampled leaf populations or an error in measurement. It should not be silently replaced by zero merely because the intended biological process is export. The observation calls for investigation of the balance and the assumptions. Likewise, a calculated efficiency greater than 100 percent is impossible for a simple balance with nonnegative final content. Such a result would indicate inappropriate background subtraction, a data-processing error or an inconsistent definition of the measured pools.

9. Which mineral nutrients can be recovered?

The ability of a plant to recover a mineral element depends partly on its chemical form and partly on its mobility within the plant. Nitrogen incorporated in proteins can be converted into transportable organic compounds, and phosphorus in many compounds can be released through hydrolysis. Potassium is often present in plant cells as a soluble ion rather than being incorporated permanently into the carbon skeleton of a macromolecule. These properties help explain why substantial amounts of several elements can move out of a senescing organ, although the fractions differ widely among plants and growing conditions.

Calcium provides a useful contrasting case. Calcium ions help stabilize cell walls, participate in signaling and contribute to the properties of membranes and extracellular structures. Much of the calcium delivered to leaves moves through the transpiration stream. Calcium has relatively restricted mobility in the phloem of many plants. Once it has accumulated in a mature transpiring leaf, it may be much less readily redistributed to a developing low-transpiration organ than nitrogen in suitable organic forms. Consequently, calcium can remain at relatively high levels in old leaves and litter even when substantial nitrogen has been recovered. This is a general tendency rather than a claim that no calcium can ever be redistributed.

Magnesium is essential in chlorophyll and also participates in many enzyme reactions and the chemistry of nucleotide complexes. Removal of magnesium from chlorophyll releases it from the pigment's coordination environment, but this chemical release is not identical to phloem transport. The free or re-associated ion still has to enter pathways that support redistribution. Its fate may differ from that of the remaining linear pigment products. Students should follow the metal and the organic ring separately when interpreting the chlorophyll degradation pathway. The same principle applies to other cofactors: breakdown of a protein complex may free a metal that is handled differently from the constituent amino acids.

Potassium contributes to osmotic regulation, electrical properties of membranes and enzyme activity. If the concentration of potassium in an old leaf decreases during the later stages of development, movement to other organs is a possible explanation. However, soluble ions can also escape from damaged tissues or be washed from fallen leaves. The distinction between regulated export and passive leakage is particularly important for highly soluble elements. The use of an intact attached leaf, measurements of ion retention and sampling of recipient tissues can help resolve the mechanism responsible for a decline.

The concept of mineral mobility also helps distinguish physiological disorders from normal senescence. A deficiency of a mobile nutrient can first become obvious in older leaves if available material is withdrawn to supply younger growing tissue. Nitrogen deficiency often produces chlorosis in older foliage in this way. A deficiency of an element that is poorly redistributed can affect young tissues more strongly, as occurs in many cases of iron deficiency. Such patterns are useful diagnostic tendencies, not infallible rules. Root damage, environmental conditions and genetic differences can change nutrient uptake and distribution, and the age distribution of visible symptoms must be supported by other evidence.

A leaf growing under limited nutrient supply can differ from a well-supplied leaf even before senescence begins. Its mature nutrient content, leaf area, thickness and relative allocation to different proteins may all change. A comparison of the final nutrient content between the two treatments is therefore insufficient to establish which recovered more efficiently. The initial pool must be measured for each treatment. A nutrient-poor leaf that ends with very little nitrogen may have started with little nitrogen; its final low content alone is not evidence of a higher withdrawal fraction.

Nutrient recovery can benefit a plant under conditions in which external supply is scarce or seasonal. However, the recovery process has costs, and the optimum time for initiating it depends on the remaining potential for photosynthesis and the needs of other organs. Leaves growing under plentiful soil nitrogen may retain substantial nitrogen in late life because continued carbon assimilation is beneficial or because the initial pool is large. In another species, early withdrawal may be favored when growing seeds have high demand. These patterns are better understood by comparing resource budgets and developmental schedules than by proposing one universal nutrient resorption percentage.

10. Tracing the origin and destination of recovered nutrients

Changes in the total nitrogen content of a leaf reveal the net change in its nitrogen pool but cannot, on their own, establish the history of the atoms in another organ. This difficulty is particularly evident in a plant that continues to take up nitrate during reproduction. Even if the amount of nitrogen in the seed increases while the amount in the leaves decreases, some of the nitrogen delivered to the seed may have come from recent uptake. Conversely, a leaf could receive some newly acquired nitrogen while releasing nitrogen that was incorporated during vegetative growth. A net balance can conceal simultaneous fluxes in opposing directions.

Stable isotopes provide a way to distinguish the histories of chemically similar atoms. Most naturally occurring nitrogen is nitrogen-14, whereas a small proportion is nitrogen-15. Both isotopes can enter the normal chemistry of plant nitrogen metabolism. An investigator can enrich a nutrient supply in nitrogen-15 during a selected period, allowing the plant to incorporate the label into its tissues. Subsequent exposure to nutrient containing the normal isotope proportions provides a chase. The distribution of excess nitrogen-15 later in development gives information about the redistribution of nitrogen acquired during the labeling period.

The experiment must distinguish isotope abundance from the amount of labeled nitrogen. If an organ contains a large quantity of total nitrogen but a small fraction of nitrogen-15, it can still contain more labeled atoms than a small organ with high isotope enrichment. One useful measure is atom percent excess, obtained by subtracting the background atom percentage from the observed atom percentage. Multiplying the excess fraction by the total amount of nitrogen in moles gives the amount of excess nitrogen-15, expressed in moles of nitrogen atoms. The calculation is most straightforward when all measurements use consistent units and the isotope background is known.

Imagine that two plants have equal amounts of excess nitrogen-15 at the beginning of a chase. At harvest, one has a greater fraction of its recovered label in the seeds than the other. This can indicate a difference in redistribution of the previously acquired nitrogen. However, the comparison becomes misleading if one plant has lost a large fraction of its total label in uncollected tissues. Both the absolute quantity of excess label recovered in seeds and the total recovery of the label across the plant should be examined. A large seed fraction can result from a reduction of other measured pools rather than an increase in seed delivery.

A pulse administered to the whole plant during vegetative growth labels several organs, including roots and stems. The appearance of that label in seeds subsequently establishes a contribution from earlier acquired nitrogen, but it does not establish that all the label came directly from leaves. To investigate a more specific source, a study needs an appropriately restricted label or a fuller analysis of the changes in the labeled pools of candidate organs. Such evidence may reveal that a stem temporarily stores nitrogen released from foliage before a portion of that nitrogen is delivered to seeds. A tracer records atom movement, not necessarily the complete route traversed between successive sampling points.

The interpretation of a chase also depends on the chemical availability of the label. Newly incorporated nitrogen may be concentrated in soluble pools soon after a pulse. With a longer interval, it may become distributed among proteins and other substances. A label introduced immediately before senescence may therefore track a different set of compounds from a label introduced early in vegetative growth. To test recycling of previously constructed proteins, the investigator must allow the label to enter the relevant protein pool or directly determine its distribution among biochemical fractions before the chase begins.

Changes in isotope enrichment can result from dilution as well as export. If a labeled leaf continues to receive nitrogen with a lower abundance of nitrogen-15, its isotope enrichment may decline while the absolute amount of excess label stays almost constant. This is another example of the distinction between a ratio and an amount. Measuring only atom percent can produce a mistaken impression of rapid label loss. The necessary calculation multiplies enrichment above background by total nitrogen content at each stage. In a well-designed experiment, measurements of isotope enrichment are always interpreted alongside pool size.

An analogous approach can be used to follow carbon. A plant supplied with labeled carbon dioxide during a defined interval incorporates labeled carbon into organic compounds through photosynthesis. After the labeling period, carbon originally fixed by a particular tissue can be detected in metabolites or other organs. Carbon tracing raises an additional complication: respiration can release a significant amount of labeled carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. A decline in labeled carbon in a leaf can therefore reflect respiratory loss as well as export. To distinguish these outcomes, the experimental boundary and possible destinations must be explicitly defined.

It is useful to compare three different types of evidence. A change in total nutrient content supports a net balance statement. A tracer experiment supports a statement about the movement of a previously labeled pool. A genetic perturbation can help establish whether a specific molecular process contributes to the observed movement. No one of these techniques automatically substitutes for the others. A convincing demonstration of nutrient remobilization links the biochemical release of material, the change in source pool size, the appearance of that material in a destination and, where possible, the consequences of perturbing the proposed machinery.

The same logic guides interpretation of seed nitrogen concentration. A plant with defective nutrient recycling might produce fewer seeds, each with a normal or even increased nitrogen concentration. A plant producing many large seeds might deliver more nitrogen in total while having a lower nitrogen concentration per gram of grain. Seed number, individual seed dry mass and nutrient concentration therefore determine different aspects of the reproductive outcome. A single measurement of grain protein percentage cannot reveal whether the efficiency of transfer from aging leaves increased. It must be interpreted in relation to the total reproductive biomass and nitrogen budget.

11. Functional decline, stay-green phenotypes and the timing of senescence

A healthy-looking green leaf and a physiologically functional green leaf are often the same organ, but they need not be. This distinction has become especially important in plant genetics and crop biology, where retention of green pigment late in development is referred to as a stay-green phenotype. The phenotype is readily scored by visual inspection or pigment measurements, yet it can arise through different mechanisms. In one plant the whole course of leaf functional decline may be delayed. In another, loss of chlorophyll may be impaired even though most photosynthetic functions have already deteriorated.

A functional stay-green phenotype retains useful physiological activity for longer than a comparable normally senescing leaf under the tested conditions. For example, a plant may maintain carbon dioxide assimilation for a longer period during grain filling. This can contribute additional assimilated carbon to the developing grains if sufficient water, light and sink demand are available. A cosmetic stay-green phenotype retains green pigment without equivalent retention of photosynthetic capacity. A defect in pigment breakdown can produce the latter outcome, especially when other aspects of chloroplast dismantling are not delayed to the same extent.

Thomas and Howarth's classification of stay-green phenotypes illustrates why the duration of visible greenness is not itself a mechanism. The onset of decline can be shifted, the rate of decline can change, or pigment may remain after physiological function has been lost. One can recognize these possibilities by plotting separate measures of chlorophyll and photosynthesis against time. A later onset with a similar subsequent rate would be distinguished from an unchanged onset followed by slower decline. An endpoint observation cannot make that distinction because it provides no information about the shape of the trajectory.

Net photosynthetic rate is commonly expressed as micromoles of carbon dioxide assimilated per square metre of leaf area per second. It is a net measurement because the exchange measured in the light reflects carbon fixation together with respiratory and photorespiratory losses. A fair comparison of genotypes requires control of light intensity, temperature, carbon dioxide concentration and water status. A leaf sampled under high light cannot automatically be compared with one measured under shade. A useful time course measures leaves of comparable developmental history under standardized measurement conditions, while separately documenting their actual environments during growth.

Chlorophyll fluorescence supplies information about the functioning of photosystem II. In a dark-adapted leaf, a weak measuring light can establish the minimum fluorescence, F zero, when most functional reaction centres are open. A saturating pulse closes the centres transiently and produces the maximal fluorescence, F m. Variable fluorescence, F v, is F m minus F zero. The ratio F v divided by F m is used as an estimate of the maximum photochemical efficiency of photosystem II under the specified measurement conditions. The value is dimensionless because the fluorescence signals have the same units.

A reduction in this ratio can indicate stress or loss of photosystem II function, but it is not a direct measurement of carbon assimilation. A leaf may retain relatively high maximum photochemical efficiency while assimilating little carbon dioxide because its stomata are closed or its carbon metabolism is limited. The fluorescence measurement also does not directly quantify the rate of amino acid export. In an analytical question, the strongest interpretation often comes from reading multiple datasets together rather than assigning all measurements to a single vaguely defined property called leaf health.

Imagine three genotypes examined at a late developmental stage. The first retains 30 percent of its mature chlorophyll and 20 percent of its original net assimilation. The second retains 80 percent of its chlorophyll and 65 percent of its assimilation. The third retains 85 percent of its chlorophyll but only 18 percent of its assimilation. The second is a candidate for functional stay-green, while the third is a candidate for cosmetic stay-green. The word candidate is important because a single late measurement cannot establish the timing of onset or the total duration of useful activity. It also cannot reveal the cause of the differences.

To assess a possible agronomic benefit, the assimilation rate should be integrated over the period during which the leaf contributes to the canopy. A plant maintaining a high instantaneous rate for a short interval might provide less total carbon than another plant maintaining a moderate rate for a longer interval. Leaf area and the light received by the leaf also influence the contribution to the whole plant. Retention of functional leaf area during grain filling may be beneficial, but the benefit is contingent on available resources and the ability of the reproductive organs to use the products of photosynthesis.

Functional retention of green leaves may alter nitrogen remobilization as well as carbon gain. Continued operation of the photosynthetic apparatus requires retention of some nitrogen-rich proteins. A genotype that maintains photosynthesis for a longer period might postpone the release of this nitrogen. If the final stage of recycling is sufficiently effective, the seeds may still receive adequate nitrogen. If the reproductive period ends before much nitrogen is recovered, delayed senescence could reduce the contribution of vegetative reserves to grain protein. Additional uptake of nitrogen from soil may compensate in some cases. This tradeoff helps explain why the best senescence timing cannot be inferred from greenness alone.

An informative experiment comparing normal and stay-green plants would include measurements before, during and after the expected onset of senescence. At each stage, it would determine pigment content, net assimilation, photosystem performance, tissue viability, leaf nitrogen content and the nitrogen appearing in developing seeds. If a mutation specifically affects a chlorophyll-degrading enzyme, measuring the appropriate intermediates could connect the phenotype to its biochemical mechanism. Comparison with a genetically complemented plant would further strengthen the causal inference. The experiment would distinguish changes in pigment turnover from changes in the coordinated decline of the whole organ.

The interpretation should also take into account the possibility of secondary effects. A mutant that accumulates a photoreactive pigment intermediate could display increased tissue damage in bright light. In that case, pigment persistence and loss of function might arise from interruption of safe catabolism rather than from ordinary aging. A difference in photosynthesis between the mutant and the wild type may partly reflect this secondary injury. Examining the conditions under which the injury occurs can help separate pigment chemistry, phototoxicity and developmental timing.

12. Petals and flowers: senescence after a different developmental task

A foliage leaf and a petal are both organs produced by a plant, but their major functions differ. The foliage leaf contributes to photosynthetic carbon acquisition, whereas a petal often contributes to the attraction or guidance of pollinators. Petals may possess pigments, patterns, scent-producing capacities and specialized epidermal surfaces. Their developmental lifetime is related to the period during which these structures are useful for reproduction. It would therefore be inappropriate to define floral senescence exclusively as the loss of chlorophyll or as the decline of carbon assimilation.

The opening of a flower depends on changes in the size and arrangement of its organs, including petal expansion. Expanded petals often rely on water uptake, osmotic solutes and the maintenance of turgor for their display. Turgor is the pressure exerted by the cell contents against the cell wall. A petal can wilt when its cells lose turgor, but this observation alone does not establish that its cells have entered an irreversible terminal stage. A detached flower deprived of water can wilt rapidly, and some of the change may be reversible on restoration of water supply. Developmental petal senescence involves additional biochemical and structural changes that cannot be inferred from wilting alone.

After the period of floral display, the plant may no longer require the same investment in the petal's structure and metabolism. In pollinated flowers, reproductive development can shift towards the ovary and its developing seeds. Substances released from petals can be redistributed in species where the relevant transport connections remain active. The degree of recovery differs among species, and it need not approach the amount of nitrogen withdrawn from a nitrogen-rich photosynthetic leaf. The comparison depends on the composition of the starting organ and the interval during which its cellular constituents can be made available for transport.

Petal senescence often includes degradation of proteins, nucleic acids and membranes. A loss of turgor may become especially conspicuous because the visible form of a petal depends on the hydration and mechanical properties of its cells. In some flowers, changes in pigment composition or cellular pH alter colour during aging. In others the dominant visual event is shrinking, wilting or the shedding of petals. A petal can thus undergo marked senescence without passing through the same sequence of green-to-yellow colour changes that is familiar in foliage leaves.

Ethylene provides a useful example of a developmental signal affecting floral longevity. It is a gaseous plant hormone, and certain flowers respond strongly to ethylene by undergoing accelerated petal senescence. In ethylene-sensitive flowers such as carnation, pollination and floral developmental changes can be associated with enhanced ethylene signaling and changes in the timing of petal decline. The relevant response depends on the particular species, tissue and developmental stage. Many other flowers senesce with much less dependence on ethylene. The correct conclusion is that ethylene has an important role in some systems, not that all petals must senesce through the same ethylene-dependent mechanism.

The difference in hormone sensitivity can be investigated by comparing flowers of two species at equivalent stages after opening. One group from each species is exposed to a controlled ethylene treatment, and another receives the same environmental conditions without the added ethylene. Changes in the time to visible wilting, membrane integrity and biochemical indicators of senescence can then be measured. A faster decline in one species supports its responsiveness under the tested conditions. Lack of response in the other species must be interpreted cautiously: the applied concentration, exposure duration and developmental competence of the tissue may influence the result. Independent evidence is needed before concluding that the hormone has no role under any circumstances.

Pollination is another useful experimental variable. A pollinated flower and an unpollinated flower on the same type of plant can be followed through their subsequent development. If pollination consistently accelerates petal decline, there is a relationship between completion of a reproductive event and the timing of petal senescence. The observation does not establish by itself which signal moves between floral organs or where that signal is perceived. An experiment using a signal-perception mutant, or carefully comparing the timing of hormonal changes and petal responses, can help identify the causal link. The detailed signaling pathways are a separate question from the organ-level outcome.

It is important to distinguish the fate of petals from the fate of the whole flower. Following fertilization, the ovary of many angiosperms continues growing as the fruit develops. Petals and stamens may wither or be shed, whereas the ovules develop into seeds. Sepals can remain attached or change function in some species. The flower is therefore not a collection of tissues that all share one obligatory terminal date. Different floral organs have distinct developmental roles, and the completion of the role of one organ can occur while another begins a phase of growth.

The difference between a petal and a leaf also provides an opportunity to think about resource allocation in ecological terms. Extending the life of an attractive petal can increase the time available for pollination if pollinators are scarce. Once the appropriate reproductive event has occurred, maintaining the petal may offer less benefit in some species. However, floral display can contribute to attraction of pollinators to other flowers on the same plant, and reproductive schedules vary. A simple claim that rapid petal senescence is always beneficial after pollination ignores the organization of the entire reproductive system.

At the cellular level, vacuolar and other degradative pathways contribute to the dismantling of petal cells in several studied species. As discussed by van Doorn and Woltering, the timing and organization of this process vary among flowers. An important experimental question is whether a given petal cell remains viable while proteins and other macromolecules are being mobilized. Measurements of respiration and membrane integrity made before and after visible wilting can help distinguish the active phase of decline from later irreversible damage. A cell whose membrane organization has completely failed cannot maintain the same selective transport processes as an intact aging cell.

The economic significance of petal senescence is visible in cut-flower horticulture. The desirable display life of a harvested flower depends not only on its natural developmental program but also on hydration, stored carbohydrate, vascular function and sensitivity to environmental signals. A harvested flower no longer has the same source–sink relationships as a flower attached to the whole plant. Consequently, interventions that prolong the visible quality of a cut flower cannot automatically be interpreted as enhancing seed development or nutrient recycling in an intact plant. These are distinct biological and practical objectives.

13. Fruit maturation and senescence are related but distinguishable

A fruit is a structure associated with the development of the ovary, often with contributions from other floral tissues. It supports seed development and can contribute to seed protection or dispersal. In fleshy fruits, the process of ripening involves coordinated changes in firmness, colour, soluble compounds, aroma and other properties. These changes can promote dispersal by animals, but they are not simply a description of all fruit cells dying. A ripe fruit may contain metabolically active tissues that continue to carry out many regulated reactions.

A green developing fruit can contain photosynthetic chloroplasts. In some fruits these plastids differentiate into chromoplasts during ripening, with a different pigment composition and internal organization. The disappearance of chlorophyll may be accompanied by the synthesis or accumulation of carotenoids. This change has a biochemical connection to chlorophyll removal during leaf senescence, but the developmental result differs. A senescing foliage leaf often reduces its investment in photosynthetic organelles while recovering resources. A ripening fruit may actively construct new pigment-containing structures and acquire properties associated with its role in seed dispersal.

The distinction is illustrated by a tomato changing from green to red. The disappearance of green pigment and accumulation of red carotenoids are not evidence that all metabolism has ceased. Many biochemical activities associated with ripening require viable cells. After ripening, continued aging, degradation and loss of tissue integrity can become increasingly prominent. Thus ripening and later fruit senescence can overlap and interact, but they should not be treated as identical developmental phases merely because both involve changes in pigment or firmness.

Ethylene is a major regulator of ripening in climacteric fruits, which typically display a characteristic increase in respiration associated with ripening. Not all fruits show the same climacteric pattern or the same dependence on ethylene for ripening. Classification of fruit ripening mechanisms belongs principally to plant physiology, but the distinction is important here because a fruit responding to ethylene does not necessarily provide evidence for the same mechanism that controls petal or leaf senescence. The developmental context determines the meaning of the response.

Fruit softening provides a useful example of the difference between altered cell-wall properties and cell death. Changes in pectins and other wall components can modify adhesion between cells and the mechanical properties of the tissue. A fruit may become soft while many cells remain alive. A measurement of firmness therefore cannot be interpreted as a direct measurement of the percentage of dead cells. When a fruit later develops extensive membrane damage or microbial decay, additional changes contribute to the loss of its integrity. To distinguish normal ripening from senescence or pathological deterioration, the history of the fruit and several independent physiological measurements are needed.

Not all fruits are fleshy. A dry fruit may acquire mechanical properties that facilitate the release of seeds rather than becoming attractive to animal dispersers. Tissue drying, cell-wall differentiation and localized separation can be part of its developmental program. An observation of dry, dead tissue in a mature fruit does not imply that every step in fruit maturation was a degenerative event. As in the xylem examples from the preceding chapter, loss of living contents in particular tissues may contribute to the acquisition of a mature structural function.

Developing fruits frequently act as strong sinks. Removal or modification of a fruit's sink activity can influence nutrient distribution and the physiology of leaves elsewhere on the plant. In this relationship, it is the aging leaf that may release nitrogen, while a growing fruit or developing seed receives it. The senescence of a fruit after its major developmental functions have been completed is a later and biologically distinct event. The terms source, sink, maturation, ripening and senescence must always be attached to a specified tissue at a specified stage.

Harvest changes the context again. Once a fruit is detached, direct nutrient import from the parent plant ends. A harvested fruit continues to metabolize compounds already present in its tissues, and some fruits can continue ripening after harvest. A postharvest increase in the concentration of a soluble substance need not mean that the substance has been imported from another organ. The result can arise through conversion of internal reserves, water loss or a combination of metabolic and physical changes.

14. Senescence, abscission and terminal cell death

A leaf falling from a tree provides a visually obvious boundary between the life of an attached organ and the fate of the separated material. The separation process itself is called abscission. It usually occurs at a specialized region near the base of an organ, the abscission zone. Cells at this location acquire properties that permit the controlled reduction of adhesion between adjacent tissues. The position of the zone ensures that separation can occur at a defined interface rather than through an arbitrary fracture of the plant.

The middle lamella between neighboring plant cells contains pectin-rich material that contributes to their adhesion. Changes in this material and other wall components can weaken the connection between cells. Hydrolytic and wall-modifying enzymes, including polygalacturonases and some glucanases, participate in separation in many abscission systems. Their expression and secretion at an appropriate site can help create a separation layer while the rest of the plant remains intact. This is a localized developmental process, not the simultaneous dissolution of all cell walls in the leaf or flower.

After detachment, the parent plant must maintain an effective surface at the exposed site. Depending on the species and organ, protective changes can develop in the tissue remaining on the plant. These changes help reduce exposure to water loss and microbial invasion. Formation or modification of a protective region is conceptually distinct from weakening the separation layer, even when both occur in the same developmental sequence. The organization of the interface explains why natural shedding can occur with far less disruption of the parent tissue than uncontrolled mechanical tearing.

The roles of senescence and abscission can be separated by considering four possible observations. A yellow senescing leaf may remain attached because its abscission zone has not become active. A green leaf may be shed under particular environmental conditions or in certain genetic backgrounds. A petal can wilt and remain on the flower for a time. Another petal can separate while it still retains much of its original appearance. In all of these cases, the visual and biochemical condition of the organ differs from the mechanical state of its attachment. Abscission and senescence can be coordinated, but neither can be diagnosed solely by the occurrence of the other.

Auxin and ethylene influence abscission in many familiar systems, but the response depends on the identity and stage of the organ. A decrease in auxin-related signaling from an aging organ can alter the competence of the abscission zone to respond to other signals. Ethylene can promote separation in many contexts, while other regulatory pathways can also contribute. The detailed hormonal control of developmental timing belongs in the next chapter and plant physiology. Here the relevant consequence is that localized wall remodeling can be activated separately from much of the biochemical decline occurring elsewhere in the organ.

A mutation that prevents the normal activation of separation-zone enzymes could cause old leaves or floral organs to remain attached. If the rest of the senescence program continues, their photosynthesis or other functions may decline on the usual schedule. The persistence of the organ on the plant would then indicate impaired abscission, not necessarily delayed senescence. Conversely, a genetic or environmental change that triggers premature separation may interrupt the time available for nutrient recovery, even if the organ would otherwise remain metabolically functional for longer.

Experiments on abscission should measure the strength of attachment as well as the condition of the organ. The force needed to detach a leaf or petal can provide evidence about the mechanical state of the separation zone. Microscopy can reveal changes at the interface, and measurements of wall-modifying enzyme activity can help identify a biochemical basis. The same organs can be assessed for pigment content, membrane integrity and metabolic function. If the force of attachment decreases before a substantial change in leaf pigmentation, the result supports a temporal difference between separation-zone activation and pigment loss.

Terminal cell death is yet another boundary. During much of senescence, cells retain sufficient integrity to maintain transport and regulated metabolism. Later, disruption of essential membranes and organelles can make the loss of viability irreversible. The timing may differ among cells in the same organ. Some vascular tissues or neighboring regions can retain function while mesophyll cells have already deteriorated considerably. It is therefore inappropriate to assign one exact instant of death to every cell based on a photograph of the whole leaf. A careful study identifies the tissue examined and the particular criterion used to assess viability.

Accidental necrotic injury can also destroy cells and lead to detachment of damaged tissue. Sudden freezing, severe heat or mechanical crushing can cause abrupt loss of membrane integrity without the extended coordinated recovery typical of gradual developmental senescence. Damage can additionally induce regulated responses in adjacent living cells, so a damaged organ may contain regions undergoing different processes at the same time. The relevant distinction is not between every stress response being accidental and every developmental response being perfect; it is between the mechanisms operating in the tissue and the evidence for their temporal organization.

The outcome of abscission places a practical limit on direct nutrient remobilization. Once a leaf has separated, it cannot deliver additional compounds to the plant through the previously intact vascular network. Nutrients remaining in the leaf can enter the soil through litter decomposition and may eventually become available to plants again. That ecological transfer may involve microorganisms and new uptake by roots. It is not the same process as the internal movement of nitrogen from an attached leaf to a developing seed. The distinction between these scales is fundamental for understanding why the timing of senescence relative to abscission matters.

15. Seasonal change, premature decline and the allocation tradeoff

Many deciduous plants exhibit a conspicuous seasonal pattern of leaf senescence. A leaf that functions during a favorable growing period can become less useful as temperature, day length and other environmental variables change. Shedding may reduce the costs or risks of maintaining exposed foliage during a later unfavorable period. Before separation, mobile nutrients can be recovered and stored in persistent tissues. The following season, new foliage can be constructed using resources from storage together with new acquisition. The plant's individual leaves have limited lifetimes even though its meristems and other organs can persist for many years.

Evergreen plants do not retain every leaf indefinitely. They may replace leaves gradually or in distinct cohorts while maintaining some green foliage throughout the year. A leaf of an evergreen species can contain structural investments that support a relatively long functional lifetime. The strategy of maintaining foliage across seasons has benefits and costs that vary with the environment. In contrast, a deciduous plant can avoid some maintenance costs by replacing its foliage at appropriate times. It is misleading to equate evergreen habit with the absence of leaf aging; the difference concerns the timing and distribution of turnover across the whole plant.

Premature senescence occurs when the decline is initiated or accelerated earlier than the expected developmental pattern under a reference condition. Drought, sustained shading and nutrient shortage can contribute in particular contexts. The term premature refers to a comparison with an appropriate baseline rather than an absolute number of days. A plant species adapted to seasonal dryness may have a normal schedule of leaf loss that differs from that of a species growing in a moist environment. An experiment should compare the treatment with a suitable control of the same species and developmental stage.

The relationship between stress and senescence depends partly on stress intensity. Mild or temporary water shortage can cause stomatal closure, reducing photosynthesis while conserving water. If the plant recovers promptly, the episode may not initiate terminal decline. Prolonged or severe stress can promote degradation and earlier loss of leaf function. Extremely severe injury may destroy cells too quickly for efficient recovery. Consequently, a decrease in net assimilation during a short drought is not sufficient evidence of accelerated senescence. Recovery after restoration of favorable conditions, together with the state of cellular structures and the movement of resources, helps identify the process that occurred.

The response to shading illustrates another source of variation. A shaded lower leaf may capture too little light to justify maintenance of its full photosynthetic apparatus. Loss of that apparatus could release nitrogen that can be used by leaves with greater access to light. But shading also produces immediate adjustments in photosynthetic physiology that can occur without senescence. A shaded leaf may acclimate, changing the relative investment in light harvesting and carbon metabolism while remaining functional. To demonstrate senescence rather than acclimation, the investigator must establish a progressive change toward loss of function and remodeling, not simply a difference between leaves growing in different light environments.

Nutrient scarcity can favor recovery of internal reserves, but it can also limit the resources needed for efficient cellular maintenance. A nitrogen-deficient plant may withdraw nitrogen from older leaves while supporting younger tissues. If this process occurs during vegetative growth rather than seed filling, the receiving sinks differ from those in a reproductive plant. The timing of nutrient limitation and the developmental stage of the plant therefore influence both the onset of senescence and the destination of recovered material. A general rule stating that nitrogen deficiency always improves nitrogen resorption efficiency would overlook the possibility that severe deficiency or injury compromises the machinery of recovery.

The central physiological tradeoff can be stated in terms of two benefits. Retaining a functional leaf can allow continued acquisition of carbon. Dismantling part of that leaf can release resources needed elsewhere. The benefits are not necessarily mutually exclusive during early senescence, when some photosynthetic activity persists while protein turnover is increasing. At later stages, however, continued retention of a nitrogen-rich photosynthetic apparatus can delay recovery of that nitrogen. The favorable schedule depends on the contribution of the leaf to current growth, the demands of sinks, future environmental conditions and the cost of maintaining the organ.

This tradeoff is particularly relevant to crops harvested for seeds. Grain yield depends partly on total carbon acquisition and allocation, while seed protein content depends partly on nitrogen acquisition and transfer. Delaying senescence may enhance carbon supply in some genotypes and conditions. Accelerating the transfer of nitrogen can support seed protein accumulation in other contexts. Neither the latest possible onset of senescence nor the earliest possible nutrient withdrawal is an unconditional breeding objective. The relevant objective is the coordinated achievement of functional leaf lifetime, resource recovery and sink development within the actual growing environment.

For plants harvested for leaves rather than seeds, the desirable outcome can differ. Prolonging the appearance and physiological integrity of the edible organ may be valuable after harvest, when the harvested tissue is no longer transferring nutrients to the parent plant. In ornamental flowers, maintenance of petal turgor and appearance may be more important to the consumer than recovery of petal nutrients. These examples demonstrate why applications must be evaluated against their biological or agricultural objective. A phenotype useful for one harvested organ or production system may be unfavorable in another.

The developmental process is therefore best understood as a coordinated transition in the role of an organ. A leaf may progress from an importing young tissue to an exporting photosynthetic tissue and then to a tissue increasingly engaged in releasing earlier investments. A petal may progress from growth to display and then to decline or separation while other floral organs continue their development. A fruit may progress through growth, maturation, ripening in relevant species and later deterioration. These schedules are not identical, but each reveals how the life of a part is related to the changing requirements of the whole plant. The following chapter will examine the age-dependent, hormonal and environmental signals that influence when these transitions occur.

Worked quantitative examples

All numerical datasets in this section are hypothetical teaching examples; units and assumptions are given explicitly.

Example 1. Net resorption with dry mass correction

Green mass 1.00 g, green concentration 20 mg N/g; senescent mass 0.60 g, senescent concentration 10 mg N/g. N₀ = 20 × 1.00 = 20 mg. N₁ = 10 × 0.60 = 6 mg. Resorption = 100(20 − 6)/20 = 70%. The concentration-only estimate is 50%, underestimating withdrawal by 20 percentage points. This is net disappearance, not proof of seed delivery.

Example 2. Concentration rises while content falls

Mature dry mass 1.0 g, nutrient amount 10 mg; final dry mass 0.4 g and nutrient amount 6 mg. Final concentration = 6/0.4 = 15 mg/g; initial concentration = 10 mg/g. Net resorption = 100(10 − 6)/10 = 40%. Concentration rose 50% despite loss of 40% of the original pool.

Example 3. Excess isotope and recovery

Background ¹⁵N is 0.37 atom%; seed enrichment is 1.37 atom% and total seed nitrogen is 2.00 mmol N. Excess fraction = (1.37 − 0.37)/100 = 0.0100. Excess label = 2.00 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mmol = 20 μmol. If 50 μmol excess was recovered in the whole plant, seeds contain 40% of recovered label. If the initial excess was 80 μmol, seeds contain 25% of initial label, and whole-plant recovery is 62.5%.

Example 4. Fluorescence and functional interpretation

Dark-adapted leaf F₀ = 200 arbitrary units; Fm = 1,000 units. Fv = 800, Fv/Fm = 0.80. At a later stage F₀ = 300 and Fm = 600; Fv/Fm = 300/600 = 0.50. Maximum PSII efficiency has fallen, but the measurements alone do not establish carbon assimilation, membrane viability or nitrogen transfer.

Example 5. Grain nitrogen content versus concentration

Plant A has 5 g dry grain at 20 mg N/g; B has 8 g at 15 mg N/g. A contains 100 mg N, B 120 mg N. B has 20% more total grain nitrogen despite 25% lower concentration. The origin of this nitrogen requires tracing or further mass balance.

Example 6. Integrated carbon assimilation

Two leaves of 0.02 m² assimilate at a constant 10 and 6 μmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹ for 4 and 8 hours respectively under hypothetical constant conditions. A = 10 × 0.02 × 14,400 = 2,880 μmol; B = 6 × 0.02 × 28,800 = 3,456 μmol. B fixes 20% more net CO₂ over these intervals. Actual daily net carbon budgets also require dark respiration and variable light.

Answer and reasoning

Revision and mastery

Organ senescence is a coordinated late developmental phase, not a synonym for pigmentation loss, tissue detachment or instantaneous death. Chloroplast remodeling releases important protein and other molecular pools. Safe pigment breakdown proceeds through defined intermediates. Proteolysis, autophagy, metabolism and living transport tissues together allow remobilization, but the amount withdrawn from the source does not by itself establish the destination. Petals, leaves and fruit differ in their original roles and later changes. Abscission localizes separation to a particular interface. The timing of senescence influences the balance between further carbon acquisition and recovery of nutrients.

Essential relationships

N = C × M; resorption (%) = 100[1 − (Cs/Cg)(Ms/Mg)]; excess isotope amount = total nitrogen amount × excess isotope fraction; Fv/Fm = (Fm − F₀)/Fm.
