1. Chromosomes, alleles and sex-dependent transmission

A genetic cross is not simply a calculation involving dominant and recessive symbols. It is an experiment in which the locations of genes determine which alleles can enter particular gametes, while the developmental environment determines how the resulting genotype is expressed. In the earlier study of Mendelian segregation, both parents contributed one allele of an autosomal locus to every diploid offspring. This symmetry may disappear for a locus situated in a region of a sex chromosome that has no counterpart on the other sex chromosome. Conversely, an autosomal locus may be transmitted symmetrically while its phenotype differs between males and females. These are separate explanations for an apparently similar observation: a character occurring more often in one sex than the other. A geneticist must identify the explanation rather than merely describe the frequency difference.

A chromosome is a physical DNA molecule associated with proteins. A locus is a position or region occupied by a gene or marker, and an allele is a particular form of the sequence at that locus. In diploid organisms, autosomes ordinarily occur in homologous pairs. Homologous chromosomes contain corresponding loci, although they may contain different alleles. Sex chromosomes can also form pairs, but their homology need not extend over their entire lengths. For example, the mammalian X and Y share regions that allow pairing and exchange, whereas much of the X has no corresponding Y sequence at the same locus. Genes in this non-pairing portion of the X cannot be treated as though the Y carried an ordinary recessive allele.

Consider a particular non-pseudoautosomal X locus in an XX individual with alleles A and a. This individual is heterozygous at the locus. An XY individual carrying X-a possesses only one copy of this locus and is described as hemizygous. Hemizygous does not mean homozygous recessive. The difference matters because a recessive phenotype may appear when only one recessive allele is present, not because recessiveness disappears, but because a second allele capable of supplying the dominant function is missing. The Y is therefore written as Y rather than Y-a or Y-A. At an autosomal locus, by contrast, a typical XY individual is just as diploid as an XX individual. A male is not haploid simply because he has one X.

Separate the steps in the causal chain. First, decide where the locus resides. Second, establish the parental genotypes and list the gametes allowed by chromosome segregation. Third, identify the zygotic genotypes and their probabilities. Fourth, translate each genotype into a phenotype using the specified dominance, penetrance and sex-specific expression rules. In more complicated questions, a fifth step corrects for viability or for conditioning on a selected subset of offspring. Keeping these stages separate prevents an assumption about expression from being mistaken for an assumption about transmission.

The word sex-linked describes a chromosomal location. In its common teaching usage, it often means X-linked, but Y-linked and Z-linked loci are also sex-chromosome loci. Sex-limited describes a phenotype that is normally expressed in only one sex even though the relevant genotype can be present in both. Sex-influenced describes a phenotype whose expression or dominance relationship depends on sex; the trait may occur in both sexes. These classifications are not logically exclusive in all biological settings. A sex-chromosome gene may itself have restricted tissue expression. In standard genetic problems, however, sex-limited and sex-influenced examples are chosen to illustrate autosomal transmission modified by the physiological context. Neither term alone demonstrates an autosomal or sex-chromosomal location.

A two-stage model of inheritance and expression

One of the most reliable ways to organize a problem about sex-dependent inheritance is to draw two different tables. The first records which alleles are inherited. The second records the phenotype predicted for each inherited genotype in a defined physiological setting. These tables are linked, but they answer different questions. If we confuse them, a change in phenotype may be misinterpreted as a change in gamete segregation. Conversely, a real difference in chromosome transmission may be hidden by combining phenotypically identical genotypes.

To see why this matters, consider two hypothetical populations with an observable phenotype P. In the first, P is caused by a recessive allele at a non-pseudoautosomal X locus. The XX parent is heterozygous and the XY parent carries the functional allele. Every daughter receives a functional paternal X, but half the sons receive a recessive maternal X. Thus the probability of P is one half in sons and zero in daughters. In the second population P is caused by a dominant autosomal allele whose expression is strictly male-limited. A heterozygous individual is crossed with a homozygous recessive individual. Half of each sex inherits the dominant allele, but only males carrying it express P. The two crosses produce identical expectations for sex and phenotype. An investigator who records only the sex and phenotype of these offspring cannot distinguish the proposed mechanisms.

The genotype data differ. In the first population the unaffected daughters may be heterozygous or homozygous for the functional allele, and their recessive allele can only have originated from the mother in the specified cross. In the second population a dominant phenotype allele can be inherited through either parent, and an unaffected daughter may transmit it to half of her offspring. A subsequent cross involving an individual whose genotype is established may be much more informative than a larger sample from the original cross. Alternatively, a molecular marker can be used to determine whether the allele is physically located on an autosome or the X chromosome. The genetic conclusion depends on the combination of transmission evidence and gene location, not on the size of a phenotype-only dataset.

A useful conceptual language for this separation is to define T(G,S) as the probability that a mating produces genotype G and sex-chromosome class S, and E(P|G,S) as the probability of the phenotype given that genotype and context. The expected phenotype probability is obtained by summing T multiplied by E over all relevant genotype and sex classes. If viability differs between classes, an additional survival factor must be included before conditioning on surviving offspring. For an autosomal locus segregating independently of sex chromosomes, T can often be written as the product of genotype probability and sex probability. For a strictly X-linked locus, the joint genotype and sex probabilities ordinarily cannot be factored in the same way because the sex chromosome class determines which alleles may be present.

This formulation also exposes the limits of the terms dominant and recessive. Dominance is not a physical property that causes an allele to enter a particular gamete more often. A dominant allele can segregate at exactly the same rate as a recessive allele. The terms describe how a genotype relates to a phenotype. Hemizygosity concerns the number of copies available for expression and is relevant even when no dominance comparison is possible in that individual. Sex limitation and sex influence modify the expression term in the model; X or Y linkage constrains the transmission term. A single gene can participate in both processes, but distinguishing them analytically makes complex problems easier to solve.

The assumptions of the idealized models in this chapter include regular meiosis, normal chromosome complements unless stated otherwise, random fertilization, and specified genotype-to-phenotype rules. Equal sex ratios are assumed only when the question requires a probability among all offspring and no alternative is given. A model in which eggs and sperm are produced equally need not predict equal numbers of surviving adults if survival depends on sex or genotype. Similarly, a model that predicts a phenotype at maturity need not predict the same observable phenotype in a juvenile. These boundaries are not technicalities; they determine which biological process is being measured.

2. Comparative sex chromosome systems

In an ordinary mammalian XX/XY system, the XX parent produces eggs carrying an X chromosome. The XY parent produces two classes of sperm, those carrying X and those carrying Y. Fusion of the egg with an X-bearing sperm produces an XX zygote; fusion with a Y-bearing sperm produces an XY zygote. Under the simplified assumptions of equal production, fertilization and survival, each zygotic chromosome class has probability one half. The XY parent is described as heterogametic because two different sex-chromosome classes of gametes are made. The XX parent is homogametic with respect to the type of sex chromosome carried. An XX parent may nevertheless make two genetically different classes of X-bearing gametes if heterozygous for a locus.

Birds typically illustrate a different arrangement, the ZZ/ZW system. The male is ZZ and the female ZW. The male produces Z-bearing sperm, whereas the female produces Z-bearing or W-bearing eggs. Consequently a Z-linked allele on a father's Z chromosome can be passed to a daughter that has no corresponding locus on W. It is incorrect to transplant mammalian rules about fathers and sons into this system without changing the chromosome labels and the identity of the heterogametic sex. In both systems the underlying rule is the same: the chromosome received by the offspring constrains the alleles available at the locus.

In some insects the chromosome complement is XX in females and XO in males. The symbol O stands for absence of a second sex chromosome, not for an O chromosome carrying genes. A male produces X-bearing sperm and sperm with no X. The female contributes an X in each egg. The resulting zygotes are XX and XO. For a locus confined to X, an XO male is hemizygous in much the same genetic sense as a mammalian XY male. The sex-determining signals that produce male and female development are not necessarily the same in the two cases.

Drosophila is a useful illustration of why the chromosome transmission system must not be equated with the mechanism of sex determination. The normal fly chromosome complement is XX or XY, but the male developmental pathway is not initiated by a mammalian-style Y-linked SRY signal. The balance of X-linked signals in relation to the autosomal context is central to initiation of the fly sex-determination pathway. The Y is important for normal male fertility, not the ordinary primary choice of sex. A fly XO individual is male and generally sterile; an XXY fly is female in the standard diploid context. Those chromosome constitutions yield different outcomes in humans, which use a distinct developmental system. The labels XX and XY describe a karyotype, while developmental sex is a consequence of the biological mechanisms acting on that karyotype.

The same distinction helps interpret experimental crosses involving sex reversal. If a genetically XX organism is experimentally induced to develop along a male physiological pathway, its chromosomal transmission cannot automatically be inferred from its outward phenotype. Gamete composition and fertility have to be established independently. For most exam calculations the ordinary karyotype, gamete production and phenotype correspondence are explicitly or implicitly assumed. State that assumption when applying a simple rule outside the usual model.

The relationship between sex determination and genetic transmission

The term sex chromosome arose because cytological observations revealed chromosome complements associated with different sexual developmental pathways. However, knowing that a chromosome is transmitted preferentially to one class of offspring does not establish the complete mechanism of sexual differentiation. Sex determination initiates a developmental pathway, whereas sex differentiation involves subsequent tissue formation, gene regulation and physiological responses. Sex linkage concerns the location and transmission of a locus on the chromosomes involved in such systems. A locus influencing eye pigment or skeletal growth may be sex-linked without contributing to the primary determination of sex.

In therian mammals, an important early genetic signal in typical XY development is expression of SRY on Y, which contributes to activation of a testis differentiation pathway including SOX9. Other genetic and developmental factors are also necessary. A different chromosome complement or a change in the activity or location of a sex-determining signal can therefore change the relationship between chromosome constitution and reproductive anatomy. It would be inaccurate to define every gene on Y as a sex-determining gene. Some Y-linked genes contribute to processes such as spermatogenesis, and the absence or altered function of these genes can affect fertility without determining the initial developmental pathway.

Drosophila provides a contrasting genetic strategy. Early X-linked signals influence whether the Sex-lethal gene establishes its female-specific regulatory state in the normal diploid embryo. Once established, the regulatory state participates in a downstream program, including alternative RNA processing, that helps coordinate sexual differentiation. Its regulation also interacts with the choice of dosage compensation machinery. The older textbook X-to-autosome ratio summarizes many classic karyotype experiments; at the molecular level, the timing and amount of X-linked regulatory signals are important, so the ratio should not be interpreted as a universal biochemical counter. For the present node, the relevant experimental prediction is that the Y chromosome is not needed to initiate ordinary male development in a diploid fly, even though it contributes to fertility.

In C. elegans, the common XX developmental form is a hermaphrodite rather than a typical female of a species with separate sexes. XO individuals are male. The identity of the XX developmental form matters when describing a mating or genetic cross. Calling the XX form female may obscure its ability to produce both oocytes and sperm in the normal life cycle. Nonetheless, the general concept of hemizygosity applies to the single X of an XO male. These comparative examples show why one should first identify the organism and its chromosome system before assigning symbols and predicting transmission.

At a non-pseudoautosomal sex-linked locus the distinction between homologous and nonhomologous regions is central. An XX individual can undergo homologous recombination between corresponding X loci in meiosis. An XY individual cannot ordinarily recombine a non-PAR X locus with a corresponding Y locus if that corresponding sequence is absent. It does not follow that every locus on X behaves identically during male meiosis: pseudoautosomal regions have homologous sequence and permit exchange. It also does not follow that every organism with an XX/XY system has the same recombination pattern. In Drosophila males, ordinary meiotic crossing over is absent, including on autosomes. The general recombination framework from 8.A.06 must therefore be applied with organism-specific assumptions.

The evolutionary origin of sex chromosomes helps explain their asymmetric content. A pair of ancestral autosomes may acquire a sex-determining factor and undergo changes in the recombination patterns of nearby regions. Reduced exchange allows the two chromosomes to diverge in sequence and gene content. Loss or modification of genes on one chromosome creates loci present in different copy numbers in different chromosome complements. Shared regions may retain recombination and pairing functions. This is a broad explanatory framework, not a claim that all sex chromosomes arose in exactly the same way or that every species has a degenerated Y or W. The consequence for the genetics problem is that homologous and sex-specific chromosome regions require distinct transmission models.

3. Morgan's reciprocal crosses and chromosome evidence

The white-eye mutation in the fruit fly provided an unusually clear opportunity to connect an observable character to chromosome transmission. In 1910 Thomas Hunt Morgan reported the inheritance of the white-eye character in Drosophila. His historical terminology included sex-limited inheritance, but modern genetic terminology calls this particular white-eye locus X-linked. The difference in terminology is important: white-eyed females can exist, so the character is not restricted to a male physiological environment. The visible phenotype is associated with the transmission of the allele and the availability of a functional allele on another X.

Write X-w+ for an X bearing the wild-type red-eye allele and X-w for an X bearing the recessive white-eye allele. In the idealized cross of a homozygous red-eyed female with a white-eyed male, every egg carries X-w+. Half the sperm carry X-w and half carry Y. All daughters are X-w+ X-w and red-eyed; all sons are X-w+ Y and red-eyed. A casual observation of the first generation might therefore suggest ordinary dominance at an autosomal locus. The difference appears when the F1 female is crossed to the F1 male. The female produces equal numbers of X-w+ and X-w eggs, while the male produces X-w+ and Y sperm.

Enumerate the four equally likely F2 chromosome combinations. X-w+ X-w+ gives a red-eyed female, X-w+ X-w gives another red-eyed female, X-w+ Y gives a red-eyed male, and X-w Y gives a white-eyed male. The aggregate ratio is three red to one white, just as in an ordinary monohybrid cross. Yet the phenotypes are not distributed equally by sex: every white-eyed fly in this ideal F2 is male. This is a warning against collapsing observations into a single phenotype ratio. Recording sex alongside eye colour retains the evidence that distinguishes the two models.

In the reciprocal parental cross, begin with a homozygous white-eyed female and a red-eyed male. Every egg now contains X-w, while sperm contain X-w+ or Y. Daughters inherit X-w+ from the father and X-w from the mother, so they are red-eyed heterozygotes. Sons inherit the maternal X-w and the paternal Y, so they are white-eyed. The reciprocal cross therefore gives a different F1 distribution from the original cross even though the same two eye-colour alleles are represented in the parents. The asymmetry follows the physical route by which the paternal X reaches daughters rather than sons.

These are theoretical predictions under ordinary transmission and complete penetrance; they are not reported historical offspring counts. The experimental strength of the design lies in changing parental sex while retaining the comparison between the wild-type and mutant character. Appropriate controls include verified parental genotypes, controlled mating, accurate scoring of offspring sex and eye phenotype, and attention to differential viability. An autosomal dominant/recessive model with no sex-dependent expression predicts equivalent reciprocal F1 distributions, so the observed association calls for a revised hypothesis. Additional chromosome evidence and later analysis of unusual segregation helped consolidate the chromosome theory. A reciprocal difference by itself is not proof of X linkage, since maternal effects, organelle inheritance and imprinting can also create parental asymmetry. The distinctive test is whether the complete distribution of genotypes and phenotypes follows the predicted X routes across generations.

The reasoning generalizes beyond flies. If a hypothetical marker lies on a chromosome that enters only one ordinary offspring sex from a particular parent, then reversing the parental source changes the offspring classes that can receive that marker. The experimental question is no longer just whether the trait is dominant, but whether the predicted chromosome carriers and observed phenotypes correspond.

Exceptions that tested the chromosome theory

A scientific model becomes especially valuable when it predicts an observation that was initially considered an exception. Morgan's X-linked model predicts that a white-eyed female crossed with a red-eyed male produces red-eyed daughters and white-eyed sons. Yet rare white-eyed daughters and red-eyed sons appeared. If the model merely specified which phenotypes tend to occur, the exceptional individuals might be dismissed as noise. Instead, Calvin Bridges investigated whether the exceptions were accompanied by an unusual transmission of the X chromosomes. The resulting genetic and cytological evidence is one of the most instructive demonstrations of the difference between explaining an observation after the fact and making a new, independently testable prediction.

Begin with an X-w X-w white female and an X-w+ Y red male. Normally, one X-w enters each egg. Suppose that in a rare meiosis the homologous maternal X chromosomes fail to separate. A product of that abnormal segregation can contain both maternal X chromosomes, whereas another may contain neither. In the simplified primary nondisjunction model, classify the unusual eggs as X-w X-w and O. The father still produces ordinary X-w+ and Y sperm. Fertilization of the two-X egg by a Y-bearing sperm gives an X-w X-w Y zygote. The fly has two maternal X chromosomes and, in the standard diploid autosomal context, develops as a female with white eyes. The allele associated with her phenotype came exclusively from the maternal X chromosomes; the paternal Y did not restore red eye pigment.

The egg lacking an X can unite with an X-w+ sperm to give an X-w+ O zygote. In the ordinary fly developmental system one X in a diploid autosomal complement produces a male, and the functional allele yields red eyes. The absence of a Y affects fertility but not the primary developmental sex of this fly. Thus both rare phenotype-and-sex categories follow directly from a single alternative segregation event. A cytological test provides an independent prediction: the exceptional white daughters should contain an extra Y, whereas the exceptional red sons should lack Y. The agreement between predicted chromosome complements and observed chromosomes gives stronger support than eye-colour ratios alone.

Other products of unusual segregation cannot be assigned the same viability as ordinary XX and XY zygotes. An egg without X fertilized by Y-bearing sperm gives a zygote without an X, which is inviable in Drosophila. Fertilization of a two-X egg by an X-bearing sperm produces an XXX chromosome complement and has different developmental and viability consequences. A textbook calculation must distinguish the probability of forming a zygote from the probability of recovering an adult. If an exam supplies differential survival values, multiply each zygotic class by its survival probability before normalizing to obtain the expected adult distribution. Without these data, reporting all exceptional zygote classes as equally likely surviving adults is unjustified.

For an illustrative model, let a fraction q of eggs be exceptional, half with two X chromosomes and half with none. If the two sperm types are equally abundant and fertilize equally, each of the four exceptional fertilization classes has probability q/4 among all conceptions. Suppose q is 0.02. Each exceptional class then represents 0.005 of conceptions, or five per thousand in expectation. The two recoverable categories described above account for ten per thousand conceptions if both survive with probability one; the other categories require their own survival assumptions. A count of ten exceptional adults per thousand adult flies need not directly equal one percent of zygotes because the denominator changes when some individuals die. This hypothetical example demonstrates the reasoning without claiming to reproduce Bridges's original data.

Bridges's 1916 account is particularly significant for genetics because the same chromosome abnormality accounts for the inheritance of a marker and the developmental sex of an exceptional fly. It established a direct correspondence between the inferred transmission of the hereditary factor and the independently observable behavior of the chromosome. It also demonstrates why apparently anomalous results deserve investigation rather than deletion. The conceptual lesson is general: if a model is correct, perturbing the underlying mechanism should create coordinated changes in more than one measurable outcome. Detailed chromosome abnormalities will be treated in Subunit 8.F; here the essential point is the use of chromosome segregation as evidence for gene location.

4. X-linked recessive inheritance and conditional probability

In a simple fully penetrant X-linked recessive model, designate the functional allele X-A and the recessive variant X-a. An XX individual with X-A X-a is commonly unaffected in the model, while X-a X-a is affected. An XY individual with X-a Y is affected because the functional allele is absent at that locus. A carrier is an individual who possesses a variant but does not show the specified phenotype under the model. The label carrier should not be used as a guarantee of absence of symptoms in a real heterozygote; dosage compensation and other modifiers can influence phenotype.

Start with a heterozygous XX parent and an unaffected XY parent. The former produces X-A and X-a eggs in equal proportions and the latter produces X-A and Y sperm in equal proportions. The daughters are half X-A X-A and half X-A X-a. The sons are half X-A Y and half X-a Y. There are four equiprobable joint outcomes, each with probability one quarter. Among sons the affected fraction is one half, whereas among all offspring the affected fraction is one quarter. These statements refer to different denominators. To calculate the probability of an affected son without prior knowledge of offspring sex, multiply the probability of a son by the probability of the recessive X conditional on being a son.

An affected XY father and a noncarrier XX mother have daughters that all inherit the variant from the father but are heterozygous. All sons receive the father's Y and the mother's functional X, so they are unaffected in the simple model. The allele has passed to a new generation even if none of the offspring exhibits the recessive phenotype. This is why a generation without affected individuals need not imply elimination of a recessive allele. A subsequent heterozygous daughter can transmit it to her children.

An affected father and a heterozygous mother yield a more informative cross. Every daughter receives X-a from the father; half also receive X-a from the mother and are affected. The other half receive X-A and are heterozygotes. Half the sons are affected because their maternal X is X-a. Thus affected females are possible, and in this cross the affected fraction is one half within each sex. The phrase X-linked recessive does not imply that the trait is impossible in females. It predicts which genotypes are required under stated assumptions.

For a probability problem involving multiple independent births from known parental genotypes, define p as the affected fraction of the specified offspring category. If the question asks for exactly k affected offspring in n births and the probability is constant across births, the binomial probability equals the number of ways to choose k births out of n multiplied by p raised to k and (1 minus p) raised to n minus k. Suppose an established heterozygous mother and an unaffected father have three children of unspecified sex. The probability of no affected child is (3/4) cubed, or 27/64. The probability of at least one is therefore 37/64. If all three children are known to be sons, the corresponding probability of at least one affected son is one minus (1/2) cubed, or 7/8. The biological cross has not changed; only the conditioning information has changed.

When parental genotype is uncertain, successive observations can be statistically dependent because they inform the probability that the parent carries the allele. Suppose a woman has prior probability one half of being heterozygous, with the remaining probability assigned to being noncarrier. Assume her partner has a functional X and the phenotype is fully penetrant. An unaffected son occurs with probability one half if she is heterozygous and probability one if she is noncarrier. The posterior carrier probability after one unaffected son is the joint probability of being a carrier and having an unaffected son divided by the overall probability of having an unaffected son: (1/2 × 1/2) divided by [(1/2 × 1/2) + (1/2 × 1)], which equals one third. The next son's affected probability is then one third times one half, or one sixth. The unaffected child's existence did not change the maternal DNA; it changed the uncertainty about the maternal genotype.

From individual crosses to informative family transmission

A pedigree is an organized record of relationships, phenotypes and sometimes genotypes across generations. Although full pedigree analysis belongs to 8.D.01, a few elementary patterns are necessary to interpret sex-linked crosses. In a simple model, an affected XY individual carries the variant X and has no alternative copy of the locus. His mother supplied that X in ordinary segregation. If a variant was not newly generated in the germ line or during development, the mother must carry it. She may be heterozygous and unaffected or she may express the phenotype herself. An apparently unaffected maternal line is therefore not evidence that the recessive allele could not have originated there.

An affected XY individual can transmit the variant to his daughters, and a daughter who receives it can later transmit it to her sons. This indirect passage from a maternal grandfather through an intervening daughter to a grandson is sometimes called criss-cross inheritance. The term summarizes the route but does not replace a genotype calculation. A heterozygous daughter transmits either X with probability one half. If she has an unaffected partner, her sons are at one-half risk within the simple model. Each of her daughters has one-half probability of carrying the allele but is predicted not to express the fully recessive phenotype. A grandson of the affected man is not automatically affected; the necessary allele may not be inherited in the intermediate transmission.

A father and son can both show an X-linked recessive phenotype in the same family without the father having transmitted his variant to the son. Suppose the father is X-a Y and the mother is X-A X-a. Their affected son has received X-a from the mother. The father's phenotype is real, but his Y, rather than his affected X, is the sex chromosome transmitted to the son. To exclude X linkage using male-to-male transmission, one needs evidence that the very same variant has passed directly from father to son under the ordinary model, not merely the observation that both individuals are affected. This distinction is essential in populations where a variant may independently be present in more than one family branch.

A useful additional example concerns an X-linked enzyme or protein deficiency with residual function. In the hemizygous state, a variant may substantially reduce the amount or activity of a gene product. An XX heterozygote may retain more average function, but the actual relationship between genotype and phenotype can depend on the fraction and distribution of cells expressing each allele. Hemophilia A and B, due to pathogenic variants affecting different clotting factor genes on X, and inherited red-green colour vision deficiency illustrate the classical relevance of X-linked transmission. They are not interchangeable mechanisms: a protein involved in a clotting cascade and a photopigment involved in visual discrimination perform distinct functions. What the examples share is the location-based transmission logic, not the detailed physiological consequences.

For exam calculations that mention an allele frequency, a simple random-mating model may connect population genotype frequencies with X-linked phenotype frequencies. Let q be the frequency of a recessive X-linked variant, assume equal allele frequency in maternal and paternal X pools, random mating, full penetrance and no sex-dependent selection. Among XY individuals the affected frequency is q because only one X allele is sampled. Among XX individuals the affected frequency is q squared because two independent variant alleles must be inherited. The heterozygote frequency among XX individuals is 2q times one minus q. When q is small, q is much larger than q squared. This difference is a consequence of allele copy number rather than an assumption that the mutation occurs more often in males.

If q equals 0.04, the expected male affected frequency is 4 percent, the XX homozygote affected frequency is 0.16 percent, and the XX heterozygote frequency is 7.68 percent. Under an equal sex ratio, the combined affected fraction is one half of 0.04 plus one half of 0.0016, giving 0.0208 or 2.08 percent. These values describe a constructed equilibrium example, not a prevalence estimate for a particular human condition. If allele frequencies differ between the sexes, the female homozygote probability is the product of the two parental X frequencies rather than the square of a single value. Population dynamics and departures from equilibrium are the subject of Subunit 8.E.

Another class of problem concerns conditional risk from a known affected individual selected out of a family. Assume an unaffected father and a mother whose genotype is either X-A X-a or X-A X-A. Before observing children, her carrier probability is c. If she has an affected son and a new mutation is excluded, the posterior carrier probability becomes one in the simple deterministic model because the noncarrier hypothesis cannot produce this outcome. If she has two unaffected sons, the likelihood is one quarter under the carrier hypothesis and one under the noncarrier hypothesis. The posterior is c/4 divided by c/4 plus one minus c. With c equal to one half, this becomes one fifth. A third son then has affected probability one tenth. The number of unaffected daughters would not change the carrier probability in this model if their phenotype alone is recorded, because both hypotheses predict every daughter is unaffected. Genotyping an unaffected daughter, however, may be informative. Evidence must be weighted according to how differently the competing hypotheses predict it.

5. X-linked dominant, Y-linked and pseudoautosomal transmission

An X-linked dominant model assigns the specified phenotype to heterozygous XX individuals and hemizygous XY individuals carrying the dominant variant. It can be useful to reserve D for the variant and d for the alternative rather than using a notation that implies a functional versus nonfunctional relationship. Dominance is a relation between genotype and phenotype; a dominant disease-associated allele need not produce more normal gene function. Haploinsufficiency, altered function and other mechanisms may produce dominance.

With an affected X-D Y father and unaffected X-d X-d mother, all daughters receive X-D and are predicted to express the phenotype, while none of the sons receives X-D from the father. With a heterozygous X-D X-d mother and an unaffected X-d Y father, one half of the daughters and one half of the sons inherit X-D. The expected affected fraction among all offspring is therefore one half. In both situations the phenotype prediction presupposes viability and complete penetrance. If a genotype is lethal during development or has a sex-specific penetrance, observed live-born ratios may differ substantially from zygotic ratios.

A nonrecombining Y locus follows a different route. The ordinary XY father passes that locus to sons via Y-bearing sperm, not to daughters via X-bearing sperm. In a fully penetrant, viable model, an affected father passes the Y-linked phenotype to every son. The condition that a trait appears exclusively in males is not sufficient to infer Y linkage. A sex-limited autosomal trait can appear exclusively in males while being transmitted through unaffected females; a rare X-linked recessive trait may appear mostly in males; and a male-biased environmental exposure may generate a similar distribution without a simple segregating allele. Evidence for a nonrecombining Y locus requires the father-to-son transmission pattern together with localization or other supporting genetic observations. If a Y variant prevents sperm production, its capacity for natural transmission is limited despite the chromosome route.

Pseudoautosomal regions are homologous regions of X and Y chromosomes, especially important for their pairing during male meiosis in humans. These regions contain loci at which an XY individual may possess two alleles. Recombination in a shared region can move a particular allele from the X-associated copy to the Y-associated copy or vice versa. This means that a father-to-son transmission of an allele initially identified on the father's X is possible for a pseudoautosomal locus. It does not contradict chromosome segregation because the allele has changed its chromosomal association before fertilization. SHOX, a gene involved in skeletal growth, is an established example of a human pseudoautosomal locus.

Consider an idealized PAR locus with an allele A initially associated with X and a initially associated with Y in a father. Let r represent the recombination fraction between the locus and a chromosome-specific reference that distinguishes X-bearing from Y-bearing sperm. Under the assumptions of reciprocal exchange and equal gamete survival, the parental gametes X-A and Y-a each have probability (1 minus r)/2, while X-a and Y-A each have probability r/2. Conditional on the sperm carrying Y, the probability that the allele is A equals r. With r equal to 0.20, the probability of a son receiving A from the father is 0.20 conditional on being a son, or 0.10 among all offspring under the usual sex ratio. At r equal to 0.50, paternal allele transmission is independent of the sex-chromosome class in this simplified calculation. The exact recombination fraction is locus dependent; this illustrative numerical example is not a measurement of SHOX recombination.

Penetrance, survival and interpretation of a dominant phenotype

Dominant transmission is often recognized by the occurrence of a phenotype in consecutive generations. The inference must be made cautiously. X-linked dominance has a distinctive chromosome route, but consecutive generations alone do not distinguish it from autosomal dominance. In both models a heterozygote can transmit the variant to half its children. The difference becomes visible when an affected XY father mates with an XX parent lacking the variant. At a non-pseudoautosomal X locus every daughter receives the paternal variant and no son receives it. For an autosomal heterozygote, half of sons and half of daughters ordinarily receive the variant. If the observed family happens to contain two affected daughters and two unaffected sons, both models are possible. Under full penetrance and known genotypes, that specified pattern has probability one in the paternal X-linked dominant model, but probability one sixteenth in the autosomal heterozygote model conditional on the recorded offspring sexes. This difference supplies likelihood evidence, not an unconditional proof in the absence of prior information and model checks.

Introduce penetrance as the conditional probability of observing a specified phenotype given the relevant genotype and circumstances. In the paternal X-linked dominant example all daughters inherit the variant. If penetrance in heterozygous daughters is 0.8, the expected affected fraction among daughters is 0.8 rather than one. If there are five independently evaluated daughters with the same penetrance, the probability that exactly four express the phenotype is five times 0.8 to the fourth power times 0.2, which equals 0.4096. An unaffected daughter may nevertheless have inherited the variant. Phenotype observation and allele transmission must therefore be kept separate. Variants, tissue context, age and environmental effects can alter penetrance, as developed in 8.A.05.

Some X-linked dominant variants have different survival consequences in hemizygous individuals and heterozygotes. Consider a theoretical variant D for which X-D Y zygotes have zero survival, while X-D X-d, X-d X-d and X-d Y zygotes all survive. A heterozygous mother and an unaffected father produce these four classes with equal probability one quarter at conception. After the loss of the X-D Y class, three equally represented classes survive. One third of surviving offspring are affected heterozygous daughters, one third are unaffected daughters, and one third are unaffected sons. Two thirds of survivors are daughters. It would be incorrect to predict a one-half affected live-born fraction from the heterozygous maternal genotype alone. The one-half value describes inheritance of D at conception, whereas only one surviving class carries D.

This reasoning extends to partial survival. Let the four zygotic probabilities be p_i and their survival probabilities be s_i. The probability of class i conditional on survival is p_i s_i divided by the sum of p_j s_j over all classes. If variant-bearing sons survive with probability one half and all other classes survive with probability one, their surviving weight is one eighth instead of one quarter. Total survival probability is seven eighths. The affected heterozygous daughter weight is one quarter, and the affected son weight is one eighth, giving a combined affected probability of three sevenths among surviving offspring. Within surviving sons, affected fraction is one eighth divided by three eighths, or one third. Explicit survival weights protect against confusing genotype segregation with selection.

Real variants need not fit rigid dominant and recessive categories in every tissue, developmental stage or sex. A phenotype may be defined by a threshold of measured function, and a different threshold may classify the same heterozygote differently. In X-linked genetics the distribution of active X chromosomes can contribute to this difference. For the purposes of a formal problem, the stated phenotype rule determines the prediction. For scientific reasoning, the classification is a useful approximation whose assumptions must remain visible.

Testing pseudoautosomal versus nonrecombining sex linkage

Pseudoautosomal inheritance provides an important boundary case because a locus lies on sex chromosomes yet is represented on both chromosomes of the heterogametic individual. For a non-PAR X-linked locus, an ordinary father cannot transmit the X allele to a son. For a strictly nonrecombining Y locus, an ordinary father does not transmit the locus to a daughter. Neither rule applies unchanged to a homologous pseudoautosomal locus. The father may carry one allele on each sex chromosome, and exchange can alter which allele is associated with a sex-chromosome-specific region at the end of meiosis.

Consider a father heterozygous A/a at a PAR locus and an XX mother homozygous aa. Suppose paternal A is initially associated with the Y chromosome, rather than X as in the earlier example. Before exchange the allele associations are X-a and Y-A. Let r be the probability that recombination produces the alternative association at the marker. Given an ordinary daughter, paternal sperm carries X, so her probability of receiving A is r. Given an ordinary son, paternal sperm carries Y, so his probability of receiving A is one minus r. If r equals 0.1, the probabilities among daughters and sons are 0.1 and 0.9. Assuming equal sex ratios, however, the overall transmission of A is still one half. The strong association of A with sons is not evidence that the allele is confined to a nonrecombining Y locus, because daughters can also inherit it after exchange.

If a sample of 100 daughters and 100 sons yields 8 A-bearing daughters and 89 A-bearing sons, a simple estimator of the recombination fraction under this specified initial phase is the number of recombinant transmissions divided by the total number of informative transmissions. The recombinant classes are A-bearing daughters and a-bearing sons, so the estimate is (8 + 11)/200, or 0.095. This reasoning assumes paternal allele origin can be identified, the phase is known, both allele classes are detected reliably and survival does not distort the ratio. The numbers are a hypothetical example for learning, not measured data. A marker in a region with very high exchange can appear almost autosomal in family transmission even though it is physically located on X and Y.

A student might ask why such an estimate is not possible using any arbitrary X-linked variant. The answer is that a recombination fraction compares transmission of two informative markers or associations. A non-PAR X locus that lacks a corresponding Y copy is not a conventional heterozygous two-copy PAR locus. Assigning fictitious alternative alleles to the Y creates impossible gamete classes. At a shared locus, by contrast, two alternative alleles may be present, and their association with an X-specific or Y-specific reference makes recombination identifiable. Detailed linkage map construction will be developed in 8.C.01.

An apparently autosomal family pattern is compatible with both an ordinary autosomal locus and a pseudoautosomal locus. Physical localization or marker cosegregation distinguishes these possibilities. A useful genetic investigation starts by determining whether the DNA region is present in both sex chromosomes. Sequence comparison can establish homology, while chromosome localization can identify the region. Family data may then provide evidence that the allele changes association with X- or Y-specific markers between generations. This combination of physical and genetic evidence is much stronger than phenotype data alone.

The SHOX locus illustrates why the distinction is biologically meaningful. The gene contributes to skeletal growth and occurs in a pseudoautosomal region of human sex chromosomes. A person with an ordinary XY constitution can therefore carry two copies of SHOX, one on each chromosome. If one functional copy is lost, the remaining copy may be insufficient for the usual level of function, producing a dosage-sensitive phenotype. This is an example of a dominant effect due to insufficient total activity, not a demonstration that a dominant variant must have an increased biochemical activity. The inheritance behavior follows the shared chromosome location; the phenotype reflects the dosage requirement of a particular gene. Clinical diagnosis is outside the scope of this chapter.

6. Z-linked inheritance and the direction of reciprocal crosses

In the standard ZZ/ZW system, assume a recessive allele z is confined to Z and a functional allele Z+ masks it in heterozygous ZZ individuals. A Zz W female is hemizygous, while a Z+ Zz male has two alleles. The female produces Zz and W eggs; the male produces Z-bearing sperm. For a homozygous normal male crossed with an affected female, sons receive one functional Z from their father and the recessive Z from their mother, making them unaffected heterozygotes. Daughters receive a functional Z from their father and W from their mother, making them unaffected. The reciprocal cross of a homozygous affected male and an unaffected female produces heterozygous unaffected sons and affected hemizygous daughters.

The reciprocal asymmetry has switched relative to the mammalian XY example because the identity of the heterogametic sex has switched. It is helpful to draw a gamete table rather than memorize a list of patterns. For a heterozygous male and a normal female, half of the daughters receive the recessive paternal Z and are affected, while no son is affected if the maternal Z carries the functional allele. The combined affected fraction under equal sex probabilities is one quarter. The paternal genotype controls the recessive risk in daughters because the maternal W cannot supply a second allele at the modeled non-W locus.

In avian breeding, Z-linked characters can sometimes be used to distinguish offspring sex at an early age when particular alleles and mating directions create characteristic differences. This is an application of inheritance, not an assertion that the visible marker determines sex. A feather-colour marker and the developmental mechanisms responsible for gonadal differentiation are distinct biological features. A geneticist must establish the parental genotypes and the marker's chromosome location before relying on the cross.

A strictly W-linked non-pseudoautosomal locus in a normal ZW female is transmitted to daughters receiving W and not to sons receiving Z. This is analogous in route to Y inheritance but not in parental sex. A W-linked phenotype is not automatically synonymous with a female-limited trait. Again, location, transmission and expression are different layers of the problem.

Sex-linked markers in avian crosses

An important application of Z-linked inheritance is the use of a visible marker whose transmission distinguishes sons from daughters in a planned cross. Consider the simplified model of a dominant barring allele B on the chicken Z chromosome and an alternative b associated with nonbarred feathers in the specified genetic background. In a cross of a nonbarred male Z-b Z-b and a barred female Z-B W, every sperm contributes Z-b. Eggs carry Z-B or W. Sons are Z-B Z-b and barred; daughters are Z-b W and nonbarred. Both sexes share a paternal Z-b, but only sons receive the maternal Z-B. The difference provides a sex-associated visual marker. It is not the marker that causes an individual to become male or female.

Reverse the cross using a homozygous barred male Z-B Z-B and a nonbarred female Z-b W. Every sperm carries Z-B. Sons receive Z-b from the mother and are Z-B Z-b; daughters receive W and are Z-B W. Both groups express barring under the dominance rule. This reciprocal cross is not useful for separating offspring sex through the visible trait because both offspring categories receive the dominant paternal allele. Notice that the difference between the crosses emerges entirely from the inheritance routes. A dominant autosomal allele inherited from a homozygous parent would instead be expected in every offspring irrespective of the mating direction.

An examiner may specify a heterozygous barred male crossed with a nonbarred female. The two paternal sperm classes carry Z-B and Z-b. Among sons, half inherit Z-B and are barred, while the other half are Z-b Z-b and nonbarred. Among daughters, half are Z-B W and barred, while the other half are Z-b W and nonbarred. Here barring has no predictive value for sex under the simple model despite being a genuinely Z-linked marker. The usefulness of a sex-linked marker for identifying sex depends on the chosen genotypes and direction of the cross rather than the mere fact that the locus lies on Z.

In an actual breeding population, other pigment genes may mask barring, and phenotypic scoring may depend on age and genetic background. Marker-based sex identification therefore requires evidence that the marker is visible in the relevant offspring and sufficiently reliable. The scientific inference concerns the link between a segregating marker and chromosome transmission, not the claim that every barred or nonbarred bird belongs to a particular sex. This is analogous to the use of a linked molecular marker in genetic mapping: the informativeness of a cross depends on the genotypes and observable distinctions available in that cross.

7. Dosage compensation, X-inactivation and mosaicism

Chromosomes contain genes that participate in many processes other than sex determination. When sex chromosomes evolve from an ancestral homologous autosomal pair, changes in gene content may leave one sex with fewer copies of genes in the affected regions. If transcriptional output were simply proportional to the number of active copies, the dosage of numerous gene products would differ. Such a difference may disrupt protein complexes, regulatory networks and metabolic balance. Dosage compensation is the collection of mechanisms that adjust expression in response to this imbalance. It is not the same as replication of the missing chromosome, and it does not necessarily equalize every gene exactly.

In placental mammalian somatic tissues, most genes on one X chromosome are repressed in cells with two X chromosomes. Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that early developmental inactivation of one X, followed by maintenance of that inactive state in cell descendants, explains mosaic expression of X-linked characters. For a heterozygous animal, some cell lineages have the maternal X active and some have the paternal X active. These cells can have identical DNA genotypes but differ in which allele is expressed. This is a functional or epigenetic mosaic, rather than the acquisition of different DNA sequences in every affected cell.

An important regulator of mammalian X-inactivation is XIST, a long noncoding RNA produced from the chromosome destined for inactivation. XIST acts predominantly in cis, associating with that chromosome and helping establish a repressive chromatin environment. Changes in transcriptional regulation, chromatin modifications and later maintenance mechanisms cooperate to stabilize the inactive state. A condensed inactive X may be observed as a Barr body. The chromosome is not physically lost, and not every gene on it is silent. Some loci escape X-inactivation, including many pseudoautosomal genes, and patterns of escape may differ among tissues or individuals. Germline reprogramming and species- or lineage-specific exceptions further limit the scope of the textbook random-inactivation rule.

A simplified calculation illustrates why the proportion of cells expressing an allele need not be exactly half. Imagine four equivalent founder cells choosing independently and with probability one half to keep X-A active. The probability all four keep X-A active is (1/2) to the fourth power, or 1/16. The probability exactly two do so is six times 1/16, or 3/8. This toy model is not a measurement of the actual number of embryonic founder cells, but it shows that random choice does not require a fixed final ratio in every animal. Differences in lineage growth, developmental timing and cell survival can further alter the final tissue fraction.

Calico and tortoiseshell coat patterns provide a visible example involving X-linked coat-colour variation in cats. In a heterozygous animal, patches of cells descended from different embryonic lineages can express different alleles. White spotting is controlled by additional genetic mechanisms and should not be confused with the two X-linked pigment states. An apparently similar patchy pattern could also result from a somatic mutation, so a coat pattern alone does not establish the chromosome mechanism in every individual. Allele-specific expression and lineage evidence strengthen the interpretation.

For ordinary mammalian interphase somatic cells, the classical Barr-body prediction is the number of X chromosomes minus one, assuming at least one X and a conventional inactivation mechanism. An XX cell is expected to have one inactive X, an XY cell none, and an XXY cell one. An XXX cell is expected to have two. This rule predicts the number of broadly inactivated chromosomes, not the number of chromosomes present, the phenotype of an individual or the exact transcript abundance of every X-linked gene. Cytogenetic disorders are treated separately in later nodes.

Comparative organisms illustrate different solutions. Male Drosophila generally increase transcription from their single X through the male-specific lethal dosage compensation machinery, involving chromatin changes such as increased histone H4 lysine 16 acetylation. The XX hermaphrodite of Caenorhabditis elegans instead reduces transcription from each X through its dosage compensation complex; the XO male does not apply the same repression to its single X. In mammals, one of two X chromosomes is broadly repressed, rather than each being uniformly reduced by one half. These descriptions express the major strategies, not universal exact twofold changes at every locus. A student must identify both the organism and the sex or chromosome complement before predicting an expression response.

To investigate compensation experimentally, compare RNA abundance for defined X-linked loci with suitable autosomal controls across chromosome complements and developmental stages. A result showing similar total abundance between sexes is consistent with compensation but is not sufficient to identify whether one X is silent, both are partially repressed or the single X is upregulated. Allele-specific RNA measurements distinguish expression from two copies versus one. Chromatin and localization measurements can provide evidence of the regulatory mechanism. Interpretation requires matched tissues, normalization and attention to genes that escape the usual pattern.

Interpreting gene expression and chromosome dosage experimentally

It is useful to construct a quantitative expression model before discussing the molecular differences between compensation mechanisms. Let e be the transcript output of one uncompensated gene copy in a specified cell type. Suppose that before the evolution of sex chromosome heteromorphism the locus had two copies and total output 2e. A chromosome with a single remaining functional copy may produce only e if all other regulatory processes remain unchanged. One potential mechanism restores output by increasing transcription of that copy; another adjusts expression in the two-copy sex so that the outputs between sexes are similar. The first objective, restoring ancestral balance relative to other genes, is not logically identical to the second objective, matching output between sexes. A mechanism can contribute to both, but the measured comparison must be stated.

For a toy model of compensation, suppose males and females each have total expression 2 arbitrary units. In a fly-like scheme, the male's one X copy produces two units and each female copy produces one unit. In a mammalian-like scheme where the active X produces two units, the second X contributes no expression at a completely silenced locus. In a worm-like scheme each of two hermaphrodite X copies produces one unit while the male single X produces two units. These invented values are not measured expression levels for any species. Their purpose is to show that equal total expression does not reveal which chromosome is transcribed. The same bar graph of total mRNA abundance could be generated by different allele-specific expression patterns.

To determine whether one chromosome is silent, investigators need distinguishing sequence variants, allowing reads or transcripts to be assigned to the maternal or paternal allele. Suppose a heterozygous XX tissue contains two clonally derived cell populations. One lineage expresses only allele A and the other expresses only a. If bulk RNA analysis detects equal quantities of A and a transcripts, it might suggest that each cell expresses both alleles. Yet the same bulk result follows if half the cells express only A and half only a at equal rates. Single-cell or clonal measurements can distinguish these models. Bulk tissue averaging is therefore insufficient evidence for biallelic expression within a cell. Likewise, skewed bulk expression could reflect unequal lineage proportions, unequal transcription per active allele or a mixture of both.

Consider two cell populations with fraction f expressing A and fraction 1 minus f expressing a. Let their per-cell transcript amounts be e_A and e_a. The bulk fraction of A reads expected from these cells is f e_A divided by f e_A plus (1 minus f)e_a. When e_A equals e_a, the fraction of A reads equals f. If e_A is twice e_a and f equals one half, the expected fraction of A reads is two thirds despite equal cell fractions. Therefore an allele-specific transcript ratio should not automatically be equated with a cell-lineage fraction. The inference requires an assumption about expression per cell and comparable measurement of the two allele sequences.

XIST provides an example of how RNA can regulate chromatin rather than encode a protein. A long noncoding RNA is transcribed but is not primarily translated into a protein. XIST accumulates over the chromosome from which it is transcribed and helps recruit or coordinate proteins that establish a repressive nuclear environment. This cis association is important because the other X must remain active in the ordinary somatic dosage compensation program. Inactivation involves reduced transcription, changes in histone modifications and chromatin organization, and stabilization through additional epigenetic mechanisms. These processes distinguish initiation of inactivation from its maintenance. Once established, a lineage can preserve the inactive-X state through many rounds of mitotic cell division. The state is not a permanent change to the base sequence and can be reprogrammed in particular developmental contexts.

An informative experimental perturbation might compare expression from an X chromosome capable of normal XIST function with one in which establishment of XIST-mediated repression is impaired. The expected result is inappropriate expression of genes that would ordinarily be repressed, with the details dependent on developmental timing and locus. Merely observing a change in XIST abundance is not sufficient to infer that every gene has changed expression in the same direction. Controls should include chromosome complement, matched tissue and stage, and expression of genes that normally escape inactivation. The relationship between chromosome-wide regulation and locus-specific exceptions is central to interpreting data.

Escape from inactivation is biologically important because two X copies can contribute to expression of certain genes. A simple calculation makes the point. Suppose an escape locus produces one unit from an active X and 0.3 units from an inactive X. An XX cell with one active and one inactive X produces 1.3 units, whereas an XY cell produces one unit from its X, before considering a possible Y homolog or other regulation. At a fully silenced locus the corresponding simplified XX output would be one unit, matching the single-X cell. The escape locus can therefore retain a dosage difference despite chromosome-wide compensation. The values are illustrative and actual escape levels vary by locus and tissue. Many PAR genes have functional copies on both X and Y; for such loci the comparison also needs to include Y-derived expression.

A related distinction involves primary and secondary skewing. Primary skewing means the initial inactivation choice is biased, such that one parental X is retained active more often than the other during establishment. Secondary skewing can arise if the initial choice was approximately balanced but the cell lineages grow or survive differently afterward. Suppose initially 50 cells express A and 50 express a. During a growth interval, A-expressing cells double in number while a-expressing cells remain constant. The final A-active fraction becomes 100 divided by 150, or two thirds. The imbalance need not reflect an initial preference for A. A single observation late in development cannot by itself identify the stage at which skewing arose. A time course, lineage markers and suitable controls can distinguish these causal explanations.

How mosaicism changes a genotype-to-phenotype prediction

A cell population with different active X chromosomes can give rise to several kinds of phenotype depending on the function of the affected gene. For a gene whose product acts largely within the cell producing it, local allele expression may produce a local phenotype. In a pigment-producing tissue, adjacent clonal cell populations may therefore differ visibly in pigmentation. If a gene product is secreted or its activity influences neighboring cells, the relationship between lineage fraction and whole-tissue phenotype may instead be indirect. Functional products from cells expressing a normal allele may partly compensate for other cells. The existence of a mosaic does not specify which physiological consequence follows; the role of the gene product must be considered.

To illustrate, suppose that a heterozygous tissue contains a fraction f of cells expressing the functional allele and the remaining cells express an allele with no activity. If activity adds linearly across the cells, total activity relative to a tissue in which all cells express the functional allele is approximately f, assuming equal cellular contributions. In a different model, the variant retains 20 percent of normal activity. The normalized tissue activity becomes f plus 0.2 times one minus f, which simplifies to 0.2 + 0.8f. If f equals 0.5, the predicted activity is 0.6 of the normal value. If f equals 0.1, the activity becomes 0.28. These calculations are illustrative and assume comparable cell numbers, linear addition and no compensatory regulation. They do not predict a clinical outcome.

If phenotype P occurs only when normalized activity falls below 0.4, the second model predicts P when 0.2 + 0.8f is less than 0.4, meaning f is less than 0.25. An individual with half of cells expressing the functional allele does not express P in this model, whereas an individual with strongly skewed expression favoring the variant may. A heterozygote is genetically the same at the locus in both cases; differences in active allele distribution can change phenotype. This example also shows how a threshold definition influences penetrance. At a different threshold the proportion of heterozygotes classified as affected could change without any difference in genotype or measured tissue activity.

The simple additive model may fail when a small number of cells have a disproportionately important role, when gene products are shared between cells, or when feedback mechanisms adjust activity. For a tissue composed of critical specialized cell types, the overall fraction of active alleles may be less informative than the distribution in a relevant cell type. A whole blood measurement of allele expression, for instance, is not guaranteed to represent the expression ratio in a different organ. The broader principle is that cell lineage and tissue context connect epigenetic regulation to observable phenotype. A meaningful experimental comparison must use cells relevant to the function under study.

Functional mosaicism must also be distinguished from genetic mosaicism. If a mutation arises after fertilization and is inherited by the descendants of one somatic cell, those descendants have a DNA sequence different from other cell lineages. In ordinary X-inactivation, lineages differ primarily in expression of existing alleles rather than the DNA sequence present. Both mechanisms can generate patches or cell populations with different phenotypes. A combination of DNA genotyping and allele-specific expression measurements can distinguish the two. If both alleles are detected in the DNA of two clones but only one allele is expressed in each, with different alleles active in the two clones, the result is consistent with epigenetic mosaicism. If a variant is absent from the DNA of one clone and present in another, a genetic mosaic model becomes more relevant.

A major challenge in comparing compensation mechanisms is normalization. A transcript count from a whole animal is influenced by cell number and by the proportions of different tissues. Suppose a male sample contains twice as many cells of a cell type that normally expresses an X-linked gene strongly. A twofold difference in bulk expression could occur even if there were no transcriptional difference per cell. Conversely, compensation at the cellular level could be concealed by different tissue composition. Appropriate comparisons match cell type and developmental stage, normalize using justified measures, and consider whether the readout is transcript abundance, protein abundance or functional activity. Dosage compensation acts on expression, but biological function may depend on later steps of regulation.

The Drosophila and C. elegans strategies illustrate the difference between chromosome-specific expression machinery and classical dominance. In the fly male, chromatin-associated machinery preferentially enhances transcription on the X chromosome. In the worm hermaphrodite, a dosage compensation complex acts on both X chromosomes to reduce their expression. Neither mechanism changes which alleles the parent passes to offspring. A mutation disrupting compensation can cause sex- or chromosome-complement-specific defects because it perturbs regulation of many genes. Such a mutation need not itself be X-linked; its gene may be located elsewhere in the genome. This is another reason that a phenotype associated with one sex cannot be used as a direct indicator of gene location.

One can construct an exam-style data comparison with three hypothetical XX systems. At a marker, a diploid cell shows one active allele with transcription rate 10 and one silent allele with rate zero. A second diploid cell has both active alleles with transcription rates five each. A third has both active at rate ten. The total transcript amounts are 10, 10 and 20. In the first two cells, the total expression is identical, yet the allele-specific distributions are different. In the third, both copies retain their full output. If a single-X cell produces 10 units, both the first and second XX cells match its total dosage; the third does not. The model is intentionally simple so that the mathematical distinction is transparent. Experimental data showing one pattern would not automatically establish the molecular machinery unless the relevant chromatin and regulatory evidence were also available.

8. Sex-limited and sex-influenced autosomal characters

An autosomal allele has no intrinsic restriction to a sperm containing X or Y in an ordinary XX/XY system. Both male and female parents can transmit it to sons and daughters. Yet expression of an allele may require an anatomical structure, a hormonal environment, a particular stage of development or a physiological response that is ordinarily present in only one sex. Thus a sex-limited phenotype can show a dramatic sex difference even when offspring genotype frequencies are identical.

Milk yield in dairy cattle is an intuitive example of a sex-limited quantitative trait in the ordinary physiological context. A bull possesses and transmits many genetic variants affecting the development and function of mammary tissue and the amount of milk produced by his daughters. His lack of a comparable lactation record does not establish that he lacks the relevant genes. Breeding values can be inferred using relatives, especially daughters, and other genetic information. Real milk production is polygenic and influenced by nutrition, health and management; it is not adequately described by a single dominant allele. A single-gene teaching model can nevertheless illustrate the fundamental distinction between transmission and expression.

Suppose an autosomal allele L permits a trait to be expressed in females of genotype LL or Ll, but males do not express it under ordinary physiological conditions. A cross Ll by ll produces half Ll and half ll in each sex. Under an equal sex ratio, half of females express the trait, no males express it, and one quarter of all offspring express it. The apparently similar one-quarter affected ratio of a carrier mother crossed to a normal father for X-linked recessive inheritance has a different cause. In the autosomal model, males carry L as often as females do. In the X-linked model, the alleles are distributed according to sex-chromosome transmission and the phenotype occurs in hemizygous sons.

A sex-influenced character may be expressed in both sexes but differs in the probability or degree of expression for the same genotype. A traditional simplified sheep horn example describes an allele H that is dominant for horn development in males but recessive in females. For the illustrative model, HH animals of both sexes develop horns, hh animals of both sexes lack horns, and Hh males have horns while Hh females do not. Actual horn inheritance is affected by breed and other genetic factors, so this model must not be presented as a universal rule for all sheep. It is valuable precisely because it isolates the logical meaning of a sex-dependent dominance relationship.

Cross Hh with Hh. The expected genotype frequencies within either sex are one quarter HH, one half Hh and one quarter hh. Applying the male expression rule, three quarters of males have horns. Applying the female rule, one quarter of females have horns. With equal numbers of males and females, the expected overall horned fraction is one half. This aggregate ratio can conceal the different within-sex ratios. If a sample contains unequal numbers of males and females, the combined frequency changes without any change to the underlying genotype proportions.

The hormonal context can modify gene expression through receptor activation, transcriptional regulation, tissue growth or developmental differentiation. In a simplified threshold interpretation, the heterozygote produces an intermediate amount of an effective signal. If a particular physiological context increases the response, the intermediate level may exceed the threshold for phenotype expression in one sex while remaining below it in the other. Such a model explains how apparent dominance can change without any change to the DNA sequence or Mendelian segregation. It is not a claim that all sex-influenced traits follow the same threshold mechanism.

An experiment can distinguish chromosome location from hormone-dependent expression by genotyping animals and comparing phenotype across physiological contexts. If the autosomal allele is present at the same expected frequency in both sexes but phenotype differs among matched genotypes, then the evidence supports sex-dependent expression rather than sex-specific segregation. If an experimental treatment changes phenotype within a known genotype, that observation can establish a role for the physiological environment, provided controls rule out alternative effects. Hormonal regulation may also influence expression of a sex-linked gene; these mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive in real organisms.

Sex-specific penetrance and quantitative prediction

The simple categories of sex limitation and sex influence can be generalized mathematically. For an autosomal genotype G and a sex-related physiological class S, write p(G,S) for the probability of expressing a defined phenotype. In a strict female-limited model p(G,male) is zero for all genotypes under ordinary conditions, while p(G,female) depends on genotype. In a sex-influenced model the penetrance values may differ between sexes but need not be zero or one. If there is a dominant allele D in males, the probabilities for DD and Dd may be similar in males, whereas Dd and dd may be similar in females. The word dominant in this model describes the genotype-to-phenotype relationship in the specified context.

For a Dd by Dd cross, the genotype probabilities are one quarter DD, one half Dd and one quarter dd in each sex if genotype segregation and survival do not differ. Suppose the phenotype penetrance values in males are 0.95, 0.80 and 0.05 respectively. The male phenotype probability is the weighted sum 0.25 times 0.95 plus 0.50 times 0.80 plus 0.25 times 0.05, equalling 0.65. In females suppose the corresponding penetrances are 0.90, 0.20 and 0.05. The weighted sum is 0.225 plus 0.10 plus 0.0125, or 0.3375. With an equal sex ratio the population phenotype probability is 0.49375. No genotype frequency differs between the sexes in this example; all of the phenotype difference arises after segregation. The calculation is a general expression of the law of total probability.

The same formula can be rearranged to infer uncertain genotypes from phenotype within a sex. In the ideal horn model, a horned male produced by Hh by Hh could be HH or Hh. The prior probabilities are one quarter and one half. Conditional on the horned phenotype, the HH probability is one quarter divided by three quarters, or one third; the Hh probability is two thirds. A horned female, in contrast, must be HH in that model. A hornless male must be hh; a hornless female could be Hh or hh and the corresponding probabilities are two thirds and one third. The same observed phenotype does not have the same evidential meaning across sexes when dominance differs by sex.

These probabilities can be applied to an informative mating. A horned male from an Hh by Hh cross is mated to a known hh female. The conditional probability that he transmits H is one third times one plus two thirds times one half, giving two thirds. His male offspring will be horned with probability two thirds under the ideal expression model because Hh males express the trait. His female offspring will not be horned because they are Hh or hh, neither of which expresses the female horn phenotype. Some of these hornless daughters will nevertheless carry H. The inference depends on the parental origin of the male and the specified genotype of the female, rather than simply the male's observable phenotype.

In a sex-limited breeding trait such as ordinary milk production, the absence of a measured male phenotype is not equivalent to a measured value of zero from the same phenotype distribution. A bull's daughters provide information about alleles he transmits; their milk yield also reflects maternal alleles and environment. An investigator comparing sires must account for different environments and the genetic contributions of mates. This is why selection on a sex-limited trait can use records from relatives rather than direct phenotype measurements in every potential parent. A genetic correlation across the sexes or across physiological traits is a quantitative genetics question addressed later in Unit 8. The essential principle here is that sex limitation can restrict the phenotype but does not restrict the presence or inheritance of the underlying autosomal alleles.

Interpreting a sex-influenced phenotype beyond a textbook cross

The traditional horn model is particularly useful for understanding genotype-dependent dominance, but it must be treated as a model rather than a universal law of sheep genetics. Horn status differs between breeds, and actual phenotypes can include variation in horn size or form rather than a single binary classification. Genetic studies have identified a strong role for the autosomal RXFP2 region in several populations. Yet a marker associated with horn status in one breed may not predict it reliably in another. This is an example of how linkage disequilibrium, different alleles and genetic background can affect the relationship between a marker genotype and phenotype. The central lesson is not to memorize that every heterozygous ram has horns. It is to understand why an autosomal genotype may have different phenotypic consequences in males and females.

Suppose an investigator examines two breeds. In breed A, all HH individuals have horns, all hh individuals lack horns, and heterozygotes differ by sex in the manner described earlier. In breed B, some HH females lack horns and a subset of hh males have horn-like structures. An attempt to force both datasets into the same two-allele complete-penetrance model would be inadequate. The deviations might reflect differences in genotype classification, incomplete penetrance, other loci, environmental effects or a biological phenotype definition that groups distinct structures together. To discriminate these possibilities, the investigator would need genotype data, a consistent phenotype classification, controlled comparisons within breeds and potentially additional genetic markers. A cross from one breed cannot establish a universal mechanism for all breeds.

The same reasoning applies to familiar examples of human sex-biased traits. A phenotype occurring more frequently in males is sometimes casually described as a dominant trait in males and recessive in females. For complex traits, this can be a misleading simplification. For example, common pattern hair loss has multiple genetic and hormonal contributors. A single classical two-allele model may help illustrate sex-influenced dominance, but it should not be substituted for the actual complex inheritance of the phenotype. Examinations may explicitly specify an idealized model for a numerical problem. The appropriate response is to apply the supplied model while recognizing that it does not establish the complete genetics of a real trait.

Sex-limited expression is likewise conditional on developmental physiology. If an autosomal locus contributes to a structure normally developed in only one sex, the phenotype may be unavailable for measurement in the other. The locus may still have functions in other tissues or developmental stages. A gene influencing ordinary lactation may also participate in cellular pathways outside mammary tissue. Therefore the statement that a trait is sex-limited should not be interpreted as the claim that every relevant gene is silent in every other cell of the opposite sex. The trait is the object of classification, not necessarily the gene's entire expression pattern. This distinction helps connect the inheritance problem to pleiotropy from 8.A.05.

A controlled experiment can examine whether a hormonal signal modifies the genotype-to-phenotype relationship. Consider a hypothetical cell or tissue system with two genotypes G1 and G2. Measure the response of each genotype under two controlled physiological conditions, A and B. If the response changes between A and B in both genotypes by the same amount, the physiological condition has an effect but there may be no genotype-by-condition interaction on the chosen measurement scale. If G1 changes little and G2 changes substantially, the physiological effect depends on genotype. Such an interaction provides a mechanistic route by which genotype dominance or penetrance can differ among physiological settings. The design requires independent replicates and appropriate controls for initial tissue state, measurement and treatment.

An illustrative response dataset makes this distinction quantitative. Suppose under condition A the mean responses of GG, Gg and gg are 12, 10 and 2 units; under condition B they are 12, 4 and 2 units. If the threshold for the named phenotype is 8 units, GG expresses the phenotype in both conditions, gg expresses it in neither, and Gg expresses it in condition A only. The heterozygote's response changes with condition, while the homozygous responses remain fixed in this invented example. This is analogous to a sex-influenced dominance relationship, but the underlying measurement is continuous. A different threshold could yield a different binary classification without changing the quantitative data. Students should therefore inspect both the continuous response and the chosen phenotype definition when interpreting experiments.

9. Discriminating inheritance models using data

An examiner may describe a family in which only males exhibit a trait. At least three genetic explanations should immediately be considered: an X-linked recessive allele, a Y-linked allele or a male-limited autosomal allele. A small family can also show an apparently sex-specific distribution by chance. The informative observations are those that differ between models. Transmission from a father to a son of the very same allele at a non-pseudoautosomal locus excludes ordinary paternal X transmission. Transmission through an unaffected XX mother to a son is expected in X-linked recessive inheritance and may also occur in autosomal sex-limited inheritance, but is not expected for a strictly Y-linked allele.

Reciprocal crosses help separate genotype location from physiological expression. In an autosomal sex-limited model with identical nuclear genotypes in reciprocal parental crosses, swapping the sexes of homozygous parents ordinarily leaves offspring nuclear genotypes unchanged. Phenotype still depends on the sex of the offspring. In an X-linked model, swapping parental genotypes can alter the distribution of the offspring's X-linked alleles. To interpret the result, record both offspring sex and phenotype and, if possible, genotype a chromosome marker. In a maternal-effect model, the offspring phenotype may depend on the maternal genotype rather than its own genotype; in mitochondrial inheritance, the organelle genome has a different transmission route. These will be considered in the following node.

Consider illustrative offspring counts from a carrier XX mother crossed with an unaffected XY father: 52 unaffected daughters, zero affected daughters, 26 unaffected sons and 22 affected sons. The expected four phenotype-and-sex counts for 100 offspring are 50, zero, 25 and 25. A chi-square test should not include a cell with expected count zero in a denominator. An observed individual in such a category would directly contradict the deterministic model under its assumptions and would prompt a check for scoring error, incomplete assumptions or an alternative model. With no such observation, one may instead test the three possible categories. The chi-square value is (52 minus 50) squared divided by 50, plus (26 minus 25) squared divided by 25, plus (22 minus 25) squared divided by 25, giving 0.48. The degrees of freedom for three fixed categories with no fitted parameter are two. The data are compatible with this simple model but do not prove it; alternative mechanisms could fit the same observations.

It is possible to extract more information by separating tests. One could condition on the 48 male offspring and test the prediction that half are affected: the expected male counts are 24 and 24, yielding a chi-square statistic of 1/3. This test addresses segregation among sons, not the overall sex ratio. Choosing the test requires identifying the biological claim. Selection, sex-biased mortality or phenotype-dependent detection can alter observed offspring counts. A ratio measured among survivors is conditional on survival and cannot automatically be used as a zygotic segregation ratio.

For another illustrative dataset, assume 40 offspring of each sex are genotyped at an autosomal locus. In both sexes, genotype counts HH, Hh and hh are 10, 20 and 10. Among males, 29 animals are horned, while among females 11 animals are horned. These approximate the simple sex-influenced model's predictions of 30 and 10. However, aggregate horned counts alone do not establish that heterozygotes account for the sex difference. Cross-tabulation of genotype and phenotype is essential. If all or most Hh males are horned and Hh females are hornless, and the homozygotes behave as predicted, the dominance-switch model has stronger support. If the difference is confined instead to HH individuals, a different penetrance model is required.

The strongest examination reasoning combines independently predicted genotype classes, transparent probabilities, and plausible mechanisms. Inference from phenotypes alone remains provisional whenever multiple genetic models are compatible with the available data. A statement that is decisive under one set of assumptions may not be decisive under another. The central skill is identifying the assumptions that make a prediction testable.

Designing an informative cross and evaluating competing predictions

Suppose a laboratory discovers a phenotype in a breeding colony. The initial observation is that several XY males express a character but no XX females do. The geneticist has two candidate models: model X is a fully penetrant recessive allele at a non-PAR X locus, and model A is a fully penetrant dominant autosomal allele that is male-limited. An affected male in model X is necessarily hemizygous. In model A his genotype might be homozygous or heterozygous. A first cross with a verified noncarrier female is useful. Under model X every daughter receives the paternal variant but is heterozygous and unaffected, and every son is unaffected. Under model A a heterozygous affected male transmits the allele to half of sons, which express the phenotype, while a homozygous male transmits it to all sons. Observing several affected sons in a controlled cross with a noncarrier female favors model A over the simple X-linked hypothesis.

An absence of affected sons does not automatically prove model X if model A specifies a heterozygous father. Under model A the chance of no affected sons among n independently produced sons is one half raised to n. With three sons, this is one eighth; with ten sons, it is one in 1024. The latter observation is much less likely under the specified autosomal model than under the X-linked model, which predicts no affected sons. Sample size determines the strength of the absence as evidence, provided the model assumptions remain valid. If a genotype is not fully penetrant or survival differs, the likelihood must be recalculated.

The next generation may supply further evidence. Cross an unaffected daughter from the original mating with a verified noncarrier male. Under model X every such daughter is a heterozygote, so half her sons are expected to express the phenotype. Under the autosomal model with a heterozygous original father, only half the daughters are expected to carry the phenotype allele, and those that do have a one-half probability of producing an affected son. Across randomly chosen daughters, the expected affected fraction among sons is one quarter. However, several sons of a particular daughter share the same uncertain maternal genotype. The sons cannot be treated as independent draws from the marginal probability one quarter without accounting for this shared uncertainty. Some daughter families are noncarrier families and produce no affected sons at all. Others are carrier families and have the same within-family one-half probability as the X-linked model. Recording families separately provides information lost by pooling the offspring.

If an informative molecular marker identifies the phenotype-associated allele itself, genotyping the daughters directly becomes more decisive than waiting for phenotype segregation. All daughters receiving a non-PAR paternal X carry the paternal X variant; half the daughters of an autosomal heterozygote ordinarily receive its variant. In a small sample, it remains possible that all autosomal daughters happen to carry the variant. Evidence is strongest when the marker's chromosomal location and allele transmission are independently established. Genetic analysis often combines a cross that predicts genotype distribution with a molecular observation that verifies the predicted physical route.

Another diagnostic distinction concerns sex-biased viability rather than expression. A dominant autosomal variant may be equally expressed in both sexes at a developmental stage but cause reduced survival in one sex. If adult counts show fewer affected females, one might incorrectly infer that females fail to express the allele. The appropriate experiment compares genotype distributions at fertilization or early development with those at the stage of phenotype scoring, if such data are available. If the allele is equally frequent in early male and female genotypes but depleted from one group later, differential survival is supported. If the allele remains equally frequent in surviving genotypes but phenotype differs, sex-dependent expression is more plausible. The same adult phenotype ratio can arise by different biological routes.

A final distinction is between maternal genotype effects and maternal transmission of a sex-linked allele. In a reciprocal X-linked cross, a son receives a particular maternal X and his phenotype ordinarily reflects his own allele. In a nuclear maternal-effect model, a product supplied by the mother's genotype may determine early offspring phenotype regardless of which allele a particular offspring receives. If heterozygous offspring of the same genotype differ depending on whether their mother had genotype A/A or a/a, maternal effect becomes a relevant hypothesis. In organelle inheritance the inherited genetic material itself often has asymmetric parental transmission. The following chapter develops these alternative mechanisms. Here the central inference is that an asymmetry between reciprocal crosses is evidence of parental dependence but does not, by itself, determine its molecular basis.

For an examination question, an efficient final check is to ask whether the proposed explanation correctly predicts all the categories that were actually observed. If the prediction includes an impossible genotype class under the stated chromosome system, the model or the calculation must be reconsidered. If the prediction gives the correct aggregate ratio but contradicts the sex-specific distribution, it is not an adequate explanation. If two models fit the same recorded phenotype classes, identify an additional observable that would distinguish them. This approach turns a collection of inheritance rules into a transferable scientific method.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Male-biased phenotype proves X linkage

    Sex bias can arise from autosomal expression, Y linkage or environment. Test actual allele transmission.

    Sex-specific prevalence is conspicuous and can be mistaken for location. An examiner may supply only affected males; ask which crosses distinguish the competing models.

  2. Hemizygous means homozygous

    Hemizygosity refers to one copy rather than two identical copies.

    Students often count recessive symbols rather than loci. A question about XY versus XX requires counting physical copies before assigning genotype terminology.

  3. A Y carries recessive alleles of all X loci

    Most non-PAR X loci have no corresponding ordinary Y allele.

    The paired chromosome drawing suggests matching genes everywhere. A distractor may invent Y-a at a non-PAR X locus; draw only the alleles physically present.

  4. An affected father passes his X to sons

    In ordinary XY transmission sons receive the paternal Y; PAR exchange is an exception to allele association.

    An affected father and affected son may look like direct transmission. Track the actual allele and distinguish PAR recombination from non-PAR segregation.

  5. X-linked recessive traits cannot affect females

    Homozygosity or other biological mechanisms can produce expression in females.

    Rare affected XX individuals may be overlooked. Calculate crosses with an affected father and carrier mother rather than memorizing male predominance.

  6. All heterozygous carriers are asymptomatic

    X-inactivation and other modifiers can alter phenotype.

    The textbook binary genotype model can conceal variation. Questions may provide skewed X-inactivation or penetrance; apply the supplied expression rule.

  7. A three-to-one F2 ratio proves autosomal inheritance

    Morgan's ideal F2 has this ratio but white flies occur in one sex.

    Familiar Mendelian ratios invite immediate classification. Preserve the sex-by-phenotype table rather than pooling informative categories.

  8. Reciprocal differences establish X linkage by themselves

    Maternal effects, imprinting and organelle inheritance can also produce reciprocal differences.

    A reciprocal asymmetry may tempt the student to stop at chromosome linkage. Compare maternal genotype effects, organelle transmission and imprinting.

  9. A male-limited trait must be Y-linked

    Autosomal alleles can pass through unaffected females.

    Restriction of phenotype is mistaken for restriction of alleles. Trace inheritance through an unaffected female carrying an autosomal variant.

  10. X-inactivation physically removes a chromosome

    It changes chromatin activity without removing the chromosome.

    A condensed chromosome is mistaken for a missing chromosome. Compare chromosome number with gene expression rather than treating a Barr body as deletion.

  11. Every gene on an inactive X is silent

    Escape genes are important exceptions.

    Chromosome-wide repression sounds absolute. Look for escape loci, which can retain activity on the inactive X.

  12. Random inactivation produces exactly 50:50 in all tissues

    Random sampling and lineage differences can produce skewing.

    Randomness is sometimes confused with guaranteed equal outcomes. Calculate founder sampling and consider subsequent lineage growth.

  13. All species compensate by silencing one X

    Flies and nematodes exemplify other major strategies.

    A familiar mammalian mechanism is overgeneralized. Check the organism: flies increase single-X expression and worms repress both Xs in XX hermaphrodites.

  14. ZW inheritance obeys mammalian father–daughter rules

    The heterogametic sex differs; enumerate gametes.

    Students memorize paternal X routes without checking the heterogametic parent. Construct Z and W gametes explicitly.

  15. Sex-influenced dominance implies unequal segregation

    The same autosomal genotype frequencies can yield distinct phenotypes.

    Dominance is often treated as a segregation force. Keep autosomal genotype probabilities unchanged and calculate expression conditional on sex.

38. Part C practice

A carrier probability is initially one half. Two unaffected sons are observed; no new mutations and complete penetrance. Posterior probability of carrier status?

Answer and explanation

1/5.

The likelihoods are 1/4 for a carrier and one for a noncarrier, giving 0.125/(0.125+0.5).

39. Part C practice

A nonbarred Z-b Z-b rooster is crossed with a barred Z-B W hen. Which offspring are barred under dominant B?

Answer and explanation

Sons only.

Sons inherit the maternal Z-B; daughters inherit maternal W and paternal Z-b.

40. Part C practice

The reciprocal cross uses a homozygous barred rooster and nonbarred hen. Can barring distinguish offspring sex?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/3
  3. 2/3
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

C. 2/3

Every sperm contributes the dominant Z-B.

Read counts depend on per-cell expression as well as lineage proportions; the weights are 1 and 0.5.

41. Part C practice

An XX tissue has half of cells expressing A only and half expressing a only, with A-active cells producing twice as much transcript. What fraction of bulk reads is A?

Answer and explanation

2/3.

The transcript weights are 0.5 × 2 and 0.5 × 1, so the fraction is 1/1.5.

42. Part C practice

The initial active-X cell proportions are 50:50. One lineage expands threefold, the other remains constant. Final expanded lineage fraction?

Answer and explanation

3/4.

The new weights are 150 and 50; unequal growth can cause secondary skewing.

43. Part C practice

A locus contributes one transcript unit from active X and 0.25 from inactive X. Predict the XX-to-XY output ratio if Y has no corresponding gene.

Answer and explanation

1.25.

The XX output is 1.25 and the XY output one unit; escape leads to incomplete equality.

44. Part C practice

Two X-linked expression models have equal bulk expression but one uses monoallelic expression and the other biallelic expression per cell. What evidence best distinguishes them?

Answer and explanation

Allele-specific measurements at the single-cell or clonal level.

Bulk equal expression can result from mixtures of different monoallelic lineages.

45. Part C practice

In the illustrative Hh × Hh sheep cross, a horned male is chosen. What is P(HH | horned male)?

Answer and explanation

1/3.

The horned male genotypes HH and Hh have probabilities 1/4 and 1/2, normalized by 3/4.

46. Part C practice

The selected horned male in the previous question mates with hh. Probability a son inherits H?

Answer and explanation

2/3.

Weight his possible genotypes: (1/3 × 1) + (2/3 × 1/2).

47. Part C practice

For Dd × Dd, male phenotype penetrance is 1, 0.5 and 0 for DD, Dd and dd. Predict the phenotype frequency among males.

Answer and explanation

1/2.

The weighted sum is 1/4 + (1/2 × 1/2).

48. Part C practice

A cross yields 100 male and 50 female offspring. In a sex-influenced model with 3/4 male and 1/4 female phenotype probability, expected aggregate frequency?

Answer and explanation

7/12, approximately 58.33%.

Expected expressing offspring are 75 plus 12.5, divided by 150.

49. Part C practice

An autosomal genotype is equally frequent in males and females, but expression occurs only in females. What model is most directly supported?

Answer and explanation

Sex-limited expression.

Equal genotype frequencies indicate transmission is distinct from physiological expression.

50. State SET practice

Assertion: An X-linked recessive trait may skip a generation. Reason: a heterozygous daughter may transmit the variant without showing the phenotype.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

An allele can be retained in an unaffected heterozygote and later appear in a hemizygous son.

51. State SET practice

Assertion: All X-linked genes are silenced on the inactive X. Reason: X-inactivation involves repressive chromatin changes.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true.

Some genes escape despite broadly repressive chromatin.

52. State SET practice

Assertion: Reciprocal crosses can differ for an X-linked locus. Reason: the two parents contribute different sex-chromosome classes in an XX/XY system.

Answer and explanation

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Switching the parental allele source can change which offspring inherit it.

53. State SET practice

Assertion: Sex-limited autosomal alleles can be transmitted through either sex. Reason: autosomal segregation does not require expression of the trait.

Answer and explanation

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Expression restriction does not imply absence of the allele.

54. State SET practice

Assertion: An XY individual is hemizygous for every sex-chromosome locus. Reason: some loci are shared by X and Y.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true.

Pseudoautosomal loci may have two copies in XY cells.

High-yield comparisons

For exam revision, keep locus copy number, transmission, penetrance and expression separate. In a regular XX/XY cross, the paternal X goes to daughters, the paternal Y to sons; this rule does not prevent recombination of pseudoautosomal alleles. In the ZZ/ZW system, a daughter receives her Z from her father. In the mammalian somatic model the classical Barr body number is X count minus one; escape genes and developmental exceptions qualify the rule. A phenotype restricted to one sex is not proof of sex linkage.
