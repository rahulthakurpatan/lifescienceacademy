1. Why plants need aromatic metabolism

A leaf must perform two chemically different tasks with the carbon it fixes. Some carbon is used to construct the machinery of growth, including proteins, membranes and nucleic acids. Other carbon is directed towards compounds that alter the way the plant interacts with its surroundings. A young leaf exposed to intense sunlight can accumulate ultraviolet-absorbing molecules; a maturing stem deposits a rigid aromatic polymer in its secondary walls; a ripening fruit may acquire colour and astringency. These changes are not unrelated accidents. Many of the molecules responsible contain aromatic carbon skeletons, and the cell must manufacture these skeletons from ordinary products of carbohydrate metabolism. The shikimate pathway is a major connection between the apparently simple chemistry of sugar metabolism and this chemically diverse world.

Aromatic has a precise chemical meaning here. It refers to a cyclic, conjugated electron system satisfying the conditions for aromatic stability, not necessarily to a molecule with a pleasant smell. Benzene is the simplest familiar example. Its six carbon atoms share delocalized electrons. An aromatic ring has a different pattern of chemical reactivity from a saturated ring, and adding substituents such as hydroxyl, carboxyl or methoxy groups modifies that reactivity. Plants do not need to begin with benzene itself: enzymes construct and subsequently modify suitable carbon skeletons under physiological conditions. The shikimate pathway generates a nonaromatic branch-point molecule, chorismate, from which several enzymes form aromatic products.

The three proteinogenic aromatic amino acids are phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. Phenylalanine contains a benzyl side chain; tyrosine contains a hydroxyl group on its aromatic ring; tryptophan contains an indole ring system with nitrogen. All three participate in protein synthesis, and all three can also supply specialized metabolism. The fact that tyrosine is a phenol in structural terms does not make its use in protein synthesis a secondary metabolic function. Classification by chemical structure and classification by physiological role answer different questions. In plant physiology it is therefore more informative to describe a compound by its structural class, metabolic origin and biological function separately.

Plants and many bacteria and fungi can synthesize aromatic amino acids through pathways originating in shikimate metabolism. Animals lack the canonical shikimate pathway. Humans obtain phenylalanine and tryptophan from the diet, and tyrosine can normally be synthesized from phenylalanine. This contrast helps explain the selectivity of inhibitors of the pathway at the level of the target enzyme, but it does not by itself establish the safety of any chemical exposure. The key biological insight is that plants must balance protein requirements with the demand for aromatic specialized metabolites, rather than simply produce aromatic amino acids as surplus material.

The preceding discussion of terpenoids used five-carbon isoprenoid units to explain the origin of chemical diversity. Phenolic chemistry requires another kind of reasoning. Some phenolics are derived from aromatic amino acids, some are made from earlier shikimate intermediates, and others receive carbon from acetate-derived units. A flavonoid can combine carbon supplied by two pathways within one molecule. A student should therefore avoid trying to assign all plant compounds to mutually exclusive boxes based only on their appearance. The carbon skeleton and its biosynthetic history need to be traced independently of the functional groups visible in the final structure.

2. Carbon entry and compartmentation

Phosphoenolpyruvate, abbreviated PEP, is a three-carbon phosphorylated intermediate associated with glycolysis. Erythrose 4-phosphate, abbreviated E4P, is a four-carbon sugar phosphate associated with the pentose phosphate pathway and the interconversion of sugar phosphates in plastids. These are the two starting carbon substrates for shikimate synthesis. The connection with carbohydrate metabolism is direct: altering the availability of either precursor can influence how much carbon enters the aromatic pathway. However, an increase in whole-leaf sugar concentration does not necessarily imply increased substrate availability in the compartment containing the relevant enzymes.

Most of the canonical plant pathway is associated with plastids. Chloroplasts are photosynthetic plastids, but roots and developing seeds possess nonphotosynthetic plastids capable of biosynthetic reactions. Thus, a root does not have to perform photosynthesis locally to make aromatic amino acids. Carbon imported from a source leaf can enter glycolytic and sugar-phosphate reactions in the sink tissue. Transport across plastid membranes helps determine the accessible precursor supply. Enzyme isoforms and alternative routes outside plastids occur in some plants, but these do not justify replacing the canonical plastidic pathway with a universally cytosolic map.

The first condensation joins three carbons from PEP to four carbons from E4P. The immediate product has seven carbon atoms. A second molecule of PEP enters at a later stage, supplying three additional carbons to yield the ten-carbon branch point chorismate. This accounting is a powerful check on a proposed pathway drawing. Seven carbon atoms are retained through the early transformations until the second PEP contribution. Carbon skeleton conservation does not imply conservation of every oxygen, hydrogen or phosphate group. Several reactions release phosphate, remove water or change oxidation state. A complete carbon account is therefore distinct from a fully balanced chemical equation.

The plant must also allocate energy and reducing power. A kinase step consumes ATP, while the conversion of dehydroshikimate to shikimate requires reducing equivalents supplied by NADPH in the usual biosynthetic direction. Enzymes have catalytic cofactors that can participate transiently without being consumed in net stoichiometric amounts. For example, the NAD-dependent chemistry within dehydroquinate synthase includes oxidation and reduction within the same catalytic sequence. Counting all cofactor names as net consumed molecules would give a misleading estimate of pathway energetics.

3. Seven reactions from PEP to chorismate

The first reaction is catalysed by 3-deoxy-D-arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate synthase, conventionally called DAHP synthase. It catalyses a carbon-carbon bond-forming condensation between PEP and E4P, yielding DAHP and inorganic phosphate. The product is a seven-carbon phosphorylated keto acid rather than an aromatic compound. PEP is often remembered as the substrate of pyruvate kinase, but this is a different fate of PEP: the carbon skeleton is incorporated into another organic molecule instead of becoming pyruvate. The reaction illustrates competition between biosynthetic pathways and other demands on central carbon metabolism.

DAHP is transformed by dehydroquinate synthase into 3-dehydroquinate. The enzyme catalyses a complex sequence of changes, including transient redox chemistry and ring formation, with net phosphate release. The student does not need to memorize every transient intermediate to understand the significance of this step. An open-chain precursor is converted into a cyclic seven-carbon skeleton. The ring is not yet aromatic; its presence alone is insufficient to infer aromaticity. A six-membered ring can be saturated, partially unsaturated or aromatic depending on the bonds and electronic structure.

Dehydroquinate dehydratase then converts 3-dehydroquinate into 3-dehydroshikimate by elimination of water. A dehydration is not the same as a decarboxylation: the former removes water and conserves all seven carbons. The resulting molecule is also an important potential branch point, because carbon can be diverted towards gallic acid in suitable tissues. The size of this branch depends on the enzymes expressed and conditions inside the cell. It should not be drawn as an obligatory step for every molecule progressing to aromatic amino acids.

The fourth step is catalysed by shikimate dehydrogenase. In the biosynthetic direction, NADPH transfers reducing equivalents to the keto group of 3-dehydroshikimate and shikimate is produced. In many plants, dehydroquinate dehydratase and shikimate dehydrogenase are domains of a bifunctional protein. Two distinct chemical reactions may therefore be performed by one polypeptide. This distinction is important when an examination question asks for the number of reactions rather than the number of enzyme proteins. A mutation affecting one domain can produce a different metabolic pattern from complete loss of the bifunctional enzyme.

Shikimate kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP to the hydroxyl group at carbon 3 of shikimate. The products are shikimate 3-phosphate and ADP. A kinase adds a phosphate group; a phosphatase typically removes a phosphate group by hydrolysis. This phosphorylation helps prepare the substrate for the next reaction. The carbon skeleton remains seven-carbon. If ATP availability falls, the reaction may become constrained, but ATP depletion affects many processes and cannot establish shikimate kinase as the sole cause of a whole-cell phenotype.

The sixth reaction adds a second PEP-derived unit. 5-Enolpyruvylshikimate 3-phosphate synthase, abbreviated EPSPS, transfers an enolpyruvyl group from PEP onto shikimate 3-phosphate. EPSP and inorganic phosphate are formed. The product contains ten carbons and still retains the phosphate introduced by shikimate kinase. Students sometimes confuse the name EPSP with excitatory postsynaptic potential from animal physiology; within this chapter it denotes a defined metabolic intermediate. EPSPS is the enzyme, whereas EPSP is its product. Changing only one letter changes the meaning of an experimental interpretation.

The seventh step is catalysed by chorismate synthase, which converts EPSP into chorismate with release of inorganic phosphate. Reduced flavin mononucleotide is required for catalysis, even though the overall transformation does not require a net oxidation or reduction of substrate. This is a valuable example of a cofactor participating in reaction chemistry without appearing as a stoichiometrically consumed reductant in the net transformation. The product is a ten-carbon organic acid carrying a ring and a substituent; its multiple double bonds do not yet constitute a benzene ring. Chorismate now supplies distinct downstream branches rather than simply being modified in one compulsory linear sequence.

A useful way to remember the entire pathway is to group the chemistry rather than memorize disconnected abbreviations. The first two reactions assemble a skeleton and create a ring. The next two remove water and reduce a carbonyl group. The fifth phosphorylates the product, the sixth adds a second three-carbon unit, and the seventh eliminates phosphate. At each stage, ask what would accumulate if the next enzyme were impaired, what immediate product would fall, and whether an alternative branch could still receive material. Such reasoning is more robust than memorizing that every metabolite must always rise or fall in an inhibited pathway.

4. Chorismate and the aromatic amino acids

Chorismate mutase catalyses a rearrangement of chorismate to prephenate. The atoms are rearranged rather than added or removed; both compounds retain ten carbons. Prephenate is the common precursor for major phenylalanine and tyrosine routes. In the prevalent plant route, prephenate receives an amino group through transamination to form arogenate. The amino group typically comes from glutamate, which is correspondingly converted into 2-oxoglutarate. Arogenate still has ten carbons, but conversion to either phenylalanine or tyrosine removes one carbon as carbon dioxide. The resulting aromatic amino acids each contain nine carbons.

Arogenate dehydratase produces phenylalanine by a combined decarboxylation and dehydration that generates an aromatic ring. Arogenate dehydrogenase produces tyrosine by oxidative decarboxylation, retaining the ring hydroxyl group. The distinction is not that one amino acid requires a ring while the other does not; both products are aromatic. Their difference concerns the reaction chemistry and final ring substitution. Alternative pathways through phenylpyruvate or 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate occur in certain plant species and tissues. It is inaccurate to assume that all plants rely exclusively on one post-chorismate route.

Tryptophan biosynthesis diverges earlier. Anthranilate synthase acts on chorismate with nitrogen commonly supplied by glutamine, forming anthranilate and pyruvate. In the glutamine-dependent reaction, glutamate is also produced. The subsequent pathway transfers a phosphoribosyl group from PRPP to anthranilate, rearranges the product and forms indole 3-glycerol phosphate. Tryptophan synthase uses the indole moiety and serine to form tryptophan, with the associated release of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate from the indole glycerol phosphate reaction. The tryptophan skeleton is thus not explained by direct conversion of a ten-carbon chorismate molecule into an eleven-carbon amino acid; additional carbon substrates participate and carbon is lost along the route.

The separation between the chorismate mutase branch and anthranilate synthase branch has predictive value. If anthranilate synthase activity is specifically reduced, tryptophan synthesis is directly affected while the capacity of chorismate mutase to form prephenate is not directly removed. A rise in phenylalanine flux is possible when more chorismate becomes available, but it is not guaranteed because feedback inhibition, transport and other constraints may intervene. Conversely, restricting the synthesis of chorismate itself can reduce precursor supply to all three branches simultaneously.

Chorismate also gives rise to compounds outside the aromatic amino acid routes. Isochorismate participates in salicylic acid production in Arabidopsis and contributes to other biosynthetic processes. Folate and quinone metabolism provide additional examples of the significance of this branch point. The biological cost of a strong upstream block may therefore extend beyond shortage of proteinogenic amino acids. Supplying one downstream amino acid is not necessarily sufficient to restore growth if other chorismate-dependent compounds remain limiting.

5. Phenolic chemistry and classification

A phenolic hydroxyl group is an OH group directly attached to an aromatic carbon. A hydroxyl group attached to an aliphatic carbon in the side chain is an alcohol group, not a phenolic group. Compare phenol, in which OH is attached to benzene, and benzyl alcohol, in which a CH2 group separates the ring from OH. Both contain an aromatic ring and one hydroxyl group, but only phenol has the defining phenolic linkage. Phenylalanine has an aromatic ring without a ring hydroxyl group, whereas tyrosine has a phenolic ring hydroxyl. This distinction is frequently more useful than memorizing the names of many minor specialized metabolites.

Phenolic hydroxyl groups can lose a proton to form phenolate ions. Delocalization of negative charge over the aromatic system contributes to the greater acidity of many phenols relative to ordinary aliphatic alcohols. The exact acidity depends on other substituents, their positions and the solvent. A compound with both a carboxyl and a phenolic hydroxyl group can have multiple ionizable groups. For example, a hydroxybenzoic acid may exist largely as a carboxylate at a pH where its phenolic hydroxyl remains predominantly protonated. It is incorrect to give every phenolic molecule a single universal pKa.

Phenolic molecules can participate in hydrogen bonding, interactions with proteins, oxidation-reduction reactions and, in some cases, metal coordination. Adjacent hydroxyl groups in a catechol motif can contribute to chelation and oxidation chemistry. However, the biological behaviour of a molecule also depends on conjugation, solvent, pH, localization and the other compounds present. A strong response in a chemical radical-scavenging assay does not by itself establish that the compound improves whole-plant stress tolerance. Biological availability and the relevant cellular concentrations must be considered.

Simple phenolic structures include phenol, catechol with two adjacent hydroxyl groups, resorcinol with hydroxyl groups in the 1,3 positions, and hydroquinone with hydroxyl groups in the 1,4 positions. These positional isomers illustrate that a formula alone cannot determine structure. Plants may contain many substituted or conjugated derivatives of such motifs; the simple parent names are reference structures, not a claim that every plant accumulates free parent compounds. A methoxy substituent is not a free hydroxyl, and glycosylation of a phenolic oxygen can alter ionization, solubility and reactivity.

A common classification separates hydroxybenzoic acids with a C6–C1 skeleton from hydroxycinnamic acids with a C6–C3 skeleton. The notation counts the six aromatic carbons and the carbon atoms of the substituent, not hydrogen or oxygen atoms. Benzoic acid itself is aromatic but has no phenolic OH; hydroxybenzoic acids such as salicylic and gallic acids do. Cinnamic acid is the unsubstituted phenylpropanoid parent, while p-coumaric acid contains a ring hydroxyl. Phenolic is a structural description, and phenylpropanoid is a description that refers to a biosynthetic family. A phenolic compound may belong to both categories or only the first.

Carbon skeleton classes provide clues but do not prove a pathway. Certain C6–C1 compounds can be formed by shortening the side chain of a phenylpropanoid precursor, while gallic acid can be produced through a route associated with an earlier shikimate intermediate. Consequently two related hydroxybenzoic acids may have different immediate precursors. Similarly, the presence of an aromatic ring does not identify shikimate as its exclusive source, since polyketide pathways can also construct aromatic rings. An isotope-tracing experiment, enzyme assay or genetic perturbation can provide evidence about origin beyond structural similarity.

6. Gallic acid: a branch before chorismate

Gallic acid is 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid. Its seven-carbon framework consists of a benzene ring and a carboxyl carbon. The three ring hydroxyl groups influence its chemical reactivity and interactions. Gallic acid supplies galloyl groups for a variety of plant compounds, including hydrolysable tannins and galloylated flavan-3-ols. The fact that it is phenolic does not mean that it necessarily passes through phenylalanine and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase. A branch associated with dehydroshikimate is an important example of a shikimate-derived phenolic that can bypass chorismate and the aromatic amino acid branch.

The route from 3-dehydroshikimate to gallic acid involves oxidation and aromatization. The early precursor already has seven carbons, so no carbon shortening is required to produce the C6–C1 framework. The usual shikimate dehydrogenase reaction instead reduces dehydroshikimate to shikimate. These are alternative chemical fates of the same intermediate. A diagram that places gallic acid obligatorily after phenylalanine misses the essential point of this branch. Evidence for contributions of particular dehydrogenase isoforms has been obtained in species such as grapevine, but enzyme participation and relative flux are not necessarily identical across plant taxa.

Bontpart and colleagues investigated grapevine shikimate dehydrogenases, combining biochemical assays and developmental information to examine their role in gallic acid production. Their work implicates particular isoforms in this branch. The important distinction is between an enzyme capable of converting a substrate under experimental conditions and its actual physiological contribution in a living tissue. Enzyme activity, tissue expression, developmental correspondence and changes in metabolite abundance are mutually informative, yet no single observation establishes the full quantitative contribution of an enzyme under every condition. The experiment is valuable because it gives a mechanistic alternative to classifying compounds by their final structure alone.

Gallic acid can be activated through formation of glucose esters such as beta-glucogallin in galloyl metabolism. Transfer of galloyl groups to suitable acceptors supplies more complex compounds. Hydrolysable tannins contain ester linkages involving gallic acid or related oxidatively coupled galloyl derivatives, whereas condensed tannins are oligomeric or polymeric flavan-3-ol-derived compounds. The details of tannin architecture belong to the later flavonoid and tannin node. Here the distinction establishes how two products with similar astringent properties may originate from different portions of the biosynthetic network.

7. Acetate–malonate chemistry and mixed origins

Acetyl-CoA carries a two-carbon acetyl group in a thioester linkage. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase uses ATP and bicarbonate to produce malonyl-CoA, a three-carbon compound. When malonyl-CoA acts as an extender in a polyketide reaction, the newly added carboxyl carbon is normally lost as carbon dioxide. The net contribution of the extender is two carbons. Decarboxylation facilitates carbon-carbon bond formation; it is not a wasteful afterthought. Fatty acid synthesis also uses related decarboxylative condensation chemistry, but the enzyme systems and final products differ.

Plant type III polyketide synthases typically use CoA-linked starter and extender substrates. Repeated additions create an elongated chain containing carbonyl groups. Different cyclization reactions and subsequent modifications can transform such a chain into aromatic compounds. This strategy differs from the shikimate pathway, which assembles a seven-carbon skeleton from PEP and E4P and subsequently adds another PEP-derived unit. Both routes are capable of contributing to aromatic natural products, so finding a benzene ring in a molecule does not identify its biosynthetic route without additional evidence.

Chalcone synthase provides the classic example of mixed origin. Its usual starter is p-coumaroyl-CoA, a nine-carbon phenylpropanoid-derived substrate. Three malonyl-CoA molecules contribute two carbons each after decarboxylation. The carbon account is nine plus six, giving a fifteen-carbon chalcone skeleton. One aromatic ring and the three-carbon linker derive from the phenylpropanoid starter; the second ring is assembled from the three malonate-derived units. Subsequent reactions can yield flavonoid scaffolds. This explains why it is correct to describe flavonoids as phenolic compounds of mixed shikimate–acetate origin rather than claiming that every carbon comes from phenylalanine.

Chalcone synthase and stilbene synthase provide a useful contrast. Both can use the same p-coumaroyl-CoA starter and three malonyl-CoA extenders, but the cyclization and termination chemistry differ. The chalcone product retains fifteen carbons while resveratrol, a stilbene, has fourteen. The extra carbon is lost during stilbene formation. Thus an identical list of substrates does not guarantee identical product carbon counts or ring arrangements. The enzyme determines how the intermediate is processed. Detailed branches are examined in the subsequent nodes; the current objective is to recognize shared precursors, mixed biosynthetic origin and the chemical basis of structural diversity.

8. Experimental evidence and quantitative reasoning

A metabolic map is a model of connected chemical transformations, not a direct measurement of rates. Researchers can test the map by incubating purified enzymes with substrates and cofactors and determining whether the predicted product appears. A control without active enzyme distinguishes spontaneous conversion from enzyme-dependent conversion, while omission of a required substrate or cofactor tests reaction specificity. Detecting the correct product is stronger evidence than monitoring the disappearance of substrate alone. Yet a purified enzyme assay establishes catalytic capacity rather than automatically proving that the same flux occurs inside a particular plant tissue.

The inhibition of EPSPS by glyphosate provides a historically important example of localizing a pathway block. Steinrücken and Amrhein identified EPSPS as the inhibited enzyme in 1980. Earlier and complementary measurements of shikimate accumulation were consistent with interference between shikimate and chorismate. In a direct EPSPS assay the enzyme's substrates are shikimate 3-phosphate and PEP; a fall in product formation on addition of an inhibitor can be related to the defined reaction. In an intact plant, upstream shikimate accumulation is an indirect readout influenced by additional pathway steps. A metabolite that accumulates in a treated leaf is not necessarily the immediate enzyme substrate.

Stable isotope tracing can distinguish sources of carbon. Imagine providing a plant tissue with a labelled precursor and measuring the isotopic pattern of its phenolic products. A carbon label can be retained in one product region and lost as carbon dioxide from another. Interpretation requires knowledge of which positions are labelled, whether the precursor reaches the relevant compartment and whether central metabolism redistributes the label. Label found in an end product demonstrates that carbon from the supplied substrate can reach it; this alone does not establish that a single direct reaction connects the two. Time-resolved patterns and positional information strengthen the inference.

For an illustrative carbon balance, assume a chalcone is produced exclusively from one C9 starter and three malonyl-CoA units. Before decarboxylation, these four substrates supply eighteen carbons in their transferable carbon groups: nine from the starter and nine from the extenders. Three extender carboxyl carbons are released as carbon dioxide. The product retains fifteen carbons, so fifteen divided by eighteen, or five sixths, is retained in the product. The calculation excludes the CoA carrier and the ATP and bicarbonate required to synthesize malonyl-CoA. If the question instead asks what fraction of product carbon derives from malonate, the correct denominator is fifteen, giving six fifteenths or forty percent.

Consider a simplified steady-state chorismate pool with input of 100 nmol per gram fresh mass per hour. Suppose 55 units enter the prephenate branch, 30 enter the anthranilate branch, and 15 enter other pathways. If the anthranilate flux is reduced to 15 while input and the other branch rates initially remain unchanged, input exceeds output by 15 units per hour. The immediate prediction is accumulation of chorismate at that rate, not necessarily diversion of exactly 15 units into phenylalanine. At a later steady state, input and output must balance again, possibly through feedback-mediated reduction of input, increased competing fluxes or both. This invented dataset illustrates why branch inhibition cannot be interpreted from arithmetic alone without specifying time and regulatory assumptions.

Metabolite concentration and metabolic flux are related but not equivalent. A large pool may turn over slowly, whereas a small pool can support rapid production if its replenishment rate is high. In an idealized system, the change of pool amount per unit time equals the sum of rates of formation minus the sum of rates of removal. At steady state this change is zero, not because reactions cease, but because their rates balance. Comparing a mutant with a control at a single time point can suggest a metabolic disturbance but cannot by itself determine the rates of all contributing processes. Measurements of labelling over time, product formation and enzyme activity add complementary information.

9. Physiological roles and limits of classification

Phenolic compounds have diverse roles because their structural features can be recruited for distinct tasks. Lignin-related aromatic metabolism contributes to secondary wall properties and the integrity of water-conducting tissues. Soluble phenolics can absorb ultraviolet radiation or participate in defence. Pigments derived from phenolic pathways contribute to interactions with pollinators and seed dispersers. Galloylated compounds can influence interactions with proteins and feeding organisms. These functions depend on which molecules are made, where they are accumulated and when they are deployed. The presence of a general functional group does not give every phenolic the same ecological activity.

The separation between plastidic aromatic amino acid synthesis and many downstream reactions also has physiological consequences. A precursor generated in one compartment may be transported into another before it is converted into a specialized product. Storage in a vacuole, association with a wall or incorporation into an insoluble polymer can change how a product interacts with the cytoplasm. Hence a high total phenolic concentration in a tissue extract may not reflect a high free concentration at an enzyme's site of action. Tissue homogenization removes the very compartment boundaries that can govern the molecule's biological effect.

The main transition to the next chapter is the reaction catalysed by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, or PAL, which removes ammonia from phenylalanine to generate cinnamate. Its carbon skeleton remains nine-carbon; the nitrogen originally assimilated into the amino acid is no longer attached. Cinnamate can be hydroxylated and activated as a CoA ester, providing the entry to the major phenylpropanoid branches. This transition connects nitrogen assimilation with carbon-rich specialized chemistry. It also shows why an amino acid-derived specialized product does not necessarily contain nitrogen in its final structure. The next chapter follows this downstream chemistry into structural and soluble phenylpropanoids.
