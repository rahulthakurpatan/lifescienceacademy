1. Why plants need aromatic metabolism

A leaf must perform two chemically different tasks with the carbon it fixes. Some carbon is used to construct the machinery of growth, including proteins, membranes and nucleic acids. Other carbon is directed towards compounds that alter the way the plant interacts with its surroundings. A young leaf exposed to intense sunlight can accumulate ultraviolet-absorbing molecules; a maturing stem deposits a rigid aromatic polymer in its secondary walls; a ripening fruit may acquire colour and astringency. These changes are not unrelated accidents. Many of the molecules responsible contain aromatic carbon skeletons, and the cell must manufacture these skeletons from ordinary products of carbohydrate metabolism. The shikimate pathway is a major connection between the apparently simple chemistry of sugar metabolism and this chemically diverse world.

Aromatic has a precise chemical meaning here. It refers to a cyclic, conjugated electron system satisfying the conditions for aromatic stability, not necessarily to a molecule with a pleasant smell. Benzene is the simplest familiar example. Its six carbon atoms share delocalized electrons. An aromatic ring has a different pattern of chemical reactivity from a saturated ring, and adding substituents such as hydroxyl, carboxyl or methoxy groups modifies that reactivity. Plants do not need to begin with benzene itself: enzymes construct and subsequently modify suitable carbon skeletons under physiological conditions. The shikimate pathway generates a nonaromatic branch-point molecule, chorismate, from which several enzymes form aromatic products.

The three proteinogenic aromatic amino acids are phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. Phenylalanine contains a benzyl side chain; tyrosine contains a hydroxyl group on its aromatic ring; tryptophan contains an indole ring system with nitrogen. All three participate in protein synthesis, and all three can also supply specialized metabolism. The fact that tyrosine is a phenol in structural terms does not make its use in protein synthesis a secondary metabolic function. Classification by chemical structure and classification by physiological role answer different questions. In plant physiology it is therefore more informative to describe a compound by its structural class, metabolic origin and biological function separately.

Plants and many bacteria and fungi can synthesize aromatic amino acids through pathways originating in shikimate metabolism. Animals lack the canonical shikimate pathway. Humans obtain phenylalanine and tryptophan from the diet, and tyrosine can normally be synthesized from phenylalanine. This contrast helps explain the selectivity of inhibitors of the pathway at the level of the target enzyme, but it does not by itself establish the safety of any chemical exposure. The key biological insight is that plants must balance protein requirements with the demand for aromatic specialized metabolites, rather than simply produce aromatic amino acids as surplus material.

The preceding discussion of terpenoids used five-carbon isoprenoid units to explain the origin of chemical diversity. Phenolic chemistry requires another kind of reasoning. Some phenolics are derived from aromatic amino acids, some are made from earlier shikimate intermediates, and others receive carbon from acetate-derived units. A flavonoid can combine carbon supplied by two pathways within one molecule. A student should therefore avoid trying to assign all plant compounds to mutually exclusive boxes based only on their appearance. The carbon skeleton and its biosynthetic history need to be traced independently of the functional groups visible in the final structure.

The difference between carbon acquisition and carbon allocation is especially apparent during germination. A seedling developing before its first green leaf opens has access to stored reserve carbohydrate, lipid or protein rather than a substantial current supply of photosynthate. It can nonetheless synthesize enzymes containing phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan, and deposit aromatic compounds in developing tissues. Storage reserves are mobilized into central metabolites and used by biosynthetic pathways in non-green plastids. This example demonstrates why the shikimate pathway is not a branch of the light reactions themselves. Photosynthesis provides carbon and energy at the whole-plant scale, but the pathway can function in cells that never carry out photosynthetic electron transport.

A useful distinction is between a precursor that supplies atoms and a regulatory molecule that changes the rate of a process. A molecule of phenylalanine incorporated into a phenylpropanoid supplies carbon atoms to the product. A hormone that stimulates the expression of a phenylpropanoid biosynthetic gene may increase production without contributing any carbon to the product. An experiment that finds more phenolics after hormone treatment therefore cannot establish that the hormone is their substrate. Conversely, tracing a labelled atom into a phenolic molecule does not necessarily establish that the labelled precursor regulates its production. The two kinds of evidence answer different biological questions.

The term specialized metabolism is preferable to the interpretation that secondary metabolism is dispensable. A plant growing under favourable laboratory conditions may survive a mutation affecting a particular defensive phenolic, whereas the same plant may be less successful under high ultraviolet exposure or attack. Moreover, the same shared pathway supplies phenylalanine for proteins and aromatic precursors for structural compounds required during normal development. A biochemical network does not contain a sharp physical boundary separating all essential and nonessential products. The context of a product's use, the taxonomic distribution of a pathway and the conditions of growth all matter.

Discovery of a common biosynthetic pathway

The name shikimate preserves a historical connection to a plant rather than the name of an enzyme. Shikimic acid was isolated in 1885 from Japanese star anise, Illicium religiosum, long before its central role in metabolism was established. Isolation of a natural product is only the first stage in explaining its biological significance. An isolated compound might be a metabolic end product, an intermediate used in many reactions or even a substance accumulated only under particular conditions. Identifying its structure does not immediately reveal which other molecules precede or follow it. In the twentieth century, a new approach based on nutritional mutants made it possible to distinguish these possibilities.

A nutritional mutant is a strain that has lost the ability to manufacture a substance required for growth under a specified culture condition. If a bacterium normally grows with a simple carbon source and inorganic nutrients, but a mutant grows only after certain amino acids are supplied, the mutation may have impaired one or more steps in amino acid production. In the early 1950s, work by Bernard Davis with aromatic auxotrophs of Escherichia coli helped establish the existence of common precursors to aromatic compounds. Some nutritional defects could be bypassed by providing shikimate, whereas defects later in the pathway could not be bypassed by the same intermediate. This logic helped assign intermediates to an ordered biosynthetic sequence.

Consider how a simplified version of this approach works. Suppose wild-type cells synthesize an essential product Z through two intermediates X and Y. Strain A cannot grow in a minimal medium, but grows when X, Y or Z is supplied separately. Strain B grows when Y or Z is supplied but not when X is supplied. Strain C grows only when Z is supplied. If the supplied compounds can enter the cells and there is only one route, the most economical explanation is that A is blocked before X, B between X and Y, and C between Y and Z. This pattern assigns the relative order of the intermediates without directly observing each reaction inside a cell.

A real aromatic auxotroph is more complicated than this imaginary linear pathway. A branch-point defect can prevent the production of several required end products simultaneously. Feeding one end product might fail to restore growth simply because the other products remain absent. Conversely, feeding a common intermediate could enable a cell to recover multiple branches at once. Nutritional rescue therefore helps distinguish defects before a branch point from defects after it. Growth rescue alone does not reveal the precise chemical reaction or gene; independent biochemical and genetic work is required. It is also possible that failure to rescue reflects poor uptake rather than an inability to convert a compound.

The application of such microbial evidence to plants requires additional care. A sequence established in a microorganism suggests a plausible route in a plant, but the two organisms may possess different enzyme architectures, compartments and branch-specific regulation. Demonstrating enzyme activities in plant tissues and identifying plant genes with corresponding functions establishes that the route operates in the plant itself. This explains how a pathway can be evolutionarily conserved while still displaying lineage-specific differences in organization. The plant bifunctional dehydratase–dehydrogenase and alternative aromatic amino acid routes are good examples of variation on a common chemical framework.

2. Carbon entry and compartmentation

Phosphoenolpyruvate, abbreviated PEP, is a three-carbon phosphorylated intermediate associated with glycolysis. Erythrose 4-phosphate, abbreviated E4P, is a four-carbon sugar phosphate associated with the pentose phosphate pathway and the interconversion of sugar phosphates in plastids. These are the two starting carbon substrates for shikimate synthesis. The connection with carbohydrate metabolism is direct: altering the availability of either precursor can influence how much carbon enters the aromatic pathway. However, an increase in whole-leaf sugar concentration does not necessarily imply increased substrate availability in the compartment containing the relevant enzymes.

Most of the canonical plant pathway is associated with plastids. Chloroplasts are photosynthetic plastids, but roots and developing seeds possess nonphotosynthetic plastids capable of biosynthetic reactions. Thus, a root does not have to perform photosynthesis locally to make aromatic amino acids. Carbon imported from a source leaf can enter glycolytic and sugar-phosphate reactions in the sink tissue. Transport across plastid membranes helps determine the accessible precursor supply. Enzyme isoforms and alternative routes outside plastids occur in some plants, but these do not justify replacing the canonical plastidic pathway with a universally cytosolic map.

The first condensation joins three carbons from PEP to four carbons from E4P. The immediate product has seven carbon atoms. A second molecule of PEP enters at a later stage, supplying three additional carbons to yield the ten-carbon branch point chorismate. This accounting is a powerful check on a proposed pathway drawing. Seven carbon atoms are retained through the early transformations until the second PEP contribution. Carbon skeleton conservation does not imply conservation of every oxygen, hydrogen or phosphate group. Several reactions release phosphate, remove water or change oxidation state. A complete carbon account is therefore distinct from a fully balanced chemical equation.

The plant must also allocate energy and reducing power. A kinase step consumes ATP, while the conversion of dehydroshikimate to shikimate requires reducing equivalents supplied by NADPH in the usual biosynthetic direction. Enzymes have catalytic cofactors that can participate transiently without being consumed in net stoichiometric amounts. For example, the NAD-dependent chemistry within dehydroquinate synthase includes oxidation and reduction within the same catalytic sequence. Counting all cofactor names as net consumed molecules would give a misleading estimate of pathway energetics.

E4P is one of the smaller sugar phosphates that can arise through reversible carbon-transfer reactions in central metabolism. Transketolase transfers a two-carbon unit between suitable sugar phosphates; transaldolase transfers a three-carbon unit. These reactions permit cells to redistribute carbon between sugars of different chain lengths without first releasing every carbon as carbon dioxide. The existence of a four-carbon sugar phosphate in the cell is therefore not surprising even though the better-known glycolytic intermediates often have six or three carbons. In a green plastid, sugar phosphate interconversion also participates in carbon fixation and regeneration chemistry. At this level the key point is that availability of E4P is connected to central carbon rearrangement, not to a separate dietary source of four-carbon molecules.

PEP is a particularly interesting precursor because cells can use it in energy-yielding reactions as well as in carbon-building reactions. The conversion of PEP to pyruvate through pyruvate kinase can be coupled to ATP formation. When PEP supplies carbon to DAHP synthase or EPSPS, that particular carbon unit does not simultaneously flow through pyruvate kinase. A plant adjusting its allocation to aromatic products may therefore face tradeoffs among reactions drawing on the same precursor. These tradeoffs cannot be calculated from PEP concentration alone. An increased PEP pool could reflect increased supply, reduced utilization or both. Enzyme activity and transport determine the rates at which the competing reactions consume it.

Compartmentation also affects interpretation of enzyme localization experiments. Suppose a fluorescently tagged shikimate enzyme appears in plastids. This observation supports localization of the tagged protein, provided the tag does not interfere with import. It does not show that all substrates are present or that the complete pathway functions there at a high rate. Conversely, activity detected in a cytosolic fraction could reflect genuine cytosolic enzyme or contamination from disrupted plastids. Marker enzymes for the purity of fractions, microscopy and biochemical assays provide complementary evidence. For a NET problem, distinguish where an enzyme can be found from where the complete sequence of reactions has been demonstrated to occur.

For a simplified precursor-limited system, let the available rates of PEP and E4P delivery be 90 and 70 nanomoles per minute. If no other demands operate and every chorismate requires two PEP molecules and one E4P, the maximum possible rate from PEP is 45 nanomoles per minute, while the limit from E4P is 70. The smaller value, 45, is the upper bound. At that rate only 45 units of E4P are consumed. This is a stoichiometric upper bound rather than a prediction of actual flux. It ignores kinetic limitations, the possibility that PEP is consumed by other pathways, and the diversion of seven-carbon intermediates before chorismate synthesis. Increasing E4P alone cannot raise the bound when PEP supply is the limiting input in this model.

3. Seven reactions from PEP to chorismate

The first reaction is catalysed by 3-deoxy-D-arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate synthase, conventionally called DAHP synthase. It catalyses a carbon-carbon bond-forming condensation between PEP and E4P, yielding DAHP and inorganic phosphate. The product is a seven-carbon phosphorylated keto acid rather than an aromatic compound. PEP is often remembered as the substrate of pyruvate kinase, but this is a different fate of PEP: the carbon skeleton is incorporated into another organic molecule instead of becoming pyruvate. The reaction illustrates competition between biosynthetic pathways and other demands on central carbon metabolism.

DAHP is transformed by dehydroquinate synthase into 3-dehydroquinate. The enzyme catalyses a complex sequence of changes, including transient redox chemistry and ring formation, with net phosphate release. The student does not need to memorize every transient intermediate to understand the significance of this step. An open-chain precursor is converted into a cyclic seven-carbon skeleton. The ring is not yet aromatic; its presence alone is insufficient to infer aromaticity. A six-membered ring can be saturated, partially unsaturated or aromatic depending on the bonds and electronic structure.

Dehydroquinate dehydratase then converts 3-dehydroquinate into 3-dehydroshikimate by elimination of water. A dehydration is not the same as a decarboxylation: the former removes water and conserves all seven carbons. The resulting molecule is also an important potential branch point, because carbon can be diverted towards gallic acid in suitable tissues. The size of this branch depends on the enzymes expressed and conditions inside the cell. It should not be drawn as an obligatory step for every molecule progressing to aromatic amino acids.

The fourth step is catalysed by shikimate dehydrogenase. In the biosynthetic direction, NADPH transfers reducing equivalents to the keto group of 3-dehydroshikimate and shikimate is produced. In many plants, dehydroquinate dehydratase and shikimate dehydrogenase are domains of a bifunctional protein. Two distinct chemical reactions may therefore be performed by one polypeptide. This distinction is important when an examination question asks for the number of reactions rather than the number of enzyme proteins. A mutation affecting one domain can produce a different metabolic pattern from complete loss of the bifunctional enzyme.

Shikimate kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP to the hydroxyl group at carbon 3 of shikimate. The products are shikimate 3-phosphate and ADP. A kinase adds a phosphate group; a phosphatase typically removes a phosphate group by hydrolysis. This phosphorylation helps prepare the substrate for the next reaction. The carbon skeleton remains seven-carbon. If ATP availability falls, the reaction may become constrained, but ATP depletion affects many processes and cannot establish shikimate kinase as the sole cause of a whole-cell phenotype.

The sixth reaction adds a second PEP-derived unit. 5-Enolpyruvylshikimate 3-phosphate synthase, abbreviated EPSPS, transfers an enolpyruvyl group from PEP onto shikimate 3-phosphate. EPSP and inorganic phosphate are formed. The product contains ten carbons and still retains the phosphate introduced by shikimate kinase. Students sometimes confuse the name EPSP with excitatory postsynaptic potential from animal physiology; within this chapter it denotes a defined metabolic intermediate. EPSPS is the enzyme, whereas EPSP is its product. Changing only one letter changes the meaning of an experimental interpretation.

The seventh step is catalysed by chorismate synthase, which converts EPSP into chorismate with release of inorganic phosphate. Reduced flavin mononucleotide is required for catalysis, even though the overall transformation does not require a net oxidation or reduction of substrate. This is a valuable example of a cofactor participating in reaction chemistry without appearing as a stoichiometrically consumed reductant in the net transformation. The product is a ten-carbon organic acid carrying a ring and a substituent; its multiple double bonds do not yet constitute a benzene ring. Chorismate now supplies distinct downstream branches rather than simply being modified in one compulsory linear sequence.

A useful way to remember the entire pathway is to group the chemistry rather than memorize disconnected abbreviations. The first two reactions assemble a skeleton and create a ring. The next two remove water and reduce a carbonyl group. The fifth phosphorylates the product, the sixth adds a second three-carbon unit, and the seventh eliminates phosphate. At each stage, ask what would accumulate if the next enzyme were impaired, what immediate product would fall, and whether an alternative branch could still receive material. Such reasoning is more robust than memorizing that every metabolite must always rise or fall in an inhibited pathway.

Reaction names reveal the type of chemical change and can be used to reconstruct forgotten pathway details. A synthase generally catalyses formation of a product without requiring the name to imply direct ATP hydrolysis. A synthetase commonly denotes a ligase reaction coupled to nucleotide hydrolysis, although historical enzyme names do not always obey a rigid distinction. The DAHP synthase and EPSP synthase reactions both exploit the chemistry of PEP and do not directly use ATP as a substrate. Shikimate kinase does use ATP. Chorismate synthase is a different enzyme again: it eliminates phosphate from EPSP. Thus, enzyme names should be checked against their actual substrates rather than treated as exact substitutes for balanced equations.

The importance of the DAHP synthase reaction can be inferred from the consequences of early precursor diversion. Carbon incorporated into DAHP is no longer present as free E4P or PEP. However, this does not mean it is irrevocably destined to appear in an aromatic amino acid. An intermediate can supply an earlier specialized branch such as gallic acid formation. The phrase committed step must therefore be used with a defined destination: entry into shikimate-associated metabolism is not identical to commitment to phenylalanine synthesis. Exam questions sometimes exploit the ambiguity by treating the first common step as a branch-specific commitment.

To distinguish the dehydration and reduction stages, focus on the oxygen-bearing groups rather than the entire structural formula. Dehydroquinate dehydratase removes the elements of water to form an unsaturated keto acid. Shikimate dehydrogenase then reduces the keto function to an alcohol, using reducing equivalents. If an assay records NADPH disappearance upon addition of 3-dehydroshikimate, this is consistent with reduction but does not identify the product unambiguously. Other NADPH-consuming reactions could occur in crude extracts. Product-specific chromatography and a no-substrate control help establish that the measured change corresponds to shikimate formation.

Shikimate 3-phosphate should not be confused with a product of shikimate dehydrogenase. The dehydrogenase modifies oxidation state, whereas the kinase changes phosphorylation state. A mutation in shikimate kinase can leave the previous reduction step active. Under suitable conditions the resulting metabolite profile could show elevated shikimate with reduced shikimate 3-phosphate. By contrast, a specific EPSPS block may initially elevate its phosphorylated substrate, and shikimate accumulation can follow through the wider network. These predictions assume comparable input rates and absence of a strong compensatory response. In long treatments the initial pattern may be modified by regulation or loss of cellular viability.

The difference between a cofactor and a cosubstrate is central to the final two reactions. In EPSPS the PEP molecule supplies a group that is incorporated into EPSP; it is a material reactant in the net equation. Reduced FMN in chorismate synthase participates in catalysis but does not supply a carbon atom to chorismate. A deficiency of a cofactor can therefore lower reaction rate without changing the formula of the product formed by each successful catalytic cycle. The presence of reduced flavin does not establish that chorismate production consumes one molecule of NADPH for every molecule of product. Net redox accounting requires examining the complete catalytic reaction.

Energetics, reaction accounting and pathway disturbances

To calculate the carbon efficiency of the common pathway, first define the boundaries. Starting with the two PEP molecules and one E4P molecule and ending with chorismate, the reactant skeletons supply three plus three plus four carbons, and the product contains ten. All input carbon is retained within this simplified boundary. This does not establish that conversion from glucose or carbon dioxide to chorismate is perfectly carbon-efficient, because production of PEP and E4P from other substrates may involve carbon losses or competing fluxes. Likewise, extension of the boundary to phenylalanine introduces a decarboxylation. A correct quantitative statement always identifies which reactions and substrates are being counted.

Phosphate accounting provides a separate check. The original PEP and E4P each contain phosphate, and the first product DAHP retains one phosphate while inorganic phosphate is released. Dehydroquinate synthase removes the remaining phosphate from the original seven-carbon intermediate. Shikimate kinase then adds a new phosphate group from ATP. EPSPS releases the phosphate of the second PEP input while retaining the phosphate on the shikimate-derived part of EPSP. Chorismate synthase removes that final phosphate. These group transfers explain why chorismate contains no phosphate despite being synthesized through several phosphorylated intermediates. A molecule can be built from phosphorylated substrates without being a phosphorylated end product.

In an isolated net accounting of the seven canonical reactions, shikimate kinase consumes one ATP and the shikimate dehydrogenase reaction consumes one NADPH per chorismate formed. These values should not be confused with the full cellular cost of supplying the required PEP, E4P, ATP, reducing equivalents and cofactors. An enzyme that requires a metal ion or flavin to function does not necessarily consume a new molecule of that cofactor with each turnover. An enzyme can cycle between chemical states as part of catalysis and return to its initial state at the end of the cycle. The distinction between stoichiometric reactants and catalytic participants is especially valuable in biological energy calculations.

An exam question may describe a sudden reduction in NADPH availability and ask which direct shikimate reaction is most obviously constrained. The relevant step is reduction of dehydroshikimate, because this reaction directly draws on NADPH. However, a living plant's response to a broad decline in reducing power will affect many other processes. Photosynthetic carbon supply, oxidative protection and biosynthetic pathways may all change. The statement that a particular reaction uses NADPH is a mechanistic prediction of sensitivity, not proof that it alone determines whole-plant aromatic metabolite production. To establish a specific bottleneck, the investigator needs evidence connecting changes in the precursor, product and catalytic activity.

An analogous comparison concerns ATP availability. A low ATP pool can limit phosphorylation of shikimate, but it also changes central carbon allocation, transport and production of other metabolites. A more specific test would use a lesion that reduces shikimate kinase while leaving the general energy supply relatively intact. Under these circumstances the immediate capacity to convert shikimate into shikimate 3-phosphate falls. If shikimate is continually produced, its pool could grow. The observation of a larger shikimate pool would be compatible with this mechanism, but the investigator would still need to distinguish increased input from decreased consumption. The value of targeted perturbations is that they narrow the set of plausible explanations compared with generalized energy starvation.

The EPSPS reaction offers another distinction between stoichiometry and regulation. It requires two organic substrates. Raising the concentration of only shikimate 3-phosphate cannot maintain high product formation when PEP is absent. An inhibitor competing with PEP at the appropriate enzyme–substrate state can affect the apparent substrate requirement for catalysis. In an isolated kinetic assay performed at a fixed adequate concentration of shikimate 3-phosphate, varying PEP can reveal inhibition behaviour. In an intact plant the concentrations accessible to the enzyme are controlled by metabolism and compartmentation, and a simple kinetic result cannot be extrapolated directly to every tissue or developmental stage.

The effect of a partial block can be different from a complete loss of pathway capacity. If an enzyme is normally present in excess relative to the flux supplied by upstream reactions, a modest reduction in its maximal activity may have little effect on product formation. As remaining capacity falls below demand, pathway flux can become constrained. In this case the effect of further activity loss may be substantial. This is why the fraction by which enzyme abundance changes is not automatically equal to the fraction by which total pathway flux changes. The same principle applies to increased expression: an enzyme whose substrate delivery is already limiting cannot create unlimited additional product.

4. Chorismate and the aromatic amino acids

Chorismate mutase catalyses a rearrangement of chorismate to prephenate. The atoms are rearranged rather than added or removed; both compounds retain ten carbons. Prephenate is the common precursor for major phenylalanine and tyrosine routes. In the prevalent plant route, prephenate receives an amino group through transamination to form arogenate. The amino group typically comes from glutamate, which is correspondingly converted into 2-oxoglutarate. Arogenate still has ten carbons, but conversion to either phenylalanine or tyrosine removes one carbon as carbon dioxide. The resulting aromatic amino acids each contain nine carbons.

Arogenate dehydratase produces phenylalanine by a combined decarboxylation and dehydration that generates an aromatic ring. Arogenate dehydrogenase produces tyrosine by oxidative decarboxylation, retaining the ring hydroxyl group. The distinction is not that one amino acid requires a ring while the other does not; both products are aromatic. Their difference concerns the reaction chemistry and final ring substitution. Alternative pathways through phenylpyruvate or 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate occur in certain plant species and tissues. It is inaccurate to assume that all plants rely exclusively on one post-chorismate route.

Tryptophan biosynthesis diverges earlier. Anthranilate synthase acts on chorismate with nitrogen commonly supplied by glutamine, forming anthranilate and pyruvate. In the glutamine-dependent reaction, glutamate is also produced. The subsequent pathway transfers a phosphoribosyl group from PRPP to anthranilate, rearranges the product and forms indole 3-glycerol phosphate. Tryptophan synthase uses the indole moiety and serine to form tryptophan, with the associated release of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate from the indole glycerol phosphate reaction. The tryptophan skeleton is thus not explained by direct conversion of a ten-carbon chorismate molecule into an eleven-carbon amino acid; additional carbon substrates participate and carbon is lost along the route.

The separation between the chorismate mutase branch and anthranilate synthase branch has predictive value. If anthranilate synthase activity is specifically reduced, tryptophan synthesis is directly affected while the capacity of chorismate mutase to form prephenate is not directly removed. A rise in phenylalanine flux is possible when more chorismate becomes available, but it is not guaranteed because feedback inhibition, transport and other constraints may intervene. Conversely, restricting the synthesis of chorismate itself can reduce precursor supply to all three branches simultaneously.

Chorismate also gives rise to compounds outside the aromatic amino acid routes. Isochorismate participates in salicylic acid production in Arabidopsis and contributes to other biosynthetic processes. Folate and quinone metabolism provide additional examples of the significance of this branch point. The biological cost of a strong upstream block may therefore extend beyond shortage of proteinogenic amino acids. Supplying one downstream amino acid is not necessarily sufficient to restore growth if other chorismate-dependent compounds remain limiting.

The prephenate-to-arogenate reaction is an example of the logic of transamination rather than direct nitrogen fixation. An amino group is transferred from an existing amino donor to a keto acid. Nitrogen entering phenylalanine via glutamate was previously incorporated through plant nitrogen metabolism. Carbon and nitrogen pathways therefore converge in aromatic amino acid production. The carbon skeleton need not gain a carbon when the amino group is added. In a simplified atom-tracing question, supplying nitrogen-labelled glutamate can label newly synthesized arogenate and subsequently phenylalanine, even when the carbons of phenylalanine come from entirely different substrates.

The arogenate dehydratase and arogenate dehydrogenase branches provide a useful lesson about branch-specific perturbations. Imagine a plant line with reduced activity of a single arogenate dehydratase isoform. If the remaining isoforms cannot compensate, phenylalanine production through that route is expected to fall. Tyrosine production is not directly blocked by that mutation because it is catalysed by a different enzyme. The actual tyrosine concentration could increase, remain stable or decrease depending on arogenate availability, feedback and overall plant health. A branch-specific enzyme lesion is therefore not equivalent to upstream precursor depletion.

Anthranilate formation incorporates nitrogen while removing a three-carbon pyruvate fragment from chorismate. Anthranilate itself has seven carbons and an amino group. Subsequent reactions recruit additional carbon from the phosphoribosyl donor and serine. In the tryptophan synthase reaction, serine contributes the three-carbon amino acid side-chain skeleton while the indole unit supplies the aromatic heterocycle. This is why a nitrogenous specialized metabolite derived from tryptophan may retain the indole nitrogen even when a subsequent transformation removes the amino nitrogen of the side chain. The fate of each chemically distinct nitrogen atom must be traced separately rather than inferred from total nitrogen content.

Feedback regulation provides a mechanism for matching synthesis to demand. A downstream product can inhibit an upstream enzyme in its own branch; tryptophan feedback regulation of anthranilate synthase is a familiar example. A high tryptophan pool can reduce additional production through this step without directly inhibiting every chorismate-consuming enzyme. Phenylalanine and tyrosine branches also have regulated enzyme isoforms, with the details varying among species. The general principle is that feedback can restrain unnecessary consumption of shared precursor. This avoids both wasteful synthesis and depletion of metabolites needed elsewhere in the network.

A feedback-insensitive enzyme provides a useful conceptual experimental tool. If a mutant anthranilate synthase retains catalytic activity at high tryptophan concentration while the wild-type enzyme is inhibited, increased branch flux may occur in the mutant under conditions of sufficient precursor supply. It does not follow that tryptophan will increase without limit. The pathway still requires chorismate, nitrogen, PRPP, serine and other cellular resources. Furthermore, the pool can be drained into protein or specialized metabolism. The experiment tests the importance of one regulatory constraint, not the absence of all other constraints on the network.

Branch-point regulation and metabolic priorities

Phenylalanine synthesis is linked to an unusually broad set of demands. Growing tissues require phenylalanine for polypeptide formation, while differentiating xylem and fibres can require large amounts of phenylalanine-derived carbon for wall polymers. The cell does not allocate a separately labelled phenylalanine pool to every biological function merely because the final products are different. Flux through particular downstream enzymes, transport between compartments and the rates of protein synthesis and degradation influence how precursor is allocated. A tissue can increase phenylalanine synthesis during a period of increased specialized metabolic demand without necessarily maintaining a very large free phenylalanine pool.

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase consumes the free amino acid in a reaction that returns its nitrogen to the cellular ammonia/ammonium pool. Under physiological conditions this nitrogen can be reassimilated by nitrogen metabolism. The carbon skeleton passes into a pathway that often produces nitrogen-free molecules. Consequently a high flux through PAL does not imply that an equivalent quantity of nitrogen must be permanently lost from the tissue. Nitrogen and carbon are coupled at the aromatic amino acid stage, but their fates can diverge at the deamination step. This relationship connects the present topic with the GS–GOGAT cycle discussed in nitrogen metabolism.

Tyrosine illustrates an alternative kind of link between amino acid synthesis and specialized metabolism. Some plant lineages use tyrosine-derived precursors prominently in the production of particular pigments or nitrogenous compounds. Tyrosine and phenylalanine share a biosynthetic relationship but are not chemically interchangeable in all enzyme reactions. An enzyme recognizing the ring hydroxyl of tyrosine may discriminate against phenylalanine, while another enzyme may have broader substrate specificity. A route mapped in one species should therefore not be assigned to a different lineage solely because both make the same broad class of compounds. The relevant enzyme activities and their contribution to physiological flux must be considered.

The two major chorismate branches are also linked indirectly through regulation. A downstream shortage can alter feedback at its branch-entry enzyme, allowing increased demand for the common substrate. If this draws down the chorismate pool, a competing branch may receive less substrate even though its own enzyme abundance is unchanged. A different response is possible if upstream production increases enough to supply both demands. To predict competition correctly, ask whether the available precursor is limiting, whether the input can adjust and whether enzyme capacities permit the alternative flux. The branch-point drawing shows the possible routes, not their fixed fractional shares.

Consider a plant with a feedback-insensitive anthranilate synthase, abundant tryptophan and a limited rate of chorismate production. If the enzyme draws more chorismate into its branch, the prephenate branch might receive less. However, if the plant also increases flux through the common pathway, phenylalanine and tyrosine production could be maintained. A multiple-choice question that supplies neither information about precursor supply nor feedback regulation cannot justify an exact prediction for the magnitude of phenylalanine change. A defensible answer would distinguish the direct alteration in anthranilate synthase regulation from uncertain downstream changes in steady-state metabolite pools.

The broader evolutionary importance of plant aromatic amino acid pathways can be understood without treating every isoform as an exam fact. A gene duplication can produce two copies of a gene encoding a biosynthetic enzyme. If one copy maintains the essential function, the other may acquire different expression patterns or catalytic properties. This can provide a route for increasing specialized metabolic capacity in particular tissues while retaining the biochemical requirements of growth. Yet a sequence similarity alone cannot establish that a duplicated enzyme has a new physiological role. Functional assays, expression information and genetic tests are needed to establish the significance of a particular copy.

5. Phenolic chemistry and classification

A phenolic hydroxyl group is an OH group directly attached to an aromatic carbon. A hydroxyl group attached to an aliphatic carbon in the side chain is an alcohol group, not a phenolic group. Compare phenol, in which OH is attached to benzene, and benzyl alcohol, in which a CH2 group separates the ring from OH. Both contain an aromatic ring and one hydroxyl group, but only phenol has the defining phenolic linkage. Phenylalanine has an aromatic ring without a ring hydroxyl group, whereas tyrosine has a phenolic ring hydroxyl. This distinction is frequently more useful than memorizing the names of many minor specialized metabolites.

Phenolic hydroxyl groups can lose a proton to form phenolate ions. Delocalization of negative charge over the aromatic system contributes to the greater acidity of many phenols relative to ordinary aliphatic alcohols. The exact acidity depends on other substituents, their positions and the solvent. A compound with both a carboxyl and a phenolic hydroxyl group can have multiple ionizable groups. For example, a hydroxybenzoic acid may exist largely as a carboxylate at a pH where its phenolic hydroxyl remains predominantly protonated. It is incorrect to give every phenolic molecule a single universal pKa.

Phenolic molecules can participate in hydrogen bonding, interactions with proteins, oxidation-reduction reactions and, in some cases, metal coordination. Adjacent hydroxyl groups in a catechol motif can contribute to chelation and oxidation chemistry. However, the biological behaviour of a molecule also depends on conjugation, solvent, pH, localization and the other compounds present. A strong response in a chemical radical-scavenging assay does not by itself establish that the compound improves whole-plant stress tolerance. Biological availability and the relevant cellular concentrations must be considered.

Simple phenolic structures include phenol, catechol with two adjacent hydroxyl groups, resorcinol with hydroxyl groups in the 1,3 positions, and hydroquinone with hydroxyl groups in the 1,4 positions. These positional isomers illustrate that a formula alone cannot determine structure. Plants may contain many substituted or conjugated derivatives of such motifs; the simple parent names are reference structures, not a claim that every plant accumulates free parent compounds. A methoxy substituent is not a free hydroxyl, and glycosylation of a phenolic oxygen can alter ionization, solubility and reactivity.

A common classification separates hydroxybenzoic acids with a C6–C1 skeleton from hydroxycinnamic acids with a C6–C3 skeleton. The notation counts the six aromatic carbons and the carbon atoms of the substituent, not hydrogen or oxygen atoms. Benzoic acid itself is aromatic but has no phenolic OH; hydroxybenzoic acids such as salicylic and gallic acids do. Cinnamic acid is the unsubstituted phenylpropanoid parent, while p-coumaric acid contains a ring hydroxyl. Phenolic is a structural description, and phenylpropanoid is a description that refers to a biosynthetic family. A phenolic compound may belong to both categories or only the first.

Carbon skeleton classes provide clues but do not prove a pathway. Certain C6–C1 compounds can be formed by shortening the side chain of a phenylpropanoid precursor, while gallic acid can be produced through a route associated with an earlier shikimate intermediate. Consequently two related hydroxybenzoic acids may have different immediate precursors. Similarly, the presence of an aromatic ring does not identify shikimate as its exclusive source, since polyketide pathways can also construct aromatic rings. An isotope-tracing experiment, enzyme assay or genetic perturbation can provide evidence about origin beyond structural similarity.

Phenolic ionization can be explored using the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation for a simple monoprotic acid. If HA is the protonated phenolic group and A minus its conjugate base, pH equals pKa plus the logarithm to base ten of the concentration ratio of A minus to HA. The fraction deprotonated is one divided by one plus ten raised to the power pKa minus pH, under the usual dilute-solution approximation. For an illustrative phenol with pKa 10, at pH 7 the ratio is 0.001 and the deprotonated fraction is approximately 0.001, or 0.1 percent. At pH 10 the ratio is one and the two forms are equally abundant. The result does not imply that every plant phenolic group has pKa 10 or that a polyprotic molecule obeys a single-ionization model over all pH ranges.

For a hydroxybenzoic acid, the carboxyl group generally ionizes more readily than the phenolic hydroxyl group. An illustrative molecule with carboxyl pKa 4 and phenolic pKa 10 would be predominantly carboxylate but retain its protonated phenolic hydroxyl around pH 7. This difference is important for thinking about solubility and transport. The molecule is not simply uncharged because its phenolic hydroxyl remains protonated. Nor is every oxygen atom negatively charged because one proton has been lost. The net charge must be calculated from the ionization state of all relevant groups. In real molecules, interactions between ionizable groups can change their pKa values.

Oxidation of an appropriate diphenol can lead to quinone formation. The loss of electrons and protons modifies the conjugated system, potentially giving products that react with nucleophilic groups in other biomolecules. In plant tissue damaged by cutting, previously separated substrates and enzymes may come into contact. Polyphenol oxidases can catalyse oxidation of suitable phenolic substrates, and subsequent reactions can produce brown material. Browning is therefore not simply the spontaneous appearance of a pre-existing brown pigment. The precise outcome depends on the available substrates, oxygen, enzyme activity and subsequent chemistry. This illustrates how compartmental organization can influence the visible consequences of phenolic metabolism.

The C6–C1 versus C6–C3 distinction can be practiced with a structural count. Salicylic acid has a six-carbon ring and one carboxyl carbon; the oxygen of its hydroxyl group does not add a carbon. Caffeic acid has a six-carbon ring and a three-carbon unsaturated carboxylic acid side chain. Methylating one phenolic OH may add a methyl carbon to the molecule without transforming the parent hydroxycinnamate framework into a different biosynthetic skeleton class. Class notation often refers to the underlying scaffold, not the total molecular carbon count after every possible substituent is added.

Benzoate diversity and aromatic substituents

Consider four members of the hydroxybenzoic acid family. In salicylic acid, the hydroxyl lies adjacent to the carboxyl-bearing ring carbon. In para-hydroxybenzoic acid, the hydroxyl lies opposite that position. Protocatechuic acid contains hydroxyl groups at positions 3 and 4, while gallic acid contains hydroxyls at positions 3, 4 and 5. The numbering refers to the ring position bearing the carboxyl group as position 1. The molecules share a seven-carbon scaffold, but the arrangement and number of hydroxyls change their chemistry. Positional relationships can influence intramolecular hydrogen bonding, metal interaction and susceptibility to oxidation. One cannot reliably rank every biological activity simply by counting hydroxyl groups.

Vanillic acid contains a hydroxyl and a methoxy substituent on a benzoate framework. A methoxy group introduces a carbon in addition to the seven-carbon parent skeleton. Thus, vanillic acid has eight total carbons, even though it is conventionally classified with hydroxybenzoic acid derivatives. This distinction between total molecular carbon count and scaffold designation also applies to esterified or glycosylated phenolics. A question asking the number of carbons in the parent biosynthetic framework differs from a question asking the molecular formula of a derivative. If the question gives a structural drawing, count the carbons actually present before assigning the answer.

Benzoate formation from a phenylpropanoid requires removal of two side-chain carbons rather than simple loss of the amino group from phenylalanine. PAL generates cinnamate, which still possesses the C6–C3 scaffold. In a CoA-dependent beta-oxidative route, side-chain shortening follows chemistry analogous to a cycle of fatty acid beta oxidation, generating a shorter aromatic acyl product. Other routes can shorten a suitable free aromatic acid through chemistry involving aldehyde intermediates and subsequent oxidation. The point at NET level is that phenylalanine-derived benzenoids can originate through more than one side-chain-shortening mechanism. There is no requirement that every benzoate use one identical route.

An important study of petunia petal metabolism by Boatright and colleagues used stable isotope tracing to investigate how phenylalanine supplies benzenoid products. The analysis supported contributions from both CoA-dependent and CoA-independent routes in the examined floral tissues. This type of result helps explain why an isolated enzyme assay demonstrating a beta-oxidative route does not prove that all benzenoid production occurs through that route. A living tissue may use parallel pathways, and the relative contributions can vary. Isotope tracing combined with a network model allows alternative flux arrangements to be evaluated, although the conclusions depend on which intermediates were measured and the assumptions of the model.

Salicylic acid provides a special example of biosynthetic diversity because it can arise through isochorismate-associated metabolism or phenylalanine-associated routes, with their relative importance depending on species and physiological context. This chapter does not need to repeat the immune signalling or biosynthetic detail of the salicylic acid hormone node. It is enough to recognize that the same final phenolic acid can be supplied through more than one biosynthetic route. The existence of alternative origins is a warning against identifying an enzyme block from the disappearance of one end product without further measurements. An alternative route may partially compensate for the lost route.

6. Gallic acid: a branch before chorismate

Gallic acid is 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid. Its seven-carbon framework consists of a benzene ring and a carboxyl carbon. The three ring hydroxyl groups influence its chemical reactivity and interactions. Gallic acid supplies galloyl groups for a variety of plant compounds, including hydrolysable tannins and galloylated flavan-3-ols. The fact that it is phenolic does not mean that it necessarily passes through phenylalanine and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase. A branch associated with dehydroshikimate is an important example of a shikimate-derived phenolic that can bypass chorismate and the aromatic amino acid branch.

The route from 3-dehydroshikimate to gallic acid involves oxidation and aromatization. The early precursor already has seven carbons, so no carbon shortening is required to produce the C6–C1 framework. The usual shikimate dehydrogenase reaction instead reduces dehydroshikimate to shikimate. These are alternative chemical fates of the same intermediate. A diagram that places gallic acid obligatorily after phenylalanine misses the essential point of this branch. Evidence for contributions of particular dehydrogenase isoforms has been obtained in species such as grapevine, but enzyme participation and relative flux are not necessarily identical across plant taxa.

Bontpart and colleagues investigated grapevine shikimate dehydrogenases, combining biochemical assays and developmental information to examine their role in gallic acid production. Their work implicates particular isoforms in this branch. The important distinction is between an enzyme capable of converting a substrate under experimental conditions and its actual physiological contribution in a living tissue. Enzyme activity, tissue expression, developmental correspondence and changes in metabolite abundance are mutually informative, yet no single observation establishes the full quantitative contribution of an enzyme under every condition. The experiment is valuable because it gives a mechanistic alternative to classifying compounds by their final structure alone.

Gallic acid can be activated through formation of glucose esters such as beta-glucogallin in galloyl metabolism. Transfer of galloyl groups to suitable acceptors supplies more complex compounds. Hydrolysable tannins contain ester linkages involving gallic acid or related oxidatively coupled galloyl derivatives, whereas condensed tannins are oligomeric or polymeric flavan-3-ol-derived compounds. The details of tannin architecture belong to the later flavonoid and tannin node. Here the distinction establishes how two products with similar astringent properties may originate from different portions of the biosynthetic network.

The gallic acid branch illustrates the difference between an upstream inhibitor and a downstream branch inhibitor. A lesion that prevents DAHP formation restricts the production of all subsequent shikimate intermediates, including the precursor available for the dehydroshikimate branch. An EPSPS lesion occurs after that branch has separated. It directly prevents conversion of shikimate 3-phosphate into EPSP but does not remove the catalytic capacity to form dehydroshikimate. It would therefore be incorrect to conclude that every shikimate-derived phenolic must immediately disappear when EPSPS is inhibited. Over time, however, regulatory responses and damage to growth could still influence formation of gallic acid. Pathway topology predicts which direct reactions are lost; physiological measurements establish the resulting flux.

A comparative enzyme experiment could test two grapevine dehydrogenase isoforms under identical substrate and cofactor conditions. Suppose isoform A mainly forms shikimate while isoform B forms detectable gallic acid from dehydroshikimate. This would support a distinction in catalytic behaviour. To establish a contribution to gallic acid accumulation in the plant, researchers would also need to consider isoform expression in the relevant tissue and the availability of appropriate substrates and cofactor states. A gene knockout or complementation experiment can provide additional evidence. If several isoforms contribute similar reactions, knocking out one gene may produce only a partial phenotype. Such genetic redundancy is a reason not to equate lack of a dramatic phenotype with absence of biochemical function.

From a phenolic acid to a conjugated product

A free phenolic acid and an ester containing the same phenolic acyl group are distinct metabolites. Esterification occurs when a carboxyl group participates in formation of an ester linkage with an appropriate hydroxyl group on an acceptor molecule. In galloyl metabolism a glucose-containing ester can serve as an activated intermediate or a conjugated form. The galloyl-derived aromatic portion can be recognized within the resulting product, even when its carboxyl group is no longer free. This is important for understanding hydrolysable tannins: hydrolysis of their ester linkages may release smaller compounds, and the identities of those products give clues to their constituent groups.

Imagine comparing two hypothetical plants with identical amounts of gallic acid when measured after complete hydrolysis of their extracts. In one plant most of the gallic acid is present as free acid, while in the other most is esterified. The reported total galloyl-derived amount could be the same, but the concentrations of free gallic acid and conjugated products would differ. These forms can differ in localization, interaction with proteins and accessibility to enzymes. Consequently a method reporting total phenolic acid after hydrolysis must not be interpreted as a direct measurement of the free intracellular precursor. The analytical preparation defines which chemical pool is being measured.

There is a similar distinction between the availability of gallic acid and the rate at which tannin-related products are formed. If a galloyltransfer reaction becomes limiting, free precursor or an activated derivative may accumulate while the downstream product declines. Conversely, an efficient transfer reaction may keep the free gallic acid pool small even during high synthesis. An assay measuring only free gallic acid cannot distinguish these situations. Measuring both the precursor and the product, preferably through a time course, can help separate increased synthesis from restricted consumption. This is a direct application of pool balance rather than an additional biosynthetic pathway to memorize.

7. Acetate–malonate chemistry and mixed origins

Acetyl-CoA carries a two-carbon acetyl group in a thioester linkage. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase uses ATP and bicarbonate to produce malonyl-CoA, a three-carbon compound. When malonyl-CoA acts as an extender in a polyketide reaction, the newly added carboxyl carbon is normally lost as carbon dioxide. The net contribution of the extender is two carbons. Decarboxylation facilitates carbon-carbon bond formation; it is not a wasteful afterthought. Fatty acid synthesis also uses related decarboxylative condensation chemistry, but the enzyme systems and final products differ.

Plant type III polyketide synthases typically use CoA-linked starter and extender substrates. Repeated additions create an elongated chain containing carbonyl groups. Different cyclization reactions and subsequent modifications can transform such a chain into aromatic compounds. This strategy differs from the shikimate pathway, which assembles a seven-carbon skeleton from PEP and E4P and subsequently adds another PEP-derived unit. Both routes are capable of contributing to aromatic natural products, so finding a benzene ring in a molecule does not identify its biosynthetic route without additional evidence.

Chalcone synthase provides the classic example of mixed origin. Its usual starter is p-coumaroyl-CoA, a nine-carbon phenylpropanoid-derived substrate. Three malonyl-CoA molecules contribute two carbons each after decarboxylation. The carbon account is nine plus six, giving a fifteen-carbon chalcone skeleton. One aromatic ring and the three-carbon linker derive from the phenylpropanoid starter; the second ring is assembled from the three malonate-derived units. Subsequent reactions can yield flavonoid scaffolds. This explains why it is correct to describe flavonoids as phenolic compounds of mixed shikimate–acetate origin rather than claiming that every carbon comes from phenylalanine.

Chalcone synthase and stilbene synthase provide a useful contrast. Both can use the same p-coumaroyl-CoA starter and three malonyl-CoA extenders, but the cyclization and termination chemistry differ. The chalcone product retains fifteen carbons while resveratrol, a stilbene, has fourteen. The extra carbon is lost during stilbene formation. Thus an identical list of substrates does not guarantee identical product carbon counts or ring arrangements. The enzyme determines how the intermediate is processed. Detailed branches are examined in the subsequent nodes; the current objective is to recognize shared precursors, mixed biosynthetic origin and the chemical basis of structural diversity.

The name acetate–malonate pathway reflects the relationship between the two-carbon acetyl unit and the three-carbon activated extender. The bicarbonate-derived carbon added by acetyl-CoA carboxylase is temporarily incorporated into malonyl-CoA and is typically removed during chain extension. If a tracer labels this added carboxyl carbon, much of that label would be expected to leave during the decarboxylative condensation itself under an idealized isolated reaction. Labelling the two acetyl-derived carbons instead permits incorporation into the growing product. In a living plant, released labelled carbon dioxide could subsequently be reassimilated, so the observed isotopic pattern may be more complicated. The distinction between a direct reaction and whole-organism carbon recycling is essential when interpreting tracer data.

The term polyketide does not mean that the final product must contain many free keto groups. The growing intermediates contain repeated carbonyl-bearing units, but cyclization, reduction, aromatization and other modifications can substantially change the final functional groups. A final aromatic phenolic may therefore have no obvious resemblance to a linear chain of acetate units. Reconstructing biosynthesis from structural formula alone requires considerable caution. In examinations, a known enzyme and its specified substrates often provide more reliable evidence about biosynthetic origin than the appearance of the final product.

Competition for malonyl-CoA can influence production of flavonoid precursors. Suppose an engineered line increases the capacity of chalcone synthase while malonyl-CoA delivery remains fixed. If the original enzyme was limiting, production may initially rise. Once extender availability becomes limiting, further increases in enzyme abundance need not increase flux. The same general argument applies to a shortage of p-coumaroyl-CoA. Because these two precursor supplies have different biosynthetic origins, increasing supply of only one cannot overcome a severe shortage of the other. This is a conceptual preview of metabolic engineering rather than a claim that one enzyme controls all plant flavonoid accumulation.

Consider a simplified tissue producing 12 nanomoles of p-coumaroyl-CoA and 24 nanomoles of malonyl-CoA per minute exclusively for chalcone production. The starter supply permits 12 nanomoles of chalcone per minute, but the three-to-one requirement of malonyl-CoA permits only eight. At the maximum feasible rate, eight nanomoles of starter and all 24 nanomoles of extender are consumed, releasing 24 nanomoles of carbon dioxide during the extension reactions. Four nanomoles of starter remain available for other uses or accumulation in this toy model. The calculation assumes quantitative conversion, no competing reactions, constant supply and no additional limitation from enzyme kinetics.

8. Experimental evidence and quantitative reasoning

A metabolic map is a model of connected chemical transformations, not a direct measurement of rates. Researchers can test the map by incubating purified enzymes with substrates and cofactors and determining whether the predicted product appears. A control without active enzyme distinguishes spontaneous conversion from enzyme-dependent conversion, while omission of a required substrate or cofactor tests reaction specificity. Detecting the correct product is stronger evidence than monitoring the disappearance of substrate alone. Yet a purified enzyme assay establishes catalytic capacity rather than automatically proving that the same flux occurs inside a particular plant tissue.

The inhibition of EPSPS by glyphosate provides a historically important example of localizing a pathway block. Steinrücken and Amrhein identified EPSPS as the inhibited enzyme in 1980. Earlier and complementary measurements of shikimate accumulation were consistent with interference between shikimate and chorismate. In a direct EPSPS assay the enzyme's substrates are shikimate 3-phosphate and PEP; a fall in product formation on addition of an inhibitor can be related to the defined reaction. In an intact plant, upstream shikimate accumulation is an indirect readout influenced by additional pathway steps. A metabolite that accumulates in a treated leaf is not necessarily the immediate enzyme substrate.

Stable isotope tracing can distinguish sources of carbon. Imagine providing a plant tissue with a labelled precursor and measuring the isotopic pattern of its phenolic products. A carbon label can be retained in one product region and lost as carbon dioxide from another. Interpretation requires knowledge of which positions are labelled, whether the precursor reaches the relevant compartment and whether central metabolism redistributes the label. Label found in an end product demonstrates that carbon from the supplied substrate can reach it; this alone does not establish that a single direct reaction connects the two. Time-resolved patterns and positional information strengthen the inference.

For an illustrative carbon balance, assume a chalcone is produced exclusively from one C9 starter and three malonyl-CoA units. Before decarboxylation, these four substrates supply eighteen carbons in their transferable carbon groups: nine from the starter and nine from the extenders. Three extender carboxyl carbons are released as carbon dioxide. The product retains fifteen carbons, so fifteen divided by eighteen, or five sixths, is retained in the product. The calculation excludes the CoA carrier and the ATP and bicarbonate required to synthesize malonyl-CoA. If the question instead asks what fraction of product carbon derives from malonate, the correct denominator is fifteen, giving six fifteenths or forty percent.

Consider a simplified steady-state chorismate pool with input of 100 nmol per gram fresh mass per hour. Suppose 55 units enter the prephenate branch, 30 enter the anthranilate branch, and 15 enter other pathways. If the anthranilate flux is reduced to 15 while input and the other branch rates initially remain unchanged, input exceeds output by 15 units per hour. The immediate prediction is accumulation of chorismate at that rate, not necessarily diversion of exactly 15 units into phenylalanine. At a later steady state, input and output must balance again, possibly through feedback-mediated reduction of input, increased competing fluxes or both. This invented dataset illustrates why branch inhibition cannot be interpreted from arithmetic alone without specifying time and regulatory assumptions.

Metabolite concentration and metabolic flux are related but not equivalent. A large pool may turn over slowly, whereas a small pool can support rapid production if its replenishment rate is high. In an idealized system, the change of pool amount per unit time equals the sum of rates of formation minus the sum of rates of removal. At steady state this change is zero, not because reactions cease, but because their rates balance. Comparing a mutant with a control at a single time point can suggest a metabolic disturbance but cannot by itself determine the rates of all contributing processes. Measurements of labelling over time, product formation and enzyme activity add complementary information.

A rescue experiment is designed to test whether supplying a downstream product bypasses an upstream block. If a plant cell culture with a lesion in an aromatic amino acid branch grows better when the appropriate amino acid is supplied, this is consistent with a shortage of that product contributing to the phenotype. The control should include the wild type with and without supplementation and the mutant under the same conditions. Rescue does not prove the identity of the defective enzyme if multiple upstream defects could be bypassed by the same product. Failure to rescue is also ambiguous because the supplied compound might not enter the cell or organelle in sufficient amounts, or additional essential products might be missing.

Metabolic measurements need appropriate normalization. If an experimental plant has less fresh mass because of altered water content, a metabolite expressed in nanomoles per gram fresh mass may appear elevated without a proportional increase in total amount per organ. Dry mass, protein content, tissue area or cell count may be suitable alternative normalizations depending on the question. A change in fresh-mass-normalized concentration is a real measurement but must be interpreted in the context of the chosen denominator. This matters when comparing stressed and unstressed plants, because stress can change both metabolism and hydration.

For an illustrative pulse-labelling experiment, imagine a precursor pool initially containing no labelled carbon. After addition of a labelled precursor, phenylalanine is 20 percent labelled at five minutes and 50 percent labelled at twenty minutes, while a downstream phenolic is 2 percent and 18 percent labelled at those respective times. These invented observations are consistent with a labelled precursor entering phenylalanine before appreciable transfer into the phenolic. They do not establish direct one-step conversion because multiple intervening reactions could produce the delay. A large pre-existing pool of unlabelled phenolic could also dilute incoming label. Interpretation should include both fractional enrichment and measured pool size.

A change in transcript abundance is useful but does not directly measure reaction rate. An mRNA may increase after exposure to ultraviolet light, yet the encoded enzyme may be translated slowly, degraded rapidly or limited by substrate availability. Conversely, enzyme activity can change through regulation without a detectable increase in its transcript. To relate gene expression to phenolic accumulation, it is useful to compare the time courses of transcripts, protein or activity and metabolite production. A temporal sequence consistent with the proposed mechanism strengthens interpretation, while alternative explanations such as reduced product degradation must remain open until tested.

Reading real experimental results without overclaiming

A strong inference about the location of a metabolic block can come from a combination of measurements that individually remain ambiguous. Suppose an enzyme inhibitor causes the accumulation of a precursor in intact tissue, reduces formation of a downstream product and inhibits a particular purified enzyme in vitro. If those observations are consistent with the substrates and products of the same reaction, they support a causal interpretation more strongly than any one observation alone. The purified assay narrows the possible target, while the intact-tissue measurements connect it to physiology. Controls for generalized injury remain necessary, especially if the treatment is prolonged or the compound affects several processes.

The 1980 work identifying EPSPS as a glyphosate-sensitive enzyme can be understood in precisely this way. Accumulation of shikimate suggested interference with its conversion to later products. Direct biochemical investigation then identified the inhibited catalytic activity. The central lesson is not that accumulation uniquely proves which enzyme is inhibited. Shikimate itself is not the immediate substrate of EPSPS; the kinase reaction intervenes. Feedback, transport and equilibration can transmit the effects of a later block upstream. A strong examination answer distinguishes the observed accumulation marker from the actual enzyme reaction that has been identified.

For a simple pulse-chase thought experiment, provide a labelled aromatic precursor for a limited interval and then replace it with unlabelled precursor. A direct downstream intermediate may acquire label after the precursor and later lose enrichment as unlabelled input replaces it. A very stable product can retain label after the precursor pool has mostly returned to its unlabelled form. This persistence does not mean that the product is still being synthesized rapidly. It may indicate slow turnover. Different temporal behaviours of labelling in intermediate and product pools can therefore reveal information that a single endpoint concentration cannot provide.

An additional issue is discrimination between isotope enrichment and absolute labelled amount. Suppose a pool contains 100 nanomoles of metabolite, of which 10 percent is labelled. Its labelled amount is 10 nanomoles. A second pool contains 20 nanomoles, of which 30 percent is labelled. Its labelled amount is only six nanomoles. The second pool has higher fractional enrichment, but the first contains more labelled material. A student comparing the percentages alone might infer the wrong relative transfer of labelled carbon. The quantity relevant to a particular question depends on whether the goal is to estimate label enrichment, total labelled product or metabolic turnover.

Identification of a compound by mass spectrometry also requires care. Two positional isomers may have the same elemental composition and molecular mass but differ in their structure. Salicylic acid and para-hydroxybenzoic acid are examples of isomeric hydroxybenzoates. Measuring the mass of a molecular ion alone does not establish which isomer is present. Chromatographic separation, comparison with authentic standards and suitable fragmentation patterns can strengthen the identification. At the level of examination reasoning, the essential point is that molecular mass, structural identity and biosynthetic origin are three different pieces of information. One measurement does not automatically establish all three.

When comparing wild type and a mutant, complementation is particularly valuable for distinguishing the effect of the target gene from an unrelated mutation or developmental difference. Restoring the functional gene to the mutant and observing a restoration of the expected biochemical phenotype supports a causal link. However, the expression pattern and localization of the introduced gene matter. Expression at an inappropriate developmental stage could fail to restore the relevant tissue response even if the protein is catalytically active. Good experimental interpretation therefore includes both genetic identity and biological context.

9. Physiological roles and limits of classification

Phenolic compounds have diverse roles because their structural features can be recruited for distinct tasks. Lignin-related aromatic metabolism contributes to secondary wall properties and the integrity of water-conducting tissues. Soluble phenolics can absorb ultraviolet radiation or participate in defence. Pigments derived from phenolic pathways contribute to interactions with pollinators and seed dispersers. Galloylated compounds can influence interactions with proteins and feeding organisms. These functions depend on which molecules are made, where they are accumulated and when they are deployed. The presence of a general functional group does not give every phenolic the same ecological activity.

The separation between plastidic aromatic amino acid synthesis and many downstream reactions also has physiological consequences. A precursor generated in one compartment may be transported into another before it is converted into a specialized product. Storage in a vacuole, association with a wall or incorporation into an insoluble polymer can change how a product interacts with the cytoplasm. Hence a high total phenolic concentration in a tissue extract may not reflect a high free concentration at an enzyme's site of action. Tissue homogenization removes the very compartment boundaries that can govern the molecule's biological effect.

The main transition to the next chapter is the reaction catalysed by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, or PAL, which removes ammonia from phenylalanine to generate cinnamate. Its carbon skeleton remains nine-carbon; the nitrogen originally assimilated into the amino acid is no longer attached. Cinnamate can be hydroxylated and activated as a CoA ester, providing the entry to the major phenylpropanoid branches. This transition connects nitrogen assimilation with carbon-rich specialized chemistry. It also shows why an amino acid-derived specialized product does not necessarily contain nitrogen in its final structure. The next chapter follows this downstream chemistry into structural and soluble phenylpropanoids.

10. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Aromatic means fragrant. Aromaticity is an electronic property of a cyclic conjugated system. Benzene-like odour is not a criterion.
  2. Every aromatic molecule is phenolic. Phenylalanine and benzoic acid are aromatic but lack a ring-bound hydroxyl.
  3. Benzyl alcohol is a phenol. Its OH is attached to a side-chain carbon. Identify the carbon bonded to oxygen.
  4. All shikimate products are aromatic. Shikimate and chorismate are nonaromatic intermediates; aromaticity develops in downstream reactions.
  5. E4P contributes two carbons. The full four-carbon E4P skeleton contributes to DAHP, together with three PEP carbons.
  6. Only one PEP enters the pathway. DAHP synthase and EPSPS each use PEP; chorismate has ten carbons.
  7. Dehydration removes CO₂. Dehydration removes water. Decarboxylation removes carbon dioxide; the carbon balance distinguishes them.
  8. Seven reactions require seven separate proteins. In many plants two steps occur on a bifunctional dehydratase/dehydrogenase protein.
  9. Shikimate kinase adds carbon. It adds phosphate from ATP; the seven-carbon skeleton is unchanged.
  10. Glyphosate directly inhibits PAL. The established target is EPSPS, upstream of chorismate. Shikimate accumulation is indirect evidence.
  11. All phenolics pass through PAL. Gallic acid can originate from dehydroshikimate; polyketide-derived phenolics may have different origins.
  12. A C6–C1 skeleton proves a direct shikimate origin. Side-chain shortening of phenylpropanoids can also produce benzoate derivatives.
  13. Malonyl-CoA adds three retained carbons per extension. Decarboxylative condensation retains two of the three carbons.
  14. Flavonoids derive entirely from phenylalanine. The usual chalcone skeleton contains nine starter carbons and six malonate-derived carbons.
  15. A larger metabolite pool proves higher flux. Pool size reflects formation and removal; time-resolved evidence is needed to infer flux.
  16. Blocking one chorismate branch necessarily increases another by the same amount. Feedback can change input and other outputs. Specify the time and assumptions.
  17. Phenolic antioxidant assays prove improved plant fitness. Chemical reactivity is not equivalent to a physiological outcome; localization, concentrations and controls matter.

11. Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which two compounds enter DAHP synthase?

  1. PEP and E4P
  2. Acetyl-CoA and pyruvate
  3. Shikimate and ATP
  4. Chorismate and glutamine
Answer and explanation

A. PEP and E4P — PEP supplies three carbons and E4P four. Shikimate kinase uses ATP later.

2. Part B — Which enzyme directly produces chorismate?

  1. Shikimate kinase
  2. Chorismate synthase
  3. Chorismate mutase
  4. Anthranilate synthase
Answer and explanation

B. Chorismate synthase — Chorismate synthase eliminates phosphate from EPSP. Mutase consumes chorismate.

3. Part B — Which molecule contains a phenolic hydroxyl?

  1. Phenylalanine
  2. Benzyl alcohol
  3. Tyrosine
  4. Benzoic acid
Answer and explanation

C. Tyrosine — Tyrosine carries OH directly attached to its aromatic ring. The other options do not.

4. Part B — What is the usual reductant for shikimate formation?

  1. ATP
  2. FMN as carbon source
  3. CoA
  4. NADPH
Answer and explanation

D. NADPH — Shikimate dehydrogenase reduces dehydroshikimate using NADPH. ATP is used by the next kinase.

5. Part B — What is the target enzyme of glyphosate in the shikimate pathway?

  1. EPSPS
  2. PAL
  3. Chalcone synthase
  4. Chorismate mutase
Answer and explanation

A. EPSPS — EPSPS uses shikimate 3-phosphate and PEP. The other enzymes are in downstream branches.

6. Part B — Which intermediate is the common precursor of all three proteinogenic aromatic amino acids?

  1. Malonyl-CoA
  2. Chorismate
  3. Cinnamate
  4. Gallic acid
Answer and explanation

B. Chorismate — Chorismate feeds the prephenate and anthranilate routes.

7. Part B — Which compound is a C6–C1 phenolic acid?

  1. Caffeic acid
  2. Phenylalanine
  3. Gallic acid
  4. Cinnamic acid
Answer and explanation

C. Gallic acid — Gallic acid has a six-carbon aromatic ring and one carboxyl carbon; caffeic and cinnamic acids have C6–C3 skeletons.

8. Part B — What is the usual net carbon contribution of each malonyl-CoA extender?

  1. One
  2. Three
  3. Four
  4. Two
Answer and explanation

D. Two — The added carboxyl carbon is released during decarboxylative condensation.

9. Part B — Which enzyme forms prephenate from chorismate?

  1. Chorismate mutase
  2. Shikimate kinase
  3. Anthranilate synthase
  4. PAL
Answer and explanation

A. Chorismate mutase — Mutase rearranges chorismate; anthranilate synthase forms the tryptophan branch.

10. Part B — The common arogenate route to phenylalanine uses which enzyme in its final step?

  1. Arogenate dehydrogenase
  2. Arogenate dehydratase
  3. EPSPS
  4. DAHP synthase
Answer and explanation

B. Arogenate dehydratase — Arogenate dehydratase produces phenylalanine, whereas dehydrogenase produces tyrosine.

11. Part B — Which amino acid supplies the amino acid side chain in the tryptophan synthase beta reaction?

  1. Glutamate
  2. Alanine
  3. Serine
  4. Phenylalanine
Answer and explanation

C. Serine — Serine combines with indole to form tryptophan.

12. Part B — Which substrate is used by PAL?

  1. Chorismate
  2. Tyrosine in the standard PAL reaction
  3. Shikimate
  4. Phenylalanine
Answer and explanation

D. Phenylalanine — PAL deaminates phenylalanine to cinnamate. Other related ammonia-lyases can use tyrosine.

13. Part C — PEP and E4P are delivered at 60 and 40 nmol/min, respectively. No competing reactions occur. What is the maximum chorismate flux?

  1. 30 nmol/min
  2. 40 nmol/min
  3. 60 nmol/min
  4. 100 nmol/min
Answer and explanation

A. 30 nmol/min — Two PEP per chorismate limit production to 60/2 = 30. E4P allows 40, so PEP is limiting.

14. Part C — A specific anthranilate synthase lesion leaves upstream synthesis intact initially. Which direct prediction is best?

  1. All chorismate synthesis stops
  2. Anthranilate production capacity falls
  3. PAL activity necessarily doubles
  4. Gallic acid production necessarily ceases
Answer and explanation

B. Anthranilate production capacity falls — Anthranilate synthase acts at one downstream branch; other responses depend on regulation.

15. Part C — A mutant enzyme consumes NADPH when given dehydroshikimate. Which additional observation most directly supports shikimate dehydrogenase activity?

  1. Higher gene transcript abundance
  2. ATP accumulation
  3. Formation of shikimate
  4. Loss of chlorophyll
Answer and explanation

C. Formation of shikimate — Direct product measurement distinguishes the predicted reaction from unrelated NADPH consumption.

16. Part C — If 18 nmol/min malonyl-CoA and 10 nmol/min p-coumaroyl-CoA are available exclusively to CHS, what is the upper bound of chalcone production?

  1. 10
  2. 18
  3. 28
  4. 6
Answer and explanation

D. 6 — Three extenders are required per product; 18/3 = 6, below the starter limit of 10.

17. Part C — Input to chorismate is 80 units/h, prephenate flux 40, anthranilate 25 and other loss 15. If anthranilate falls to 5 and all other rates initially stay fixed, what is initial accumulation?

  1. 20 units/h
  2. 0
  3. 5 units/h
  4. 40 units/h
Answer and explanation

A. 20 units/h — Input minus output = 80 − (40+5+15) = 20. This is not necessarily a later steady state.

18. Part C — An isolated EPSPS block occurs after the dehydroshikimate branch. Which outcome is directly compatible with pathway topology?

  1. Gallic acid synthesis is necessarily abolished
  2. The direct catalytic route to gallic acid may remain available
  3. All upstream compounds must vanish
  4. Phenylalanine synthesis no longer requires chorismate
Answer and explanation

B. The direct catalytic route to gallic acid may remain available — The early branch can receive precursor without EPSPS, although long-term physiology may change.

19. Part C — A C15 chalcone is synthesized from one fully carbon-labelled C9 starter and three unlabelled malonyl-CoA extenders, without recycling. How many labelled carbons are retained?

  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 9
  4. 15
Answer and explanation

C. 9 — The starter supplies nine retained carbons; the three malonate units supply six unlabelled carbons.

20. Part C — A metabolite pool stays constant during rapid incorporation of labelled carbon. What follows?

  1. There is no metabolism
  2. The pathway is blocked
  3. The concentration must increase
  4. Formation and removal can balance while turnover continues
Answer and explanation

D. Formation and removal can balance while turnover continues — Steady pool size does not imply zero flux; labelling demonstrates turnover.

21. Part C — A phenolic group has pKa 10. At pH 9, what fraction is deprotonated in a monoprotic model?

  1. About 9.1%
  2. 50%
  3. 90.9%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

A. About 9.1% — A−/HA = 10^−1 = 0.1; fraction = 0.1/1.1 = 0.0909.

22. Part C — An enzyme variant retains anthranilate synthase activity despite high tryptophan. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Tryptophan becomes independent of all substrates
  2. One feedback constraint has been weakened
  3. Chorismate mutase must be inactive
  4. The mutation creates a new carbon source
Answer and explanation

B. One feedback constraint has been weakened — Feedback insensitivity removes a regulatory limitation, not material requirements.

23. Part C — In a pulse experiment phenylalanine labels before a phenolic product. Which conclusion is best?

  1. The phenolic is necessarily formed by one direct reaction
  2. The phenolic cannot derive from phenylalanine
  3. The temporal pattern is consistent with precursor–product transfer but not conclusive alone
  4. No label entered the pathway
Answer and explanation

C. The temporal pattern is consistent with precursor–product transfer but not conclusive alone — Intervening steps and pool dilution can explain delays; further evidence is necessary.

24. Part C — A purified dehydrogenase isoform produces gallic acid. What additional evidence most strengthens its proposed in vivo role?

  1. An unrelated enzyme uses ATP
  2. All other plant phenolics disappear
  3. Its protein is larger than another isoform
  4. A specific genetic perturbation alters gallic acid formation and complementation restores it
Answer and explanation

D. A specific genetic perturbation alters gallic acid formation and complementation restores it — Functional genetic evidence complements catalytic capacity. Expression and localization also matter.

12. Revision and mastery

Trace PEP and E4P to chorismate, distinguish its aromatic amino acid branches, recognize the earlier gallic acid branch, and account for mixed malonate-derived products. Use reaction chemistry rather than compound names alone to reason through changes in flux.
