1. The developmental problem: a repeated but flexible body

A seedling may begin with a very small shoot apex, yet the mature plant may contain hundreds of leaves and many independent branches. The increase in size is only one part of this transformation. Each newly produced leaf occupies a particular position on a stem, each stem segment has an internal organization, and some of the junctions between leaves and stems contain a new centre capable of producing an entire lateral shoot. A satisfactory explanation of shoot development must therefore account for the repetition of a structural unit and for the selective use of the developmental possibilities created by that repetition. The mature shape of a plant is not a simple enlargement of its embryo.

Consider two young plants that have produced the same number of leaves. One has a compact axis, and the other has a long slender axis. They have performed approximately the same number of leaf initiation events, but they need not have achieved the same amount of longitudinal growth. Now consider two plants of equal height with similar leaf counts. The first has a single conspicuous main stem, whereas the second has several vigorous side branches. Again, similar initial organ production has resulted in quite different bodies. Developmental analysis must separate the production of potential sites for growth from the actual growth that occurs at those sites.

A shoot comprises an axis and the organs associated with that axis. The stem is the axial portion, whereas a leaf is a lateral organ. A node is a region of the stem at which one or more leaves are attached; an internode is the portion of the axis between successive nodes. An axil is the angle on the upper, or adaxial, side of a leaf base where the leaf meets the supporting stem. The axil can contain an axillary meristem, which may become enclosed within a bud. The meristem is the organized population of cells with the capacity to produce new shoot tissues. The bud is a larger structural entity that includes the meristem and may include small leaf primordia or protective structures.

A convenient descriptive unit of shoot construction is the phytomer. In a simple representation it consists of a node with its associated leaf and axillary growth site, together with an adjoining internode. Authors may choose the internode above or below a node as part of the unit, provided the convention is consistent. A phytomer is not an indivisible embryonic segment comparable in every respect to an animal somite. Its components arise through spatially connected but partially separable developmental processes. One can therefore recognize a normal node even when the associated axillary meristem fails to develop.

The shoot apical meristem, or SAM, continuously supplies cells to the growing shoot during vegetative development. The previously established SAM organization explains how a population of undifferentiated cells can persist while descendants are recruited into organs. The present problem begins at the point where descendants are recruited. Some will participate in constructing the main axis; others will contribute to leaves; and a small population in suitable positions can retain or reacquire the organization needed for a new meristem. These developmental outcomes allow an initially narrow apex to create a complex branching body.

Primary shoot growth means the construction and extension of the primary body. It should be distinguished from secondary growth, which increases the girth of an already established axis through the activity of lateral meristems in plants that possess them. A newly formed internode may increase in both length and diameter while still undergoing primary development. An increase in diameter alone is therefore not sufficient to identify secondary growth. One must establish the source and developmental context of the newly formed tissue.

2. From the shoot apex to the mature stem

The apex is not simply a cap of rapidly dividing cells. It is an organized structure whose regions contribute differently to the developing shoot. The central domain retains a population capable of renewing the apex. The flanks produce lateral organ primordia. Beneath the central region, cells contribute to internal tissues of the axis. The rib region is especially relevant to the establishment of the central stem body. These domains provide a useful functional description of the source of new tissues, although the exact boundaries and contributions vary with developmental stage and species.

A small developing shoot can be studied in longitudinal section. At its extreme tip, the outlines of leaf primordia appear close to the apex. Older primordia occur farther from the tip because new material has been produced above them and intervening stem tissue has grown. This positional succession supplies a useful developmental series: one section may contain several different ages of organs. The regions are not necessarily equivalent to fixed distances from the tip. If a mutation changes growth rates, a primordium located one millimetre from the apex in the mutant may differ in age from a primordium at the same distance in the wild type.

The cell walls of plant cells strongly constrain cell rearrangement. A daughter cell generally remains next to its former sister after cytokinesis, unlike cells in many animal tissues that can migrate over considerable distances. Patterning of the shoot must consequently be accomplished mainly by controlling the position and orientation of cell divisions and the relative rates and directions of expansion. This constraint explains why division planes can be important even when the total number of divisions is unchanged. Divisions that add cells along an axial file have a different geometric consequence from divisions that add files across the diameter.

The terms anticlinal and periclinal describe division orientation relative to the local tissue surface. An anticlinal division forms a new wall approximately perpendicular to the surface and can increase the number of cells within a surface layer. A periclinal division forms a wall approximately parallel to the surface and can generate additional layers. Neither term automatically specifies the axial length change of the entire stem because the local geometry may be curved. A longitudinal section and a transverse section provide complementary views of the same developmental process.

During early organ production, the apex creates the primary arrangements of nodes and internal tissues. Subsequent expansion separates many of the initially crowded nodes. New stem tissue is therefore not produced exclusively at a geometrical point at the very tip. Regions displaced from the extreme apex can remain proliferative or expansive. The developmental distance over which this occurs depends on the kind of shoot. A young eudicot internode can contain diffusely distributed dividing cells, whereas certain grass internodes maintain a concentrated proliferative region near the base.

The axial pattern is also coordinated with the differentiation of tissues. If the outer surface expanded substantially more slowly than the interior, mechanical stress would build between the tissues; if the interior expanded without establishing continuous conducting strands, transport to young organs would be compromised. Actual development couples growth with wall remodeling, vascular differentiation and the maintenance of tissue continuity. The coordinated behavior of a multicellular organ is not equivalent to summing the behavior of isolated cells growing independently in culture.

3. Nodes, internodes and the three primary tissue systems

The node is a recognizable point of organization because lateral organs are attached there. A leaf trace is a vascular connection between the vascular system of the stem and that of the leaf. The nodal region may therefore have a complex internal geometry in which conducting strands change direction or connect to organs. When an axillary bud becomes a growing lateral shoot, its supply and export routes also need to become functionally continuous with those of the supporting axis. A section taken through a node can look quite different from a section through the middle of an internode.

The internode is not merely an empty spacer. It contains the dermal, ground and vascular tissue systems that collectively support an elongated axis. The surface must protect growing tissues and permit appropriate exchange with the environment. The interior must transport water, mineral nutrients and assimilates while maintaining mechanical integrity. As the internode extends, these functions need to remain connected across increasing distances. The final length of an internode is consequently limited not only by developmental programs for elongation but also by the supply of materials and the ability of the tissues to accommodate deformation.

The protoderm is a primary meristematic tissue that contributes to the epidermis. Young epidermal cells remain connected in an external sheet. Their walls and cuticle provide a boundary between the living interior and the surroundings. In a growing internode, this sheet must accommodate increasing surface area through cell division and expansion. Some epidermal cells differentiate into specialized structures, including stomata or hairs in appropriate species and regions. The exact complement of epidermal cell types is not identical across stems; developmental identity should not be inferred solely from the presence of one surface structure.

Ground meristem gives rise to much of the ground tissue of the primary axis. In a typical young eudicot stem the ground tissue can be described in terms of a peripheral cortex and a central pith. Parenchyma cells often retain living protoplasts and can participate in storage or metabolism. Collenchyma provides flexible support in many growing organs through unevenly thickened primary walls. Cells that develop strongly thickened secondary walls, such as some sclerenchyma cells, provide more rigid reinforcement. Not all young stems contain all these cell types in the same arrangement. Their differentiation is related to the mechanical demands and developmental age of the organ.

Procambium establishes the primary vascular strands. Primary xylem and phloem differentiate in spatial relationship to one another and to the developing organs. Xylem provides routes for water and dissolved mineral transport and contributes to support. Phloem carries assimilates between sources and sinks. In a young stem that is still increasing in length, some early xylem conducting cells possess wall reinforcement compatible with elongation, whereas later elements may develop more extensive patterns of secondary wall deposition. The developmental distinction between protoxylem and metaxylem is thus connected to when conducting cells differentiate relative to surrounding growth.

A typical herbaceous eudicot stem has vascular bundles arranged in a ring, with xylem toward the centre and phloem toward the outside of each collateral bundle. In many monocot stems, such as grasses, bundles are distributed through the ground tissue rather than arranged in a single conspicuous ring. This is a comparison of common anatomical patterns, not a universal diagnostic rule for every angiosperm. The essential developmental principle is that procambial differentiation establishes connected vascular paths while the ground and dermal systems acquire their own characteristic organization.

Stem anatomy should be distinguished from root anatomy. A transverse section of a primary root frequently shows a central vascular cylinder in which xylem and phloem occupy alternating radial positions, whereas stem vascular strands commonly contain xylem and phloem side by side within a bundle. The next chapters investigate root meristem organization and root tissue differentiation in detail. For present purposes the distinction prevents a student from assuming that every axis has the same internal pattern simply because both roots and shoots elongate.

The connection of a new branch to the parent vascular system develops as a spatial pattern, not simply as the result of a tube being pushed into a pre-existing vessel. Young procambial cells differentiate along paths that link the developing axis and its lateral organs. Directional auxin movement and cell responses can contribute to the selection and reinforcement of such paths. This is a useful developmental bridge between the canalization concept and the mature anatomy of a shoot. It is important, however, to distinguish a functional vascular connection demonstrated by transport of a tracer from an anatomical strand identified by its shape or staining. A young strand may be present before all of its cells have reached functional maturity.

The time at which an axillary bud is examined also influences what appears to be its vascular connection. A small inhibited bud may have a connection with the parent stem sufficient for its maintenance while still lacking the transport properties required for rapid sustained growth. When the bud expands and produces new organs, the conducting capacity and differentiation of its tissues can change. Thus the observation that a dormant bud is supplied with water does not establish that it can immediately support a large branch. A useful comparative experiment would measure both transport into the bud and development of its export pathway at several stages of activation. Each observation would address a different aspect of the developing connection.

In the normal stem, successive conducting elements align to create pathways through tissues with very different developmental ages. The early xylem elements that mature while an internode is elongating often have annular or helical secondary wall thickenings, permitting surrounding tissue to extend more readily than if the entire wall were rigidly reinforced. Later elements may possess more extensive secondary reinforcement. Differentiation of water-conducting tracheary elements includes regulated loss of the protoplast, whereas mature conducting sieve elements of angiosperms remain living and depend on companion cells for support. This difference illustrates that tissue function can involve different developmental outcomes even in closely associated cell populations. Programmed cell death in xylem differentiation will receive fuller treatment in Subunit 5.E.

An experimental challenge is to distinguish a developmental failure of vascular connection from a growth defect that has secondarily prevented vascular maturation. If a new bud remains small because it has not activated, it may also show limited development of its conducting tissues. The correlation between low transport capacity and poor growth does not by itself establish which occurred first. An intervention that alters transport differentiation before the appearance of growth differences, combined with a time series of bud activation and anatomical observations, would provide a more informative causal test. Conversely, if activation occurs normally and only later extension fails as the branch becomes larger, limited transport capacity becomes a plausible explanation for the late phenotype.

The orientation of cellular growth can also be studied using an idealized cylindrical stem. If its length doubles while its radius remains constant, its approximate volume doubles; if its radius doubles with length unchanged, volume increases fourfold. This distinction matters when a mutant produces a thin elongated axis or a thick compact one. Equal increases in axial length do not imply equal tissue production, and equal fresh masses do not imply equal architecture. A sufficiently careful description of stem growth includes longitudinal extension, radial expansion and the developmental stage of the tissue. Comparisons of young herbaceous axes should also avoid assuming that all radial growth comes from secondary cambial activity.

4. How cells lengthen an internode

A plant cell increases its volume when water enters and the surrounding wall yields sufficiently to accommodate expansion. Turgor pressure is the hydrostatic pressure of the cell contents against the wall. It creates a mechanical driving force, but the existence of turgor does not establish that the cell is growing. A mature cell can remain turgid for long periods while its dimensions change very little because its wall does not yield irreversibly at the existing pressure. Growth requires a permissive wall as well as an adequate supply of water.

The primary wall consists of a hydrated network in which cellulose microfibrils provide strong reinforcement and matrix polymers contribute to its mechanical behavior. In an elongating cell the direction of reinforcement can influence the direction in which the wall yields most readily. In a simplified example, circumferential reinforcement limits radial expansion and permits preferential longitudinal extension. This is a useful geometric model rather than an assertion that all growing cells have a perfectly transverse and uniform cellulose arrangement. Newly deposited wall material, changing microfibril orientations and constraints from neighboring cells complicate actual tissue mechanics.

The vacuole plays an important role in enlargement by allowing cells to increase volume without synthesizing a proportional volume of dense cytoplasm. Solute accumulation lowers the osmotic component of water potential, permitting water uptake when an appropriate gradient exists. Membrane transport, metabolism and wall modification all require resources. An increase in fresh mass may therefore primarily reflect water uptake rather than a comparable increase in dry organic matter. In a growth experiment, fresh mass, dry mass, cell length and whole-organ length answer different questions.

A simple wall-yield model expresses relative volumetric growth rate as the product of wall extensibility and the excess of turgor over a yield threshold. When pressure is below the threshold the model predicts no irreversible expansion; above it, increasing pressure or extensibility can increase growth. This idealization assumes that hydraulic supply and wall properties are adequately described by constant parameters over the measurement interval. It does not directly predict axial elongation unless the relationship between changes in volume and changes in length is known. A cylindrical cell that expands mainly in length behaves differently from a cell expanding in three dimensions.

Cell production and expansion are also temporally coupled. A proliferating cell must attain sufficient size and biosynthetic capacity to complete the cell cycle. Its daughters inherit walls and cytoplasmic components and can subsequently expand. Later, many descendants cease division while continuing to enlarge. The number of cells in a mature axial file thus reflects the history of cell production, and their mean length reflects a combination of earlier division, expansion and differentiation. A final measurement cannot uniquely reconstruct these histories without observations at earlier stages.

Gibberellins are plant growth regulators that often promote internode elongation. Their developmental effect can involve changes in cell proliferation, cell expansion or both. A plant that is deficient in active gibberellin may be short because its internodes elongate less, even though it produces recognizable nodes. In a standard response pathway, bioactive gibberellin promotes removal of DELLA growth-repressing proteins through regulated proteolysis. A mutation that prevents hormone synthesis and one that prevents normal removal of a growth repressor can therefore produce similar short phenotypes yet differ in their response to externally supplied hormone. Detailed hormone metabolism belongs to plant physiology; here the important distinction is between production of organ sites and extension of the axis.

Mechanical constraints can modify the expression of growth potential. If an inner tissue is capable of faster expansion than a tightly constrained outer layer, the final tissue deformation depends on both. Removing a constraint can cause immediate elastic changes as well as later biological growth; these should not be confused. A short-term displacement after cutting a tissue is not, by itself, evidence that new cell wall material has been synthesized. Time-resolved measurements distinguish reversible deformation from persistent growth.

At the molecular level, the gibberellin receptor GID1 binds the hormone and interacts with DELLA proteins. In a canonical response, this promotes recognition of DELLA by a ubiquitin-ligase system and degradation through the proteasome. DELLA proteins normally constrain several growth-promoting transcriptional programs, so reducing their inhibitory activity permits a stronger growth response. The pathway is a useful example of regulation through removal of a repressor rather than direct synthesis of a new structural component. Some experimental contexts also reveal relief of DELLA-mediated repression without complete degradation, so the canonical model should not be presented as the only conceivable means of changing DELLA activity. The mechanistic distinction between reduced hormone supply and a stable inhibitory protein explains why some dwarf phenotypes respond to supplied gibberellin whereas others do not.

5. Why length and developmental rate must be measured separately

One can represent the length of a simple stem segment as the sum of the lengths of cells along an axial file. If the file contains N cells with average axial length l, its length is approximately N times l. This relation is useful for separating possible contributions of proliferation and expansion, but the approximation depends on an appropriate sampling geometry. A curved cell file, overlapping cells or a measurement taken obliquely can cause errors. It is also necessary to compare the same developmental region rather than unrelated parts of two plants.

Suppose an internode has a mature representative file of 100 cells with mean axial length 150 micrometres. The estimated file length is 15 millimetres. Another internode has 80 cells with mean length 180 micrometres, giving 14.4 millimetres. The second internode contains fewer cells even though its cells are longer. The similar organ lengths conceal two distinct cellular outcomes. A student should therefore resist interpreting a nearly normal final organ length as evidence that all of its developmental processes are normal.

The relative rate of elongation can be more informative than the absolute rate when initial lengths differ. If a segment increases from 5 to 10 millimetres in a day, its absolute increase is 5 millimetres. A segment increasing from 20 to 25 millimetres has the same absolute increment but a smaller proportional change. Under an exponential-growth approximation the mean relative elongation rate is the logarithm of the final-to-initial length ratio divided by elapsed time. This calculation does not imply that the organ actually follows exponential growth throughout its life; it summarizes proportional change over a defined interval.

A growing stem is also a record of organ production through time. The interval between successive leaf initiation events is called a plastochron. If a plant initiates organs faster, it may produce more nodes per unit time without necessarily increasing the length of each internode. A plant with slower organ initiation could nevertheless have a longer stem if its internodes elongate sufficiently. The rates of leaf placement and internodal extension are distinct variables, even though they may respond to shared developmental and environmental signals.

In a hypothetical comparison, plant A has twelve mature internodes averaging two centimetres and plant B has eight mature internodes averaging three centimetres. Each has a total internodal length of twenty-four centimetres. Plant A has more repeated axial positions at which lateral organs and potential branches can occur. The plants may therefore differ substantially in future architecture despite equal current stem length. A branching study that reports only height and branch number cannot determine whether the difference reflects branch activation probability, internode growth or the number of available axils.

Developmental timing is particularly important when interpreting a dwarf mutant. A plant examined at the same chronological age as the wild type may be at an earlier developmental stage. Its fewer leaves may reflect slower organ production, not failure to specify an organ at a particular node. Conversely, a dwarf with normal node count but shorter internodes offers more direct evidence of reduced axial elongation. Ideally, measurements include both time since germination and a staging criterion such as the number of initiated leaves or the age of a particular internode.

6. A lateral shoot begins in a leaf axil

Branch formation begins before an obvious side shoot appears. A young leaf forms at the apex and establishes a boundary with the stem. Near its adaxial base, a group of cells occupies a position in which a new axillary meristem can develop. This process is distinct from the formation of the leaf itself. A leaf can develop even if a normal axillary meristem fails to form, and a meristem can exist without producing a long lateral shoot. The relationship between leaf production and branch formation is therefore close but not obligatory at every subsequent step.

The new axillary meristem must organize a renewing cell population and the spatial relationships characteristic of a shoot apex. Once formed, it can initiate its own leaves and a new stem. Its descendants can eventually produce additional axillary meristems in the new axis. Branching thus creates a hierarchy: a primary shoot gives rise to a secondary shoot, which may generate tertiary shoots. These terms refer to the relative origin of axes, not to their absolute sizes. A secondary axis can become longer than the original axis if its growth conditions and developmental state favor it.

The distinction between specification of an axillary meristem and activation of a bud is central to developmental genetics. A defect in meristem specification reduces the number of sites from which a normal branch can arise. A defect in bud activation acts at a later stage: the potential branch site exists, but the bud does not enter sustained outgrowth. Mature plants with both kinds of defect can look similar from a distance. Inspection of the leaf axils, early developmental markers and responses to release from inhibition are required to separate the causes.

Evidence from Arabidopsis supports the retention of meristematic competence in cells within the developing leaf axil. Expression of SHOOT MERISTEMLESS, a KNOX-family transcription factor associated with shoot meristem identity, helps identify this developmental population. In this model, cells with appropriate competence remain at the boundary as neighboring leaf tissues pursue differentiation. The persistence of a molecular marker is not alone proof that every marked cell becomes a functional stem cell; lineage and perturbation experiments are needed to establish the relation between marker expression and future meristem formation.

Local signaling at the leaf axil differs from the signaling conditions that position a new leaf primordium. In Arabidopsis, a local reduction in auxin signaling is associated with the establishment of the axillary meristematic population, followed by a cytokinin response associated with niche formation. This context dependence matters. It is incorrect to reason that because auxin accumulation helps initiate a leaf primordium, increasing auxin everywhere on the flank of a shoot apex must also promote axillary meristem specification. The same hormone can have different developmental consequences depending on position, competence and timing.

Genes such as LATERAL SUPPRESSOR and REGULATOR OF AXILLARY MERISTEMS contribute to proper axillary meristem formation in model plants. Their mutant phenotypes help establish that normal leaf axils require specific developmental regulation rather than automatically becoming lateral shoots. The exact extent of a phenotype depends on genotype, developmental phase and the node examined. An examination question may describe failure of meristems in only a subset of axils; the correct reasoning is to recognize a defect in formation at those sites, not to assume that a gene has no role merely because some axils remain normal.

7. Establishing a new stem-cell niche

In the developing axil, a group of competent cells must undergo a change in organization before it can function as an autonomous shoot apex. Competence describes the ability to respond to a developmental signal, not the guarantee that an organ will form. An axil may contain a population that retains meristem-associated gene expression for some time before a recognizable dome emerges. At this stage it is misleading to describe the axil as containing a miniature branch with all the organs already specified. The establishment of the new axis and the production of its organs occur progressively.

The boundary between a leaf primordium and the primary axis is developmentally important because the two adjacent tissues are pursuing different patterns of growth. A leaf primordium expands outward, while the stem must maintain axial continuity. Cells in the boundary can differ from their neighbors in rates of division, expansion and expression of regulatory genes. The relatively restrained expansion of a boundary can maintain separation between neighboring structures. A meristem located within the boundary must ultimately initiate a new local pattern of growth without abolishing the distinction between the pre-existing leaf and stem.

To understand the evidence for this process, imagine observing the same developing axil at successive ages with a fluorescent reporter for SHOOT MERISTEMLESS expression. At an early stage a small population shows reporter activity. As the leaf grows and its tissues become distinguishable, activity persists in a restricted population near the boundary. Later a subset of the axil develops a recognizable meristem. Such a sequence supports the idea that meristematic competence has been maintained during organ differentiation. An alternative explanation would be that competent cells were lost and meristem identity was independently induced again. Distinguishing these alternatives requires tracking individual cells rather than simply comparing pictures from separate specimens.

Live imaging is valuable because it establishes the continuity of a cellular population. However, the fluorescence reporter has limitations: the time required to produce and degrade its protein can differ from the timing of endogenous transcription. A cell can remain fluorescent after its transcription rate changes. Imaging and marker expression therefore need to be interpreted together with independent indicators of cell behavior. If a regulatory gene is removed and the competent population disappears before niche formation, the combination of timing and genetic perturbation is much stronger evidence than a single photograph of a mature mutant.

A local auxin minimum can be created by the redistribution of auxin away from a population of cells. This does not require that the entire shoot be auxin deficient. A small domain at the leaf axil and a neighboring leaf primordium can experience distinct hormone environments because transporters are distributed asymmetrically and local cells differ in their capacity to respond. It is the spatial contrast, rather than a globally averaged concentration, that helps explain why one region can retain meristematic properties while another region begins the development of a determinate lateral organ.

Cytokinin signaling can then participate in establishing the functional niche. This does not mean that an isolated cell exposed to cytokinin necessarily becomes a complete axillary meristem. The appropriate cell population, positional environment and regulatory identity must be present. One can test this distinction with a developmental intervention that increases cytokinin response in an axil that has lost its meristematic competence. A failure to restore a normal meristem could indicate that the earlier developmental defect cannot be bypassed by the later signal alone. A successful rescue would instead suggest that some responsive cells remain or can regain the appropriate identity. Neither conclusion can be made without knowing the spatial and temporal specificity of the intervention.

Regulatory genes that maintain a population of competent cells and genes that allow the later population to organize a niche need not act at precisely the same time. For example, a perturbation that reduces STM expression from the beginning could produce a different defect from one introduced after the axillary dome has appeared. The first may prevent formation, while the second may impair maintenance of an already established meristem. The general principle is important for interpreting developmental genes: observing a missing mature structure does not identify the earliest causal stage. The stage of gene action must be inferred from observations made while the structure is developing.

Once a functional axillary meristem is established, its organizing functions are conceptually related to those of the primary SAM. It must maintain a sufficiently stable stem-cell population while generating cells for lateral organs and internal tissues. However, the size, position and timing of the two meristems may differ substantially. The lateral meristem develops in the context of an existing parent shoot and its environmental and physiological conditions, whereas the primary SAM was established during embryogenesis. They are related developmental systems, not anatomically identical structures in every respect.

8. The axillary bud and the decision to grow

A bud is not equivalent to an actively extending branch. It often contains an organized meristem and several small organ primordia, but these structures may remain compact for a long interval. During this time the meristem can be alive, and limited development or metabolism may continue. Growth inhibition should therefore not be equated with death or complete physiological inactivity. A viable bud has the potential to begin or resume a more extensive developmental program if conditions change.

It is helpful to distinguish bud formation, bud activation and sustained branch elongation as successive operational stages. Formation establishes the organized lateral growth site. Activation refers to the early transition toward increased developmental activity, which can be detected by growth measurements, changes in metabolism or appropriate molecular markers. Sustained outgrowth entails continued production and expansion of branch tissues. These stages overlap in time, and no single universally applicable molecular threshold separates them. Nevertheless, they identify different classes of developmental problems.

For example, a bud may swell after the removal of the main apex and then cease extending. Swelling is evidence that something has changed in the bud, but it does not establish that a self-maintaining branch has formed. The growth could be a transient response to water uptake or mobilization of reserves. A more informative experiment records bud length at several intervals, the appearance of new leaves and the persistence of the growth response. Establishment of a lateral shoot requires not only activation but also continuing access to nutrients and a functioning relationship with the main axis.

The term dormancy covers several biologically distinct conditions. In a growing herbaceous plant, inhibition of a viable bud by other actively growing parts of the same plant is often described as paradormancy. In some perennial plants, a bud can become internally dormant and require particular seasonal conditions before it becomes capable of renewed development. Growth may also be prevented by unfavorable external conditions despite the absence of a strong intrinsic block. The latter situations are commonly distinguished as endodormancy and ecodormancy, respectively. These categories are useful conceptual distinctions, but they should not be treated as identical processes simply because none produces visible branch elongation at the time of observation.

An axillary bud normally receives signals and resources from several tissues. Its connection to the parent axis determines access to water and assimilates, while nearby leaves and growing apices can influence the allocation of those resources. The ability of the bud to develop its own export routes and later its own photosynthetic surfaces changes these relationships over time. The developing branch is not an independent organism isolated from the supporting plant; it is a new developmental axis embedded within an integrated physiological system.

A useful examination distinction follows from the fact that not every available bud becomes a visible branch. Suppose a plant forms twenty axillary buds but only four produce sustained branches. Another plant forms ten buds and four branches. The raw branch count is the same, but the proportion of available buds that grew is twenty percent in the first plant and forty percent in the second. The denominator is essential. If the question concerns bud activation, the relevant denominator should be viable buds rather than simply all nodes, especially when comparing mutants that differ in axillary meristem formation.

9. Apical dominance and the logic of classical experiments

The characteristic architecture of many young plants includes a vigorous main axis and a smaller number of lateral branches. In such plants, removal of the growing apex often permits one or more axillary buds below the cut to grow. This phenomenon is the basis of the classical concept of apical dominance. The apex is dominant in the sense that its presence contributes to suppression of growth at certain lateral sites. It need not completely inhibit every bud, and the strength of suppression differs among species, genotypes and environmental conditions.

Classical experiments separated the effects of removing a shoot apex from those of replacing a chemical signal associated with the apex. In the standard design, an intact plant is compared with a decapitated plant and with a decapitated plant to which auxin is supplied at the cut surface. If lateral buds grow after removal of the tip but are inhibited when auxin is supplied to the stump, auxin can restore part of the inhibitory influence under those conditions. Historically this type of experimental reasoning, associated particularly with work by Thimann and Skoog, established a connection between auxin and apical dominance. It did not establish that the natural inhibitory action of the apex consists only of auxin diffusing directly into every dormant bud.

A rigorous design includes a decapitated plant with the same application vehicle but no added auxin. A paste or solvent could affect the wound environment, and the cut itself could change water relations and initiate wound responses. The plants should be matched for developmental stage, bud position and growth conditions. Because buds at different nodes can behave differently, comparing the uppermost bud of one plant with a basal bud of another is not a clean test. Measurements need to be made at equivalent positions in independently replicated plants.

The auxin replacement experiment provides evidence of sufficiency in a limited experimental sense: supplied auxin can suppress outgrowth in a decapitated context. It does not establish that all other effects of decapitation have been restored or that auxin is the only necessary component of the intact apex's influence. For example, a growing apex consumes assimilates, whereas a paste containing auxin does not reproduce the same metabolic demand. This difference becomes especially important when examining the earliest events that occur after removal of the tip.

Apical dominance should be distinguished from apical control in the broader study of plant architecture. The latter expression is often used for the influence of a dominant axis on the relative growth or orientation of branches that are already developing, particularly in woody plants. The distinction is useful because branch initiation, early activation and the later hierarchy of existing branches are not interchangeable events. An intervention that allows suppressed buds to activate may not produce a final plant in which every lateral axis becomes equally long.

Removal of the primary tip can also change which remaining shoot becomes dominant. If one lateral branch grows rapidly and develops its own active apex, it can influence other growth sites. The result may be a new leader rather than a permanently leaderless plant with equivalent branches. This observation reveals the dynamic nature of branching regulation: the primary axis is not uniquely privileged because of an immutable embryonic label. Relative positions, ongoing growth and physiological connections can allow a lateral axis to assume a dominant role.

A strong conceptual test of apical dominance involves contrasting a branching-deficient plant whose axillary meristems are absent with one whose buds are merely suppressed. Removing the tip can release an existing competent bud but cannot instantly replace a missing meristem. If a branch later appears at an initially empty site, the experiment must consider delayed meristem initiation or adventitious regeneration, not simply activation of an invisible dormant bud. Early anatomical observations make the later phenotype interpretable.

10. Auxin and the indirect regulation of bud outgrowth

Auxin is produced in growing shoot tissues including the apex and young leaves. Directional transport through the stem creates a basipetal auxin transport stream, meaning that the net movement in this context is toward the base of the shoot. This does not imply that auxin is transported identically in every tissue of the plant. In particular, the direction of transport through a mature stem and the local transport patterns within a developing leaf axil are distinct spatial situations.

In many classical branching models, the main stem's auxin transport stream influences an axillary bud indirectly. The auxin supplied by the primary apex need not enter the bud at a concentration sufficient to act as a direct local inhibitor. An indirect explanation is necessary because the developmental relationship between the bud and the parent axis depends on the transport and signaling properties of the connecting tissue. In a simplified model, changes in stem auxin status alter the production or availability of other signals that do act within or near the bud.

Cytokinins often promote bud outgrowth. Changes in auxin status of the main axis can modify cytokinin production, transport or availability in the nodal environment. Cytokinins may originate from roots or from shoot tissues, and their effects depend on the plant's developmental and nutritional context. It is therefore insufficient to memorize that roots make cytokinin and shoots make auxin as if the hormones had exclusive sources. The meaningful developmental inference is that the influence of a growing apex can be transmitted through changes in the signaling environment of lateral growth sites.

A second explanation concerns the ability of the lateral bud to establish an auxin export pathway into the parent stem. An actively growing shoot produces auxin and normally exports it toward its base. If an existing strong transport stream makes it difficult for a lateral bud to establish an effective export route, its sustained outgrowth may be inhibited. The canalization model links directional transport to a feedback process in which auxin flow and the organization of transport capacity reinforce one another. This provides a possible mechanistic basis for competition between different growing apices without requiring the apical auxin itself to act as a direct inhibitor inside the bud.

The second-messenger and canalization explanations emphasize different aspects of an integrated process. The first stresses regulation of local growth-promoting or growth-inhibiting signals by the main axis. The second stresses the establishment and competition of transport pathways. A result in which manipulation of stem auxin changes lateral outgrowth may be compatible with both. To distinguish the contributions, one would need measurements of signaling responses, transport capacity and developmental timing rather than relying only on branch counts.

Directional movement is especially relevant in interpreting inhibitor experiments. If an inhibitor of polar auxin transport is applied to the main stem, its effects will depend on where it is placed relative to the apex and the bud. Interfering with the main axis above a bud is not equivalent to interfering with the developing bud's own export route. Transport inhibitors can also affect other auxin-dependent processes, including vascular differentiation and organ positioning. A whole-plant treatment that produces fewer branches is therefore not decisive evidence for a single simple direction of auxin action.

The same distinction helps explain why auxin can be associated with both growth promotion and branching suppression. Within an elongating tissue, auxin can participate in the regulation of wall properties and cellular expansion. Within the integrated shoot, a strong apical auxin stream can contribute indirectly to maintaining lateral buds in an inhibited state. These effects operate at different levels of organization. A molecule has no universal label as either a promoter or inhibitor of the whole plant's growth; its developmental effect must be specified with respect to the process, tissue, concentration and time being considered.

11. Strigolactones and the integration of branch-inhibitory signals

Strigolactones are a class of carotenoid-derived signaling compounds with important roles in plant development. One of their major shoot-related effects in many species is suppression of excessive branching. Genetic studies in several model plants revealed unusually branched mutants in which production of, or response to, these signals is impaired. In Arabidopsis, mutations in members of the MORE AXILLARY GROWTH pathway can produce such phenotypes. In rice, related developmental regulation influences the number of lateral shoots called tillers.

A hormone pathway can be disrupted at several different steps. A biosynthetic mutant may fail to produce enough active signal while retaining the capacity to respond to it. A perception mutant may produce the compound but fail to recognize or transmit its presence. Both classes may have similar high-branching phenotypes in untreated plants. A suitable treatment with a biologically active signal or analogue can sometimes distinguish them: a biosynthetic deficiency may be rescued, whereas a fully insensitive genotype should not show the corresponding specific rescue. Differences in delivery, compound stability and partial alleles must be considered when actual experimental responses are interpreted.

At an introductory mechanistic level, the strigolactone receptor D14 participates in signaling that involves the MAX2/D3 family of F-box proteins. Regulated degradation of downstream repressors in the SMXL/D53 family is a key part of the signaling logic. Arabidopsis and rice differ in the names and precise relationships of the relevant proteins, so terminology should be tied to the species. The important developmental consequence is that an intact signaling pathway can help constrain bud outgrowth, while impaired signaling can release some of this restraint. Detailed biosynthetic reactions and receptor chemistry are reserved for plant physiology.

The pathway does not operate independently of auxin and cytokinin. Changes in stem auxin status can influence the production of branch-regulating signals, and strigolactone responses can affect properties of auxin transport and the state of the bud. A model in which each hormone has an isolated, additive effect is useful only as a first approximation. Real developmental regulation may involve interactions: a cytokinin response might have a different effect when strigolactone signaling is strongly active than when it is impaired.

The Arabidopsis transcription factor BRANCHED1, or BRC1, is a member of the TCP family and has been studied as a local integrator of signals affecting branching. Higher BRC1 activity is commonly associated with inhibition of axillary bud outgrowth in the contexts in which it functions. Reduced BRC1 function can allow greater branching. Its relationship to the related maize gene teosinte branched1 illustrates how regulatory genes can have conserved roles in shoot architecture while their exact expression patterns and phenotypic effects differ among species.

An association between BRC1 expression and bud growth does not establish that BRC1 is the sole switch controlling every axillary bud. Buds integrate several constraints, including assimilate availability, developmental state and transport relationships. A bud with reduced BRC1 function may still fail to develop if it lacks sufficient resources or a competent meristem. Similarly, high BRC1 expression in a suppressed bud might be part of the causal mechanism, a response to another signal or both. A gene's expression pattern should be interpreted together with loss-of-function, rescue and other functional evidence.

A useful genetic thought experiment combines a mutation reducing axillary meristem formation with a mutation reducing branch suppression. If the first mutation eliminates the competent lateral growth sites completely, the second cannot generate ordinary branches from those missing sites merely by releasing a downstream inhibitory pathway. Where meristems remain, the second mutation may increase the probability of outgrowth. This conditional prediction is more biologically meaningful than asserting that one mutant phenotype will always dominate another regardless of developmental context.

An important advance in the study of branching came when highly branched mutants were used to connect previously inferred growth-inhibitory signals to strigolactone-related compounds. The 2008 work of Umehara and colleagues linked such compounds with suppression of rice tiller outgrowth. At a later molecular level, the discovery of regulated turnover of D53-like proteins made it possible to explain why a defect in signal perception can have a phenotype resembling insufficient signal production. In Arabidopsis, SMXL6, SMXL7 and SMXL8 contribute to this pathway. A mutant protein that remains active despite the presence of ligand can therefore produce a different response from a simple loss of biosynthetic capacity. This is a clear instance in which the direction of the phenotype depends on whether a mutation removes a positive component of signaling or prevents removal of an inhibitory component.

12. Carbon supply, timing and the earliest release of a bud

A growing shoot apex requires a continuous supply of carbon compounds. It produces new cells and cell walls, maintains metabolism and establishes developing organs. The very young leaves surrounding it may not yet export enough assimilates to support their own growth. Such regions are sinks, meaning that they import resources from other tissues. Mature photosynthesizing leaves commonly function as sources of assimilates. An axillary bud begins in a physiological environment where several sinks can compete for supplies from the same plant.

Decapitation simultaneously removes a source of regulatory signals and a strong sink. These two effects are difficult to separate if observations are made only after several days. However, measuring changes soon after decapitation makes it possible to ask whether the first detectable growth of the bud occurs before or after an expected change in another variable. Work in pea by Mason and colleagues in 2014 provided evidence that changes in sugar availability can contribute to the early release of buds after decapitation. The results motivated an integrated explanation of apical dominance rather than a replacement of every hormone-dependent mechanism by a single nutritional cause.

Temporal reasoning is essential to the interpretation. Suppose a bud begins to increase in length within a few hours after removal of the apex, while a measurable decline in auxin in the relevant part of the stem occurs later. The later decline cannot be the sole immediately preceding trigger for the observed early growth under those conditions. However, the measurement may not detect a localized or earlier change in some cells. Moreover, auxin can still be important for maintaining or terminating subsequent growth. An experiment demonstrating that one process precedes another narrows the causal possibilities without necessarily establishing a complete mechanism.

Sugars contribute both matter and signals to growing tissues. A developing bud requires carbon skeletons for new cellular components and substrates for respiratory metabolism. An increase in available assimilates may also alter gene expression and growth-regulating processes before the additional carbon has been incorporated into visible new organs. These two roles are not easily separated by observing growth after external sugar application. Appropriate controls should distinguish metabolic provision from changes in osmotic conditions and consider whether the concentration used is physiologically meaningful.

A plant with many established branches has more growing apices and often more young leaves competing for resources. As some leaves mature, they may become sources that support further branch development. Hence the effect of producing an additional branch is not necessarily a permanent reduction in the resources available to all other shoots. During its early development the branch may be a net sink; later, its mature leaves may contribute to the assimilate supply of the plant. Changes in branch growth need to be interpreted over the appropriate time scale.

The nutrient status of the plant influences this resource economy. A well illuminated plant can still have limited growth if mineral nutrition restricts the formation of proteins, nucleic acids or other components required for proliferation. A nutrient-deficient plant can alter the relative investment in roots and shoots. Branching responses may involve hormonal integration of nutrient status rather than simple passive competition for carbon alone. For example, phosphate availability can influence strigolactone-related responses in several species, but the direction and magnitude of a whole-plant branching phenotype depend on the wider physiological context.

A student can design an informative conceptual experiment by comparing four groups of otherwise similar plants: intact controls, decapitated plants, plants receiving additional assimilate at a suitable site without decapitation, and decapitated plants in which source availability is reduced. Bud growth, sugar availability and stem auxin should be assessed at equivalent nodes over time. No single treatment is perfectly specific, since source manipulation can change other signals. The advantage of the comparison is that several alternative mechanisms produce different patterns of early and late responses.

If increased sugar availability is followed by early bud expansion but later growth ceases under sustained apical dominance, the observation suggests that early release and sustained branch development can have different limiting requirements. A sugar-related early response is not a contradiction of a later role for auxin export or strigolactone-dependent regulation. The complete developmental process requires a sequence of permissive states, not a competition among explanations in which only one variable is allowed to matter.

13. Grass shoots and intercalary growth

A grass shoot provides a particularly instructive comparison with a typical herbaceous eudicot. A grass stem, often called a culm, contains conspicuous nodes separated by internodes. The leaves commonly have a sheath that surrounds part of the stem and a blade that extends away from it. During some stages the successive nodes are crowded near the base of the shoot. When internodes elongate, the axis changes greatly in height even though much of the underlying nodal pattern was established earlier.

An intercalary meristem is a proliferative region located between more differentiated tissues rather than at the extreme tip of an organ. In many grasses, intercalary activity occurs near the bases of developing internodes or in the basal growth regions of leaves. Its location allows new cells to be added locally while previously formed cells farther along the organ enlarge and mature. A grass internode can therefore continue extending from a growth region near its base rather than relying solely on the direct displacement of cells from the terminal SAM.

The direction in which newly produced cells are displaced can be understood by marking positions on a young internode. Suppose a series of ink marks is placed along the surface of a growing stem segment. If most elongation occurs near the base, the distances between marks in that region increase more than distances between marks in the mature upper region. The marks farther above the growing zone are carried upward as new tissue extends beneath them. Their displacement in space should not be mistaken for local growth at their own positions. Local growth is revealed by a change in the distance between neighboring marks, not by the total displacement of an individual mark.

In many lawn grasses, removal of the upper parts of leaf blades does not necessarily eliminate the entire population of cells capable of producing further leaf length. Basal growth zones may remain intact. This helps explain the capacity of some grasses to recover from grazing or mowing. Nevertheless, the response depends on the position of the cut, the developmental stage and the amount of functional leaf tissue and reserves remaining. A treatment that removes the shoot apex, basal meristems and viable buds cannot be expected to produce the same recovery as a treatment that removes only distal leaf tissue.

A grass branch commonly develops as a tiller. Tillers arise from axillary sites and can establish their own series of leaves, nodes and internodes. Some later become reproductive shoots. Their developmental origin distinguishes them from adventitious roots that may also emerge near basal nodes. The concentration of basal branching in many grasses can produce a tufted form, but not all grasses share the same arrangement. Rhizomes and stolons provide additional examples of horizontal stem growth and new shoot production at nodes.

A rhizome is a stem that commonly grows below the ground and can form new aerial shoots from buds. A stolon is a stem that grows horizontally along or near a surface and can establish new shoots or roots at its nodes. The distinction between these structures and roots is developmental as well as anatomical: their nodes, reduced or expanded leaves and associated buds reveal their shoot identity. A horizontal orientation or subterranean position alone is not sufficient to classify an organ as a root. The presence of characteristic nodal organization is especially useful when a modified stem has a form very different from an upright aerial shoot.

The placement of intercalary growth regions also changes the consequences of a perturbation. In an experiment where an apical meristem is removed after several grass internodes have already been specified, some extension of existing internodes may continue if their local growth zones remain functional. New terminal organ initiation, however, cannot continue normally from a removed apex. If a surviving axillary meristem begins a new shoot, that is a separate source of growth. It would be inaccurate to conclude from continued height increase alone that the original shoot apical meristem had regenerated.

Arabidopsis provides a contrasting arrangement. During its vegetative rosette stage, many leaves are produced in a compact cluster because the corresponding internodes remain short. When the main shoot undergoes the reproductive transition, axial extension can produce a conspicuous flowering stem. This change illustrates that production of nodes and elongation of internodes are developmentally regulated independently enough to yield sharply different architectures in a single organism. The signals that initiate reproductive identity will be considered separately in the flowering chapter.

14. Architectural patterns and changes in axis identity

Shoot architecture is the spatial arrangement of the main axis and lateral axes, including the positions from which branches arise, the lengths of those axes and their relative orientations. This form changes throughout the life of a plant. A young seedling, a mature vegetative plant and a flowering individual may show quite different architectural patterns even when their genotype is the same. The developmental state of meristems and their interactions with environmental conditions are important determinants of the visible body.

In monopodial growth, a single persistent primary axis contributes substantially to the extension of the shoot while lateral axes arise from it. In sympodial growth, the apparent continuation of an axis can be produced by successive lateral meristems after the preceding terminal meristem ceases its extension or changes identity. A continuous-looking stem is therefore not always the product of one uninterrupted apical meristem. The developmental origin of successive segments must be traced to determine whether the architectural pattern is monopodial or sympodial.

A determinate shoot has a developmental limit to the extension of its axis, for example when its terminal meristem becomes a structure that does not continue producing new axial units. An indeterminate shoot retains the capacity to continue producing organs for an extended period under suitable conditions. Indeterminate does not mean immortal. Physical damage, unfavorable conditions, senescence or a developmental transition can terminate growth. Conversely, termination of one shoot axis does not necessarily terminate development of the entire plant if additional meristems remain capable of growth.

The concepts of monopodial and sympodial development are distinct from the concepts of determinate and indeterminate growth at the level of an entire plant. A plant can maintain continuing overall shoot production by repeatedly using determinate modules. The succession of axes can create an apparently indeterminate architectural system even though individual constituent shoots terminate. This illustrates why it is necessary to specify the biological level at which a property is being described: a single meristem, a particular axis or the entire plant.

The position of a branch does not completely determine its ultimate orientation. Branch growth angle can depend on differential growth within the branch, mechanical loading, responses to gravity and light and the developmental identity of the branch. A branch emerging from a particular side of a node might become vertical, horizontal or oblique. The location of its meristem determines the initial site of origin, whereas its later shape and orientation reflect subsequent growth. The detailed mechanisms of tropisms belong to plant physiology; the developmental distinction between origin and orientation is sufficient here.

Branch length similarly cannot be inferred from meristem number. A shoot that activates several buds may distribute growth among many short axes, while a shoot with fewer activated buds may produce a small number of long axes. To compare architecture quantitatively, one can record branch order, position, length and the number of nodes on each branch. A single branching index is useful for a defined question but can conceal major differences in the arrangement of the resulting shoot system.

The distinction between terminal and axillary axes also has implications for the response to damage. A primary apex that has been destroyed is no longer available to produce its normal sequence of organs. A surviving axillary bud can compensate by initiating a new axis, but the resulting architecture may differ from the original. Even if the plant eventually reaches a similar height, the developmental history is different. Replacement by an existing lateral meristem should not be described as restoration of the same original apex unless anatomical or lineage evidence demonstrates that restoration.

Some plants possess additional buds that may form near existing axillary structures or arise adventitiously outside the usual leaf-axil positions. These alternative routes increase the flexibility of architecture and regeneration. Their existence does not invalidate the typical axillary origin of ordinary shoot branches. Rather, they illustrate that the developmental competence of plant tissues is not always limited to the normal position of meristems. Establishing the origin of a new shoot is necessary before assigning it to a particular developmental pathway.

15. Environmental plasticity and comparative genetics

The extent of shoot branching often depends on light conditions. In a crowded plant community, neighboring vegetation can change the spectral quality of the light reaching a shoot even before severe reductions in total light occur. A low ratio of red to far-red light can act as a signal of nearby vegetation in many species. Developmental responses frequently include changes in stem elongation and reduced growth of lateral buds. Such a response can favor vertical extension under competition, although the magnitude and combination of responses are species dependent.

The effect of spectral quality must be distinguished from the effect of reduced photosynthetic resource supply. Both can occur under a canopy, but they are not equivalent interventions. If an experiment changes the red-to-far-red ratio while maintaining comparable photosynthetically useful light, it can help isolate a signaling effect from a simple reduction in carbon assimilation. Conversely, reducing irradiance while keeping spectral quality comparable may alter the assimilate economy without reproducing all responses to neighboring vegetation. The developmental phenotype is a useful readout of how environmental information is integrated with growth.

Water limitation can restrict internodal expansion through changes in water availability and the regulation of growth. A plant might develop fewer branches under dry conditions because some buds remain inactive or because growing branches fail to sustain extension. The same final branch count could result from earlier developmental differences if the drought occurred during the establishment of axillary meristems. A time-resolved study of the environmental treatment is necessary to determine which stage is affected. The phenotype of a mature plant is the accumulated result of conditions experienced at several developmental stages.

Temperature can alter the timing of organ production and tissue maturation. If one plant produces nodes more slowly at a low temperature, it may have fewer axils available for branching at a particular chronological age. A lower branch count in that plant does not automatically indicate stronger apical dominance. A fair comparison may require counting the proportion of eligible axils that form branches, examining plants at equivalent developmental stages and distinguishing differences in activation from differences in the time available for outgrowth.

The contrast between domesticated maize and its wild relatives illustrates that changes in developmental regulation can contribute to striking evolutionary differences in shoot form. The gene teosinte branched1 is an important contributor to the suppression of lateral shoot growth associated with the more strongly dominant main axis of domesticated maize. Genetic evidence connects variation in regulation of shoot branching to changes in plant architecture during domestication. It would be an oversimplification to attribute every morphological distinction between maize and teosinte to this single locus, since domestication involved numerous genetic and developmental changes.

Comparisons of maize TB1 with Arabidopsis BRC1 demonstrate the value of studying related regulators in different organisms. Shared ancestry and similarities in molecular function can suggest that related processes use related regulatory components. However, the developmental structures controlled by those components occur in different architectural contexts. A tiller in a grass and a branch arising from a rosette leaf axil in Arabidopsis both represent lateral shoot development, but their growth patterns, timing and contributions to the final plant differ. Comparative developmental reasoning must preserve both the common principle and the organism-specific context.

Selection on architecture can affect agricultural performance, yet there is no universal optimal number of branches. In some cereals, a limited number of vigorous reproductive shoots may be desirable under particular cultivation conditions. In other crops, greater branching may support more flowering sites or improve canopy coverage. Resource supply, planting density and the developmental fate of the branches all influence the outcome. The useful lesson is that variation in meristem placement and bud activation can generate selectable differences in architecture, not that one branching phenotype is always superior.

The effect of genotype on branching is also not independent of the environment. A mutation that increases bud outgrowth under favorable conditions may produce little visible change when resource availability is strongly limiting. Conversely, a mutation that alters the sensitivity of buds to an environmental signal may show a strong phenotype only under the relevant light or nutrient regime. Such genotype-by-environment interactions are biologically meaningful and should not be dismissed as inconsistent results. Controlled comparisons across environments can help identify which aspect of the regulatory system has changed.

The relationship between light and branching was tested particularly clearly in Arabidopsis by comparing normal plants with plants defective in BRC1 under light treatments differing in red-to-far-red ratio. The observation that loss of BRC1 weakens the suppression of buds in the tested shade context supports a role for this local regulator in conveying environmental information to developmental behavior. Matching photosynthetically active radiation makes the experiment more informative than merely moving one plant into darkness. It still does not imply that BRC1 accounts for every effect of shade on plant architecture. Stem extension, changes in leaf orientation and altered developmental timing involve additional responses, so the specific bud-growth measurement is important for locating the contribution of the tested gene.

16. Evidence from growth measurements and tissue development

The observation that a shoot becomes longer is relatively easy to make. Identifying the tissue region responsible for this change requires a more carefully designed measurement. One approach is to place a succession of small reference marks along a growing internode and photograph their positions at defined times. The intervals between marks define segments whose changes in length can be calculated separately. A segment that doubles in length in a day is behaving differently from one whose length remains constant, even if both are carried upward by the growth of tissue below them.

Consider a hypothetical internode with four successive regions, each initially five millimetres long. After one day their lengths, from base to apex, are eight, seven, five and five millimetres. The net growth of the marked interval is five millimetres, but sixty percent of that growth occurred in the first region and forty percent in the second. The other regions may contain metabolically active cells without undergoing measurable axial expansion. These results are consistent with concentrated growth near the base, but they do not prove the existence of an intercalary meristem: the expanding basal region might contain cells that are elongating without dividing. Histological evidence of proliferating cells would be necessary to identify the meristematic contribution.

Cell division can be investigated using markers associated with DNA synthesis or mitotic activity. A labeling pulse for DNA synthesis marks cells undergoing the S phase during the exposure period. A mitotic index instead reports the fraction of examined cells that are in identifiable stages of mitosis. Neither measurement is identical to the rate of cell production. A high mitotic index can result from a long mitotic duration rather than a high frequency of cell division. Similarly, a low fraction of labeled nuclei might reflect the timing of a short pulse relative to cell-cycle dynamics. Combining markers with cell counts and repeated observations gives a more reliable picture of the developing internode.

The choice of sampling denominator affects interpretation of tissue sections. If a mutant has larger cells, an equal-area microscopic field can contain fewer cells even when the total number in the entire internode is not reduced. Counting cells per unit area would then confound cell dimensions with cell production. A student should ask whether the measured quantity is cell density, total cell number, cells per representative axial file or a fraction of cells in a particular state. The biological conclusion must correspond to the actual quantity measured.

Cell elongation itself can be estimated by measuring cell dimensions at successive positions or stages. A positional series is useful only if the relationship between distance and developmental age is reasonably understood. Cells in an upper portion of a grass internode may be older than those near its basal growth zone. In a different organ, the arrangement may not be the same. A mutation that changes displacement rates can alter the relationship between position and age, which is why comparing equally aged cells or consistently staged tissues is important.

The quantitative relation between cell number and length can be extended to compare fractional changes in both variables. Let an internode's initial representative file length be L0 = N0 l0 and its later length L1 = N1 l1. Dividing the expressions gives L1/L0 = (N1/N0)(l1/l0). If cell number increases by twenty percent and average cell length by fifty percent, the resulting file length is 1.2 times 1.5, or 1.8 times its initial value. The two fractional changes multiply rather than simply add. This is because enlargement occurs in a file whose number of cells has also changed. The relation describes the measured endpoints; it does not identify whether cells divided before, during or after the period of rapid enlargement.

For sufficiently small simultaneous changes, the change in the natural logarithm of length equals the sum of the changes in the logarithms of cell number and mean cell length. Thus the relative length-growth rate can be decomposed into contributions from the relative cell-number change and relative change in mean length. This follows by differentiating the logarithm of the product, not from an independent biological assumption about gene regulation. If the file loses cells from a defined measurement region because cells move out of it as growth proceeds, its net cell-number change must not be interpreted as the number of divisions without accounting for that movement.

In a real multicellular stem, many files contribute to growth, and different cell types need not share a single mean length or division history. A comparison based on one epidermal file may identify a useful surface phenotype without explaining changes in vascular or internal ground tissues. A strong exam problem might supply separate measurements from two tissue layers. If their final axial dimensions must remain approximately coordinated, differences in average cell length can be compensated by different cell numbers. This shows why the whole-organ phenotype and the cellular phenotype cannot be treated as interchangeable observations.

17. Pressure, wall yielding and the energetics of elongation

An elongating internode must sustain water entry and controlled yielding of its cell walls. Water movement is governed by water potential, which in a simplified description includes a pressure component and a solute component. Solute accumulation lowers the solute potential of the cell, whereas turgor raises its pressure potential. At equilibrium with an external water source the net flow can approach zero even though the cell retains substantial turgor. Persistent growth requires the capacity for continued wall expansion and water uptake, not simply a high equilibrium pressure.

The wall-yield model associated with Lockhart can be introduced as a balance between mechanical driving force and the wall's resistance to irreversible deformation. Let P denote turgor pressure and Y denote a threshold pressure for wall yielding, both in megapascals. If the wall extensibility coefficient phi is expressed in inverse megapascals per hour, the product phi times the positive difference between P and Y has units of inverse hours. It represents a relative rate of irreversible expansion in an appropriate idealized model. The rate is set to zero when P does not exceed Y, since a negative value from the simple expression should not be interpreted as irreversible contraction.

The model clarifies why the same turgor can be associated with different growth rates. Suppose two cells both have a pressure of 0.6 megapascals and a yield threshold of 0.4 megapascals. If the second cell's effective wall extensibility is twice that of the first, its predicted irreversible relative expansion rate is twice as great. On the other hand, increasing pressure from 0.6 to 0.7 megapascals with the threshold and extensibility unchanged increases the excess pressure from 0.2 to 0.3 megapascals, or by fifty percent. The growth response is related to the excess above the threshold, not to the percentage change in total pressure alone.

When a growing cell's wall yields, its volume can increase and pressure can fall unless water enters to compensate. The pressure measured in a living growing cell is therefore not independent of wall mechanics and hydraulic supply. A cell with more extensible walls may grow faster without exhibiting greater measured turgor than a slower-growing cell. Conversely, a cell under water limitation may be unable to sustain the predicted wall-yield rate. The simple equation is most useful when its assumptions are explicit and when pressure, wall properties and observed growth can be compared over the same interval.

Growth also requires metabolic work beyond the mechanical expansion of an existing wall. Active transport can maintain solute gradients, membrane synthesis supplies material for an increasing surface area, and wall biosynthesis replenishes or reorganizes the polymer network. Respiration supplies energy for many of these processes. Hydrostatic pressure provides a driving force for expansion, but it should not be regarded as a substitute for the metabolic capacity needed to construct living tissue. This explains why cells with similar osmotic conditions may behave differently when their developmental or metabolic states differ.

Expansins are a useful example of proteins associated with regulation of wall extensibility. Their action can facilitate wall yielding without simply digesting the entire cellulose framework. The extracellular pH can influence aspects of wall behavior, and proton transport across the plasma membrane contributes to the regulation of that environment. The acid-growth concept provides a framework for connecting a hormonal input to wall mechanics in suitable growing tissues. It is not a claim that every elongating internode can be fully explained by a single decrease in extracellular pH. Developmental stage, wall composition, tissue interactions and hydraulic supply remain important.

There is an important distinction between an increase in elastic compliance and an increase in irreversible wall yielding. An elastic tissue changes dimensions when a force is applied and can return toward its original dimensions when the force is removed. Irreversible growth involves persistent changes in geometry accompanied over time by wall remodeling and addition of material. A measurement of extension under a brief applied force may contain both components. If the length returns to its initial value after removal of the force, the observed extension cannot all be classified as permanent biological growth.

These considerations are relevant to an internode under temporary water stress. A decrease in turgor may cause a rapid reversible reduction in dimensions as well as inhibition of irreversible growth. When water availability is restored, some apparent increase in length may be recovery of elastic deformation rather than production of new tissue. Measurements taken only immediately before and after rewatering may therefore overestimate newly generated length. Longer observations and appropriately hydrated controls help distinguish the two contributions.

No numerical value for wall extensibility is universal across plant species or tissues. Even within an individual internode, the properties of a young expanding cell may differ from those of a mature cell with secondary wall reinforcement. The purpose of the mathematical model is to make explicit predictions about relative changes under stated assumptions. It cannot replace developmental evidence about the type of tissue, location of growth zones and signals responsible for changing their properties.

18. Testing hormone and gene function using developmental phenotypes

Developmental genetic experiments are particularly informative when a phenotype is assigned to a defined stage. Consider three hypothetical Arabidopsis genotypes. The first produces normal leaves but lacks recognizable meristems in several axils. The second has organized buds at those positions but very few branches. The third produces many branches whose internodes remain unusually short. A casual description of all three as abnormal shoot mutants loses the most important information. Each may affect a different developmental process: specification of lateral growth sites, activation of buds or axial extension after activation.

The first genotype can be studied by inspecting young axils before mature architecture is apparent. If early meristem-associated markers fail to appear, the defect may precede niche establishment. If early markers occur normally but are lost before a stable dome forms, the defect may instead affect maintenance or the transition to an organized niche. If the axillary dome forms normally but later degenerates, the absence of a mature bud has yet another explanation. Time of observation is therefore a central component of the causal interpretation.

The second genotype invites a different type of experiment. The presence of organized and viable axillary buds suggests that their formation has occurred. Removal of the dominant apex could reveal whether those buds can be released by changing the physiological context. If they grow after decapitation, the phenotype is compatible with stronger suppression or altered sensitivity to conditions in the intact plant. If they fail to grow, the defect might be intrinsic to the bud, or the parent plant might lack a resource or signaling capacity required for outgrowth. One should not immediately conclude that meristem identity is defective simply because removal of the apex did not restore a branch.

For the third genotype, measurements of cell number and mean length within mature branch internodes provide a direct way to distinguish potential cellular causes. A treatment that restores internode length without changing branch number may rescue axial growth while leaving regulation of bud activation unaltered. The result does not imply that the treatment is specific to one gene; it identifies the developmental output that was rescued. Genetic and biochemical evidence would be needed to determine whether the treatment acted directly on the disrupted pathway or compensated for the defect through a parallel mechanism.

Hormone-rescue experiments must include both an untreated wild type and an untreated mutant. A treated wild type is also useful because a compound may have substantial effects on normal plants. If the mutant changes from a highly branched form to a nearly wild-type form after a signal is supplied, that is informative only when the treatment is delivered under conditions in which the signal is active and the comparison is appropriately staged. A rescue at a nonphysiological concentration may bypass normal signaling or cause unrelated growth inhibition. Thus the mere convergence of final branch counts is not necessarily evidence of a specific molecular rescue.

Grafting offers another conceptual way to distinguish a defect in production of a mobile signal from a defect in response within a target tissue. A shoot of one genotype can be connected to a compatible root system of another genotype, with appropriate controls for graft healing. If a normal root system partly corrects a highly branched shoot phenotype, the result is consistent with a mobile influence originating below the graft or with a change in the general nutritional or physiological state of the shoot. It does not identify the transported molecule by itself. Conversely, failure of a normal root system to rescue a shoot phenotype does not prove that the pathway is entirely cell autonomous, since the signal may normally be produced in a shoot region or may fail to reach the target under the experimental conditions.

Reciprocal grafts are valuable because they separate the genotype of the shoot from the genotype of the supporting root. A normal shoot on a mutant root may behave differently from a mutant shoot on a normal root. The interpretation depends on the production and response functions of both genotypes. In an idealized system where the root is the sole source of a mobile inhibitory signal, restoring signal production below the graft would be expected to rescue a biosynthetic-deficient but responsive shoot, whereas an unresponsive shoot would remain insensitive. Actual branching signals can originate in more than one organ, so the idealized prediction is not a universal description of every graft experiment.

Genetic interactions can also be interpreted through developmental sequence. If a gene is required to form axillary meristems and another gene normally suppresses outgrowth of established buds, the absence of the former's function can remove the physical substrate on which the latter acts. A double mutant may therefore lack lateral shoots at positions where meristems never form even though the downstream inhibitory pathway is disrupted. This illustrates a type of developmental dependence. It does not necessarily prove that the products of the two genes interact directly or that they form a single linear biochemical pathway.

A reporter for signaling response should likewise be distinguished from a direct measurement of hormone concentration. A reporter may respond differently in cells with altered receptor abundance or transcriptional competence. Two cells exposed to the same concentration of a hormone may therefore exhibit different reporter activity. A mutant with diminished cytokinin-reporter fluorescence could have lower cytokinin availability, impaired signaling, or a change in the identity of the cells being measured. Additional evidence is needed to discriminate among these explanations.

The strongest reasoning combines complementary approaches: anatomical observations locate the affected developmental stage, genetic perturbation tests the importance of candidate regulators, rescue experiments assess the capacity of downstream functions, and temporal measurements determine whether a proposed cause precedes its effect. None of these methods alone answers every question about shoot development. Together they make it possible to explain rather than merely classify an abnormal shoot phenotype.

19. Integrating the shoot as a developing system

The completed primary shoot provides a physical record of successive developmental decisions. Its node count records repeated organ production, its internode lengths reflect regional growth and maturation, and its branches reveal which potential lateral axes became established and active. These observations can be organized into a simple developmental sequence, but the sequence is not a rigid schedule. In a living plant, a new node may be forming at the apex while an older internode elongates, an older axillary bud becomes activated and leaves farther down the shoot supply assimilates to both processes.

The coexistence of these events explains why a whole-plant mutation may affect several features of architecture. A gene that regulates meristem identity can change the number of available lateral sites. A gene involved in cell expansion can change the physical distances between those sites. A signal regulating bud activity can alter the number of competing growing axes. These effects may interact because changing the number or vigor of axes alters the physiological environment experienced by other meristems. A final phenotype is thus not necessarily the sum of independent defects measured in separate tissues.

The relationship can be expressed using a conditional framework. Let the total number of eligible nodes on a specified primary shoot be n, and let m be the number of those nodes with viable axillary buds at the start of an observation period. If b of those buds produce sustained lateral shoots during the period, then b/n describes realized branching per eligible node. The fraction m/n describes the availability of bud sites, and b/m describes the conditional outgrowth of the buds, provided m is nonzero. Multiplying the latter two fractions gives b/n. This identity is not a complex model of development; it makes explicit why a change in either site availability or activation can affect the same final outcome.

The assumption that the set of buds is fixed during the period is important. New buds can form, some buds may cease to be viable, and a branch can produce its own nodes and buds. If these new axes are included in the branch count but their source nodes are omitted from the denominator, the calculated proportion can be misleading. The experiment must specify whether it concerns first-order branches on one parent axis or branches throughout the whole plant. The number of branches per primary node can even exceed one in unusual cases involving additional buds, so the simple framework applies only when each eligible node contributes at most one counted bud and one corresponding branch.

The interaction between developmental state and external signals has implications for interpreting plant plasticity. An environmental cue may be applied at a stage when some axils have competent meristems and others have not yet established them. The resulting mature plant can exhibit a positional pattern of response because older and younger regions experienced the same external treatment at different developmental stages. Such a pattern should not automatically be attributed to a gradient of the treatment itself. A uniform treatment can generate a nonuniform phenotype when tissue competence varies along the axis.

The coordinated development of tissues is equally important in explaining why individual parts of an expanding shoot cannot be considered independently. A new branch requires functional transport routes to the main axis, enough ground tissue for support and an expanding epidermal surface that remains continuous. An experimental manipulation that enhances meristem initiation without enabling these supporting tissues to develop would not necessarily result in viable mature branches. Developmental success is measured by the construction of a functional organ system, not by expression of one meristem marker.

The root system develops in parallel with the shoot and provides water, nutrients and physiological signals that influence its growth. Roots themselves have their own apical meristems and can initiate lateral organs, but the developmental origins of ordinary lateral roots and axillary shoots differ. A lateral root commonly originates internally from specific cell populations of the parent root, whereas an ordinary lateral shoot begins at an axillary meristem near the surface of the stem and leaf boundary. The next chapter examines how the root apex maintains its stem-cell population while constructing a different kind of axial tissue organization.

A student who understands shoot development should be able to move between the anatomical description of a plant and explanations at the cellular and regulatory levels. Seeing a compact shoot should prompt questions about organ initiation and internode length. Seeing a branchless plant should prompt questions about the existence and viability of buds, the conditions controlling their activation and the ability to sustain outgrowth. Seeing a plant with many short branches should prompt separate measurements of the number of active axes and the growth of each axis. This approach is more reliable than assigning a hormone or a gene to a phenotype from memory without asking what developmental process has changed.

Worked quantitative examples

All numerical datasets below are hypothetical teaching examples.

Example 1. Cell count and elongation

A reference axial file has 150 cells of mean length 80 µm. A mutant has 100 cells of mean length 120 µm. Estimate both lengths.

L = N × l. Control: 150 × 80 µm = 12,000 µm = 12 mm. Mutant: 100 × 120 µm = 12,000 µm = 12 mm. Both lengths are equal despite different cell number and dimensions. A representative straight file and comparable stages are assumed.

Example 2. Relative elongation rate

An internode extends from 10 to 16 mm during 12 hours. Estimate mean absolute and logarithmic relative growth rates.

Absolute rate = (16 − 10)/12 = 0.50 mm h⁻¹. Mean relative rate = ln(16/10)/12 = 0.470004/12 = 0.03917 h⁻¹. The relative value summarizes fractional growth over the interval; it does not prove a constant instantaneous rate.

Example 3. Wall yielding

Use relative growth rate g = phi × max(P − Y, 0), with phi = 0.25 MPa⁻¹ h⁻¹ and Y = 0.35 MPa. Compare P = 0.55 and 0.30 MPa.

At 0.55 MPa, g = 0.25 × 0.20 = 0.05 h⁻¹. At 0.30 MPa, the pressure is below threshold, so g = 0. The model assumes fixed effective wall properties and adequate hydraulic supply; it describes irreversible relative expansion, not all mechanical deformation.

Example 4. Partitioning branching defects

Two genotypes each have 24 eligible nodes. A has 18 viable buds and 9 sustained branches. B has 12 viable buds and 9 branches.

A: availability = 18/24 = 75%; conditional outgrowth = 9/18 = 50%; realized branching = 9/24 = 37.5%. B: availability = 12/24 = 50%; conditional outgrowth = 9/12 = 75%; realized branching = 37.5%. Equal realized branching masks different formation and activation outcomes. Counts concern first-order buds only.

Example 5. Local internode growth

Four equal marked segments start at 4 mm each and end at 7, 6, 4 and 4 mm. Determine total increase and its basal-region contribution.

Initial total = 16 mm; final total = 21 mm; increase = 5 mm. Region one increases by 3 mm, accounting for 3/5 = 60%. Region two accounts for 40%; other regions show no measured local extension. The result locates expansion but cannot distinguish cell division from enlargement.

Example 6. Multiplicative cell growth

A representative axial file changes from 100 cells of mean length 90 µm to 125 cells of mean length 126 µm.

Initial L = 9,000 µm = 9 mm. Final L = 15,750 µm = 15.75 mm. Ratio = 1.25 × 1.4 = 1.75, a 75% increase. Adding 25% and 40% would give 65%, missing the interaction term. Straight comparable files and no unmeasured movement across the sampling boundary are assumed.

Question 1. Which structure is the normal site of initiation of an ordinary lateral shoot?

  1. Mature vessel element
  2. Root cap
  3. Axillary meristem
  4. Internodal epidermis at an arbitrary position
Answer and reasoning — C

Axillary meristem

The axillary meristem establishes a new shoot axis in the leaf axil. Mature vessels conduct water, root caps protect root tips, and an arbitrary internodal cell is not the normal site of ordinary axillary branching.

Question 2. Which description best distinguishes a node from an internode?

  1. A node bears a leaf attachment; an internode lies between successive nodes.
  2. A node is always the only dividing part; internodes never divide.
  3. A node is an underground organ; internodes are aerial.
  4. An internode is the meristem inside a bud.
Answer and reasoning — A

A node bears a leaf attachment; an internode lies between successive nodes.

The distinction is positional and anatomical. Internodes can contain proliferating and expanding tissues; neither location above ground nor bud identity defines a node.

Question 3. The immediate developmental precursor of primary vascular tissue in the stem is:

  1. Protoderm
  2. Ground meristem alone
  3. Mature pith
  4. Procambium
Answer and reasoning — D

Procambium

Procambial strands establish the primary xylem and phloem. Protoderm gives rise to epidermis, and ground meristem to ground tissue; mature pith is not the normal immediate vascular precursor.

Question 4. Which statement about primary shoot growth is most accurate?

  1. It consists exclusively of cambial activity.
  2. It can include both cell production and enlargement of the primary axis.
  3. It is identical to formation of secondary xylem.
  4. It ceases whenever a leaf is initiated.
Answer and reasoning — B

It can include both cell production and enlargement of the primary axis.

Primary development creates and extends the primary body. Cambial secondary growth is distinct; lateral organ initiation and primary axial growth can occur together.

Question 5. A plant cell has turgor but is not increasing in size. Which explanation is compatible with this observation?

  1. Turgor necessarily means that the cell is dividing.
  2. Its wall does not yield irreversibly at the prevailing pressure.
  3. The cell must have no water.
  4. All cellulose has disappeared.
Answer and reasoning — B

Its wall does not yield irreversibly at the prevailing pressure.

Turgor is a mechanical driving force, not sufficient evidence of growth. Wall yielding and water supply are needed; turgid cells contain water, and neither mitosis nor cellulose disappearance follows from pressure.

Question 6. In a common young eudicot stem collateral vascular bundle, which arrangement is typical?

  1. Xylem faces outward and phloem faces inward in all bundles.
  2. Both tissues are always outside the epidermis.
  3. Neither tissue is derived from procambium.
  4. Xylem faces inward and phloem faces outward.
Answer and reasoning — D

Xylem faces inward and phloem faces outward.

The common collateral bundle has internal xylem and external phloem. The other options contradict typical stem organization or developmental origin; atypical patterns exist across species.

Question 7. Which term describes the interval between successive leaf initiation events?

  1. Plastochron
  2. Mitotic index
  3. Yield threshold
  4. Relative water content
Answer and reasoning — A

Plastochron

A plastochron is an organ initiation interval. The mitotic index is a cell-state fraction; yield threshold is mechanical, and relative water content describes hydration.

Question 8. What does an axillary bud usually contain?

  1. Only fully mature xylem elements
  2. A root cap and columella
  3. A meristem and often small organ primordia
  4. An already fully elongated branch
Answer and reasoning — C

A meristem and often small organ primordia

A bud contains the prospective shoot growth centre and frequently small organs. It need not have extended into a visible branch, and root-cap structures are characteristic of roots.

Question 9. In Arabidopsis axillary meristem establishment, which signaling sequence is supported by developmental evidence?

  2. A universal high-auxin maximum in every axil and no cytokinin response

  4. Complete elimination of meristem-associated expression in the entire axil

A local auxin minimum followed by cytokinin-associated niche establishment

A local low auxin response and subsequent cytokinin signaling are consistent with the establishment of a competent axillary niche. A leaf initiation auxin maximum cannot be generalized to the axil, and competence remains important.

Maintenance of meristem-associated identity and competence

SHOOT MERISTEMLESS is a KNOX transcription factor involved in meristem identity. It is not a lignin enzyme or a photosynthetic product and is not defined by root-cap function.

Removing a dominant apex and observing outgrowth of existing lateral buds

Favor outgrowth in an appropriate context.

Cytokinin often favors bud activation; its effect is context dependent. It is neither a universal inhibitor of mitosis nor a structural wall polymer.

Apical auxin can influence buds indirectly through other signals and transport relationships.

Increased outgrowth of available buds

Near its base

Answer and reasoning — B

The later detected decline cannot alone account for the earlier observed response.

A measured event occurring later cannot be the sole immediate precursor of an earlier event. Local undetected changes and later hormonal effects remain possible.

Question 37. A plant under low red-to-far-red light has long stems and few branches. Which comparison isolates a spectral signaling effect most effectively?

  1. Change spectral ratio, temperature and water simultaneously.
  2. Compare spectral ratios while holding photosynthetically useful light and other conditions comparable.
  3. Compare a drought-stressed plant with an unwatered mutant of unknown age.
  4. Measure only chlorophyll color in one plant.
Answer and reasoning — B

Compare spectral ratios while holding photosynthetically useful light and other conditions comparable.

Controlling light quantity and other variables reduces confounding. Simultaneous changes make it difficult to attribute the phenotype to spectral quality.

Question 38. A short-stem mutant has normal numbers of initiated leaves. Applied gibberellin restores internode length, without altering leaf count. What is supported?

  1. The mutant must have no SAM.
  2. Gibberellin created the missing nodes.
  3. The treatment rescues axial extension, not necessarily the entire causal pathway.
  4. The treatment proves the exact mutated gene.
Answer and reasoning — C

The treatment rescues axial extension, not necessarily the entire causal pathway.

Normal node production and restored elongation distinguish the developmental outputs. Hormonal rescue does not by itself identify the disrupted gene or prove direct action.

Question 39. A rootstock of wild-type genotype partly suppresses overbranching of a grafted mutant shoot. Which conclusion is defensible?

  1. A mobile or systemic influence may contribute; chemical identity requires more evidence.
  2. The transported molecule is definitely strigolactone.
  3. All shoot regulation occurs only in roots.
  4. The mutant shoot has no receptor for any signal.
Answer and reasoning — A

A mobile or systemic influence may contribute; chemical identity requires more evidence.

Graft rescue is compatible with a systemic signal or improved physiological state. It is not enough to name the molecule or exclude shoot contributions.

Question 40. A stem has 80 cells of average length 120 µm in a representative file. Later it has 100 cells of average length 180 µm. By what factor did the estimated length increase?

  1. 1.25
  2. 1.50
  3. 2.75
  4. 1.875
Answer and reasoning — D

1.875

The ratio is (100/80) × (180/120) = 1.25 × 1.5 = 1.875. The other options ignore one factor or add instead of multiply.

Question 41. A bud swells for 6 h after a treatment but produces no new organs or persistent length increase over the next week. Which interpretation is best?

  1. A fully functional mature lateral branch is proven.
  2. The axil had no living cells.
  3. An early response occurred, but sustained branch outgrowth was not established.
  4. The bud must have lost all vascular tissue.
Answer and reasoning — C

An early response occurred, but sustained branch outgrowth was not established.

Transient swelling is insufficient evidence of continuing organogenesis or stable extension; it does not establish death or complete vascular absence.

Question 42. A mutant has early axillary STM expression but the signal disappears before an organized meristem appears. Which conclusion is most precise?

  1. Early competence-associated expression occurred, but subsequent maintenance or organization may be defective.
  2. Meristem formation is proven complete.
  3. The gene must directly synthesize auxin.
  4. The observed loss can only reflect a defect in mature branch elongation.
Answer and reasoning — A

Early competence-associated expression occurred, but subsequent maintenance or organization may be defective.

The timing places the observed problem between early marker expression and dome formation. A reporter alone cannot prove the molecular cause.

Question 43. A plant has 8 mature internodes of 3 cm each and another has 12 of 2 cm each. Which is correct?

  1. The second is twice as tall.
  2. Both span 24 cm of internodes, but they differ in nodal opportunity.
  3. The first necessarily has more branches.
  4. Both have the same plastochron.
Answer and reasoning — B

Both span 24 cm of internodes, but they differ in nodal opportunity.

Equal aggregate length does not imply equal node count or branching probability. A plastochron requires time-resolved initiation data.

Question 44. Two adjacent tissue layers have the same mature axial length. One contains twice as many cells per representative file as the other. Under the file model what follows?

  1. Its mean axial cell length must be twice as large.
  2. Its mitotic index is necessarily twice as high.
  3. Its cells must have twice the turgor.
  4. Its mean axial cell length is approximately half as large.
Answer and reasoning — D

Its mean axial cell length is approximately half as large.

For equal Nl, doubling N requires halving l. The final dimensions alone do not establish mitotic index or pressure.

Integrated examination formats

Question 45. Assertion: An intact axillary meristem can coexist with a branchless phenotype. Reason: A viable bud may be inhibited after meristem formation.

  1. Both statements are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
  3. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  4. Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and reasoning — D

Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion.

Bud formation and outgrowth are separable; suppression of an existing bud explains why a plant with axillary meristems may lack visible branches.

Question 46. Assertion: Equal final internode lengths prove equal cell production. Reason: Length depends on both number and mean axial length of cells in an idealized file.

  1. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  2. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  3. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  4. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
Answer and reasoning — A

The assertion is false, but the reason is true.

Compensating cell-number and length changes can produce identical organ lengths. The reason describes the model correctly and contradicts the assertion.

Question 47. Match I protoderm, II procambium, III ground meristem with a primary derivative: 1 vascular tissue, 2 epidermis, 3 pith/cortex.

  1. I–1, II–2, III–3
  2. I–3, II–1, III–2
  3. I–2, II–1, III–3
  4. I–2, II–3, III–1
Answer and reasoning — C

I–2, II–1, III–3

Protoderm contributes the epidermis; procambium establishes primary vascular tissues; ground meristem contributes ground tissues such as pith and cortex.

Question 48. Which statements are correct? I. A plastochron measures an initiation interval. II. Intercalary growth can displace distal tissue. III. A viable inhibited bud is necessarily dead. IV. Primary growth can involve cell expansion.

  1. I and III only
  2. I, II and IV
  3. II and III only
  4. III and IV only
Answer and reasoning — B

I, II and IV

I, II and IV describe distinct developmental phenomena correctly. III contradicts the meaning of a viable inhibited bud.

Question 49. A hypothetical dose experiment yields 10 branches in a control, 5 after ligand, 9 in a perception mutant without ligand and 9 after ligand. Which statement is warranted?

  1. The pattern is consistent with reduced specific responsiveness of the mutant.
  2. The ligand necessarily destroys five meristems in every plant.
  3. The mutant has a complete absence of all branches.
  4. The experiment proves the ligand's biosynthesis occurs in the root cap.
Answer and reasoning — A

The pattern is consistent with reduced specific responsiveness of the mutant.

Only the normal genotype shows a large response under these conditions. Counting branches cannot locate signal synthesis, demonstrate meristem destruction or prove complete insensitivity without additional doses and measurements.

Question 51. A grass shoot keeps elongating existing internodes after its terminal apex is removed, but no new terminal leaves appear. Which combined explanation is strongest?

  1. A new primary apex must have formed immediately.
  2. Local growth zones remain active while terminal organ initiation has been lost.
  3. No cell division or expansion is needed for elongation.
  4. The plant's root cap took over leaf initiation.
Answer and reasoning — B

Local growth zones remain active while terminal organ initiation has been lost.

Local growth of existing internodes and continued initiation of new terminal organs are distinct. The observation does not show regeneration of the removed apex.

Question 52. An experiment counts 20 primary axils, 16 viable buds and 8 first-order branches. Later branches themselves produce 12 second-order shoots. Which statistic describes primary bud outgrowth?

  1. 20/16 = 125%
  2. 20/20 = 100%
  3. 8/16 = 50%
  4. 12/20 = 60%
Answer and reasoning — C

8/16 = 50%

The numerator must count outgrowth of the initial primary buds. Second-order shoots belong to another developmental level; including them changes the question and denominator.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Node equals meristem. A node is a structural region of attachment. Meristematic cells can be present in its axil but the terms are not interchangeable. Examination focus: Identify which structure actually produces a branch.
  2. Internodes never divide. Young internodes can proliferate, and some grass internodes have intercalary meristems. Examination focus: Do not infer mature tissue from location alone.
  3. SAM supplies all height increase directly. Cells below the apex divide and expand; existing internodes can extend after tip loss. Examination focus: Separate terminal initiation from axial elongation.
  4. Primary growth means length only. Primary body construction includes radial development as well as extension. Examination focus: Distinguish tissue origin from geometric direction.
  5. High turgor guarantees growth. Irreversible expansion also requires wall yielding and sufficient water supply. Examination focus: Apply the threshold before calculating growth.
  6. Equal stem length means equal cell number. Cell number and mean cell length may compensate. Examination focus: Calculate N × l rather than comparing lengths alone.
  7. Axillary meristem equals visible branch. A bud can remain viable but inhibited. Examination focus: Check early anatomy before assigning a branching defect.
  8. Auxin promotes every meristem at high concentration. The Arabidopsis leaf axil uses a local low-auxin context, distinct from leaf initiation sites. Examination focus: Do not transfer a leaf-primordium mechanism to the axil.
  9. Decapitation tests only auxin. It removes a signal source, a metabolic sink and tissue, producing several simultaneous changes. Examination focus: Demand controls and early time-course evidence.
  10. Apical auxin necessarily enters the bud. Indirect hormonal and transport-based models do not require direct entry. Examination focus: Separate whole-stem treatment from local action.
  11. Cytokinin is made only in roots. Shoot and root sources contribute depending on context. Examination focus: Avoid exclusive-source assumptions.
  12. Strigolactone pathway mutants all respond to ligand. A complete perception defect cannot be specifically rescued simply by supplying ligand. Examination focus: Distinguish biosynthesis from perception.
  13. BRC1 is sufficient to explain all branching. It is an important integrator, but resources, transport and competence also matter. Examination focus: Interpret double mutants and environmental effects carefully.
  14. Bud swelling proves a sustained branch. A brief early response may arrest. Examination focus: Require persistent extension and organ production.
  15. A horizontal underground organ is a root. Rhizomes are stems recognizable by nodes and buds. Examination focus: Classify by anatomy and developmental origin.
  16. Indeterminate means never ending. Meristems retain growth capacity under suitable conditions but can cease or change identity. Examination focus: Specify individual axis versus whole plant.
  17. A moving marker demonstrates local growth. Movement can result from expansion elsewhere; interval change measures local extension. Examination focus: Use neighboring reference marks.
  18. A reporter measures hormone concentration directly. Reporter output depends on response competence and protein kinetics. Examination focus: Use independent concentration and response measurements.

Revision and student mastery

Integrated conceptual summary

Primary shoot development creates a repeated nodal pattern, continuous dermal, ground and vascular tissues, and elongating internodes. A new axillary meristem establishes a lateral growth site; subsequent activation and sustained outgrowth are separately regulated. Apical signals, transport relationships, resource supply and the developmental competence of the bud shape the final branching architecture. Grass intercalary growth illustrates how existing segments may extend away from the terminal apex.

Essential relations

Representative axial file: L ≈ N × l. Mean relative elongation rate: ln(L₂/L₁)/(t₂ − t₁). Idealized wall yielding: g = phi × max(P − Y, 0). For one viable bud per eligible node: realized branch fraction = bud availability × conditional outgrowth.
